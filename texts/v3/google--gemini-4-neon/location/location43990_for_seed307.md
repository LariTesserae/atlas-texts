Far above the treeline—far above the last stunted lichens, above the altitude where clouds flatten into dull white shelves—the mountain narrows to a blade of black rock that looks as though it were split from the earth only yesterday. Its flanks are sheer, vitreous, and unweathered, scored with fresh conchoidal fractures whose edges could still slice a boot sole clean through. Nothing has had time to round them.

Tucked into the windward cheek of this spire, invisible from twenty paces off, is a pocket no larger than a pantry.

A twin fault in the young basalt has pried the rock apart just enough to leave an alcove four paces deep and barely two across, roofed on one side by a leaning fang of obsidian and left open on the other to the ordinary, unremarkable glare of afternoon daylight. From the lip of the pocket, the world drops straight away into pale blue emptiness and distant cloud-tops. Inside, the air hits like the mouth of a kiln.

It is an impossible heat for this height—thick, radiant, and wet. The freshly sheared walls sweat without pause. Even though the black stone is hot enough to blister bare skin on contact, condensation beads across every vertical face in heavy, shivering droplets that cling longer than water should before sliding down into shallow scallops along the floor. Warm mineral trickles seep from hairline seams in the rock, carrying pale sulfur crusts and greasy translucent films of silica—the only patina on the raw stone. There is no moss here, no grass, no root-thread or green spore, not even the scorched ghost of a plant. Only glass, basalt, mineral salt, and hot water pooling in finger-deep basins that never quite cool and never quite manage to boil away.

At first glance, the wet rock merely looks rough, crusted with pale pebbles and dark blisters. Then a blister shifts, reveals six translucent legs, and drags a splinter of chipped glass across a wet shelf.

The entire pocket is packed with living things.

They layer the walls two and three deep, crowded into every millimeter of slick stone, wedged shoulder to shoulder along the steaming waterlines, suspended in the warm basins, clinging upside down under the obsidian overhang. Most are small—no bigger than a thumb, a walnut, a flattened palm—and built for heat that would cook ordinary flesh. There are pale, vitreous-shelled crawlers with jointed limbs like blown glass; wide, pleated ribbon-things that ripple over the wet sulfur crusts; squat, many-eyed scavengers whose damp hides look boiled pink; quick, needle-legged hunters that skate across the scalding meniscus without breaking it. Where one creature ends, another presses against its flank. The floor of the pocket is less a stone surface than a slow-churning mosaic of carapaces, wet membranes, and twitching palps, all gleaming under plain midday sun.

And almost all of it happens in silence.

There should be a roar here—water hissing on furnace stone, thousands of hard shells clacking, mandibles grinding, tiny fights erupting every second. Instead, the alcove holds a dry, muffled hush. The extreme altitude thins the air, yet the quiet feels heavier than thin air can explain, as though sound here has to push through warm oil. A sudden scuffle between two armored crawlers raises only a faint, papery rustle. A thermal vent weeping superheated water at the back of the niche makes no hiss at all—only a dull, steady pressure felt behind the molars. Now and then a sharp *tick* arrives from somewhere near your ankle, half a breath after you watched one creature strike another across the pocket.

That strike is what makes you look closer at the stones lying in the pools.

Nothing in this cleft is built. There are no burrows lined with mud, no stacked cairns, no woven cups, no sheltered lodges. The creatures do not assemble anything, nor do they keep territory long enough to shape it. Yet the pocket is strewn with tools.

Because the rock is so young and the heat cycles continually stress the seams, razor-thin flakes of obsidian and sharp triangular chips of basalt regularly pop loose from the walls. The fauna have learned every angle of those splinters. Along a steaming ledge no wider than a forearm, a cluster of pale, knuckle-sized scavengers grip black glass wedges in their wet fore-claws, prying them beneath the clamped shells of dome-backed mineral grazers. One uses a round, denser pebble of basalt—carried from a lower basin, smooth from generations of being clutched and dropped—to crack the chalky rim of a prey's carapace with quick, rhythmic taps. Each tap lands silently; the tiny *clack* drifts upward a beat later from the wrong wall.

Nearby, a slender, six-limbed predator holds a hollow calcified spine taken from a dead competitor, dipping it between the plates of a larger crawler to siphon heat-rich fluid while pinning the victim down with a wedge of raw quartz. In a shallow pool, two flat-bodied scavengers wrestle over a single curved shard of volcanic glass—not for the meat on it, which is already gone, but for the edge itself, fresh and keen enough to shear through the tough mineral biofilm that coats the deepest crevices. Everywhere you look, sharp flakes, blunt hammering stones, and stripped chitin levers are being lifted, jammed into seams, dropped in panic, and snatched up again by something smaller before they settle. It is an entire frantic Stone Age compressed into the space of a closet, operating without walls, fire, or language.

Watch long enough in the clear, honest daylight, and you begin to see that the silence is not the only law failing here.

A heavy bead of condensation swells on the underside of the obsidian roof. Before it lets go, concentric ripples bloom smoothly across the warm basin beneath it—widening, intersecting the pool's edge, and only then does the drop detach and fall neatly into the center of its own aftermath. The water in the basin itself behaves strangely at the margins: where the heat is fiercest, tiny silver bubbles form inside the liquid and shrink inward instead of rising, vanishing without disturbing the mirror-flat surface.

Then you notice the shadows. The afternoon sun pours straight into the open mouth of the cleft, crisp and ordinary, casting sharp dark silhouettes beneath every crawling body. Yet several shadows sit a finger's width beside the things that cast them. A needle-legged hunter darts across a wet shelf and vanishes behind a fold of basalt, while its shadow lingers on the sunlit stone for two full heartbeats before sliding briskly after it.

The space itself refuses to keep an honest tally of its own measurements.

You can see the whole pocket at a glance—you could nearly bridge its width with your elbows—yet a palm-sized slab of weeping rock holds far more ground than its edges allow. Follow a pink-hided scavenger as it flees across that narrow ledge with a basalt hammer-stone clutched to its chest: it runs furiously, legs blurring, past three steaming rivulets and four other hunts, for fifteen unbroken seconds without coming any closer to the lip of the shelf. The fresh fissures in the young rock do not simply go inward. A translucent crawler slips headfirst into a hairline crack on the sunlit left wall, and before its rear legs have finished disappearing, its head and gripping claws emerge from a dripping seam on the shaded right wall, dragging a shard of obsidian that is still wedged in the first crack.

Even the creatures themselves seem only half-bound to remaining singular.

On a blisteringly hot dome of wet glass near the center of the floor, a broad, plated grazer molts. Its old shell splits down the dorsal seam with a faint, delayed pop. The pale soft body crawls out, immediately snatches a flake of fresh basalt from the pool, and begins scraping sulfur-crust—while the shed shell closes its seam, lifts a separate splinter of glass in its hollow pincers, and scuttles off in the opposite direction to hunt. Neither seems troubled by the other. Two hunters lunging for the same quarry pass briefly through one another's flanks like reflections crossing on dark water, then abruptly turn dense and violent again the instant one brings a stone down onto the prey's back.

At intervals—perhaps every half hour, though time feels slack and uneven in the heat—the spire beneath the pocket exhales.

A slow pulse of deeper furnace-warmth rises through the fractured floor. The basalt groans with a vibration felt only in the soles of the feet, and fresh chips of black glass spring cleanly from the walls, skittering across the pools without a sound. Instantly, the weeping intensifies. Sheets of scalding condensate sheet down the cliff-interior, flooding the shallow basins until they brim and spill over the pocket's outer lip. The moment that hot, crowded water crosses the invisible boundary into the open mountain air, it obeys the real world again—exploding instantly into ragged plumes of ice-crystal mist that blow away across the empty sky miles above the clouds.

Inside the cleft, the surge sets off a quiet frenzy. The flooding basins lift hundreds of small bodies at once, rearranging the living mosaic in dense, writhing currents. Tool-users abandon half-cracked shells to scramble for the newly popped obsidian edges, their claws clashing soundlessly in the shimmering air. Predators drop from the upper fractures where the extra water has loosened their grip, landing on lower ledges—or landing first, and falling a moment later. In the middle of the bright afternoon light, whole clusters of wet, gleaming organisms flicker slightly out of alignment with themselves, crowded so tightly into the folded inches of rock that you can no longer tell which limb belongs to which body, or whether the same chipped stone is being wielded in three pools at once.

Then the deep heat eases back down into the young mountain.

The overflow stops. The basins settle to their usual finger-deep shimmer, steaming lazily beneath the plain blue sky. Along every razor-sharp seam and wet shelf, the multitudes resume their breathless, near-silent work—wedging, tapping, prying, scraping—packed tight inside a blister of broken physics no bigger than a doorway, perched on the edge of nothing.
