The water sits heavy here, cold enough to numb bone, pressing down with the settled weight of a ceiling that has no visible top. Far above—so far that weather and daylight are only rumors—there is a faint, bruised thinning of the dark, a cold pewter stain that tells you which way is up without ever offering a glimpse of sky. Down along the floor, light barely survives the descent. Everything is drawn in charcoal, damp slate, bruised plum, and the near-black green of leaves starved for sun.

There is no natural seabed beneath the gloom. Every yard of the floor was laid by hand.

Where the silt thins along the crest of a low ridge, fitted flagstones show through—broad slabs of limestone and dark basalt, quarried with blunt chisels and dragged into place before the water ever came. Step off a paving stone and your foot drops into a lined drainage channel, or strikes the rim of a sunken cistern, or catches on the threshold of a doorway whose timber lintel rotted away an age ago. Dig through the muck of a courtyard and you do not reach raw earth; you reach an older course of cobbles, and beneath that, packed rubble, crushed pottery ground into dull terracotta pebbles, and the foundations of earlier walls. Across hundreds of miles, the substrate of this world is human work, stacked, terraced, ditched, and paved until the original ground disappeared entirely beneath it.

It is primitive masonry, built by muscle, wedge, and patience rather than mortar or iron. Dry-stone walls run in endless, intersecting grids—low field boundaries, retaining dikes, animal pens, raised causeways, narrow alleys barely two shoulders wide. Larger structures are assembled from massive, rough-dressed blocks wedged tight with smaller stones, their doorways capped by single heavy slabs or closed by low, clumsy corbeled arches. Nothing was built tall; the architecture hugs the low contours of the plain, spreading outward instead of up, stepping down across gentle, continental terraces that descend only a few feet over a mile.

Millennia beneath the cold water have finished what age began. Every sharp arris has been softened away. The blocks have the rounded, patient look of river stones, their corners dissolved to smooth curves, their faces pitted and dulled. Mortar, where any was ever used, leached out long ago into pale lime-silt. Roofs slumped inward ages before the sediment settled, leaving roofless stone cells filled halfway to their sills with dark debris. Walls lean against one another at settled, permanent angles, no longer falling—everything that could collapse has already collapsed, finding its final resting posture centuries ago.

And over all of it, choking the alleys and draping the lintels, the vegetation grows in an unchecked, cold-water riot.

It seems at first as though nothing green should live in such dimness, yet the flora here is built for gloom and patience. Massive, slow-growing kelp and sea-wrack crowd every open square, their gnarled holdfasts wedged deep into the chinks between stones, gripping the masonry with thick, rubbery fingers. From those anchors rise dense, overlapping curtains of broad, leathery blades—not bright green, but deep umber, dark mahogany, and ink-maroon, their dark pigments tuned to harvest the last weak scatter of blue-green light filtering down from the distant surface. They grow twenty, thirty feet upward into the dusk, brushing against one another in thick, tiered canopies that turn the wider avenues into dim, vaulted tunnels.

Closer to the stone, the world is upholstered. Every wall-top, fallen lintel, and dished-out stair tread is buried under a plush, sodden pelt of dark olive moss, filamentous algae, and shaggy, fine-branched sea-weeds. The growth is inches thick, dense as wet wool, softening the already worn outlines of the ruins until low walls look like sleeping embankments and cairns resemble mounded hummocks. Inside the deeper rooms, where even the faint overhead greyness dies completely, pale, papery crusts of slow-growing algae and slick carpets of dark biofilm coat the wet stone.

What gives the forest its strange, suffocating fullness is the absence of anything to eat it.

There are no fish slipping through the kelp stems. No crabs scuttle across the flagstones; no sea urchins rasp the algae from the parapets; no barnacles, mussels, or limpets crust the drowned lintels. The silt holds no broken shells, no pale ribs, no curled carapaces. Whatever cold, heavy chemistry or ancient isolation shaped this sea excluded animal life entirely, leaving the plants with no grazers and no scavengers. Every frond grows to its full, leathery span completely unbitten, its margins whole and smooth, until age alone dulls it and it tears free in a slow current.

Without burrowers or bottom-feeders to stir the floor, the dead foliage simply sinks and stays. In the sheltered pockets of sunken courtyards and the bottoms of stone-lined canals, fallen leaves lie stacked flat in dense, cold-preserved mats, layer upon layer of dark, rubbery tissue slowly compressing into underwater peat. The frigid water—just a few degrees above freezing, dense and sluggish—keeps decay nearly at a standstill. A detached blade might lie intact on a doorstep for years before finally softening into dark floc.

The silence is almost total, dense and muffled, the heavy hush of deep cold water. Without the snapping of shrimp, the click of shells, or the rustle of moving creatures, the landscape keeps its own quiet. If you hang motionless above a sunken lane, the only sound is the faintest, rubbery sigh of kelp blades sliding past one another in the drift. Now and then—so rarely it barely belongs to the scale of a single day—a stone hollowed by ten thousand years of slow dissolution gives way by half an inch, sending a dull, low thud rolling softly across a mile of dark water, followed by a lazy, pale bloom of lime-silt that takes hours to settle back onto the leaves. Sometimes a single heavy bead of marsh-gas works free from a peat-choked cistern, rising through the tangled canopy with a solitary, muted *bloop* before vanishing into the gloom above.

Yet the place is not entirely static. Because the drowned plain is vast—stretching across continental distances with no shore, no rising headland, no abrupt drop-off—it generates a slow weather of its own.

Great, sluggish currents wheel across the submerged lowlands over the span of days, driven by distant temperature shifts and the slow settling of colder water. When a flow builds, it does not rush; it leans. Across dozens of miles, the dense canopies of maroon and black-green fronds bend together in unison, tilting twenty degrees along the axis of an ancient avenue, parting just enough to reveal the smooth, moss-furred spines of buried walls and the straight, endless runs of irrigation channels. For half a day the water carries a faint haze of fine organic snow—tiny flakes of decayed weed drifting horizontally through doorways and over low terraces. Then, just as gradually, the pressure eases, the current dies, and millions of heavy blades straighten back toward the dim ceiling.

Occasionally, a vast plume of milky silt—stirred by some slow bottom-shift fifty miles away—creeps into a district like an underwater fog bank, dimming the already near-black alleys into absolute blindness for days at a time before thinning out and laying down another microscopic skin of grey dust over the stonework. And when a great storm crosses the unseen surface far overhead, none of its violence reaches the bottom—only a slow, ghostly swell, a rhythmic sway every dozen seconds that rocks the tall stalks gently back and forth, like fields of dark grain breathing in the night.

Travel across it, and the scale makes itself felt through quiet repetition and subtle change. For leagues, there are tight warrens of low, windowless stone rooms linked by winding, stepped alleys now packed shoulder-high with tangled cord-weed. Then the walls open out, giving way to an immense, rectilinear plain of sunken terraced paddocks and narrow stone dikes, running straight and level into the dark until perspective disappears in forty feet of murky water. Beyond those, a broad, slab-paved road—thirty feet wide, flanked by waist-high stone kerbs and rhythmic rows of weathered monoliths furred in ten-foot manes of dark weed—cuts dead-straight across the gentle slope for thirty miles, crossing silted-up aqueduct arches and dry-stone causeways before arriving at a circular plaza of fitted basalt slabs, empty save for the dense, swaying curtains of dark fronds and the cold, unhurried drift of the water.

Nothing hurries here. A frond takes seasons to lengthen by a hand's breadth; a wall takes centuries to bow another inch; a layer of silt takes lifetimes to thicken by the width of a fingernail. Beneath the cold, dark ceiling of water, the hand-built world and the silent forest that wraps it have settled into an immense, enduring equilibrium—dark, chilled, densely grown, and entirely at rest.
