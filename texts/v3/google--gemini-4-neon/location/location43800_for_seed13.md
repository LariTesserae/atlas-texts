The basin lies high enough that the sky above it has thinned to a pale, washed cobalt, bright and clear without ever growing warm. Air enters the lungs crisp and slightly damp, carrying the clean, mineral scent of wet slate, crushed juniper needles, and cold moss. Around and across the expanse—a wide valley several miles from rim to rim—spines of dark, vitreous stone jut upward at a sharp, uniform tilt. They look as though the earth cracked open yesterday and shoved them through the turf: knife-edged crests, flat cleavage planes, raw triangular facets untouched by the rounding work of glaciers or floodwater. Even the scree below the ridges consists of clean-angled shards that have never been tumbled smooth.

Nothing here is moving toward another hour. The light is the bright, cool clarity of a high-altitude midmorning, and it holds its station without drifting. A single white band of cloud sits wedged against the highest eastern teeth of rock, neither thickening into rain nor thinning into blue. Shadows lie across the stone and grass like permanent ink-strokes—sharp-edged, unshifting, mapped into the ground itself.

Across this wide, jagged bowl stretches a city, though at first glance the eye has to learn how to measure it. It is unmistakably urban in its ambition and organization—a continuous web of causeways, stepped terraces, drainage channels, public basins, and layered residential halls spanning from ridge to ridge—yet it refuses to crowd itself. Between one stone quarter and the next lie broad, unbuilt intervals of wild fern-slope, lichen-crusted boulder fields, and marshy pockets of sedge. Great flagged plazas open onto empty sky; long, low-walled avenues run for hundreds of paces beside nothing but heather and sharp stone before meeting another cluster of slate-roofed halls and timber galleries. The city does not huddle against the mountains. It lies across them loosely, like a net cast wide over sharp knuckles, holding vast tracts of emptiness within its mesh.

Everything built here shows the mark of patient hands and simple tools. The builders worked with the natural fracture of the young rock rather than fighting it, splitting the dark slabs along their seams with hammered copper wedges and wet wooden pins, then dressing the faces with river-sand and stone mauls. Older foundations of massive, dry-laid boulders carry upper tiers of finer fitted ashlar, topped in turn by lighter frameworks of mortised dark timber, woven rush-screens, and heavy slate shingles pinned with bone and copper cramps. There are no wheels on the causeways—only wide, shallow stone stairs and smooth, mossy ramps—and no chimneys sending up smoke. Where warmth is needed, small braziers of compressed peat burn with a faint, nearly smokeless glow inside deep-walled stone rooms, barely scenting the cool air outside.

Water governs the layout of every street, though there is no river roaring through the basin and no rain ever falls from the clear sky. Instead, the city harvests the cold air itself. Along the crests of the sharp ridges and at the upper edges of every terrace stand tall, rhythmic arrays of "combs"—frames of weathered cedar strung tightly with woven horsehair and split reeds, alternating with upright fins of rough-chiselled stone. Because the cool, moisture-laden air drifts in a slow, unvarying breath down from the cloud-pinned peaks, dew forms continuously on every cold fiber and mineral face. It gathers into beads, slips without hurry down vertical flutes carved into the stone posts, and feeds a network of shallow, open runnels that trace the edges of the causeways and descend through the neighborhoods in gentle tiers.

The water never rushes. It moves as a gleaming skin over dark rock, collecting in broad, mirror-still stone cisterns and stepped catch-basins set into the plazas and courtyards. Because the air is so still and cool, the basins brim to their exact carved rims, the surface tension bulging a hair's breadth above the lip like clear glass. Where the excess slips out through narrow overflow notches, it spreads into the sunken gardens and marshy hollows between quarters, keeping the dark soil perpetually cool and damp without ever drowning it. At the same rate, an invisible, steady evaporation lifts moisture back toward the high cold teeth of the rim, closing a loop that never swells and never dries.

Out of this constant cool dampness, the basin grows lush, thick, and strange in its boundaries. Because the shadows never migrate, the vegetation obeys the fixed geometry of light like ink inside ruled lines. On the sun-struck sides of ridges and dry-stone walls, the rock is pale with crusty gold and silver lichens, tufted with tight cushions of alpine thyme, gray-leaved succulents, and low, resinous shrubs whose waxy, pale-yellow blooms stay open indefinitely. Step three inches across a shadow-line—whether cast by a jagged peak or the eave of a stone colonnade—and the ground turns plush and dripping. There, deep pillows of emerald feather-moss and sphagnum upholster the razor-sharp stone edges until the terrain looks cushioned in dark velvet. Dense stands of waist-high bracken, pleated broad-leafed greens, and glossy dark-berried bushes pack the shaded troughs, while gnarled dwarf pines and twisted junipers cling to the damp crevices, draped in long curtains of pale-green beard-lichen that hang straight down, unswayed.

The city's terraced gardens follow the same strict partition. Builders laid out their dwellings and plots to straddle the knife-edge borders between light and shade: dry, sun-warmed stone porches and grain-drying ledges on one side of a wall; cool, perpetually damp root-cellars, moss-beds, and fern-shaded cisterns directly behind it. On the cultivated ledges, orderly rows of thick-stalked tubers, silver-veined greens, and climbing pale-blossomed vines grow in a curious, unhurried abundance. With no seasons to force a single harvest, each plant carries tight buds, open waxy flowers, and mature seed-pods at the same time, renewing itself leaf by leaf in a slow, steady murmur of growth.

The valley teems with animal life, yet the first thing a traveler notices after the sharp stillness of the light is how quiet it all is. Sound seems to sink into the thick moss and open space without returning.

Large, long-legged birds with slate-gray plumage and wide, solemn faces—something between cranes and owls—roost in scores along the stone parapets and wade through the shallow public basins. Their wing-feathers are softly fringed; when a dozen lift together from a cistern and glide across a fern-choked hollow to the next ridge, they do so in complete silence, not even a whisper of air beneath their spans. They spear tiny, translucent shrimp and pale aquatic snails from the brim-full pools, leaving a single slow ring on the water that widens, touches the stone lip, and disappears.

Across the wide green intervals between city quarters, herds of compact, shaggy mountain grazers move slowly over the damp turf. They have broad, curved horns and coats of tangled cream and charcoal wool that bead with fine mist; their hooves are wide and strangely spongy, suited to gripping wet, sharp-edged rock without slipping, and they make only a faint, muffled scuff as they browse the sedges and moss-cushions. They pass unbothered beneath the arched stone causeways and sometimes bed down in the empty corners of outlying plazas, chewing silently with half-closed eyes.

Look closer at any shaded wall, any damp runnel, any curled frond of bracken, and the smaller life reveals itself in dense, unhurried layers. Along the wet stone lips of the aqueducts sit rows of flat-bodied, jewel-backed salamanders and speckled highland frogs, glistening cold in the shade, breathing through their skin; none of them croak. On the sun-warmed upper stones just inches away, thick-furred, tailless rock-mammals stretch out in bright-eyed clusters, communicating not with whistles or barks but with quick flicks of their dark ears and subtle shifts of posture. Through the bright, cool air drift loose clouds of large, pale velvet moths and slow, copper-shelled beetles moving from blossom to blossom; their flight carries no hum, only the faintest dry brush if one drifts near a cheek.

Now and then, at great distances across the open basin, the people of the city appear—sparse, unhurried figures wrapped in thick, undyed felted wool fastened with hammered copper pins. Two might be seen tending a high ridge of horsehair dew-nets, combing out loose lichen with bone rakes; another kneels beside a terraced garden-bed with a short bronze-bladed trowel, thinning moss back to a carved stone groove; a small group walks single-file along an elevated causeway half a mile away, carrying woven hampers of pale roots and folded cloth. They rarely call out to one another. Where voices carry at all, they are low, brief, and quickly swallowed by the open air.

There is no rush in their posture, and no sense of unfinished work. Nothing is being torn down to make room for something larger, and nothing is visibly crumbling into ruin. Because the sharp rock never suffers the violent split of freeze and thaw, because no storm-wind ever wrenches a timber frame or floods a terrace, the city exists at a point of exact, almost eerie balance—neither new nor decaying, merely held. A chipped slate shingle is replaced with an identical one split from the same dark seam; a cracked copper cramp is lifted out, cold-hammered, and seated again; an overgrown fern is trimmed back to the permanent edge of its shadow.

Stand on one of the high corbelled bridges spanning a mossy trough, and the whole basin reads like a diagram drawn in cold stone, green velvet, and steady light. Far below, a shaggy grazer shifts its weight on a wet slab. A single drop of condensation gathers on the carved snout of a stone spout, swells until it can hold no longer, and falls into a brim-full basin with one clear, hollow *tock*. A slate-gray bird turns its head without a rustle. On the bright side of the parapet, the stone is dry and faintly warm beneath the palm; on the shaded side, an inch away, it is slick and cold as well-water. Above the jagged eastern teeth, the lone ribbon of cloud holds its exact shape against the pale sky, and the cool, luminous morning remains—complete, abundant, and utterly still.
