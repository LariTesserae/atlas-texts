The first thing you notice is that you cannot hear your own footsteps, though you can feel them—a dull, taut rebound under the soles, like walking across the slack head of an enormous drum.

What takes the place of silence is a wall of noise so wide and constant it ceases to feel like something passing through the air and becomes the air itself. It presses flat against the eardrums. Closer to the ground, it is a dry, saw-toothed rasp of billions of rubbing legs and parchment wings; beneath that runs a low, shuddering thrum, half wind and half vibration; and cutting straight through both are long, brassy trills, wet rhythmic croaks, and high, tearing screams that drop out of the bright sky like thrown stones. To speak to someone standing an arm’s length away, you have to lean in and shout into the side of their neck, and even then your words thin out and vanish into the roar.

Yet when you look up, the world appears startlingly empty.

This is the roof of an ancient mountain range, ground down over ages too long to measure into a sweeping, gently rolling tableland suspended near the ceiling of the sky. Every peak and ridge that once broke the horizon has long since weathered away into slow, shallow swells and broad saucer-basins that run for leagues under a crisp, clean daylight. The air is thin enough to make a newcomer’s breath come quick and light, and the sky overhead deepens toward a hard, clear indigo at the zenith—yet the place is warm. Updrafts from unseen lowlands miles below spill over the far rims of the plateau, carrying soft pockets of heat and occasional, ragged ribbons of pale mist that drift across the swells, dampen everything for ten minutes, and dissolve in the sun.

Between the ground and the high, thin clouds, there is almost nothing to interrupt the eye. No forests, no cliffs, no walls of rock. Miles of hollow, sunlit volume lie between one landmark and the next.

What passes for the ground is not stone or earth. You can walk for three days and never set foot on bare mineral. Over millennia, the worn-down bedrock has been completely sealed beneath a continuous, springy crust of living plant—a fused, interlocked mat of woody cushion-shrubs, iron-tough creeping stems, pale fibrous mosses, and fleshy, water-hoarding rosettes. Dead growth does not rot away quickly in this warm, breezy height; instead it braids and compacts into a dry, spongy lattice beneath the green-ochre surface, ten or fifteen feet deep in the shallow basins. The entire plateau is carpeted in a single, unbroken botanical roof.

Beneath that roof, the ground is hollow.

Press your boot hard into a seam between two gnarled cushion-mounds and you can feel the give of empty chambers below—a dry, tangled architecture of root-vaults, collapsed stem-tunnels, and shaded pockets where the brief mist-rains and night condensation collect. Water rarely lies open to the sky here; the warm wind drinks puddles too fast. Instead, moisture gathers on the fine silvery hairs of the surface foliage, beads in papery leaf-cups, and trickles down through the woven crust to pool in dark, tea-colored hollows in the cool under-mat. Walk across a low swale after a passing shower and the turf smells faintly of warm bark, crushed thyme, and standing rain-water locked somewhere out of sight beneath your heels.

Rising out of this low, rolling skin at wide, lonely intervals—sometimes half a mile apart, sometimes clustered in threes or fours along the lip of a sunken basin—are the vent-stalks. They are tall, bleached, fluted chimneys grown by the deepest layers of the plant-mat, pale as sun-dried bone and as thick around as a person’s waist, perforated along their sides with oval pores. They serve the living floor as lungs and condensers, drawing dampness from passing mist-plumes and venting warm air from the root-galleries below. In the vast, flat openness, a single vent-stalk stands out like a mast on a calm sea.

And they are why the deafening noise never fully dies, even when the midday sun slows the surface swarms. The stalks are hollow from root to crown, and every one of them is packed with tenants.

For all the staggering emptiness of the vista, the plateau is utterly overrun with animal life. You do not see it all at once; the space is too wide, and much of it hides inside the living floor. Stand still for a minute, though, and the ochre-green turf around your boots resolves into relentless motion. Tiny, armored stridulators the color of dry lichen cling three deep beneath every curled leaf, sawing their hind legs in staggered waves that roll across the swales like wind over wheat. Through the gaps between the cushion-stems flicker slender, copper-backed lizards, needle-nosed shrews, and pale, clicking beetles that haul bits of seed-pulp down into the dark. Beneath the crust, communal burrowing rodents hammer their hind feet against the taut woody roots whenever a shadow passes overhead—a subterranean telegraph that travels faster than a runner and makes the turf buzz against your ankles.

Inside the damp, shaded pockets under the mat breed swarms of thumb-sized, bright-throated frogs whose calls are absurdly loud for their bodies. They crowd into the bases of the hollow vent-stalks and sing into the flutes; the dried woody walls catch the sound and throw it out across the open plateau like brass horns. Higher up inside the same chimneys cling colonies of bristle-winged swifts and shrill, aggressive roosting bats, squabbling for the shaded pores while the warm afternoon wind groans across the open rims above their heads.

Lift your eyes from the turf, and the great hollow volume of the sky is layered with hunters. In the highest reaches of the warm thermals hang loose, slow-turning wheels of enormous, hollow-boned vultures and fork-tailed kites, specks against the clean blue, watching the miles of open ground below. Lower down, fast narrow-winged falcons and scissor-beaked insect-hunters cut back and forth through the thin air, diving straight into sudden eruptions of bronze-winged flies that boil up out of the damp depressions like dark smoke, clatter fiercely for five minutes, and scatter down-wind.

Across the broad, sunlit undulations move larger Presences, spaced far apart yet unmistakable against the flat horizon. Long, straggling ribbons of spindly, long-legged grazers—pale-coated, with wide splayed hooves that distribute their weight over the springy mat—drift steadily from basin to basin, cropping the swollen water-buds and leaving faint, bruised trails that spring back up within days. They are never truly at peace. From the shallow folds of the swells come small, fast packs of lean, tufted-eared predators, running low and silently with their shoulders brushing the cushion-tops, splitting a straggler from the ribbon while the rest of the herd bolts in a thunder of hollow, drumming impacts across the plant-floor. Overhead, the thermal gyres tighten and begin to drop before the chase is even finished.

Nothing stays settled for long. A band of mist blows in from the unseen edge of the world, turning the crisp daylight milky for a quarter of an hour; as soon as the dampness touches the leaves, the stridulating insects fall abruptly half-quiet, and in that brief drop of pitch you hear the frogs surge up from underfoot, the wind hooting through the distant vent-stalks, and the furious wing-beats of thousands of small ground-birds flushing from cover to drink the fresh droplets off the foliage. Then the cloud rips apart, bright warm sun slams back down onto the rolling green-brown expanse, steam lifts faintly off the turf, and the insect roar climbs back up to full, skull-rattling pitch.

People do not build cities up here. The air is too thin, the open water too hidden, the noise too relentless to bear for a whole lifetime without losing pieces of your hearing or your sleep. Yet across the immense distances, faint marks of human craft persist—scattered, small, and humble, fitted cautiously into the living skin of the plateau.

Every few leagues, tucked into the lee of a shallow swale, sits a low dome-shaped shelter built from cut blocks of dried, dense peat-turf and arched ribs of bleached grazer-bone, lashed tight with braided plant-sinew. Most are sunk half-way down into the upper mat, their roofs already knitted over with creeping stems and pale moss until they look like natural humps in the terrain, betrayed only by a dark low doorway hung with stiff scraped hide and a narrow, fire-blackened vent-hole. Outside an occupied hut, you might find a dew-catcher: a wide apron of stitched skins or tightly woven reed-cloth tilted toward the mist-winds on lashed wooden poles, channeling its slow silver trickle down into a buried clay jar or a hollowed gourd trough.

Near the hunting paths—which are no more than faint, compressed troughs in the springy mat, marked at wide intervals by single upright leg-bones bound with strips of bright dyed wool—you come upon older, abandoned traces: a ring of fire-cracked stones dragged up from some rare deep sinkhole where the bedrock shows through; a collapsed snare-frame of bent wood and twisted gut; a pair of small, hand-hammered copper bowls green with tarnish, wedged beneath a root-arch where someone cached them and never returned.

The few folk who come up onto the roof—seasonal herders, resin-tappers, hunters of the high grazers and the fat burrowing rodents—move through the open expanse in small, quiet groups, communicating mostly by sharp hand-signs across the deafening din. They pack their ears with pellets of softened wax and greased wool, carry simple slings, bone-tipped spears, and short knives of beaten copper or flaked stone, and wear broad, flat soles woven from dried plant-fiber to keep from punching through the thinner spots of the hollow crust. When they camp for the night, they do not build tall shelters against the open wind; they cut straight down into the dry, spongy under-mat, roof the shallow pit with stretched hide to deaden the noise, and burn dense plugs of dried woody root that give off a thin, sweet smoke and barely a spark.

Toward evening, the warm updrafts slacken and the sun drops low across the flat, ancient horizon, throwing absurdly long, razor-sharp shadows from every lone vent-stalk and distant herd. For a brief span, the golden light turns the whole rolling carpet of living turf the color of beaten brass. The high soaring flocks begin to spiral down toward the unseen cliffs at the plateau’s edge; the diurnal insects falter as the surface cools by a few degrees; and the night-feeders—pale, wide-eyed, long-legged things—begin to push up through the gaps in the woody moss.

The pitch of the plateau shifts from a blinding, dry rasp to a deeper, wetter chorus of booming throats, rattling wings, and wind-struck chimes from the hollow stalks. It never grows quiet. Under the first sharp stars, miles above the hidden valleys, the ancient living floor hums and shudders with the weight of everything living inside it, wide open to the empty dark.
