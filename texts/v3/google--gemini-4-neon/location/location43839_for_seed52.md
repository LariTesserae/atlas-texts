The basin lies low enough that the air feels pooled here, heavy with a steady, skin-warm dampness that never quite turns to rain. Overhead, the sky is a dim, bruised ceiling of slow-drifting slate bands—so high and uniform it is hard to tell whether they are cloud, mineral haze, or the underside of some vast, unlit lattice miles up in the atmosphere. What light filters through is the color of water in a pewter bowl: a permanent, shadowless dusk in which distances lose their sharpness and the low rim of the basin, many miles out, dissolves into grey-violet obscurity.

Underfoot, the ground is shockingly young. Nothing here has had time to round off. The basin floor is a vast, stepped expanse of dark, glassy stone and pale-veined slabs that look freshly cleaved, as if the crust had buckled and cooled only a few seasons ago. Edges meet at razor-clean angles; fissures drop straight down into hairline cracks without talus or gravel to soften them. In places, a freshly parted seam still breathes a faint, warm ribbon of mineral-scented air from below, leaving a pale crust of salts along its lip.

Across this wide, open floor stands a city—yet a city laid out with an extraordinary appetite for emptiness.

Its wards are deeply built, layered six and ten stories deep with cantilevered terraces, sunken courts, stacked pavilions, and dense networks of elevated causeways. By the complexity of its infrastructure, it is unmistakably urban. Yet each built cluster sits separated from its neighbors by half a kilometer or more of open, jagged stone and thick vegetation, as though the structures themselves required wide margins of quiet air to keep from interfering with one another. Looking across the basin from a raised terrace, you see a sparse constellation of pale, matte-skinned complexes rising out of a dark, plush sea of foliage, linked only by hair-thin transit filaments that span the gaps in impossibly shallow arcs, unsupported by cables or piers.

The engineering refuses to explain itself to the eye. Whole multi-tiered city blocks hover two or three meters above the raw, sharp-edged rock, casting pools of deep gloom beneath their flat bellies, with only a faint shimmer in the damp air to suggest the invisible stresses holding them aloft. Other towers rest on three needle-fine points of bone-white ceramic that meet the fractured basalt without bolts, mortar, or visible weight. The walls themselves are neither metal nor stone, but a pale, satin-matte substance that seems to drink the dim light rather than reflect it—smooth to the point of looking softly out of focus when you stand too close. In certain broad avenues, the geometry stretches or compresses subtly as you walk: a colonnade whose far archway looks fifty paces away takes ten quiet minutes to reach, the air halfway down rippling once like a heat mirage over dry road, though the air here is cool against the cheeks and faintly sweet with wet stone.

Because there are no rivers and no open pools of any size, the city makes its own water from the warm dusk.

Every vertical surface of the pale architecture sits a fraction of a degree cooler than the ambient air. Moisture gathers on them continuously, beading into a silent sheen that slips downward in unbroken films, disappearing into hairline capillary grooves cut along the edges of plazas and walkways. From there, hidden channels bleed the water out into the raw fractures of the basin floor. Where the stone drops in crisp hexagonal steps, shallow, mirror-still puddles collect—black glass no deeper than a knuckle, reflecting the faint ash-white glow of interior shutters and the dark undersides of hovering walkways. Every few hours, a lateral drift of warm mizzle—too fine to be called rain, too heavy to stay suspended as fog—moves lazily across the open basin. It darkens the stone, beads along every horizontal edge, and passes on, leaving the whole city ticking softly with falling drops.

In this dim, warm, damp shelter, the plant life is astonishingly lush, though nothing about it resembles the bright green of sunlit worlds.

Soil barely exists on the young rock, so the flora anchors into raw seams, climbs the deliberately porous faces of the lower foundations, and hangs in immense, damp curtains from the undersides of the floating causeways. Broad, matte-black and deep plum dish-leaves spread across the open flats between wards in dense, overlapping rosettes, their velvety surfaces tilted upward not for sunlight—there is almost none—but to catch the faint thermal glow rising from the ground and the pale, geometric caustics that periodically drift across the basin.

Those caustics are the only visible sign of the city's deeper work. Every so often, a net of faint silver-ochre light—like sun-ripples on the bottom of a deep well, though there is no sun and no water overhead—glides slowly across the black stone and over the backs of the leaves, silently warping as it passes a structural anchor before fading into the dusk. Wherever the light-net passes, the broad dark leaves pivot almost imperceptibly on their thick, rubbery stems, following its warmth with a faint, papery rustle. Beside them grow tangled thickets of oiled-bronze ferns, hanging ropes of soot-green moss swollen with dew, and pale, translucent stalks that root directly in the condensation-slick cracks of the plazas, unfurling feathery fronds that gleam wetly whenever a weak breeze moves through.

The air smells of damp slate, warm crushed herbs, ozone so faint it is almost sweet, and the clean, chalky scent of newly broken rock.

Despite its scale and the quiet complexity of its machinery, the place is hushed. The wide vegetated spaces swallow sound before it can travel between wards. There are no engines, no grinding gears, no roar of transit. The city's activity moves at a slower, nearly silent cadence. Now and then, a long aerial walkway quietly detaches from one terraced ward and begins to drift—an inch a minute, utterly smooth—across the dim void to join a distant spire. You would miss the motion entirely if not for the long, wet curtains of plum-dark vines dangling from its underside, whose tips drag gently over the jagged basalt flats below, leaving narrow glistening tracks across the dustless stone. Far overhead, a transit filament cycles with a sound so low it is felt in the breastbone rather than heard—a three-second sigh, like wind passing over the mouth of an immense empty vessel—before the stillness closes back in.

Deep beneath the floor, perhaps once in an afternoon, the young basin settles a hair's breadth. A clean, crystalline *tink* travels up through the glassy strata, like a thick rod of quartz snapping underwater, and a hundred meters away a hairline fissure widens by the breadth of a fingernail, letting out a brief warm plume of vapor that curls into the hanging ferns.

Animals are rare here, and when one appears it feels like an accidental glimpse into a private rhythm. Along the damp, sharp edges of a sunken plaza, a tall, pale-grey strider—all impossibly thin, jointed legs and a narrow, eyeless-looking head held motionless at shoulder height—steps delicately between the dark rosettes, lowering a needle-like beak to pierce the swollen water-bladders that grow at the base of the bronze ferns. It makes no call; only the tiny, dry click of its feet on the matte paving betrays it, and if disturbed it simply folds into the shadow beneath a hovering foundation and vanishes among the hanging moss. Higher up, between the widely spaced towers, a solitary glider occasionally banks into view—flat and wide-winged as a ray, its hide the color of wet slate edged with dim amber pores—riding the silent column of warmth that rises from a central courtyard without a single beat of its wings, until the violet haze takes it again. Closer to the ground, small armored grazers the size of plums, their backs dull iridescent green, move slowly along the salt-crusted lips of the warm fissures, scraping the fresh mineral rime with a faint, rhythmic ticking.

As for whoever built or tends this basin, their presence is as sparse and unhurried as everything else. High up in the layered wards, behind translucent matte screens that soften light to the color of old tallow, faint glows brighten and fade at irregular intervals. Broad stone benches sit wiped clean of moss along the overlooks; narrow footpaths wind neatly through the heavy black-green thickets between the wards, their edges sharply trimmed back from the slick basalt. Once in a long while, a solitary figure crosses a high, impossibly slender filament bridge half a mile away—a dark vertical speck moving smoothly against the bruised sky, unhurried, casting no shadow on the damp air beneath—before slipping into the layered shadows of a distant terrace and leaving the wide, warm, dripping twilight to itself.
