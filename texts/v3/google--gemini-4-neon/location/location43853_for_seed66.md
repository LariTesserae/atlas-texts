High up where the continental shelf breaks into sunlight, the land does not end in a peak or a ridge; it spreads outward for hundreds of miles in vast, stepped aprons of grey limestone and cast concrete, slick as wet slate and warm under a bright, ordinary sun. Far below the outer escarpments—too far down to see through the luminous floor of cloud—warm seas breathe upward without stopping. The updrafts climb the cliff walls, cool just enough against the high plateau to thicken into tepid mist and steady, frequent rain, and spill across the terraces in a slow tide of water, salt-haze, and airborne organic dust.

Nothing green grows here. Not a blade of grass forces its way between the worn paving blocks; no vine hangs from a cornice; no moss softens the joints of the retaining walls. Where a wet, warm highland ought to be buried in fern and canopy, there is only naked stone, riveted iron, glazed brick, and animals—so many animals, layered so densely across every ledge, gutter, girder, and yard of open ground, that the eye mistakes them at a distance for the missing foliage.

From the upper switchbacks, the city-plateau reads like an immense geological terraced quarry that someone began lining with masonry a thousand years ago and never finished replacing. Mile after mile of flat-roofed boiler houses, tiered settling basins, thick-walled tenements, and arched viaducts descend in broad, shallow steps toward a horizon lost in shining haze. The stone and the infrastructure have been together so long that the join between them has weathered smooth. Natural limestone buttresses have been squared off with iron tie-rods and brick buttressing; brick viaduct piers have been worn back into fluted rock by centuries of warm runoff. Every horizontal surface has a slight engineered pitch, channeling the constant condensation and rain into wide, open granite gutters that run along the streets and drop from tier to tier over broad, stepped spillways.

The light is bright, high-altitude daylight—clean, strong, and almost white—broken only when slow, continent-sized banks of sunlit mist drift across the upper decks over the course of an afternoon. Because there are no leaves to filter it, the glare off the wet masonry, wet rails, and millions of wet hides is fierce, while the underside of every overhead gantry, pipe-bridge, and arcade drops into hard, ink-dark shadow. Steam lifts lazily from the roofs of the rendering works and condenser towers, hanging in bright, heavy curtains before dissolving into the humid air.

The sound hits before the eye can separate what it is looking at. It is not a sharp or frantic noise, but a dense, continuous, chest-vibrating roar that fills the entire highland from horizon to horizon. Underneath everything is the water: thousands of spillways pouring steadily into tiered basins, sluices rushing beneath iron floor-grates, rain drumming on corrugated copper roofs, and the low, floor-shaking hum of immense hydroelectric turbines buried inside the retaining cliffs, turning at a heavy, unhurried pace. Above that sits the industrial pulse—the slow, rhythmic *thud-hiss* of reciprocating water pumps cycling once every six or eight seconds, the groan of geared hoists, the long, wet screech of iron wheels on slick rails. And woven through all of it, louder than the water and the engines combined, is the biological din: a vast, wet, grinding chorus of rasping mouths, clicking shells, muffled bellows, and the breathless, rhythmic *huff-huff-huff* of millions of living siphons drawing air.

Look closely at any windward cliff-face, cooling tower, or viaduct pier, and you see where that breathing comes from. Encrusting the wet masonry in thick, terraced mats—sometimes six feet deep, swallowing old brickwork and iron ladders alike—are the cliff-siphons: colonial, calcareous animals the color of pale ochre and bruised plum. They grow in clustered, fluted chimneys like organ pipes fused directly to the stone, anchored by rock-hard basal plates that slowly weld themselves into the mortar. Every few seconds, rows of leathery, bristle-rimmed apertures along a wall dilate in slow, rolling waves, pulling in the warm, plankton-rich mist and exhaling filtered water in fine, glistening trickles. Where a factory wall has stood untouched for a century, these colonies hang over the lower walkways like vast, meaty stalactites, their outer crusts chalk-white with mineral buildup and warm to the touch.

They are only the foundation layer. On the open avenues, loading plazas, and terraced stone causeways, the ground itself seems to be slowly migrating.

Herds of broad, low-slung grazers cover the wet pavements in packed, shifting carpets. Each is the size of a heavy calf, built like an armored cross between a tortoise and a flat-bodied tapir: six short, splayed, rubbery feet gripping the slick stone, a segmented dome of dull olive-brown horn scarred by old scrapes, and a wide, fleshy hood extending forward over the pavement. They do not forage on plants—there are none—but rasp away the thin, slippery, amber-grey microbial film that forms wherever warm water lingers on stone and concrete. Hundreds of thousands of calcified tongues scrape across the granite at once, producing a dry, sandpapery hiss audible even beneath the roar of the spillways. Over generations, their feeding has polished the curbstones and street cobbles to a pale, satin sheen, wearing shallow, smooth troughs down the centers of the oldest thoroughfares.

They move with staggering patience. A herd blocking an intersection may take twenty minutes to clear it, shells knocking together with dull, wooden clacks, rainwater streaming off their ridges in gleaming threads. The city does not fight them; it has grown around their mass and inertia. Every tram—long, boxy, electrically driven freight cars powered by heavy copper catenaries overhead—mounts a low, curved iron prow lined with thick coiled rope. As the tram crawls along the flooded tracks at a steady walking pace, sounding a low, mournful brass horn that echoes for miles between the concrete tiers, the prow gently nudges the broad flanks aside. The grazers barely flinch; they pivot a few inches on their broad pads, let the wet steel rumble past, and lower their rasping hoods straight back to the stone.

Above the streets, every elevated line, crane boom, railing, and roof parapet bows under the weight of the perchers. They are heavy-bodied, pale-bellied gliders with wide, leathery wings fringed in dense, water-shedding filaments instead of true feathers, and broad, slit-nostriled beaks built to skim the airborne drift. They pack shoulder-to-shoulder along the sagging power cables in swaying, mile-long garlands, dropping pale, chalky droppings that wash constantly into the gutters and turn the runoff milky. Most of the time they sit motionless, wings half-draped to catch the warm air, muttering in a guttural, rattling clatter. Then, as a slow thermal builds over a district of lime-kilns or sunlit retaining walls, an entire hillside of roofs lifts off at once—not in panic, simply riding the rising air—with a sound like ten thousand wet sails snapping taut. For an hour at a time, enormous, lazy spirals of dark wings wheel silently overhead, dimming the fierce daylight in slow, sweeping bands across the concrete terraces below.

In the warm runoff channels, the settling basins, and the open industrial canals that grid the lower decks, the water is never clear and never still. It churns continuously with dense, tangled masses of aquatic life: pale, blunt-headed salamanders the length of a human arm, thick-bodied segmented swimmers, and matted ropes of filter-worms anchored to the sluice grates. They feed on the endless wash of guano, shed husks, and organic silt carried down from the upper city. Iron bar-screens cross every turbine intake, and automated chain-rakes—clanking slowly on rusted gears—lift dripping mats of shells, drowned crawlers, and calcified debris out of the channels day and night, dumping them onto stone aprons where smaller, many-legged scavengers immediately swarm out from under the curb overhangs to pick them clean.

Even the dry interiors of the built world are crowded at the margins. Inside the brick transformer houses, beneath the eaves of the stone tenements, and along the warm steam-pipes that run in thick, riveted bundles down the alleys, glistening black millipedes as thick as a thumb and wet-shelled beetles cluster in dense, motionless drifts, drawn to the steady heat of the copper conduits. Maintenance crews walk the catwalks in oiled canvas coats and heavy boots, carrying long wooden paddles—never metal near the busbars—to scrape the huddled insects out of the switchgear housings before they bridge a circuit and arc.

This is not a ruined city overrun by nature, nor a pristine machine holding nature back. It is an old, vast, industrial metabolism built squarely on top of a biological one, settled into a balance so deep and slow that neither could easily exist without the other.

The factories—broad, low-slung complexes of dark brick, glazed tile, and weathered galvanized iron—run on schedules measured in seasons and decades rather than hours. From the outer cliffs, massive crane-booms reach slowly down along the escarpment walls, lowered by geared winches, to shear off thick slabs of the older, dead calcareous strata laid down by the cliff-siphons over previous centuries. Those porous, mineral-rich blocks are hauled up in open electric skips, crushed in slow jaw-mills whose deep, crunching rumble can be felt through the soles of one's boots three streets away, and fed into vast, continuously burning lime-kilns. Elsewhere, wide terraced vats steam gently in the open sun, rendering oils, chitin-glues, and dense nitrogenous cakes skimmed from the settling canals. Smelters extract trace copper, iron, and zinc concentrated by generations of colonial filter-feeders pulling metallic dust out of the highland mist.

Nothing here is built to be light, or swift, or temporary. The staircases connecting the terraces are wide flights of deeply grooved granite with waist-high stone parapets worn glossy by rain, hides, and hands. Residential blocks are stacked directly into the faces of the retaining walls—deep, vaulted masonry chambers with narrow, shuttered windows, thick slate sills pitched sharply outward to discourage roosting, and heavy iron rain-hoods bolted above every doorway. Laundry hangs inside covered, drafty stone arcades heated by exhaust flues from the pumping stations below; anything hung in the open air would be wet within ten minutes and coated with salt-grit and droppings within an hour.

The air carries a thick, unmistakable blend of smells, heavy in the warmth: wet limestone, hot machine oil, ozone from the sagging high-voltage lines, damp wool and copper, and underneath it all a pervasive, briny, ammoniacal musk of living flesh and wet shell. It is not the sharp stench of rot—scavengers are far too numerous for anything dead to last an hour—but the warm, dense breath of an overcrowded coastal rookery stretched across an inland continent.

Time moves with immense weight across the plateau. The weather does not snap from storm to clear sky; instead, a pale, blinding glare holds for four or five days while the mist hovers in a flat ceiling just below the lower rim, and then the warm vapor slowly climbs the cliffs, engulfing tier after tier in three days of steady, lukewarm drizzle before sinking back again. The great iron flywheels in the hydro-halls spin at the same steady cadence they held fifty years ago. A freight convoy loaded with wet shell-lime takes an entire day to creep twelve miles along the contour-hugging switchbacks from the outer quarry terraces to the central kilns, its brass warning horn sounding every two minutes across the echoing stone canyons.

When evening finally comes, the high sun drops toward the cloud-floor far beyond the plateau's edge, turning the rising mist the color of heated brass and casting miles-long shadows from the condenser towers and gantry cranes. Strings of heavy, caged incandescent lamps flicker on along the viaducts, canals, and loading docks—warm amber points gleaming double in the flooded streets. The sky-flocks settle back onto the power lines until the copper cables sag low over the alleys in dark, trembling arcs. The grazers never truly stop; all night long, under the yellow streetlamps and the warm, drifting mist, the wet scraping of millions of mouths on stone continues beneath the steady thunder of the spillways and the slow, unhurried heartbeat of the pumps.
