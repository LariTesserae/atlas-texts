High above the tree line, wedged into the freshly split crown of a dark mountain ridge, lies a hollow no larger than the nave of a stone cathedral. Beyond its jagged rims the air drops away into thin, pale nothingness, and overhead a low ceiling of cold vapor presses down like the underside of a slab, sealing the basin in a perpetual, heavy twilight. You hear the place long before you cross its sharp lip: a steady, chest-rattling roar, the unmistakable acoustic weight of a turbine hall running at full load.

Yet when you look down into the hollow, there is nothing built inside it. No masonry, no ironwork, no bolted plates, no roofs or cables or sluice gates. Not a single stone has been dressed by a hand or laid atop another.

The rock itself is startlingly young, dark basalt and magnetite cleft into towering, angular facets whose edges remain razor-sharp, as if the ridge had cracked open ten minutes ago and cooled instantly. Water sheets down these vertical planes in cold, trembling films, weeping from horizontal seams and dripping steadily from the overhanging mist. Despite the relentless wetness, not one edge is rounded. Not a pebble has been tumbled smooth, and no silt gathers in the troughs. The stone refuses to wear. Instead, it vibrates. Whole slabs of fractured cliff press against one another in microscopic, tireless strokes, grinding hidden seams of quartz until the sheer rock faces hum with immense alternating current. Raw tendrils of native copper and graphite bristle straight out of the wet cracks like mineral lichen, carrying heavy loads of voltage that hiss and spit in the damp air.

Down in the basin, gravity does not behave like a smooth slope. When a heavy drop of cold water detaches from an overhang, it falls in crisp, audible increments—tick, tick, tick—stepping downward through the dim air as though engaged with the teeth of an invisible escapement before striking the sharp slate below. The waterfalls plunging from the upper rim do not merely splash; they drop into narrow, uncarved funnels in the bedrock that compress the trapped mist and blow it back out through neighboring crevices in deafening, rhythmic exhalations. It sounds exactly like a bank of pneumatic valves cycling under colossal pressure, though there is no boiler, no brass, and no piston—only wet, jagged mountain rock performing the physics of heavy industry by natural inclination.

The air is bitingly cool, hovering just above the point where water stiffens, and thick with a baffling, impossible scent: crushed mountain moss, cold iron, sharp ozone, and the distinct, heavy reek of heated transformer oil—though nothing in the hollow is warm, and no oil exists anywhere on its gleaming wet surfaces. Light barely reaches the floor. The mist-ceiling filters the high-altitude sky down to a bruised pewter gloom, broken only by the pale, jittering corona discharge that runs in blue-white beads along the sharpest rock ridges, and by sudden, silent arcs that snap across the damp fissures like sparks off a commutator.

Despite its modest boundaries, the hollow feels strangely hollow and cavernous, full of wide, bare negative space. Vast sheets of slick black rock and broad corridors of dim, echoing mist separate everything that lives here. Yet wherever life does take hold, it erupts with an almost violent lushness. Anchored to isolated spires and deep wet cracks are swollen, dripping islands of vivid emerald: giant tree-ferns with fronds wide as shutters, dense bolsters of liverwort and sphagnum engorged with cold water, braided curtains of thick, waxy vines and translucent white-stemmed orchids. Between these bursting green sanctuaries lie twenty or thirty paces of sheer, bare, electrified stone and empty air.

The plants do not sway in any wind—there is no true draft here, only the standing pressure waves of the roaring rock. Instead, every fern frond shivers at a precise harmonic of the cliff-dynamos. The waxy vines wind tightly around protruding spurs of magnetized stone in neat, uniform coils, conducting a faint trickle of ground-current so that the pale tips of their unopened buds glow with a steady, dim filament-light. None of the foliage ever yellows. There is no fallen leaf litter on the jagged floor, no rot, no soft humus tucked into the seams. Every fiddlehead stands permanently half-unfurled, slick with condensation, taut with stored tension.

And within these sparse pockets and the empty air between them, animals teem in startling numbers. Along the weeping horizontal cracks, hundreds of pale, translucent salamanders lie packed shoulder to shoulder in rigid rows; their feathery external gills are tarnished the color of wet copper and flare in absolute, metronomic unison—sixty beats a minute, synchronized to the hum of the basalt beneath their bellies. Out in the dim void between the fern-pillars hang dense, roaring clouds of heavy-bodied insects with dark, iridescent carapaces and veined wings that look unnervingly like etched metal foil. They do not wander. Each swarm remains pinned to an invisible curved corridor in the empty air, buzzing with a harsh sawtooth whine that layers over the deep bass of the stone turbines.

Farther up, perched alone on separate, knife-edged pinnacles with wide stretches of mist between them, crouch large, wet-pelted creatures with thick moss growing along their spines and long, splayed toes gripping the slick stone. Their broad ribcages expand and collapse like leather bellows, ending each breath with a sharp, percussive clack that echoes across the nave. They lean down to tear mouthfuls from the lush fern-islands at their feet, chewing tirelessly—yet the fronds never show a torn edge, and the foliage never thins. High near the flat cloud-ceiling, dozens of broad-winged, silent gliders bank in identical, overlapping ovals, riding the invisible acoustic ripples of the hollow without ever flapping or descending to rest.

Stay long enough in the cold din, and the deeper fracture in the place makes itself felt. Distance behaves poorly here; a mossy monolith thirty paces away looks far off through the dim mist, yet the dry click of a beetle’s wing-case on its face sounds as crisp as if it were inside your own collar. Every few seconds, an animal or a trembling fern-crown slips half an inch out of phase with itself—briefly doubling into a crisp, pale blue cross-section of its own bones, nerves, or vascular channels hovering cleanly beside its dripping flesh, like an industrial diagram projected onto damp air, before snapping seamlessly back into wet matter.

Watch the shallow pools where the waterfalls strike the jagged floor: torrents pour into them constantly, foaming white and loud, yet the black water never rises by a hair's breadth, and there is no channel leading out of the basin. At the very bottom of each pool, the falling water simply ceases to have fallen, reappearing instantaneously as cold condensation on the flat vapor ceiling above without ever crossing the air between.

Nothing accumulates here, and nothing decays. The roaring cliff-engines produce no goods, generate no heat, and wear down no bearings. The lushness neither spreads into the bare stone voids nor retreats from them. The cool dimness never brightens toward noon or deepens into night. Time in this small, screaming mountain vault does not flow forward at all; it oscillates—an alternating current locked at fixed voltage, hammering the exact same young, wet, teeming instant back and forth forever, loud as a factory floor and still as a stone.
