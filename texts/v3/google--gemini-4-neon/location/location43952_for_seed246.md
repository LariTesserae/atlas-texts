You hear it long before the ground gives any hint that it is there.

For miles across the upland, there is nothing to interrupt the sky. The mountains here finished dying ages ago; whatever peaks once rose have been planed down to a pale, gently rolling pavement of schist and iron-stained gneiss, lifted high into the thin air and left to settle into its final, stubborn flatness. The sunlight is crisp and ordinary—bright, cool-edged highland day—and the cold sits clean in the nostrils, sharp enough to dry the rims of the eyes. There are no boulders, no scree slopes, no jagged breaks in the stone. Everything has been sanded to the dull sheen of an old coin.

Then, carried flat across the empty shelf, comes a sustained, layered roar. At first it sounds like a river dropping down a gorge, though there is no gorge anywhere on the horizon. A hundred paces closer, the roar separates into chords: a cavernous bass thrum that vibrates through the soles of your boots, a dry, flute-like howl sliding up and down a fifth, and beneath both, an intermittent sharp cracking, like dry timber splitting in frost.

Only when you reach the smooth, worn lip of a shallow depression—no more than a quarter-mile from rim to rim, the footprint of a small hill-town or a sprawling abbey precinct—does the ground open to show what is making the noise.

Inside the bowl sits a city.

Not ruins, and not a trick of weathered cliffs. Straight boulevards intersect at crisp right angles, dividing the basin into stepped quarters that descend toward a wide central plaza. Colonaded galleries run along the terraces. Overhead flumes bridge the wider streets, feeding into three tall, cylindrical towers clustered near the heart of the basin. Even the smaller structures obey an unmistakable civic discipline: repeating bays, level parapets, uniform rows of deep alcoves turned cleanly toward the morning sun.

Yet the longer you stare down from the rim, the stranger the proportions become. Almost nothing fills the space. The avenues are far too wide for the frail, skeletal walls that border them. The towers are roofless drums perforated with tiers of long vertical slots, so open that you can see bright wedges of pale blue sky straight through their centers. The entire town is mostly air—vast, clean volumes of freezing wind framed by slender ribs and hollow shells. Nothing moves in the streets. No track leads in across the flat plateau, and none leads out.

Down inside the basin, the sound hits like a physical wall.

Walking the center of one of the main avenues is like wading against a dry surf. Wind sweeps off the flat shelf above, drops cleanly into the smooth stone bowl, and accelerates down the straight corridors with a deafening, steady rush. Your coat snaps furiously; you cannot hear your own boots strike the polished bedrock.

Take two steps to the left, however—right up against the base of a colonnade—and the roaring drops away into a bizarre, humming pocket of stillness. The air trembles gently against your cheekbones, vibrating like the skin of a drum, yet a loose thread on your sleeve hangs almost limp. Step two yards back toward the center of the street, and the gale slams into you again.

That is the secret of the place, and the reason there is not a chisel mark, a mortar joint, or a single worked stone anywhere in the basin.

Nobody built this town. The wind measured it out, and the plants obeyed.

The bedrock dish beneath the avenues is so ancient and evenly eroded that it acts like the shallow bell of an instrument. When the high-altitude winds rake across the plateau, standing waves lock across the basin—violent corridors of roaring air separated by narrow, motionless seams where opposing pressures cancel one another out. For uncounted millennia, nothing could survive in the scouring antinodes; those strips stayed stripped to bare, pewter-smooth bedrock, becoming the wide, empty streets and open plazas.

Along the quiet nodal lines, however, freezing mist settled. And there, a strange, slow-growing colonial flora took hold.

Up close, the walls are not stone at all, though their lowest courses have fused into the bedrock so seamlessly it is impossible to tell where geology ends and biology begins. They are built of densely braided, hollow fibrous stalks—rigid as cane, pale grey-bone where the cold has killed them—cemented together over centuries by mineral-rich condensation. Each generation of stalks grows straight upward inside the calm pressure seams, wicking moisture from passing clouds and precipitating thin coats of pale silica and frost-lime along its outer husks. As the walls rise, they bend the wind into finer, secondary harmonics, carving new pockets of stillness above and beside them: transverse cross-streets, arched openings where the wind punches through, cantilevered ledges, and hollow cylindrical towers spiraling around the basin's deepest eddies.

Old walls die, petrify into porous, feather-light mineral shells, and become foundations for the next century's growth. The urbanism is purely stratigraphic—layer upon layer of self-fossilizing plant matter tuned strictly to the geometry of moving air.

Because the wind holds the broad spaces empty, the living vegetation packs itself with startling opulence into every sheltered crease, sunken basin, and leeward ledge.

It is astonishingly lush for air this cold. Where moisture gathers along the inner lips of the hollow towers and across the shadowed floors of the sunken courtyards, the pale mineral framework disappears beneath thick, pillowy mats of dark-emerald and bruised-plum moss. Fleshy, wax-skinned rosettes—some as wide as cartwheels, their scalloped leaves ribbed like bellows—crowd the damp channels beneath the overhead flumes. Long curtains of black-green highland fern, glossy with cold dew, hang from the undersides of the arches, twitching in the resonant hum. The foliage is dark-pigmented to drink every watt of the bright, thin sunlight; pressing a palm into one of the thick moss-cushions, you feel a surprising, faintly clammy warmth trapped half an inch below the frosty tips.

Every hour or so, the light shifts and the city changes its voice.

Far below the rim of the ancient plateau, unseen valleys push sheets of cloud upward until they crest the flat shelf and spill over into the basin. You see the front coming as a luminous white curtain racing across the open avenues. Within seconds, the sharp sunlight softens to a brilliant, milky glare, and the entire town begins to drink.

The wind drops a half-step in pitch, heavier now with suspended water. Millions of micro-droplets comb out against the porous mineral lattices and the broad, cupped blades of the rosette plants. Almost immediately, a new layer of sound joins the bass roar: a bright, rushing hiss of water running along the overhead botanical flumes, spilling over calcified lips, and plunging down into the dark cistern-wells hollowed beneath the central forum. The hollow drums amplify the pouring water into a rolling, subterranean thunder. The moss-beds swell visibly, turning deep bottle-green; clear pools brim over the smooth stone plazas in thin, shivering sheets.

Then, as swiftly as it arrived, the cloud-wall tears past the far rim.

Hard blue sky snaps back overhead. The bright sun strikes the wet, freezing walls, and the town breaks into a fusillade of sharp cracks and crystalline chimes. Thin skins of needle-ice, flash-frozen along the windward edges of the colonnades during the cloud's passage, expand and shatter in the returning sun, raining glittering needles down into the empty avenues. For ten minutes, wisps of white vapor curl off the dark moss-mats in the sun-trapped courtyards. Tiny, pale-yellow star-blossoms—barely the size of fingernails—crank open along the sheltered parapets, stay open long enough to catch a passing gust of pollen, and furl tight again as the air chills back toward frost.

It is during these brief, bright, steaming intervals that the basin's few animals show themselves.

Fauna is scarce here—the emptiness of the plazas and the punishing roar leave little room for ordinary life—and what endures is deeply peculiar. In the shallow, sun-warmed cisterns of the sunken quarters, plump, slow-moving grazers haul themselves out of the saturated moss-beds. They are low-slung, blunt-headed creatures about the size of hares, coated in a pale, tallow-colored waxy skin rather than fur, with broad fringed feet and no visible ear openings at all; they sense the world through the vibration of the stone beneath their bellies. They creep out along the calm nodal ledges to crop the tender crimson shoots emerging from the dark rosettes, moving with a cautious, deliberate rhythm that halts whenever the wind shifts chord.

They have good reason to listen through their feet.

High above the hollow towers, almost invisible against the bright sky, a pair of gaunt, pale-grey wind-hunters circle on long, narrow wings. They resemble some sparse highland cross between a harrier and a stork, their faces narrow, their ear-slits sealed beneath smooth flaps of leathery skin. They rarely flap; they ride the invisible ridges where the town's updrafts meet the shelf-wind, watching the clear, empty streets far below. When a grazer strays a few inches too far from an overhanging moss-mat, one of the birds folds its wings and drops straight down a calm nodal shaft—plunging silently through a pocket of still air where no warning downdraft precedes it—snatching its prey off the damp stone before banking hard back into the gale.

Sometimes, too, when a sunlit courtyard falls briefly warm, a faint bronze haze rises from the wet moss: tiny, stub-winged glacier-midges hatching in a sudden frantic cloud, dancing for twenty minutes in the lee of a calcified wall before the next white bank of mist rolls over the rim and sweeps them into the cisterns.

At the very center of the basin stands the tallest of the three hollow towers, its lower walls worn so smooth and old they resemble weathered ivory, its upper rim ragged with vibrant, living green stalks reaching into the clear blue light. Stepping inside its base through an arched wind-gap is like stepping inside the chest of a giant, breathing beast.

Above you, fifty feet of empty, perforated cylinder frames a circle of dazzling sky. Water drips steadily down the interior ribs, coating the dark inner moss in a sheen that flashes like wet slate whenever the sun hits the upper slots. The sound inside is no longer wind at all, nor water—it is a single, immense, resonant note, steady and cold and clean, vibrating in your ribs and teeth until your own pulse seems to fall in step with it.

Outside, the avenues stretch away—wide, immaculate, brilliantly lit, and utterly uninhabited. Fresh mist is already feathering over the western rim of the ancient plateau. The leaves on the high parapets tilt upward in unison, the ice along the shadowed sills gives a faint warning crack, and the city draws in its breath for the next chord.
