Up here, miles above whatever lower world once existed, the sea has no surface and no shore. It sits on the roof of the earth like a warm, bruised-amber mantle, drowning the crowns of a mountain range so unimaginably old that its peaks have been ground down to broad, rolling swells of pale bedrock. There are no crags left, no scree, no loose stones sharp enough to cut a palm. Everything has been rubbed down by eons of slow, warm water into the smooth, rounded geometry of worn teeth and wet soap.

The water is blood-warm in the shallows of the plateaus and fever-warm where vast convection plumes rise out of fissures in the ancient stone. You feel the altitude in an odd, sideways way—not as cold or thin air, for there is no air, only liquid—but as an immense, buoyant openness pressing just beyond the dim horizon. The plateaus span continents, terraced shelves and shallow bowls stretching farther than sight could ever reach, yet between the drowned ridges lie deep saddles where the bedrock simply ends. Out there, the ocean does not fall. It hangs over miles of pale, unseen void, held together by a trembling, translucent skin of water—an inverted surface from which a faint, milky light seeps upward into the depths.

Light has no single source here. It is dim and tea-colored, shifting slowly through muted jade, tarnished amber, and slate-violet as vast currents turn overhead. It does not travel cleanly. Years without number have worn down more than the mountains; the ordinary agreements of space, time, and weight have eroded alongside the rock, softened at their edges until nothing quite insists on its own boundaries. Light moves thickly, like dye in warm oil. When a canopy of foliage parts to let a pale band of brightness through, the glow lingers in the water long after the leaves have closed again, hanging there in a soft, fading smear.

And the water is crowded—saturated with life, suspended silt, and overlapping layers of itself. You cannot stretch an arm without brushing something. Lush forests of aquatic flora cling to every smooth undulation of the drowned highlands, packed tight into tiers that fill the water column from bedrock to dim overhead gloom. Immense ribbon-kelps rise from holdfasts fused like wax to the stone, their broad, pleated blades velvety with epiphytic mosses and threaded through with translucent bladder-vines. Between the tall ribbons grow dense thickets of feathery, rust-colored plumes and papery, bowl-shaped fans that rotate slowly on supple stalks, tracking the warmth rather than the sun. Above them all drift continent-sized mats of tangled filaments and spongy moss-rafts, hanging suspended at mid-depth where two layers of water of different densities meet.

Despite the constant, sprawling motion of it all, the place is almost utterly silent.

Huge internal waves roll silently across the plateaus—slow, mile-wide swells of heavier, slightly cooler water that bend the kelp forests flat for half an hour at a time before passing on. Thermal chimneys bloom from the worn stone, sending towering columns of shimmering, hotter liquid curling upward like ghosts. Twice in a cycle, the canopy sheds, releasing billions of golden-brown seed-filaments that drift sideways in dense, blinding curtains for days, coating every frond and ridge in a fine, living snow. Vast meadows tear loose from the plateaus and migrate for hundreds of leagues along the shear-lines between currents. Yet all of this happens in a hush so deep it feels like pressure against the ears. The water is thick with microscopic spores and silky mucilage that drink vibration; the stone is too smooth to knock or grind; and even sound itself seems weathered thin here, traveling only a few paces before unraveling into a faint, indistinct warmth against the skin. The loudest thing one might hear in a week is the dull, muffled *thump* of a giant gas-bladder splitting under its own buoyancy, or the barely audible silk-on-silk hiss of a million fronds turning together.

Closer inspection reveals that the forest does not obey a single downward pull. Gravity here behaves like weather. It drifts across the submerged highlands in vast, slow fronts. In one basin, weight settles heavily, pressing the velvet moss-mats tight against the pale stone and dragging stray gas bubbles down instead of up. Three miles away, weight thins to a suggestion; silt lifts off the bedrock in leisurely vertical veils, and the kelp grows sideways, spiraling in long horizontal corkscrews or looping cleanly back into the rock from which it sprouted. Sometimes a gravity front passes directly through a forest, and for an hour the entire column of water tilts, holding a sheer, trembling wall of liquid against a pocket of luminous, empty mist before sliding smoothly back into place.

Other seams are subtler, and stranger. Swimming through the dense, dim curtains of foliage, one passes without warning through shimmering panes—refraction lines where no temperature changes, only geometry. Step or drift across one, and the valley that seemed fifty yards wide stretches into an afternoon’s passage; look back, and the smooth ridge you just left hangs inverted directly overhead, its moss-mats swaying downward like stalactites from a ceiling of stone that was empty water a moment before. The very oldest flora shows the wear of time’s loosening most clearly: a single swaying ribbon of kelp will sometimes appear slightly smudged at its edges, occupying two or three adjacent instants of its own curve at once, soft and overlapping, as if the water cannot quite decide where the leaf was a heartbeat ago and where it is now. Even causality grows polite and unhurried here. In the quieter bowls, ripples occasionally gather inward across a pane of suspended silt a second before a heavy seed-pod drifts down to touch it.

Animals are vanishingly rare. You can travel for leagues through the warm, shadowed tangle and see nothing that moves under its own will—only the relentless, choreographed sway of plants and currents. When a living creature does appear, it barely seems separate from the medium around it. Once in a great while, an immense, eye-less grazer glides through the dim mid-water: a translucent sheet as wide as a house and thin as wet parchment, rippling along its edges with an impossibly slow, unhurried undulation. It has no skeleton and no haste. It drifts over the kelp-tops, pressing its underside briefly against the leaves to drink the film of spores and mineral dust, leaving behind a clean, briefly sharpened lane in the hazy water before vanishing into the amber gloom. Closer to the bedrock, solitary thumb-sized things with spiraled, chalky shells creep along the shimmering seams where space thins; they crawl into a line of distortion and emerge twenty paces away, grazing undisturbed on stone that has begun to soften at its margins into slow, gray ribbons of slurry.

Scattered across the immense plateaus—so sparse you might cross a hundred miles of drowned highland without finding one—are marks that neither geology nor biology quite explains. There is no metal anywhere, no cut angles, no trace of fire or tool. Only occasional rings of rounded stones, heavy as torsos, wedged into the shallow hollows of a ridge and now so deeply fused into the bedrock by calcium and age that they look like ribs pushing through skin. Once in a while, beneath an overhang of velvety kelp, one finds a low mound of pressed pale clay and fused shells, shaped like an open bowl or a shallow seat, or a line of faint, palm-sized depressions worn evenly along a smooth ledge. Whatever placed them used only hands, bodies, and patience, and did so so long ago that the very alignments they set have drifted askew as the space around them stretched and folded. Now the moss grows evenly over them, and the slow grazers pass above without turning.

Far out, where the broadest plateau finally shelves away into the suspended ocean-edge, the kelp gives way to solitary, pale streamers that trail horizontally into nothingness. Down beneath the trembling inverted surface of the sea, miles below, nothing solid can be seen—only an endless, muted glow rising out of an unfathomable depth of warm mist. Above, there is only more water, thickening gradually into darker, warmer, older layers where the light dies altogether. Between the two, the drowned roof of the world continues its immense, silent turning: warm, overgrown, worn smooth past the bone of reality, swaying gently in the dimness long after everything sharp has been forgotten.
