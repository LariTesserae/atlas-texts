The light arrives without warmth, bright enough to blanch the edges of your vision. It comes from a pale, milk-white sky that shows no sun, only a long, horizontal smudge of sharper incandescence low along the rim, as if the heavens were an enormous sheet of translucent paper pressed close against a cold lamp. Everything beneath it is cool—a crisp, dry chill that nips at exposed skin and stiffens loose fabric—and completely arid. There is no dampness in the air, no dew on any leaf, no mud, no mist, not even the rumor of water. When you breathe in, your throat registers only cold ozone, chalky chaff, and the faint, dry-sweet smell of sun-baked bakelite and hay.

There is no ground, either, in the usual sense. What holds your weight is a springy, unyielding deck of interwoven dry bast-fiber, galvanized grating, and lignified conveyor-slats fused together by centuries of pale, woody callus. Every square inch is both grown and fabricated. Where a riveted zinc plate has weathered and cracked, tough, cream-colored root-tendrils have bridged the split and hardened into structural gussets; where a broad bark-beam has bowed under load, a neat brass turnbuckle has budded directly from the cambium to draw it taut again. Look down through the diamond gaps in the decking and you do not see soil or stone. You see another tier of the same dense, pale infrastructure—catwalks of braided reed-cable, rows of dusty glass-like seed-insulators, slow-turning shafts wrapped in papery bark—and below that another, and another, descending into a cool, well-lit depth where the struts and foliage crowd so closely together that the air itself seems partitioned into narrow vertical shafts.

The whole basin lies flat and low, a sunken continental floor of packed plant-machinery stretching unbroken to a pale horizon that never rises into hills. From any slightly higher gantry-bough, the canopy looks like a vast, low-lying textile mill grown out of bleached botanical matter: mile upon mile of flat, rectangular louvers—each one a broad, thin leaf with the sheen of whitewashed tin and the crispness of dried magnolia parchment—hinged on woody, copper-veined stems. They tilt in slow, coordinated ripples across the plain, tracking invisible shifts in the cold glare. Between the louver-groves rise dense thickets of stator-reeds, hollow dry canes the color of bone and old verdigris, packed shoulder to shoulder so tightly you have to turn sideways to slip between them. Inside each hollow stalk runs a hairline coil of natural copper. As the cold wind sweeps over the plain, millions of reeds flex together, and a silent current drains down into the root-busbars below.

Because nothing here drinks water, the vegetation runs on dryness, light, and charge. Break a twig off a loom-hedge and nothing bleeds. Instead, a dry puff of ivory dust escapes—powdered resin and mineral pollen—and the two severed ends twitch slightly as a pale, hair-thin spark bridges the millimeter gap between them. The sap is an anhydrous charge carried alongside pressurized chalky powders through hollow, parchment-lined vessels. Even the oldest trunks, thick as factory chimneys and banded with corrugated iron-bark, are light and dry as seasoned cork. Everything is built to conserve friction and static. The joints of the bending gantry-branches are padded with dry, felt-like lichen; the root-cables sheath themselves in flaky white mica-bark to keep their voltage from leaking into the neighboring grates.

And the entire continent is in motion—busily, relentlessly active—yet almost completely hushed.

Beside you, a flywheel forty feet across, grown from laminated hardwood and spoked with tensioned vine-wire, turns steadily on an oiled-graphite axle. A few yards further, an acre of tall, fluted radiometer-trees spins its broad, black-and-silver papery vanes briskly under the sheer pressure of the blinding light, driving rows of vertical pith-pistons that pump dry mineral pollen up from the lower strata. Belts of woven straw and braided wire glide continuously over bark-sheathed pulleys, emerging from knotholes in one truss-trunk and vanishing into slotted apertures in another. Massive shears of calcified thorn descend cleanly onto moving ribbons of pressed leaf-pulp, stamping them into neat geometric wafers that drop into waiting hopper-fronds.

You watch all of this happen in a silence deep enough to hear the dry click of your own eyelids.

Part of the quiet is physical: every hard surface has grown a velvet-dry nap of acoustic down, porous cork baffles, and fine chalky fuzz that drinks vibration before it can become sound. A steel-hard seed-pod the size of a fist drops from an overhead sorting-limb onto a metal-bark chute, bounces twice, and makes only a faint, muffled *tup*, as if heard from beneath a heavy quilt. The rest of the quiet is something wrong with the air itself. Sound refuses to carry far or stay loyal to its causes. Strike a hollow girder-trunk with your knuckles and your hand stings, dust jumps cleanly off the rivets three feet away, and nothing reaches your ears until ten seconds later, when a tiny, polite tap seems to sound from somewhere behind your left shoulder. The industrial roar that ought to shake this plain has been stripped away, leaving only intimate, papery textures: the whisper of dry leaves adjusting their angle, a faint electrostatic tick in the stator-reeds, the sand-on-paper hush of miles of conveyor-vines sliding through their guides.

Because the basin is so vast and uninterrupted, the plant-factories make their own weather. No rain ever gathers in the flat white sky, yet the air is always cycling. Every few hours, a dry static front rolls across the low expanse like an invisible tide. You know it is coming before you feel the wind, because half a mile out, the feathery aerial-fronds atop the condenser-trees suddenly stand upright in a sharp advancing line, pointing stiffly toward the glare. When the front washes over you, your hair lifts gently, the dry dust on the grating rises an inch and hovers in sparkling suspension, and the louver-canopies lock flat to drink the surge.

Behind the static fronts come the chalk-squalls—cold, brisk winds pouring down from some distant, unseen rim of the basin, carrying bright clouds of translucent mica-seeds, dry husks, and zinc-ash vented from squat, porous spore-stacks scattered across the flats. The dust-snow drifts without melting, piling into crisp white wedges along the windward sides of the loom-hedges. Within an hour of a squall’s passing, slow-sweeping broom-fronds unfold from the lower catwalks, brushing the drifts neatly back into root-intakes so the whole dry cycle can be chewed down and extruded again.

Then there are the lumen-tides, when the cold brightness swells past ordinary glare. During a high tide, the air saturates with light so completely that distant cooling-trellises lose their opacity and turn ghostly, allowing you to see the machinery behind them through their own solid trunks, like an overexposed print laid on top of itself. Shadows behave poorly during these hours. Some shrink to razor-sharp needles pointing away from no discernible source; others detach altogether from the stanchions that cast them, sliding lazily across the sunlit gratings like dark, flat sheets of tissue before catching on a snag of copper-briar, where dry white roots eventually grow into them and pull them down into the decking.

This is not a new catastrophe. Everything about the strangeness here is worn smooth, oxidized, and settled into routine. The cracks in how space and time behave have been here so long that the plant-machinery has grown flanges around them and wired them into the production line.

Walk down a straight aisle between two dense walls of pulley-vines, counting the arched iron-bark ribs overhead—one, two, three, four—and glance back to find seventeen ribs stretching behind you toward a horizon that curves slightly upward at the margins, like a dry page curling in a cold room. Follow a broad braided conveyor-belt as it climbs a truss, crosses a bridge, descends a spiral stair of perforated cellulose, and feeds into a dry press; watch closely, and you will see the stamped wafers emerge from the far side of the press a fraction of a second before the raw ribbon enters it, the slight causal snag cushioned by a thick growth of pale, spongy lichen that seems to thrive exclusively on repeated instants. Even the wear on the wooden-iron bearings shows it: shallow, glassy grooves rubbed smooth by the same half-second cycling past for generations.

What the factories produce is harder to parse than how they work. Open drying-trays woven from split cane sit at regular intervals along the lower decks, brimming with finished goods that the air around them seems reluctant to hold. In one tray lie stacks of porcelain-bark hinges that fold inward into an angle your eye slides off of; in another, neat spools of braided dry-fiber wire that feel noticeably heavier when you unwind a length and lighter when you coil it back up. Further along, automated sorting-fronds bundle crisp sheets of pressed tin-leaf embossed with neat rows of diagrams for machines with negative teeth, or tie up compact bales of something that looks like folded glare—cold, papery blocks that sting your eyes if you look straight at them and cast a faint pale brightness onto the underside of your chin.

No trains arrive to haul these goods away. No hands come to claim them. After resting in the trays for a few cycles, the conveyor-vines scoop the finished pieces up again and carry them toward older, more weathered terraces where the decking is silvered and frayed. There, left exposed to the cold light and the chalk-squalls, the impossible objects slowly dry out further, crack along their improbable edges, and crumble back into fine mineral dust, which sifts through the grates to feed the lower roots. Production and decay have folded into the same quiet loop.

Animals are scarce in all this brightness. You can walk for half a day across the dense, clicking aisles and see nothing that moves on its own behalf. Then, stopping to rest by a warm transformer-burl—one of the few spots in the basin that gives off a faint, dry heat—you might notice a fuse-locust clinging motionless to a ribbed ceramic bud. It is the size of your palm, pale as bleached paper, its body jointed like dry bamboo and its folded wings marked with crisp, schematic lines of graphite-grey. Its mouthparts are pressed delicately to a weathered copper seam, sipping a microscopic trickle of leaked voltage. If you reach toward it, it does not buzz or startle; it simply unfolds its papery wings and slips sideways into the glare, vanishing cleanly as soon as it passes behind a detached shadow.

Even rarer are the pale surveyors—tall, sparse creatures built of articulated plates of matte bone and dry amber-chitin, stepping on three spindly, shock-absorbed legs along the upper catwalks. You catch sight of one perhaps once in a week, moving with unhurried precision through the maze of louver-trunks. They ignore the fruit, the pollen, and the wiring alike. Instead, they halt beside the weathered spatial seams where the geometry of the catwalks pinches or repeats, lower their flat, eyeless heads, and nibble quietly at the fuzzy static-drift and stray echoes that collect in the joints of the world. Where a surveyor steps, the decking does not creak; for a heartbeat, the creature’s silhouette doubles slightly out of register, like a misprinted stamp, before snapping back to a single clean outline as it moves on into the crowded white forest of gantries.

Stand still long enough on one of the middle decks, and the scale of the place settles over you without drama. Above, the dense tiers of whitewashed leaves tilt in unison as the glare shifts a fraction of a degree, casting crisp, sliding grids of light across the braided walkways. Below, through layer after layer of dry roots, spinning vanes, and hushed looms, the continental machine continues its vast, cold, waterless breath—drawing in light and chalk-dust, spinning them through impossible gears, weathering its own contradictions into balance, and letting the whole immense enterprise turn in a bright, papery calm that feels as if it has never once needed to hurry, and never intends to stop.
