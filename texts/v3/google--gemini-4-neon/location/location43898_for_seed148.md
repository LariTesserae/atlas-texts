It sits low, sunk beneath whatever ground used to be above it, a cool, dark cavity no wider than a small market town and no taller than a nave. You can sense the walls even when you cannot see them—a curved, inward-pressing boundary a few hundred paces off in every direction—yet almost the entire volume inside is empty. Not open like a field, nor spacious like a hall, but *hollow*, the way the inside of a freshly split reed is hollow: vast, cold pockets of nothing separated by very little substance.

What substance there is, is entirely plant.

There is no stone down here, no poured concrete, no smelted iron. The narrow catwalk beneath your boots is lignified stalk, black-green and slick with condensation, its deck ribbed like the underside of a giant water-lily leaf. The bolted I-beams bracing the viaduct overhead are hardened xylem, extruded in crisp, geometric flanges with the sap still wet in their corners. Nothing here is weathered. Nothing has been worn smooth by decades of feet or rounded by wind. Every edge is embryonic, razor-clean, and faintly damp, as though the whole settlement were pushed out of the dark ten minutes ago and has not yet finished cooling.

At the ends of the streets, the town is literally still growing. A three-lane boulevard of woven root-plank simply stops in mid-air over the dark gulf, terminating in a cluster of pale, blind meristem tips—soft white buds the size of barrels, pulsing slowly as they divide and lay down new trusses, new curbs, new streetlamps into the empty dark. You can watch a railing elongate by a finger's width if you stand still and count your breaths. Farther out, a half-formed warehouse hangs from four tension-vines anchored to the unseen ceiling; its lower half is crisp, louvered wood-pith housing rows of quiet turbines, while its upper stories dissolve into curled, damp fiddleheads that have not yet decided whether to become a roof or a smokestack.

Because the density of matter is so thin, the whole town reads like a diagram drawn in dark ink across a black room. Streets do not sit on earth; they span the void on spindly arches of braided cane, layered three and four tiers deep, with enormous gulfs of cool, unoccupied dark between one thoroughfare and the next. Standing on a junction platform, you can drop a pebble straight past four levels of suspended infrastructure—past a tram-wire, past a conduit-bundle, past the ribbed hull of a condensing tank—and never hear it hit the bottom. Sometimes you don't hear it because the bottom is a bed of soft, wet moss-pulp far below. Sometimes you don't hear it because halfway down, the pebble slows to a halt and hangs there in the cool air, suspended alongside three beads of rainwater that stopped falling an hour ago, trapped in a pocket where downwardness has not yet been fully implemented.

Light barely exists here. There is no sky, only a low, matte darkness that absorbs sight a few yards beyond any ledge. What illumination there is leaks from the infrastructure itself. Thick phloem cables run along the undersides of the viaducts, carrying a damp, bio-galvanic current from the outer membrane toward the central works; wherever condensation bridges a poorly insulated node, a cold blue-green spark pops softly, lighting up the slick black ribs of a factory truss for half a second before the dark folds back in. Faint, greenish-white blisters glow along the joints of the tram-rails, bright enough to show the sheen of wet wax on the floorboards and the pale steam of your breath, never bright enough to reveal the far side of the chasm.

And yet the place is unmistakably industrial. Organized, gridded, numbered. Municipal plates grow directly out of the bark at every junction like flat, pale shelf-fungi, their faces marked with raised dark digits—though the numbering is unreliable. One crossing is marked *12*, the next *12*, the third *19*, and the fourth bears a numeral that looks familiar until you try to read it aloud, at which point it twists into a neat botanical diagram of a seed-coat. Along the main spine of the cavity, factories operate at a steady, hushed cadence. Osmotic boilers—swollen, gourd-like vats hooped in dark, calcified vine—draw moisture out of the cool air and build quiet pressure to drive reciprocating pistons of dense, oily heartwood. Electrified looms strung between two parallel viaducts clatter silently—or nearly silently—weaving long translucent ribbons of pale cambium-cloth that spool onto slowly turning drums. Automated tram-pods, shaped like elongated seed-husks, glide along greased silk-cables from the raw boundary wall into the interior, carrying bundles of pale, dripping pith to the dynamos.

It should be deafening, all this industry packed into a small enclosed space. Instead, it is almost quiet.

The plant-matter drinks sound like cold wet felt, and the vast empty spaces between structures do not carry noise the way ordinary air does. A massive wooden press fifty feet across the void drops its hammer with visible force, shivering the vines that hold it, and produces nothing at all—until forty seconds later, when a muffled, damp *thup* arrives from directly behind your left ear, soft as a thumb pressing into wet dough. The electrical substations do not roar; they produce a dry, sub-audible vibration that you feel mostly in the hinges of your jaw. What you actually hear, standing on a damp street-rib in the dark, is the faint intermittent *tick-tock* of condensation dripping from waxy eaves into shallow leaf-sumps, the papery rustle of unseen traffic along the overhead cables, and the wet, minuscule *creak* of new city blocks expanding into the dark.

The air smells of crushed fern-stem, cold rain on iron, ozone, and raw green wood. It is always cellar-cool, damp enough that moisture collects on every upward-facing surface without ever forming proper rivers. Water lives here in pockets: shallow, glass-still pools cradled in the cupped bases of structural leaves; slender trickle-channels running down the center of the viaducts to feed the boiler sumps; occasional cold, silent mist-fronts that bloom abruptly in the middle of the cavity, coat every girder in fresh beads of dew, and vanish three minutes later as the osmotic pumps drink them back down.

Into this sparse, spindly skeleton of a factory town is packed an astonishing abundance of animal life.

At first, because the light is so poor and the space so hollow, the cavity looks uninhabited. Then your eyes adjust to the faint galvanic flicker, and you realize every single thin surface is swarming. There is almost no volume of solid matter here, so the fauna has become thin, clingy, interstitial, and relentless.

Along the underside of the power-vines, hundreds of pale, eyeless grazers move in brisk, upside-down processions, scraping the electric lichen off the bark with comb-like mouths. They are no thicker than stiff paper, long-limbed and jointless, sliding over one another without a sound. Inside the hollow pith of the tram-supports live dense colonies of fistula-beetles—glossy, wet-shelled creatures the size of plums with needle-like mandibles—that chew out the soft interior of unfinished buildings while leaving the load-bearing vascular bundles untouched, effectively pruning the town's architecture as it grows. Larger hunters stalk the dark junctions: spindly, lantern-bellied stalkers with six backwards-bending knees that hang motionless beneath a viaduct for minutes at a time, then drop like falling leaves onto a passing tram-pod to pry the grazing swarms off its hull.

Some of the fauna barely obeys physical residency at all. Schools of flat, dark silhouettes—shaped vaguely like wading birds, or like jointed pond-skaters—glide across the wet decks of the factories, hunting actual, corporeal grubs. The silhouettes have no bodies casting them; when one catches a pale cambium-maggot, the maggot simply vanishes with a tiny wet snap, and the shadow grows marginally darker and splits into two. In the dim green glare of a short-circuiting transformer, you can watch moths made of folded damp membrane—wings veined like dead leaves, thoraxes humming with tiny borrowed charges—swarming around a spark, only for half the flock to fly straight through an iron-hard lignum girder as though the girder were still only a proposal.

Because reality itself is not holding together down here.

It is not a violent breakdown—no shattered skies, no howling rifts. It is quieter and far more unsettling: a failure of commitment. The place is brand-new, embryonic, and its rules have not cured.

Walk east along Junction 12 toward the central dynamo and it takes forty measured paces. Turn immediately around and walk back to where you started, and you arrive in nine steps, with the cool mist now blowing from behind you instead of into your face. A row of six osmotic looms sits humming in a damp, dark bay; while you watch, the fourth loom loses its grip on being industrial machinery and quietly becomes a dense cluster of black-stemmed tree ferns swaying in a draft that isn't there. The cambium ribbon feeding into it continues right through the fronds, emerging on the far side already woven, until ten seconds later the ferns snap back into gears, bobbins, and greased wooden levers without a skipped beat.

Even the boundary of the small cavity refuses to stay polite. Look down a narrow side-alley of extruded root-slate, and you can see the curved, fibrous outer wall of the entire world perhaps eighty yards away—damp, dark, and threaded with pale feeder-roots. Yet a freight-tram glides down that alley, passes through a dim green signal-post, and continues receding into the dark for five full minutes, shrinking to a pinpoint far past where the wall should have stopped it. If you throw a stick toward that same wall, it bounces off solid wet bark eighty yards away. The tram and the wall simply occupy different agreements about how much room exists in that direction.

Whole tremors of this un-calibration roll through the cavity like weather. They arrive without wind or sound: a sudden drop in the already cool temperature, a brief dimming of the blue-green galvanic leaks, and then a swath of the settlement slips sideways in its definitions. A three-story pumping station turns smoothly inside-out over the span of four heartbeats, exposing its damp hollow intestines of coiled root-pipes and frantic, startled beetle-colonies to the dark air, before folding cleanly back into its proper shell. A shallow condensate pool lifts off its foundation-leaf as a single cohesive sheet of trembling cold water, drifts six feet horizontally through a catwalk, and settles into a boiler trough on the next tier down, while dozens of translucent, shrimp-like scavengers kick madly inside it, completely unbothered by the commute.

The wildlife does not panic during these squalls; it exploits them. The predators know which junctions tend to shorten in distance, waiting at the far end of a forty-pace bridge that abruptly becomes four paces wide, snatching startled grazers before they can register the lost distance. Certain blind, waxy spiders spin webs not between girders, but across the pockets of stalled gravity, catching suspended drops of condensation and the drifting spores that hang frozen there, harvesting a pantry that hangs motionless in mid-empty air.

What the factories are manufacturing is never fully clear, though the whole settlement works at it with hushed, relentless urgency. Raw pale pith is harvested from the curved outer wall by slow, claw-tipped vine-cranes, hauled across the dark voids by silent trams, steeped in the cool condensate boilers, pressed through wooden rollers, and spun on the flickering looms into endless bolts of damp, slightly luminous white fabric. The fabric is then carried out to the growing meristem tips at the unfinished ends of the streets—where the new architecture drinks it up, darkening and hardening as it extrudes another girder, another stair, another numbered municipal plate. Meanwhile, the abundant wildlife eats the pith off the trams, nests inside the boilers, short-circuits the cables to drink the sparks, and drags strips of fresh cambium-cloth down into the damp dark hollows to line its burrows.

A town growing itself out of living wood inside a small, cool, near-empty dark, using industrial engines to weave provisional reality fast enough to outpace the creatures eating it—and the raw, unformed void that keeps forgetting what a gear, a step, or a foot of distance is supposed to be.

Stand still long enough on one of the damp black catwalks, and the place begins to treat you as part of the draft. Condensation beads coolly on your sleeves. A pale, paper-thin grazer tests the leather of your boot with its silent, comb-like mouth, finds no electric lichen, and glides effortlessly onto the railing beside you. Across the dark gulf, a faint green spark blooms on an overhead vine, illuminating for a split second three tiers of skeletal plant-factories, a dozen hanging tram-lines, a cloud of motionless mid-air raindrops, and thousands of tiny, pale shapes streaming briskly along every narrow thread of solid matter—and then the dark closes back in, cool and damp and mostly quiet, while somewhere beneath your feet, with a soft wet creak, another few inches of street push blindly out into the nothing.
