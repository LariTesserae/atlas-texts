It sits inside the crown of a mountain so old it has forgotten how to be sharp.

Across the vast, pale expanse below, the rest of the range has been ground down by geological ages into low, ribbed humps of rust-colored stone and flat salt-pan basins, a landscape long past its upheavals and settled into its final bone. Only this one plug of dark, fused basalt and iron-shot sandstone held out against the wind, rising alone into the thin upper air. Even here, the stone has been smoothed to a dull satin finish—every edge rounded, every softer vein scooped out into honeycombed flutes and deep pockets. At its summit, millennia of dry grit-storms and salt weathering hollowed out a wide, overhanging cauldron, no more than a hundred and fifty paces across from lip to lip, curling slightly inward at the rim like the mouth of an earthen jar.

Inside that bowl, crammed from the dusty floor to the curving overhang, is a city.

You hear it before you see it. Even from the narrow, wind-scoured approach trail outside—where the air is so thin a stranger's lungs burn and the distant valleys look bleached and flat as old parchment—a steady, muffled roar rises out of the rock. Step through one of the low, shoulder-wide wind-slots cut through the rim, and the sound closes around you at once. The stone hollow acts like the body of a wooden drum. Above, the high-altitude wind shears continuously across the rock lip, producing a deep, dry bass hum that never quite drops into the sheltered interior. Beneath that ceiling of wind, everything inside is loud: thousands of cliff-swifts and pale rock-doves chattering and whistling from the honeycombed overhang, their wings rattling like shaken canvas whenever a flock shifts; the ceaseless, metallic saw of dry-country cicadas hidden in the roof-thickets; the hollow clack of weighted wooden looms, the rhythmic thump of stone pestles grinding roasted seeds in deeply hollowed mortars, the metallic *tink-tink* of cold-worked copper being hammered thin on smooth riverless boulders, goats bleating from stacked wicker pens, and human voices calling back and forth across alleys narrow enough to span with outstretched arms.

The light is dim and brown-gold, even at noon. The overhanging cliff-lips steal half the sky, and what remains of the open mouth is knitted almost shut by an ancient, impossibly tangled canopy. Huge, gnarled trees—cousins to figs and thorny bottle-woods, with swollen, fibrous trunks wedged into deep stone fissures—lean out from the upper ledges and interlock their branches across the void. Over their crooked limbs, dense curtains of woody, waxy-leaved creepers and hanging dry-mosses weave a thick roof, patched further by the inhabitants with sewn goat-hide awnings and mats of split reed. Only dusty, slanting shafts of pale sunlight break through the gaps, striking a balcony here, a heap of drying clay jars there, while the lower tiers and winding passages swim in a warm, perpetual twilight lit by small, smoking saucers of tallow and the orange eyes of sunken dung-hearths.

Despite the extreme height, the hollow is stiflingly warm. The dark outer flanks of the monolith soak up the fierce, unfiltered high-altitude sun all day and radiate the heat inward long after dark. Sheltered from the wind, packed with bodies, kilns, penned animals, and composting dry foliage, the bowl holds the dry, close warmth of a bakery at the end of a long afternoon. The air smells of warm limestone dust, dry goat manure, crushed resinous leaves, roasted millet, woodsmoke, bird-lime, and the faint, sweet musk of dried fruit skins.

There is no running water here—no spring, no cistern fed by rain, not even a damp puddle in the deepest cellar. The sky yields almost nothing. Yet the place is astonishingly, aggressively lush. Every wall, crooked roof-beam, and stone terrace overflows with thick, tough, water-hoarding life. Giant rosettes of pale blue-green succulents—each leaf as thick as a forearm and coated in a chalky bloom—sit packed shoulder-to-shoulder along the flat roofs, their overlapping funnels angled to catch the scant condensation that forms on the coldest stone lips just before dawn. Fleshy, beaded vines cascade in heavy green curtains down the plaster facades, brushing the heads of passersby. Broad clay troughs, lashed to wooden balconies with rawhide, brim with dusty, silver-leafed herbs, thorny berry-shrubs, and fibrous tubers. Grey-green lichens crust every shadowed lintel, crisp as paper by afternoon, swelling faintly in the thin pre-dawn mist that occasionally brushes the peak before the sun burns it off. Nothing here wastes a drop. Steam from covered cooking pots is caught on suspended terracotta lids; morning dew is scraped from waxed-hide mist-nets hung in the upper wind-slots with bone spatulas and poured into narrow-necked clay amphorae kept in the coolest, deepest rock vaults; the moisture of breath and waste feeds the deep, slow taproots coiled beneath the flagstones.

Built into this tangled greenery is an urban knot of staggering density. Seven and eight stories of narrow rooms cling to the curved inner cliffs, stacked one atop another and stepping inward toward the shadowed floor until only a labyrinth of corkscrewing lanes, low tunnels, and creaking timber bridges remains open. Every structure is made from what the peak and its scrub can yield: sun-baked mud-brick tempered with chaff and animal hair, dry-laid slabs of ancient flat stone, and warped, knotty beams salvaged over centuries from deadfall trees. No timber is ever cut fresh—these trees grow a thumb's width in a human lifetime—so every beam and post has been reused five or ten times, glossy with hand-oil, scarred with old lash-marks, bound together with braided sinew, hemp cord, and notched hardwood pegs. There is no ironwork, no glass, no wheeled carts. Goods move in woven baskets slung across hunched shoulders or rise up the cliff-faces on squeaking wooden pulley-wheels greased with animal fat. Tools are modest and endlessly repaired: hammered bronze knives worn slender from sharpening, copper needles, obsidian scrapers, clay spindle-whorls, smooth bone awls.

And everywhere, woven straight through the human settlement as if no line separates them, is the wildlife. This is a crowded sanctuary in an otherwise barren sky. Nimble, small-bodied goats with hooves worn blunt on the stairs share the lower courtyards with fat, tailless rock-hyraxes that sun themselves in the rare shafts of light on the upper ledges and bark sharply when startled. Tiny copper-backed skinks and pale geckos swarm over every warm plaster wall, snapping up the clouds of dust-beetles, dry-moths, and biting midges that drift through the dim lanes. Beneath the eaves of every doorway hang the mud-gourd nests of swifts; in the deeper crevices of the rock, small fruit-and-insect bats rustle softly through the day, waiting for dusk to pour out through the wind-slots into the cold air outside. Even the roof-gardens rustle constantly with scratching finches and small, burrowing rodents that the people trap with horsehair snares for meat and fur. Nothing here is shy. A goat will shoulder a weaver aside on a narrow stair; a lizard will perch inches from a potter's spinning hand, waiting for an unearthed grub.

For all its noise and crowding, the place moves with a strange, geological patience. Nothing changes quickly at this altitude. The ancient stairs cut into the living stone of the bowl have been worn into smooth, glossy troughs four inches deep by generations of bare heels and rawhide sandals. Houses are never demolished; when a mud wall cracks, another skin of pale ochre plaster is laid over it, and when a lintel sags, another crooked prop is wedged beneath it, until the whole town looks less built than accreted, like a centuries-old wasp nest glued into the ribs of a skull. A single gourd-vine may drape across three generations of the same family's doorway. The dust motes hang almost motionless in the narrow gold shafts of light.

Every evening, as the sun drops behind the distant, flattened horizon of dead mountains, the temperature outside plunges toward freezing, and the wind over the rim rises a pitch higher, singing through the dry branches and the strings of hollow bird-bones hung in the clefts. Inside the bowl, the shadows thicken to soot, the tallow lamps flicker awake along the tiered galleries, and the warm stone gives back the day's heat slowly, hour by hour, holding the dense, chattering, leafy little world intact against the cold emptiness of the sky.
