High on the shoulder of a pale slate ridge, where the air thins enough to make the chest work for every breath, the mountain opens into a hollow bell of rock.

It is no wider than a cathedral nave and about as tall, though its roof has long since worn away into a ragged oval mouth. For untold millennia, dry wind has worked this pocket from both ends: mountain gales shearing across the upper lip while warm, bone-dry drafts rise from narrow fissures deep in the floor. Between them, the rock has been licked into sweeping flutes, arched ribs, and rounded eaves smooth as old bone. There is no water here—not a trickle, not a seep, not even the dark stain of ancient dampness. Nothing rots in this basin. When something dies, it turns to hollow shell, fragrant dust, or dry parchment, and the wind takes it apart by evening.

Even at midday, the light inside the bowl is dim and restless. The steep overhanging rim blocks the direct sun, leaving only a bruised, pewter-amber glow filtered through high clouds of suspended mineral dust. That glow never stays steady. Great shadows sweep across the scoured floor every few seconds—ribbons of torn foliage, sudden columns of ochre grit, wheeling ribbons of winged creatures crossing the open mouth overhead.

The first thing that strikes anyone stepping through the weathered western notch is the heat and the noise.

The updrafts pouring out of the floor-cracks carry the dry, steady warmth of an oven after the coals have been raked out, smelling sharply of toasted fennel, hot beeswax, and struck flint. They collide constantly with the colder mountain shear overhead, turning the open center of the bowl into a churning, unstable engine. Vortices spin up out of nowhere, race along the curved stone walls, slam into each other, and collapse with a pressure drop that pops the ears. The whole chamber roars like a bellows fed into a chimney, layered with whistling flutes, snapping canvas, rapid dry clattering, and sudden, pistol-sharp cracks.

Yet the floor itself is surprisingly bare. Wide sweeps of pale, honeycombed rock lie completely naked between presences, scrubbed clean by flying grit. The emptiness is essential; it is the room the storm needs to turn.

Clinging to the rims of the warm fissures, draped down the leeward alcoves, and cantilevered off the overhanging stone ribs are immense, exuberant islands of dry vegetation. Separated from one another by long stretches of empty air and bare rock, each colony is a dense, tangled riot of non-aqueous growth. Without a drop of water to swell their cells, these plants run on thick aromatic oils, pliable waxes, and electrostatic charge harvested straight from the friction of the blowing grit. 

Huge, pleated curtains of vellum-fern hang from the upper ledges, their fronds as wide as cloaks and thin as hammered brass foil, tinted verdigris, russet, and pale bone. Around the floor vents, tall thickets of oil-reed and gnarled tallow-shrubs crowd together in lush, multi-layered mats, their bark cracking into glossy amber scales that ooze warm, fragrant resin whenever the gusts bend them double. Their foliage is never quiet. As a vortex whips past, the fronds thrash and rattle like sheets of tin; ribbons tear free by the hundreds, whirl in bright green-and-copper clouds across the empty center of the chamber, and plaster themselves against distant colonies, where specialized root-hairs immediately latch on and draw the oils back out of the shredded leaf. What the wind rips away by morning, the warm resin-veins push back out by dusk. Along the sharpest, metallic-veined tips of the upper vines, faint violet sparks of static snap off into the dim air whenever the dust thickens.

The life moving through this roaring hollow is just as restless, and far louder.

Out in the wide open air between the stone ribs, hundreds of parchment-kites ride the violent eddies. Roughly the size of ravens, they have rigid, translucent wings stretched over hollow struts of dry chitin, lubricated at the hinge-joints with secreted wax. They rarely flap; instead they bank, fold, and snap their wings open to catch sudden cross-shears, producing loud, whip-like cracks that echo off the fluted walls. In frantic, constantly shifting ribbons, they dive into the thrashing plant-islands to scrape oily nectar from exposed seed-cups, then peel upward on the thermal draft before the next gust can dash them against the rock.

Along the shadowed stone ribs themselves, bands of dry-shelled climbers—flat, six-legged creatures plated in chalky ochre and dull bronze—scuttle rapidly from shelter to shelter. Because no soft call could ever be heard over the gale, they speak by percussion, hammering their hardened forelimbs directly against the resonant, hollow-fluted stone or rubbing serrated abdominal plates together in rapid, polyrhythmic bursts. In the narrow lee behind the ribs, colonial insects pack the crevices with papery, flute-shaped nests glued together from chewed fibers and warm plant-wax. The wind blows straight across the nest mouths and makes them howl. As the eddies shift and the internal draft changes, thousands of tiny workers rush to chew open new vents or plug old ones with soft wax pellets, regulating the heat inside their brood chambers. The result is an eerie, sliding chord—half a dozen low moans and high reedy shrieks continuously retuning themselves in real time as the storm shifts direction.

Anchored to the exposed stone arches between the colonies sit the bowl’s heavier hunters: lean, long-limbed beasts plated in overlapping, feather-thin keratin scales that hiss softly as the air slides past them. Their toes lock into the pitted rock with hooked claws, and their wide, fan-shaped ear-frills twitch constantly against the tumult, tracking the subtle pressure-ripples of approaching kites. When a kite tacks too close to the rock, a hunter lunges sideways—never quite releasing its hind anchors—snatching the glider from the air in a burst of snapping chitin and drifting chaff.

Scattered sparingly through the quietest seams of the stone are the marks of people who know how to endure this place, though nobody lives here for long.

Tucked deep under the southern eave, out of the worst vertical shear, stand three low wind-blinds—curved shells of stacked flat rock mortared together with darkened plant-resin and grit, barely tall enough to crouch inside. Braided cables of dried sinew and oil-vine stretch from hammered copper pins wedged into old chisel-holes in the rock, humming like taut bass strings in the gale. A few paces away, mounted on a worn stone socket, sits a hand-built baffle: a crude oval frame of fire-hardened wood stretched tight with cured hide, designed to be pivoted by a heavy lever so a worker can deflect a passing eddy and funnel the storm of loose wax-seeds into a hanging leather sack.

Inside the largest blind, sheltered from the flying grit, a flat slab of slate bears rows of incised tally-marks, and a wooden peg holds three pairs of carved bone-and-wax ear-plugs dangling on greased leather thongs. Further along the ledge, near a warm fissure choked with tallow-shrubs, a single primitive press has been wedged beneath an overhang—two grooved boulders and a long timber beam weighted with tied stones, its wooden trough crusted thick with layers of hardened, dark-gold resin from past harvests. A few small copper bells tied to a tether-line beside it thrash wildly on frayed cord, though their clanging is nearly drowned out by the chamber itself.

Every quarter-hour or so, the colliding drafts find a brief, violent balance, and the pressure inside the stone bell drops all at once.

When it happens, the howling hives drop a full tone in unison. Across the lush overhangs, hundreds of swollen, gas-filled seed-bladders detonate like muffled firecrackers, flinging sudden blizzards of oily silver fluff into the dim air. For ten frenzied seconds the empty center of the bowl turns into a boiling bazaar: kites wheeling sharply downward in crackling sheets, climbers spilling recklessly onto the naked floor, hunters lunging from their arches, and dry torn fronds spinning upward toward the overcast rim like embers in a chimney. A sharp blue-white spark leaps from the tip of a copper anchor-pin to a thrashing bronze vine with a crisp snap.

Then the upper shear slams back across the mouth of the rock. The vortex reverses, sweeping the silver chaff straight up into the thin mountain sky, the plants bend back hard against their ledges, the hives climb back up into their high, trembling whine, and the smooth old stone holds the roar as steadily as it has for ten thousand dry years.
