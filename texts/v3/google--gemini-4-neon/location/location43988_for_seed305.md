The light arrives here without mercy. At this altitude the air is too thin to soften it, too cold to hold a haze that might blunt its edge; it strikes the freshly broken rock like a welding arc, whitening every upward facet and slicing black, razor-sharp shadows into the clefts below. If you squint toward the horizon, the world does not fade into atmospheric blue. It simply continues—shelf after jagged shelf of newly uplifted highland, terraced plateaus of shattered basalt and raw quartz, rising toward knife-edged ridges so far away they appear etched onto the pale sky with a diamond needle.

Nothing up here has had time to round off. The mountains are young, thrust out of the crust with violent, unhealed fractures: sheared cliffs of dark magnetite, splintered scree slopes whose stones still carry glass-sharp margins, exposed veins of native copper and pale sulfur gleaming raw in the sun. Yet almost none of that stone is actually bare.

Every ledge, every fracture, every vertical wall is packed tight. There is no gap between one living thing and the next.

Underfoot—where you can even find a foothold between the bodies—the ground is buried beneath a deep, springy, damp pelt of vegetation. It looks at first glance like a tangle of frost-bitten scrub and alpine moss, until you notice the gleam along the stems. The flora here is lush in a stubborn, metallic way: thick cushions of tarnished-teal lichen laced with fibrous copper; dense thickets of black, waxy foil-ferns whose flat blades drink the blinding light and convert it directly into charge; braided mats of wire-heather gripping the newly split stone with acid-secreting roots that mine raw ore straight from the fissures. Every leaf and filament is beaded with cold moisture. There are no streams up here and no true rain—only the relentless condensation of thin air meeting surfaces colder than ice, leaving the entire carpet slick with freezing dew, dark brine, and a fine, wet rime that never quite dries and never quite pools.

And wedged into this lush, glistening mat, shoulder to shell to carapace, covering the plateaus from cliff-lip to horizon, is the fauna.

The first thing you register is not their shape, but the noise. It is deafening, yet it has nothing frantic in it. There is no chittering, no stampeding, no sudden cry of pursuit. Instead, the highlands roar with the immense, grinding volume of something running at impossible torque and glacial speed. A deep, chest-rattling thrum vibrates up through the soles of your boots—the collective percussion of millions of heavy biological pistons cycling once every twenty seconds. Over it sits a continuous, shrieking hiss of venting steam, the wet groan of mineral-plated joints turning against one another under tons of pressure, and a loud, dry crackle like burning brush that runs in waves across the slopes as static arcs leap between living conductors.

Nothing here hurries. In the biting cold, speed is a waste of heat. Everything moves with the patience of a press.

Packed flank to flank across a broad terrace of fractured stone is a herd of kiln-backs—creatures the bulk of boulders, low-slung on six columnar legs whose joints lock with audible, calcified ratchets. You can watch one for a full minute and barely see it advance half a pace. Its wide, shovel-like lower jaw—sheared from dense, iron-rich enamel—drags slowly across the wet wire-heather and raw mineral scree, scraping up foliage, gravel, and sulfur dust alike. Inside its vaulted, heavily insulated ribcage, a series of chambered guts works like a slow-burning blast furnace. You can hear the low, cavernous roar of internal oxidation if you stand beside one, and smell the sharp tang of hot slag, roasted moss, and ozone. Along its spine run rows of bony, fluted chimneys; every few minutes, a fleshy valve parts with a loud *hiss*, releasing a plume of scalding white vapor into the subzero air. The steam climbs ten feet, freezes instantly into glittering dust, and settles back onto the backs of the neighboring beasts as a fresh film of damp frost.

Beneath the kiln-backs, filling the narrow spaces between their legs and cramming every cleft in the young rock, live the dynamo-colonies. They resemble flattened, interlocking bivalves the size of dinner plates, their shells ribbed with piezoelectric quartz grown directly from the minerals they lick from the stone. Tens of thousands of them pack a single vertical crevice so tightly their shells deform to fit one another like irregular masonry. They do not scuttle or flee. Instead, an entire cliff face of them contracts in a single, agonizingly slow wave—taking minutes to compress by a quarter of an inch—and then releases. The friction and pressure generate immense voltage. Blue-white sparks snap lazily across their wet, mossy shells, jumping from colony to colony in loud, sharp *cracks* that light up the black shadows of the fissures. The current keeps their internal fluids from freezing and splits the thin films of condensed moisture into wisps of gas that the surrounding flora eagerly absorbs.

Nothing here was built. If you stand back on a high spur and look down across the twenty-mile basin below, your eyes will try to trick you into seeing an open-pit foundry, a continental hydroelectric plant, an endless mechanized yard of smelters and condensers. You see the drifting plumes of white exhaust, the rhythmic rise and fall of massive ribbed housings, the glinting threads stretched taut from crag to crag, the dull orange glow leaking from beneath armored flaps in the deep ravines, the neat, terraced heaps of refined metallic pellets excreted beneath the resting herds.

But there is not a straight line anywhere, nor a single rivet, nor a wall raised by a hand.

The gleaming cables slung across the gorges—humming loudly in the freezing air, sagging under beads of rime—are spun by foil-wings, broad, slow-drifting moths with mica-thin membranes that spend days floating on the cold thermal updrafts rising from the herds. Their spinnerets extrude conductive, copper-doped silk, anchoring one crowded plateau to the next so that excess electrical charge and warmth equalize slowly across miles of terrain. Where a cable snaps under the weight of ice, it lashes down into the dense pack of creatures with a thunderous whip-crack and a shower of violet sparks; the beasts it strikes barely flinch, merely shifting their heavy, insulated hides a fraction of an inch over the next quarter-hour to absorb the surge.

In the shadowed troughs between the razor-edged ridges, where the blinding light cannot reach directly, the air is thicker with freezing fog and the stink of hot oil. Here live the bellows-mats—vast, leathery sheets of colonial flesh anchored directly to the wet basalt, swelling over ten minutes until they bulge tight as drums, then deflating with a booming, resonant sigh that blasts dry air across fields of luminous, iron-digesting lichens. The lichens burn a dull, chemical crimson under the forced draft, melting tiny pockets of slush in the rock crevices. Slender, hydraulic-legged siphoners—tall, spindly things whose translucent limbs pulse with pressurized, antifreeze-thick green blood—stand wedged shoulder to shoulder around these wet pockets, dipping hollow brass-colored proboscises into the lukewarm mineral soup and pumping it upward with a loud, wet, rhythmic *clunk-clunk-clunk* that echoes off the fresh cliff walls.

Every organism here is both a factory and a raw material for the neighbor pressed against its flank. Nothing is wasted because there is nowhere empty to throw waste.

Watch a ridge long enough—give it an hour, then two—and the slow choreography of the place begins to reveal itself. The herds do not migrate in dashes; they creep like glaciers along the newly exposed mineral seams, taking a full season to clear a single mile of copper-moss and raw quartz. Behind them, they leave trails of warm, steaming excrement rich in smelted iron nodules and purified sulfur. Almost immediately, before the heaps can even freeze solid, dense carpets of silver-plated creeping vines uncoil from the crevices, threading themselves through the cooling slag, drawing out the residual heat while swarms of heavy-jawed mill-beetles wedge themselves into the pile, slowly grinding the metallic lumps down into fine dust with a sound like a thousand dry files scraping stone.

Even death is slow and mechanical. When a kiln-back finally loses its internal fire, its massive body cools over days, its chimneys falling silent while the frost thickens across its ribbed back. The surrounding herd does not scatter; there is no room to scatter. Its neighbors simply press closer, leaning their warm flanks against the cooling bulk, while bone-borers with spiraling, drill-like radulae slowly bore into the hardened carcass, harvesting the refined copper tendons, the dense iron teeth, the pressurized oil sacs—disassembling the dead engine piece by piece over months while new foil-ferns sprout directly from the damp seams of its carapace.

Step back, climb higher onto a raw spire of fractured obsidian where only the thinnest crust of electrified moss and flattened static-limpets cling to the stone, and the scale of the place becomes dizzying.

This is not a valley or a single mountain range. It is a whole continental uplift—hundreds of miles of jagged, newly born highlands under a sky white with high-altitude glare. And because every square yard of it is packed with slow-burning, steam-venting, charge-generating life, the landscape makes its own strange weather.

Far out across the blinding plateaus, immense, sluggish banks of condensation-mist hang low over the densest herds—never rising into true clouds, just pooling like pale milk in the tectonic basins before freezing into fine, glittering sleet that drifts sideways in the thin wind. Above them, invisible until the air suddenly tastes of pennies and every metallic frond on the ridges stands rigid and humming, vast electrostatic fronts drift across the continent at a walking pace. When one passes overhead, the ambient roar doubles. Billions of creatures shift their internal resistances in slow unison; arcs of white fire dance harmlessly across the tangled silk cables spanning the gorges; the cold air smells sharply of scorched frost and wet iron.

Then the front passes, moving on toward the next distant range of razor-sharp peaks. The light remains blinding. The air remains bitterly, achingly cold. Below, without a single wall or chimney built by any mind, the packed highlands keep grinding, hissing, humming, and sweating damp frost into the brilliant sky—a raw, untouched continent running steadily on bone, ore, sunlight, and patience.
