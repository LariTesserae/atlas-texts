The air is warm before you even reach the bottom of the descent—a heavy, bath-warm stillness that sits against the skin and does not move. Down here, low in the bedrock, the space is no wider than a small meeting hall, yet you can never see across it. Every foot of it is crowded. Thick, swollen pillars of travertine rise from floor to ceiling, standing so close together that in many places a person has to turn sideways to pass between them. Between the columns hang pleated curtains of pale stone, bulbous ribs, and low drooping bosses that force the head down. There is no open center, no clearing, no bare stretch of ground—only a dense, petrified thicket of wet limestone occupying nearly every cubic foot of the chamber.

Light enters only from a narrow, crooked cleft high in the eastern shoulder of the roof, partly choked with wedged boulders from the ground above. What survives the drop is a dim, flat grayness, barely strong enough to catch the wet sheen on the nearest pillars. A few paces inward it weakens to the color of damp ash, and beyond that the deeper alcoves hold a warm, velvety shadow where shapes are felt more than seen. There is no wind to shift the gloom, no moving foliage above the fissure to dapple it. Only at midday does the pale gleam on the slickest columns brighten by a fraction; for the rest of the cycle, the chamber rests in a steady, heavy dusk.

Everything is wet. Warm water, rising invisibly through hairline fractures in the low floor, keeps the entire basin saturated. It does not rush or channel. Instead, it fills hundreds of tightly packed, interlocking rimstone pools that cover the floor from wall to wall like overlapping scales. Each basin is edged with a low, scalloped lip of deposited calcite, no thicker than a thumb and worn smooth as tallow. The pools sit brim-full, holding water a few inches to a knee’s depth, and wherever one overflows, the water slips across the rounded rim in a silent, unbroken sheet scarcely thicker than a fingernail. Beads of condensation cling to every ceiling drape and pillar flank, swelling slowly until weight overcomes surface tension and a single heavy drop lets go.

Because the stone formations crowd so tightly together, sound does not carry or echo here. There is no cavernous ring. The wet surfaces and close columns swallow noise almost where it starts. What remains is a hushed, unhurried quiet: an occasional muffled *plip* where a drop strikes a shallow pool behind a curtain of rock, the faint tick of another hitting a glazed ledge, and beneath it all a silence so dense and damp you can hear the small shift of your own breathing.

The stone itself looks old and completely settled. Nothing is jagged or freshly broken. Millennia of mineral-rich seepage have softened every angle, burying sharp fractures under thick skins of flowstone in muted bands of bone-white, wet ochre, dull iron-brown, and pewter gray. Where a boulder fell long ago, it has been welded to the floor by layers of pale sinter until it reads as part of the bedrock. The place gives the impression of having reached its final shape ages ago and simply holding it—warm, humid, and sealed off from the weather above. Outside, seasons may crack the surface rock with frost or bake it dry, but down here the temperature never wavers, the water never drops a finger's width, and the air holds the exact same heavy warmth year after year.

Nothing truly green lives in it. The light is too weak and the stone too bare for moss or fern, save for a dull, slippery smudge of olive-brown film clinging to one wet pillar flank directly beneath the overhead cleft—more a stain on the rock than a plant. In the dimness beyond that patch, life is almost invisible. If you stand motionless beside one of the deeper rimstone basins and let the faint surface glare settle, you may eventually notice a pale, translucent shrimp no longer than a grain of wheat drifting lazily along the submerged silt, or two or three tiny, paper-shelled blind snails stuck fast to the underside of a wet rim. High in a dry pocket tucked between fused stalactites, a single pallid harvestman with thread-thin legs rests without moving for hours. You can spend half a day among the columns and believe the chamber completely empty of creatures.

Yet people have been here—not recently enough to leave a footprint or a smell of smoke, and never in numbers great enough to remake the place, only enough to leave scattered, quiet marks woven into the stone. Along one winding path where the pillars pinch closest together, shallow footholds and hand-wide notches have been pecked into the slick travertine lips with a stone point or a soft metal chisel, now half-glazed over again by fresh mineral film. In a few places, flat shards of dark limestone have been carefully wedged atop natural rimstone ridges to dam a pool a palm’s breadth deeper, though calcite has already cemented the seams shut so tightly they cannot be pried loose.

Tucked into narrow niches between columns sit small, thick-walled vessels of coarse unglazed clay—wide-mouthed bowls and pinched cups left directly beneath the steadiest ceiling drips. Some are brimming with clear, warm water; two or three have sat undisturbed so long that a white collar of travertine has grown up around their bases, fusing them permanently to the shelf. On a waist-high ledge near the edge of the skylight's reach rest three shallow stone dishes, their hollowed centers blackened faintly with old tallow soot, now cold and lightly frosted with damp mineral dust. On the broad, smooth belly of a central pillar, someone once scored a neat row of short vertical cuts—seventeen faint grooves, half-filled with pale lime—and nearby, lying on the floor of a clear ankle-deep pool, is the stub of a carved wooden scoop, no longer wood at all, encased in a brittle gray crust of stone that preserves the rough marks of the knife that shaped it.

None of these things disrupt the chamber. The bowls, the notches, the wedged slabs and soot-stained saucers are absorbed into the same crowded stillness as the dripstone curtains and the pools. Whatever purpose brought hands down here with chisels and clay—gathering mineral water, sitting in the constant heat, leaving small offerings in the dark—has left no hurry behind it. The water keeps welling silently from beneath the floor. The air holds its heavy, unchanging warmth. A drop gathers on the tip of a low stone tongue, swells for a long half-minute, and falls into a brimming clay bowl, sending a single ring across the surface before the water settles flat again.
