You feel the walls before you see them, because there is almost nothing to see. The hollow is a narrow cleft cut high into the spine of a young mountain range, nearly four thousand meters up, tucked right inside the cold underside of the cloud deck. It is barely the length of a cathedral nave and half as wide, pinched between jagged, nearly vertical walls of dark basalt that lean inward and vanish overhead into moonless cloud. At the far end, the rock pinches into a ragged throat opening onto sheer drop. There is no sky here, only a slightly thinner blackness above the jagged rim, and inside the bowl the darkness is nearly absolute.

The air is thin, cold enough to stiffen wet fingers, and never still for half a second. A mountain gale funnels through the upper throat and slams into the rock bowl, where it collides with the roaring updraft blown out of raw steel ventilation ducts pinned directly into the cliff face. The two forces tear each other into violent, unpredictable eddies. Mist does not fall as rain; it drives sideways in cold, stinging wisps, condensing on every sharp stone edge, every wet leaf, and every vibrating steel housing until everything is slick and gleaming in the rare pinpricks of light. There is no stream, no pond—only pockets of dark rainwater trapped in jagged basalt splits and the tight rosettes of bromeliads, trembling continuously under the vibration of the ground.

Beneath your boots, the stone is geologically young and unforgiving. Frost-heave, deep tremors, and subterranean blasting have splintered the dark volcanic rock into knife-edged shelves and fresh angular scree that has never had time to wear smooth. Loose shards skitter down the steep walls in the gusts, clattering dryly against metal and dropping out of sight into the undergrowth. Nothing here has been graded, paved, or built upon. There are no walls, no roofs, no concrete pads, no roads or shelters—not even a cleared path.

Yet the hollow is choked with heavy industry.

Bolted straight into the bare, razor-edged rock are steel anchor rings holding massive chains, and on the raw ledges sit skid-mounted diesel-electric generators and high-pressure air compressors, airlifted or winched in without a scrap of architecture around them. They roar blindly in the dark, laboring furiously in the oxygen-starved altitude. Their turbochargers emit a high, desperate whine as they force thin mountain air into hot cylinders, coughing intermittent plumes of rich black soot and glowing carbon sparks that are instantly shredded and chilled by the gale. Thick black armored power cables snake unburied across the floor of the cleft, looped over jagged outcrops and draped loosely through the tangled branches, carrying high voltage from the skids down into steel borehole casings driven directly into the fractured rock. Deep below the stone floor, unseen drills and pumps shudder continuously, sending a hard, numbing vibration up through the basalt.

High overhead, strung between two jagged rock shoulders across the narrow blackness of the throat, an automated aerial ore tramway runs without pause. Heavy iron buckets rattle past on thick greased haul-cables, swaying violently in the crosswinds, their pulleys shrieking high above the foliage as tons of crushed rock travel from some unseen upper cut toward the valley far below. Every minute or so, a pneumatic relief valve down on a borehole head snaps open with a gunshot crack, venting a scalding, ear-splitting jet of compressed air and oily vapor that turns instantly to dense white fog in the cold wind, whirling across the cleft before vanishing into the dark.

And crowding right up against every vibrating engine, swallowing the slack of the cables, pressing hard against the sharp rock walls, is a suffocating density of high-altitude vegetation.

Nothing about the cold, the dark, or the violence of the wind has kept the mountain flora from packing this small pocket three and four layers deep. Stunted, iron-hard cloud-forest trees with peeling wet bark twist out of narrow crevices, their limbs interlocked so tightly there is scarcely space to pass a shoulder between them. Beneath them, dense thickets of mountain bamboo and giant tree-ferns crowd every foot of uneven ground, overlapping, tangled with tough woody vines and heavy curtains of wet clubmoss. Epiphytic bromeliads and bloated cushions of cold sphagnum cling not only to every branch and basalt face, but to the chains, the steel skids, and the cold rubber jackets of the power cables wherever a line stays unheated.

In the constant turbulence, the entire mass of greenery is in a state of frantic, chaotic motion. Bamboo stalks bend nearly double and whip back with sharp snaps; heavy fern fronds slap repeatedly against hot steel exhaust guards and tear themselves into green ribbons against the splintered rock. The air is thick with bruised leaves, shreds of lichen, and dark droplets flicked off thrashing branches, all caught in wild spinning vortices that rise, stall, and slam back down into the thicket. Leaves nearest the diesel stacks are glazed with a greasy film of carbon soot mixed with condensation, which drips steadily down into the moss below. Dry chaff and torn wet fronds pile against the wire intake grates of the roaring compressors, sucked tight against the mesh until the wind shifts and rips them loose again.

Light exists here only as accident and warning. Deep in the wet tangle, the scratched glass faces of pressure gauges glow a dim, jittering amber, illuminating nothing more than an inch of trembling needle and a bead of condensation. Far up near the jagged eastern rim, a single red obstruction beacon mounted to a cable strut blinks on a slow rhythm, briefly turning the boiling cloud-mist a bruised crimson before letting the dark swallow it again. Closer to the ground, where dampness and thin mountain air lower the resistance around a spliced high-voltage junction, pale blue-violet corona sparks spit and crackle against wet fern tips, throwing a sudden, razor-sharp flash across slick basalt edges and dripping leaves before snapping back to pitch black.

The smell is dense and layered: crushed green stems, cold wet rock, sharp ozone from the crackling lines, hot gear oil, and unburned diesel hanging low in the eddies before a fresh gust scours the hollow clean and packs it again with cold, mineral-scented cloud.

For all the lushness crammed into this small stone vessel, nothing moves of its own will. There are no birds roosting in the gnarled branches, no bats skimming the throat of the cleft, no frogs hidden in the water-filled cups of the bromeliads. Even around the faint amber glow of the gauge faces, not a single moth or midge appears. The relentless, multi-layered roar—the pounding engines, the metallic scream of overhead cables, the sub-bass grinding of rock beneath the floor, the whip-crack of venting pressure—paired with the bitter fumes and ceaseless trembling of every surface, has emptied the cleft of animal life completely. Not a web spans the gaps between bamboo stalks; nothing could anchor one in air this violent, and nothing flies here to be caught.

Only the plants and the machines endure, locked together in the dark. The roots grip the fractured basalt and curl tightly around cold armored conduits; the engines shudder on their raw steel skids, coughing heat and black smoke into the wet, thrashing leaves; the gale keeps pouring over the sharp stone lip, turning the whole cramped hollow into a churning, deafening cauldron of cold mist, tearing foliage, and live industrial current, changing shape every second yet held tightly inside its narrow walls of jagged rock.
