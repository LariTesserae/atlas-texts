The sky here has almost no blue left in it. At this altitude, under a sun that hangs pale and surgical near the zenith, the air above the plateau is a bleached, shimmering silver-white, and the ground answers it glare for glare. For miles in every direction, a broad, shallow tableland stretches toward blunted rims of chalk-colored rock, weathered down over ages into smooth, low ledges and stepped basins. Everywhere across that expanse, water lies thin as glass over stone.

It is brutally, steadily hot—not the dry, brittle bake of a desert, but a radiant, scalding heat that rises directly from the rock underfoot as well as pressing down from the sky. The plateau sweats. Hot brine and silica-heavy water well up continually through deep, hair-thin fissures in the basin floor, never erupting, never frothing, simply rising until the terraces brim and spill across their pale lips in sheets only a few millimeters thick. Walking out onto the flats—if anyone foolishly left the raised stoneways—would mean stepping into water hot enough to redden skin, clear as gin over smooth, pearlescent sinter.

And yet there is no roar of runoff, no hiss of steam, no chatter of wind through leaves.

There are no leaves. Nothing green grows on the plateau. The heat and mineral saturation have excluded roots, stalks, and foliage altogether, leaving the terrain stripped to bare geology and the faint, slick films that tolerate scalding brine: papery crusts of ochre and rust-orange microbial mat clinging to the warmest runnels, and here and there, high on the dry coping of an old wall, a brittle scab of salt-bleached lichen indistinguishable from efflorescing lime. Without branches to catch the breeze or soil to hold a root system, the basin feels exposed, mineral, stripped down to bone and mirror.

The silence is the first thing that unnerves a newcomer, and the last thing one ever stops noticing. It is not the dead silence of an empty ruin; the plateau is heavily worked and densely inhabited by living things. It is a hushed, deliberate, almost architectural quiet, thick with heat-shimmer and the faint, metallic taste of wet chalk, sulfur, and ozone.

Across the vast bowl of the tableland spreads a city—if a thing this wide and sparsely laid out can still be called a city. Its quarters do not crowd together. Great distances of flooded evaporation pan, gleaming travertine flat, and steaming drainage grid separate one built cluster from another, half a mile or more of blinding open space between a residential terrace and an electrolytic yard, or between a condenser block and a switching station. Heat demands distance here. Nothing can be packed tight without cooking itself. Seen from the worn western escarpment, the settlement reads like a pale circuit laid out across a white dish: clean-edged rectangles of refractory brick and glazed green-white ceramic tile, linked by ruler-straight causeways riding high on widely spaced stone piers.

Everything built here is heavy, thick-walled, and pale to throw back the sun. Deep recessed colonnades slice black-violet wedges of shadow into the bases of the buildings, the only true darkness on the plateau. Above the flat roofs rise clusters of tall, louvered convection towers—hollow chimneys of porous pumice-block and whitewashed slate that pull hot air upward in silent, steady drafts, cooling the shaded cisterns and galleries below without a single turning fan.

Between the quarters run the arteries of an old, vast industry. Insulated copper busbars thick as a man's thigh travel along the flanks of the causeways on porcelain standoffs, carrying direct current out to acres of open-air refining pans. In those basins, thousands of submerged anode racks sit in shallow, gently circulating mineral liquor, precipitating rare salts and pale crystalline metals out of the geothermal water. Tiny pinprick bubbles rise continually to the surface of the vats—millions of them across an acre—yet they break so finely that the sound never rises above a dry whisper swallowed by the bright air.

Farther out, slow blackened-bronze wheels forty feet across dip their slatted rims into deep brine trenches, turning at a single revolution every few minutes, lifted by counterweight and thermal pressure rather than combustion. Great glass-and-slate drums sit in gravel yards, concentrating solar heat into sealed wax-jacketed pipes. Narrow rails run along the tops of the causeways, and every so often a flat, low-slung electric bogie glides past laden with white salt-cakes or wrapped copper ingots. Its wheels are shod in dense, vulcanized compound; its bearings run in heavy oil; it passes like a shadow sliding along a wall, leaving only a faint warm draft and the ghost of a prickle in the air from its collector shoe.

Over decades, the water has claimed the lower halves of the infrastructure. Where the hot brine laps continually against concrete piers, sluice gates, and old iron pipe-runs, it deposits smooth, glossy collars of pale sinter and verdigris-stained mineral crust. Older pump-houses and abandoned settling tanks have grown half-calcified, their sharp industrial angles rounded into bulbous white ledges that look extruded directly from the plateau rock. Even the active factories weep: warm vapor condenses along the shaded undersides of metal fins and overhanging ceramic eaves, gathering into thick drops that fall onto carefully placed beds of soft salt-silt and porous pumice grit—nothing here is allowed to drip onto tin or hollow stone.

Nothing percussive is permitted at all. Along the causeway parapets, painted ceramic plaques display simple black glyphs—a cracked circle, a hand held flat—reminding crews of the old discipline. Chains are wrapped in oiled felt. Tools have leather-capped heads. Workers who emerge from the shaded arcades to tend the anode racks wear wide-brimmed white hats, heavy reflective linen smocks, dark slit-goggles against the glare, and thick cork-soled boots that make no scrape on the travertine flagstones. When two crews need to coordinate across an evaporation yard, nobody shouts; a watcher on the gantry tilts a small brass mirror, sending sharp, rhythmic flashes of white light across the simmering pans, or raises a sequence of matte-black canvas flags that snap cleanly against the bright sky.

Part of this quiet is geology: the plateau sits on a thin, taut mineral shelf above a pressurized aquifer, and the oldest sections of the city still bear hairline fractures from an era when men used steam-hammers and blast-charges. The other part walks on two-meter legs through the shallow pans right beside the machinery.

For a place with no plants and almost no audible life, the plateau is astonishingly full of animals.

Because the hot springs and industrial discharges continually feed rich sheets of mineral slime and thermophilic biofilm across the flats, an entire food web has built itself from the bottom up on heat, brine, and stray electricity—and every member of it operates in near-total silence.

Look closely into any of the wide, mirror-flat settling basins and the smooth floor appears to be shifting. Hundreds of flat, plate-sized grazers—translucent, silica-shelled things shaped like broad lenses—creep across the submerged sinter, rasping away the orange bacterial mats and fresh crystalline scale. Their shells are grown from the same dissolved glass that coats the factory piers, so in the blinding light they are almost invisible until one tilts slightly and throws a sharp wedge of rainbow across the water. Beneath the discharge sluices, dense colonies of crimson brine-threads ripple in the laminar current, instantly snapping flat in a synchronized wave whenever the shadow of a gliding rail-bogie crosses them.

Stalking among the factory piers and out across the open pans are the tall waders—gaunt, startlingly elegant creatures that look at first glance like spindly white tripods left behind by surveyors. Their long, multi-jointed legs are sheathed in pale, heat-insulating horn that lets them stand indefinitely in scalding water without discomfort, holding slender, silver-plumaged torsos well above the steam-line. They move with agonizing slowness, lifting one needle-thin foot and setting it down without raising a ripple, then plunging long, serrated beaks straight down to impale the glass-shelled grazers beneath the surface. They never cry out. Instead, when two waders contest a rich runoff trench, they stand ten paces apart and flash the undersides of their throat-frills—sudden, geometric shutters of velvety matte black opening and closing against the white glare until the smaller bird folds its frill and steps stiffly aside.

High above the transformer yards and the shimmering roofs of the refining halls, where columns of superheated air rise steadily toward the bleached sky, pale ribbon-winged gliders wheel in vast, unhurried spirals. Their wings are thin as oiled paper and nearly transparent, built to ride thermals without a single wingbeat; dozens of them can hang over a single condenser tower like scraps of bright ash caught in an updraft, trailing fine silver filaments from their tails that snag tiny heat-midges rising from the lukewarm drainage ditches and—oddly—seem to collect static charge off the high-voltage busbars, sparking faintly when the sun dips low.

In the dark, damp shade under the causeways and along the weeping ceramic flanks of the boiler houses live the heavy wall-baskers: flat, armored creatures the length of a forearm, with porous slate-grey hides that drink moisture straight from the sweating brick and sweat it back out across ridged dorsal plates to keep their innards cool. They cling motionless to the masonry for hours, almost indistinguishable from mineral encrustations, waiting to ambush anything that climbs out of the brine onto dry stone—pale rock-crabs, wandering grazer-larvae, or the occasional ribbon-glider forced down by a sudden downdraft. When a rival intrudes onto a warm pipe-ledge, neither hisses nor snaps; their throats and ribbed flanks flush rapidly from dull ash to violent cinnabar and sulfur-yellow in silent, pulsing waves of warning until one loosens its grip and slides down into the dark cisterns below.

And if, by some rare accident, a heavy mineral crust fractures underground—or a careless apprentice drops a bronze spanner onto bare concrete—the whole strange truce of the plateau reveals itself in an instant. Across half a mile of glittering flats, every tall wader freezes mid-stride, its black throat-frill snapping shut. Every wall-basker flattens tight against the weeping tile. Even the ribbon-gliders bank sharply away from the disturbance, peeling off their thermals in a wide, panicked wheel. For twenty seconds the glare seems to sharpen, the heat-haze trembling over the still pans while city and fauna alike wait, rigid and listening, to feel whether the deep shelf beneath the water will answer back.

Nothing answers, and slowly the rhythm resumes.

The basin has its own pulse, slower than tides and steadier than weather. Through the peak of midday, the glare reaches a pitch where shadow shrinks to narrow slits under the eaves and the air above the copper busbars warps the distant mesas into trembling ribbons. Evaporation accelerates; the laminar sheets thin out across the wider terraces, leaving lacy white rims of fresh salt drying along the edges of the pans. The electrolytic yards pull peak power from the geothermal wells and solar drums. Workers retreat into the deep vaulted galleries beneath the pale brick blocks, where cool air drawn down through damp pumice shafts smells faintly of wet chalk and old copper. Out in the glare, the waders lock their joints and stand like bleached poles over the mirror-water, motionless as the concrete piers beside them.

Then, late in the afternoon, when the sun begins its long slide toward the eroded western rim and a thin, high-altitude drift cools the upper air by a few degrees, the barometric pressure eases and the plateau breathes out.

From deep beneath the fissured floor comes the slow swelling of the aquifer—the weeping tide. Without a murmur, the water table rises by the width of two fingers across the entire basin. Dry, salt-crusted terraces glaze over as thin, glassy tongues of fresh hot brine creep smoothly across the sinter. Along the industrial channels, counterweighted bronze sluice-gates feel the added pressure and tilt gently open on oiled pivots, redirecting the surge through secondary cooling pans and wide evaporation flats. Faint, ghostly veils of condensation form briefly along the shaded undersides of the causeways, shedding slow, thick drops onto the pumice beds below.

At once the basin quickens. Thousands of glass-shelled grazers slip out from the shaded crevices to feed on the freshly wetted ochre mats. The tall waders unfreeze and begin their silent, deliberate march across the newly flooded mirrors, black throat-frills winking like semaphore across the white water. Beneath the arcades, figures in reflective linen step back out into the softening glare, pushing long flat-bottomed punts with padded poles out among the anode racks to harvest the day's crust of pale crystal. The afternoon light turns from blinding white to a fierce, burnished brass, catching the wet stone, the spinning bronze wheels, the drifting gliders overhead, and the slow, mirror-bright spread of water across miles of flat mineral earth—a vast, scalding engine of stone, current, brine, and living bone, turning steadily in the bright and breathless quiet.
