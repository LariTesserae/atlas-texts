Near the crown of a knife-edged massif—high enough that clouds pass far below as flattened grey plates, and the air outside is thin, raw, and stripped of every trace of moisture—a vertical crease opens in the leeward face of the summit stone. It is scarcely wide enough to admit a shoulder. Step through it, turn once around a smooth rib of weathered gneiss, and the sky vanishes behind you.

The pocket inside is no larger than a narrow root cellar, perhaps twenty paces deep and half as wide, roofed by two leaning slabs of mountain rock that wedged together ages ago and stayed there. Yet the moment you cross the bend, the place hits you with its weight—first in the ears, then across the skin, then in the sudden, impossible crush of green.

It is loud in here. Not the erratic battering of high-altitude wind, which never reaches past the crooked entrance, nor any clatter of loose parts, but a thick, continuous wall of sound that fills the chamber like pressure. At the bottom of it sits a heavy, chest-rattling bass drone carried straight through the granite floor—the deep mechanical hum of iron cores under constant electrical load. Layered over that is a tight metallic singing from taut conductors and porcelain mounts, and over both rides a fierce, steady dry hiss, loud as pouring grain, rising from millions of stiff leaves trembling invisibly against one another in the standing waves. You would have to put your mouth to someone’s ear to speak, though the noise never surges or breaks. It holds a single flat chord without drift.

Outside on the ridge, the cold is lethal. Inside this tight stone sleeve, waste heat from buried machinery holds the trapped air at a steady, brisk coolness—cellar-cool, crisp in the lungs, sharp with the smell of ozone, old insulating varnish, fine slate dust, and the resinous tang of dry leaves. There is no dampness anywhere. Not a bead of condensation gathers on the iron; no rime coats the rock; no mildew softens the crevices. The air pulls moisture off your lips within minutes, thin and bone-dry.

Light barely reaches the floor. High along the wedged ceiling slabs, a hairline seam of mountain sky glimmers in bruised indigo, choked almost shut by roots and mineral crust. Most of what lets you see comes from within the pocket itself: four or five deeply recessed glass indicator domes glowing a dull, under-volted amber, paired with a pale violet corona that hugs the rims of cracked ceramic insulators whenever the dimness settles around them.

Through that gloom, the bones of the chamber show themselves only in fragments. The rock is ancient and settled, worn into smooth concavities and shallow flutes by dry scour long before the massif rose to its present height. Nothing here was quarried into neat walls or laid with masonry. Instead, scattered industrial assemblies have been bolted straight into the native curves of the stone: three ribbed cast-iron transformer housings wedged into wall-niches like dark organs; an arched frame of riveted steel straddling a narrow fissure in the floor; heavy conduits wrapped in brittle pitch-soaked cloth, pinned along the overhang with iron dogs before vanishing into drilled boreholes that sink straight down into the mountain’s core. A bronze inspection hatch sits flush in the rock beside a brass pressure dial whose needle rests motionless against chipped white enamel. None of the metal bears wet rust. Instead, the brass has gone a dry, matte chocolate, and the cast iron wears a thin dry coat of pale mineral flour.

And every remaining cubic inch of the pocket is packed with vegetation.

There is no path through it, no bare patch of stone larger than a palm, no breathing room between stems. Lush, layered, and utterly waterless, the flora fills the chamber from floor-fissure to ceiling-seam, crowding around the iron ribs and draping over the conduit runs in dense, tangled curtains. Pushing into it feels like wading through stacked parchment and fine wire mesh. Broad, overlapping fronds of tarnished olive and slate-green fan out from thick woody crowns wedged into the eroded stone grooves; their leaves are crisp and cool, veined with a dull silvery silica that catches the amber pilot-lights. Beneath them, tight cushions of papery rosettes pack every ledge and bolt-head so closely that their edges interlock like fish scales.

Nothing here drips sap if snapped. The stems hold no water—only dry, resinous cellular lattices and conductive mineral veins tuned to the chamber’s constant electrical bleed and bedrock vibration. Closer to the iron casings, the growth grows stranger. Fine, dark-green botanical filaments—thin as horsehair and dry as winter grass—sprout from the seams of the transformer housings and refuse to hang downward. Caught in the steady magnetic fields bridging the terminals, thousands of these hair-ferns arch sideways and upward in rigid, symmetrical loops, holding the invisible geometry of the current in midair like frozen harps. Small, translucent seed-pods cluster along their lengths, stiff and glossy as mica flakes, humming audibly when brushed.

Look closely at any frond, any cushion, any hair-thin tendril, and a quieter strangeness settles in: every single margin is unbroken. No leaf has been chewed, mined, curled by gall, or dusted with silk. Nothing darts away from your boots. No beetles click in the dry litter, no mites cross the warmish iron, no bones or droppings lie in the hollows. Without water or animal life, decay itself has lost its grip. Nothing rots into soil here; the plants grip bare fluted rock and cast iron alike, drawing what trace minerals they need from the fine grey dust vibrated loose from the walls over centuries.

That dust itself obeys the room. Along the flat upper flanges of the iron housings, stone flour has arranged itself into crisp, pale ridges and geometric rings—standing acoustic patterns locked in place by the unvarying pitch of the cavern. They do not blur or shift.

In fact, nothing shifts. Despite the deafening volume and the riotous press of greenery, the pocket rests in a complete, stubborn equilibrium. Whatever deep power-line or telluric circuit feeds up through the floor boreholes delivers the exact same load hour after hour, year after year, never peaking and never failing. The plants have long since filled every available span of space, claimed every stray micro-current and pocket of faint light, and settled onto a flat plateau where growth and decline cancel to zero. No fresh shoots unfurl; no old fronds brown or drop. Every leaf holds the exact angle carved out for it by the standing waves and magnetic arcs, vibrating steadily in place—cool, dry, crowded, and deafeningly alive, locked inside a stone cleft at the barren roof of the world while time passes somewhere far outside the seam.
