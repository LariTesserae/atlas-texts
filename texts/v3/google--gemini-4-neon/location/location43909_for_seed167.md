You know the place first by its warmth, and then by how close the walls are.

The water is blood-warm—not the violent boil of a volcanic crack, just a heavy, enveloping heat that holds steady against the skin like a bath left in a shuttered room. It tastes faintly of chalk, sulfur, and old shell. There is no surface within reach, and no sun. Darkness sits against the eyes with the weight of velvet, broken only when a passing fin or shoulder brushes too hard against a ledge and wakes a pinprick of cold, pale green in the disturbed silt—a spark barely wide enough to show a thumb's width of pitted lime before it dies.

Move a yard in any direction and your outstretched hand meets stone that is not quite stone. It is warm, slightly yielding at the surface where a thin skin of mucus and micro-life coats it, and firm beneath—packed marl, ground bivalve husk, and secreted calcium laid down in overlapping bands. Follow it sideways and it curves inward into a smooth, hip-wide archway. Follow it upward and it steps back into a shelf, then another overhang, then a clustered lip of hollow tubes. Step forward and the floor drops an inch into a shallow, polished trough where a gentle current slides along your shins, noticeably warmer than the water around your shoulders.

Only after moving through three or four of these turns does the scale of it begin to assemble itself in the dark. You are not inside a cave, nor a reef. You are in a street.

***

The basin lies low—a sunken, flat-bottomed trough folded deep beneath surrounding underwater ridges, miles below the reach of wave action and too far down for daylight to arrive as anything more than an unprovable rumor. Across its floor, a wide bed of fractured basalt leaks geothermally heated water through ten thousand hair-thin seams. Nowhere does the floor erupt; it simply sweats warmth. Because the rim of the basin rises all around it like the lip of a sunken bowl, that heated water does not scatter into the cold abyss. It pools. It thickens with dissolved minerals, rises slowly in broad, languid columns, cools near the upper dark, and curls back down along the basin walls to be warmed again.

Inside that trapped, slow-turning bath, over a span of time that has worn away every sharp edge, the basin has been built over from rim to rim.

It is the size of a broad valley town, roughly oval, denser at the center where the seepage is steadiest and spreading outward into lower, wider terraces as the heat thins toward the flanking rock. Nothing here was quarried, chiselled, knotted, or fired. There is not a tool-mark in the entire valley, not a scrap of worked metal or cut timber, not even a stone chipped deliberately against another stone. Every pier, vault, gallery, and flue was made by mouths, feet, glands, and patience—chewed from bottom-silt and dead shell, bound with tough protein glues, stacked in wet courses, and grown over by colonies of calcifying organisms that knit the joints shut.

Yet it has the unmistakable logic of a city.

Broad avenues run from the hot center toward the outer rim, kept clear of debris and curved gently to guide the thermal drift rather than fight it. Their floors are worn smooth as soapstone by generations of heavy bellies and armored feet. Where two avenues meet, the walls widen into circular plazas—shallow bowls ringed by tiered ledges, pitched so that the rising mineral snow swirls slowly in the center before lifting away. Off the avenues, narrower alleys branch and split, climbing by ramped galleries or dropping into sunken underpasses. Overhead, the upper stories lean close across the lanes, linked by sagging, braided bridges of leathery worm-tubes and calcified arches so crowded that a swimmer moving through the mid-water passes from shadow into deeper shadow, brushing past dangling curtains of pale siphon-sheaths and clustered brood-cells glued under every eave.

Everywhere, the water moves through planned spaces. The towers at the basin's core—some rising forty or fifty feet from the silt—are not solid mounds. They are honeycombed with vertical flues and side-vents, thick-walled chimneys that draw warm seepage up from the cracked bedrock and vent it out through rows of rounded, collar-like openings near their tops. Put a palm near one of those upper vents and you feel a steady, gentle breath, two degrees warmer than the surrounding dark. Inside the walls of those chimneys, insulated from the cooler cross-drafts, sit thousands of tiered chambers, each sized cleanly to a body, with smooth rounded thresholds and small drainage pores at their base.

Occasionally, under the highest domed vaults of the central blocks, a pocket of swamp-sweet volcanic gas gets trapped against the ceiling—a flat, shivering mirror of non-water, an inch or two deep, trembling whenever a large body passes below. They are the only dry spaces in the valley, and nothing breathes them; they hang there like pale, inverted puddles, collecting drifting oil-films and faint mineral crusts along their rims.

***

Because the city is old, it does not look newly made. It looks *settled*, as if its walls have come to an agreement with the water that dissolves them.

Warm, mineral-laden water is a slow thief and a slow mason at once. Along the main thoroughfares, where traffic and current constantly rub the masonry, the lower walls have been eaten into soft flutes and overhanging grooves, exposing older layers beneath—bands of darker silt-mortar, ancient compressed clam-plates, the fossilized cross-sections of abandoned brood-galleries that were sealed off centuries ago and built over. Higher up, where the water drifts more lazily, dissolved lime precipitates back out of solution, glazing the upper balustrades and bridges in a smooth, pale, cauliflower-like rind. Nothing stands sharp-cornered. Every doorway is beveled by passing hides; every parapet has melted into a thick, rounded lip. Where an old tower finally slumped under its own weight, no one cleared the rubble away—instead, new galleries were molded right over the slumped ribs, turning fallen spires into broad stepped ramps and cellar-warrens.

Plant life is scarce in this lightless warmth, and what little exists looks ghostly and provisional. No broad leaves, no tangled kelp. Along the warmest flue-lips and the slow, nutrient-heavy ledges of the central plazas, sparse tufts of pale, thread-thin filaments sway in the upward draft—chemosynthetic mats and colorless bacterial hairs that feed on the faint sulfur in the water. In the quieter outer quarters, where fine organic snow settles undisturbed on flat roofs, thin, leafless stalks of a leathery, fungus-like growth rise in scattered clutches, no taller than a wrist, swaying slowly like pale eyelashes before breaking off in the current to drift away. You can swim down an entire avenue and brush against only a handful of these thin patches; the walls are bare lime, living shell, and slick mineral film.

***

What the valley lacks in greenery, it makes up for in sheer, quiet crowd.

The darkness disguises it at first, because almost nothing makes a fuss. There are no breaking waves here, no wind, no barking crowds or snapping reefs. The basin is mostly quiet—a dense, pressurized hush filled only by the faintest low murmur of warm water rising through porous flues. Yet if you stay still in an intersection and hold your breath, you begin to register how packed the volume around you really is.

A soft, papery *shush* slides past your knee: a column of Sweepers moving in single file down the center of the avenue. They are broad, low-slung, plate-backed things, blind and pale as old tallow, riding on rows of feathery legs that disturb almost no silt. They graze the thin bacterial film off the street-floors and herd loose grit toward the low gutters at the bases of the walls, keeping the avenues open and slick.

Higher up, just beneath a sagging arch, a dry, muted *tick—tick* sounds once, twice, then stops. One of the Masons is at work. Large, slow, and thick-bodied, with velvet-smooth, pigmentless skin and six stubby limbs tipped in flattened calcified pads, a Mason clings to the crumbling rim of a vent-collar. It has no eyes—only long, sensitive barbels fanning the water around its broad head to read temperature, current, and the exact shape of the gap before it. From a pouch behind its jaw it works a ball of chewed shell-grit and thick glandular paste, pressing it into the weathered notch with slow, rhythmic pats, smoothing the seam with the underside of its chin until the draft stops leaking sideways. A second Mason waits patiently half a body-length down the wall, holding a flat, palm-sized clam valve wedged under its forelimbs, ready to press it into the wet mortar as a keystone.

Every niche around them holds someone else. Inside the recessed balconies of the tenements, pale colonial filter-feeders—neither quite anemone nor worm—unfurl delicate, translucent crowns into the rising warmth, withdrawing in a silent ripple whenever a larger body drifts past. Tiny, glassy crustaceans swarm like quiet dust-motes around the vent-lips, grazing on flakes of shed skin and drifting mucus. Through the narrow cross-alleys, long, ribbon-flat eels with rows of faint sensory pores along their flanks glide without a ripple, slipping in and out of vertical shafts like dark threads drawn through lace.

It is an astonishingly dense neighborhood, layered cheek-by-jowl from the warm bedrock flues to the hanging bridges forty feet above, yet almost nothing collides. Everything reads the water—the tiny bow-wave ahead of a passing flank, the chemical trace of a neighbor's burrow, the slight cooling that warns of an open plaza ahead.

***

The valley is not static; it breathes on a slow, deep rhythm.

Every several hours—perhaps driven by the distant weight of surface tides pressing down on the buried rock-strata, perhaps by the slow cycling of deep geothermal pockets beneath the basalt—the warmth swells. The seepage through the floor intensifies. You feel it first as a subtle rise in pressure against the eardrums, then as a distinct, silky draft lifting up through the central chimneys. The quiet murmur inside the hollow walls deepens by a fraction of a tone.

This is the valley's rush hour.

As the warm plumes strengthen, they lift clouds of fine, nutritious detritus off the lower terraces and draw them upward through the streets and plazas. Across entire districts, thousands of small, hinged shell-doors click softly open—a sound like faint rain on distant leather, lasting a few seconds before fading back into quiet. Pale feathery fans bloom from every tier of the chimney-blocks, turning the bare lime towers fuzzy with feeding appendages. Sweepers double their pace along the avenues, trailing faint wisps of disturbed grit that trigger brief, pinprick flashes of cold green light in the dark.

With the grazers and filter-feeders out, the hunters begin their circuits.

They come down from the high bridges and the cooler outer eaves—lean, pale, rubbery predators with broad sensory snouts and silent, undulating tails. They do not chase through the open plazas; instead they hang motionless just outside the draft of a busy flue, letting the warm current mask their scent, waiting for a careless swimmer to round a blind corner. When a strike happens, it is over in half a heartbeat: a dull, muffled *thump* against a wall, a brief swirl of colder water, a scatter of alarmed blue-green sparks that blink out almost before the eye can catch them. Within minutes, smaller scavengers slip from nearby cracks to strip what the hunter drops. By the end of the surge, nothing remains on the avenue floor except a clean plate of bone or shell—and before the next cycle turns, a Mason will drag it away to wedge into the retaining walls along the outer terraces, where generations of sorted shells form pale, sloping embankments that keep the valley's silt from sliding inward.

Then, slowly, the pressure eases. The floor-seepage slackens. Cooler, denser water from the high rim of the basin begins to spill gently down over the rooftops, sinking through the plazas like invisible fog.

One by one, the upper balconies empty. Filter-fans fold back into leathery tubes. Heavy opercula pull shut over burrow-mouths with hushed, muffled clicks. The Masons retreat from the exposed outer parapets down into the deep, heat-retaining galleries at the base of the chimneys, where the thick walls stay warm long after the draft dies. The traffic drops from the upper bridges to the sheltered lower arcades. For a long stretch, the upper city hangs almost deserted in the cool, dark drift—only the slow creep of armored scavengers on the roofs, the faint shiver of a trapped gas-mirror under a vault, and the soft, patient hiss of the warm core waiting to rise again.

***

Move outward from the dense chimneys and the town changes character, quarter by quarter, following the drop in temperature.

Past the tall, tightly packed chimney-blocks of the core lies a broad middle ring of low, interconnected warrens—broad-roofed, flat-topped structures pierced with wide horizontal tunnels rather than vertical flues, built to trap the slow sideways spill of warm water drifting off the center. Here the streets are wider, the ceilings lower, and the architecture feels older and heavier, fused into massive, bulbous blocks separated by canyon-like lanes. Colonies of tube-weavers stretch dense, hammock-like meshes across the intersections here, catching drifting organic flakes like fishing nets without fishermen; every few yards, you have to duck beneath a sagging, living canopy that yields softly if brushed.

Beyond the warrens lie the outer terraces—stepped, crescent-shaped ledges curving up toward the dark basalt rim of the basin. The water here is noticeably cooler, merely tepid rather than blood-warm, and the buildings grow sparse and squat: low dome-mounds, thick windbreak walls angled against the slow downward slide of cold rim-water, and broad, shallow sorting-yards where dead shells and calcified fragments accumulate in long, neat ridges packed tight by current and busy forelimbs. Sparse, colorless stalk-tufts quiver along the terrace lips. Out here, you can finally swim straight up without hitting a bridge or an overhang, rising into a wide, empty ceiling of cold black water—and if you look down from just above the rim, there is nothing at all to see. No glow, no skyline, no lamps in windows. Only darkness over darkness, and beneath it, a faint, steady column of warmth rising like the breath of a sleeping animal.

Down inside, the work goes on without sight, without speech, without a single tool ever lifted. Walls slowly dissolve and are patched in the same breath; avenues stay clear because too many bodies need them clear; towers rise because warm water rises, and living things follow warmth upward, stacking chamber on chamber until a valley becomes a city. Everything fits because anything that did not fit was worn down, sealed off, or built over long before memory could have started. In the heavy, lukewarm quiet, another grain of shell-paste is pressed smoothly into a rounded arch, a vent breathes softly into the dark, and ten thousand blind neighbors slip past one another without touching.
