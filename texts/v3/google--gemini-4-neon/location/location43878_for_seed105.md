The first thing you notice is that the water has weight in the wrong direction. It does not press down with the dull, dead stillness of a sea floor; it moves like mountain air—thin, bitter, fast along the ridges, pooling colder in the hollows. You are three thousand feet above the lowlands, maybe more, yet there is no sky anywhere. Every pass, every scree slope, every saddle of weathered dark granite sits thirty fathoms under a cold, black freshwater sea held in by a ring of higher peaks and capped, far overhead, by a shifting ceiling of iron-grey ice and storm-beaten slush.

Almost no daylight reaches the rock. Around midday, a thin pewter bruise opens somewhere above the central valley where the ice-pack stretches and thins, turning the water from total ink to a deep, smoked slate. Shapes separate from distance only slowly: the long, clean spine of a ridge curving away for miles; the drop of a drowned cliff falling into a darker trench; the faint, hunched silhouettes of stone mounds perched on the high shoulders. Everywhere else, the light is little more than a memory of grey, except down in the sheltered folds of the rock where the flora gives off a weak, tallow-colored sourness—not quite a glow, more a pale, chalky sheen that lets you see the edges of leaves an arm’s length away and nothing beyond.

It is brutally loud.

Without air, sound does not arrive from a single direction; it enters through the breastbone and the teeth. High above, the ice-roof is always working—settling, grinding against the confining mountain walls, splitting with slow, tearing gunshots that roll downward and ricochet off the submerged cliffs until the valley hums like a struck anvil. Beneath that runs a steadier roar: the sound of gravel moving. Supercooled meltwater pours through the upper notches of the surrounding range, heavier than the surrounding lake, and tumbles down the steep valley sides in invisible rivers. Where those density-currents hit the talus fans, cobbles knock and roll in a continuous, dry-sounding clatter, like an avalanche happening in slow motion just beyond sight.

Nothing swims here. Nothing crawls, burrows, or grazes. If you wait an hour beside a crevice, or drift a mile across the open basin, you will never see a flick of fin, a scuttle of shell, a worm threading silt, or a single tiny fleck of drifting larva. The cold water is sterile of animal life—clear as cut glass where the silt settles, smelling sharply, if you could smell it, of wet slate, crushed pine resin, and cold iron.

Yet the valley is far from barren.

Across the wide stretches of bare, ice-scoured stone and empty water, the land holds itself sparse and austere, full of clean distances. Then you come over a brow of rock into a wind-shadow—a current-shadow—or along a seam where mineral-heavy water bleeds out of the fractured bedrock, and the place turns suddenly, extravagantly lush.

Thick, heavy groves of dark highland flora crowd the terraced ledges and choke the old stream-beds. They do not look like ordinary kelp or pondweed. Built for bitter cold, near-darkness, and the violence of the underwater gales, the plants here are muscular, leathery, and strange. Great stands of *siphon-pines* rise twenty feet from clutches of gnarled, stone-gripping roots: their trunks are pale, segmented cartilage, hollow and ribbed, topped with dense crowns of bruised-violet fronds that feel like oiled hide. Around their bases, waist-deep cushions of black velvet-moss swallow boulders whole, oozing a thin, cold mucus that keeps the gravel from abrading them. Along the steeper cliffs hang broad, translucent curtains of ribbon-leaf—some the color of old bone, others a deep, wet spruce-green—anchored by fibrous cables thick as a wrist.

Because there are no animals to carry pollen, chew back overgrowth, or stir the muck, the plants have taken over the work of violence and motion themselves.

The loudest sound on the valley floor, after the grinding rock and the cracking ice-roof, comes from the *drum-pods*. They grow in dense, swollen clusters along the seams of the ridges—black, matte bladders the size of a human torso, tethered to the bedrock by coiled, elastic stems. For days they sip dissolved gases from the mineral seeps and from the slow, cold fermentation of peat trapped under the moss-mats. As they fill, their skins stretch tight and gleaming, lifting straight upright into the water column, straining at their cords until the fibers whine. When a pod reaches pressure—or when a sudden surge of colder current slams over the ridge and jars it—it splits along a fibrous seam with a sharp, concussive *crack* that jolts your ribs fifty yards away. A dense plume of pale, waxy spores jets out in a spinning ring, riding the current across the empty miles of open water toward the next bare slope, while the spent bladder collapses with a wet shudder and hangs like a torn coat until its own roots digest it back down.

Entire slopes go off in ragged volleys when the currents shift. Between the reports, thickets of calcified reed-stalks—stiff, hollow tubes plated in white mineral crust drawn straight from the water—sway and clack together in dry, frantic choruses, sounding like thousands of bone rods tumbled in a barrel. Broad frond-sails catch the fast flow through the mountain passes and vibrate at a low, shuddering pitch that turns the water between two cliffs into a resonant throat.

The valley is vast enough that you can stand on a high terrace and look out across a mile of dark, open water to the opposite massif, watching the pale curtains of silt-fog pour down its chutes and spread across the basin floor. It is an old place. The cliffs have long since shed their sharpest edges; the scree fans sit in settled, graceful curves; the root-mats have grown into the cracks of the granite so deeply that plant and mountain have begun to share a single architecture. Everything here has been working out its balance for centuries.

And scattered thinly through it—spaced far apart, almost lost in the scale of the ridges—are the marks of hands.

They are never clustered into towns, never grand. A line of dry-stone cairns, each boulder wedged with flat slate shims and lashed against the current with blackened, braided plant-cord, marches along the high spine of the western ridge. The cords are stiff with mineral rime, preserved by the cold and the absence of rot-worms, though here and there a creeping moss has pried a stone loose and let it tumble into the dark. Down in the lee of an overhang, tucked out of the path of the stone-rolling currents, sits a low beehive shelter built of unmortared schist slabs, its heavy roof weighted with notched river-cobbles. Inside, the floor is clean swept silt. Against the curved wall rests a row of grooved clay sinkers, three carved stone chisels, and a tangle of knotted fiber net whose floats—cured drum-pod skins stopped with wooden plugs—still strain weakly toward the ceiling, half-buoyant after ages underwater.

Even stranger are the frames planted in the saddles between peaks. Wherever a cold cascade spills from one high basin into the next, someone long ago wedged tripods of dark, water-hardened timber into the bedrock, braced with piled stones and bound with sinew-like vine and crude bands of hammered copper gone crusted and black-green. Suspended inside each tripod is a heavy slab of bored slate and a cluster of hollowed wooden clappers. They are not broken. As the water surges through the pass, the frames groan, the slates swing, and the clappers strike them in slow, irregular, ringing blows—*tonk, tonk-tonk, tonk*—carrying miles through the dark.

You cannot tell whether the builders lived here before the peaks sealed and drowned, or whether they came down from somewhere above the ice in weighted suits of oiled hide, or whether they built these things to read the valley’s currents from afar by listening to the pitch of the slates through the ice. No bones lie in the huts. No carvings show a face. Only tally-notches cut neatly into lintel stones, and long, anchored guide-lines of braided frond-fiber strung across the widest, emptiest stretches of open basin, still humming tautly in the dark flow so a traveler who could not see three paces ahead could haul themselves hand-over-hand from one ridge to the next.

Twice every cycle—driven by unseen storms hammering the world above the ice, or by some slow alpine thaw feeding the upper chutes—the valley wakes fully.

You feel it first as a drop in temperature sharp enough to ache in the joints, accompanied by a deepening of the low rumble from the eastern pass. Then the surge arrives: a thick, heavy tongue of pale, glacial water cresting the high ridge and spilling down the valley walls like a silent wall of cloud. As it hits the lush pockets, the siphon-pines bend nearly flat, their purple crowns streaming horizontally like banner-flags. The ribbon-kelp snaps and cracks like wet canvas. The stone-frames in the passes hammer furiously, their ringing clatter doubling and tripling into a frantic, metallic rhythm.

Triggered by the sudden drop in pressure and the violent tug of the water, the drum-pods begin to go. First a few scattered snaps along the high ledges, then a rolling cascade down both sides of the valley—dozens, then hundreds of ruptures booming through the gloom, filling the rushing water with pale, drifting ribbons of spores that catch the faint tallow-sheen of the moss below. Loose mats of torn velvet-weed peel off the upper rocks and tumble outward in slow, turning clouds, sailing over the empty dark between the ridges until they snag against a cairn or settle into a fresh crease of bare granite, already extending pale, searching root-hairs into the grit before the surge has even spent itself.

For an hour, the whole drowned basin is a churning engine of cold water, rolling gravel, booming ice, snapping fronds, and ringing stone—fiercely alive, furiously loud, moving with the purpose of a crowded world, yet without a single heartbeat anywhere inside it.

Then the heavy water finishes draining into the lower trenches at the valley’s far mouth. The silt settles back onto the roofs of the empty huts. The bent trees slowly haul themselves upright, creaking like ship timbers. The clappers in the passes slow to a tired, occasional knock. In the bruised half-darkness, the torn bladders hang slack, the fresh spores begin to root along the cold slate, and far overhead the ice-ceiling groans once more as it seals the dark back in.
