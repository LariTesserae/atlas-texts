At first, there is only the warmth, the thinness of the air, and the dark.

You are high up—high enough that every breath takes a slight, deliberate extra pull, and high enough that sound gives up quickly, thinning out into nothing a few dozen paces from its source. Yet the chill that ought to belong to this altitude is missing. The air against your face is steady and dry-warm, like the heat radiating off a paved yard hours after sundown, held down by a sky that gives back no light at all. Above you lies an unbroken, lightless ceiling of high cloud and dark particulate, vast and featureless. There are no stars, no moon, no horizon-glow to speak of—only, now and then, a faint dull bruising of slate-gray miles away where the overcast thins just enough to betray how enormous the space is.

Nothing stands between you and that distance. There are no towers, no walls, no roads, no roofs, no skylines. Wherever you walk across the plateau, your boots meet only ancient, weathered stone—dark basalt and ironstone planed smooth by wind and time, stepped in broad, shallow terraces that drop gradually toward unseen escarpments leagues away. The rock is rounded at every edge. The talus slopes are deeply settled, their scree long since locked into place by fine black grit. Whatever shaped this highland finished its rough work ages ago; the land has the quiet, finished posture of something that has existed in this exact state for tens of thousands of years.

It takes a few minutes of standing still in the quiet before you realize the stillness is a trick of your ears.

The soundscape is hushed—just the soft slide of warm air across flat stone, the occasional dry rustle of grit—yet through the soles of your boots, a rhythm is climbing into your ankles. It is too low to properly hear, more a pressure in the jaw and the sternum: a deep, heavy, continuous hum, threaded through with a slow, utterly regular cadence. A distant mass turning. A stroke completing itself, pausing a fraction of a second, and reversing. Somewhere hundreds of feet beneath the weathered basalt, engines of continental scale are running.

You cannot see a single rivet or hatch to prove it. Nothing was built *on* this plateau. There are no factory floors, no smokestacks, no scaffolding, no artifacts scattered across the flats. If you walk for twenty miles, you might cross a straight, arrow-true seam where the stone is slightly warmer than the rock beside it, refusing to follow the natural grain of the bedrock; once in fifty miles, you might scuff your heel against a broad, sunken disc of vitrified ceramic worn completely flush with the surrounding basalt and half-buried in dark dust. That is all. Whoever bored and wired the roots of this massif left the surface untouched, because the plateau itself—all several thousand miles of its open, high, weathered back—serves as the heat-sink, the condenser, and the open-air bed for what runs beneath it.

Once you feel the hum, other details begin to align themselves in the dark.

Kneel and pass a hand lightly over the fine iron-rich sand pooled in a wind-hollow, and you will find it does not lie in wind-ripples. The grit has arranged itself into tens of thousands of crisp, parallel micro-ridges, marching in exact alignment toward a point beyond the dark horizon, held there by immense direct current moving along conductive ore-veins buried in the strata below. Bring a piece of steel close to a damp crack in the stone, and it gives a faint, unmistakable tug. The air carries a clean, sharp trace of ozone layered over the older smells of warm rock, damp iron, and faint mineral steam.

Water is scarce here, never forming rivers or deep lakes, yet the plateau is never wholly parched. Across the vast flats, widely spaced and unannounced by any built chimney, deep natural blowholes and weathered rock fissures serve as the pressure-relief mouths of the buried works. Every few minutes, far out in the dark, one of them sighs—a muted, bass-heavy exhalation that barely carries across the thin air—sending a slow, invisible column of warm, humid exhaust straight up into the colder dark aloft.

On a plain this large, those plumes are enough to make weather of their own. Over hours, vast, sluggish convection cells build above the warmer sectors of the plateau, lifting moisture until it chills against the upper dark and drifts sideways across the tablelands. Downwind, the air grows heavy and close; warm mist settles over the terraces, beading slickly on the undersides of flat rocks, and now and then a brief, warm drizzle sweeps across a basin before evaporating back into the heat.

Where that intermittent moisture collects in weathered stone pans and shallow, braided trickle-beds, the plateau reveals its strangest quiet alchemy. The shallow pools—rarely more than ankle-deep, dark as oil—sit directly across the stray voltage leaking upward from the electrified bedrock. Lean close to one of these pans in the silence and you can hear it: a delicate, continuous *fizz*, like cold soda in a glass. Tiny pinpricks of gas rise steadily across the water's skin. Dissolved copper, zinc, and pale mineral salts carried down from the grit are silently plated out of the runoff; lift a wet pebble from the bottom of a basin and its underside is coated in a thin, dull metallic crust, refined in place by nothing more than rain, rock, and current.

Between these damp hollows stretch wide, spare expanses of open stone and magnetized dust. Life does not crowd the ground here—the spaces between presences are wide and clean—yet it is never truly barren. With almost no light, nothing wastes its energy on green leaves. Instead, scattered sparingly across the dark terraces and clustered more thickly along the damp seams, grows a low, tough, dark-bronze scrub whose wiry roots drive straight into the warm, conductive fissures. Rather than drinking sun, these plants feed on the chemical and electrical gradients of the wet mineralized stone, combing trace moisture from passing mist with fine, stiff bristles.

Beside the seepage basins, broad, matte-gray mats—rubbery and leathery to the touch, somewhere between lichen and tough fungus—spread in slow rings across the damp basalt, absorbing waste heat and trace metals. In the shallowest electrolytic pans stand sparse clusters of hollow, reed-like stalks, pale and slightly translucent if you ever catch them by spark-light. When a warm weather front builds overhead and the ambient charge climbs, faint beads of pale blue corona fire—cold, pinhead-sized, completely silent—appear along the tips of those stalks and along the sharp crests of distant ridges, outlining the shape of the terrain for a few minutes before guttering back into darkness.

It is easy, during your first hour on the plateau, to think the place is almost empty of animals. The terrain is too vast, the dark too deep, the silence too thorough. You have to stop walking, let your breath settle, and stop trusting your eyes.

Then the darkness begins to move.

Just ten yards away, what looked like low, weathered boulders along the rim of a damp pan shift smoothly forward on wide, padded feet. They are broad, low-slung, heavy-bodied grazers, completely blind, with thick insulating hides dusted gray with iron grit and broad, whisker-rimmed muzzles that sweep slowly across the wet rock. You hear only the soft, rhythmic *scritch* of their hard, mineral-enameled teeth scraping the rubbery lichen mats and copper-crusted rims clean. There are dozens of them, spread loosely across half a mile of dark flat, keeping wide intervals between one another, orienting themselves effortlessly along the ground's buried current lines. When they lie down to rest between feeding bouts, every animal in the herd aligns its spine along the exact same compass bearing.

Down inside the fizzing pools and beneath every flat, damp stone, the silence holds a denser crowding. Thousands of small, slick, matte-black crawlers—flat-shelled and multi-legged—pick steadily through the wet metallic silt, grazing on chemosynthetic film and the soft precipitates along the water's edge. Every now and then a dry, faint clicking rises from a hollow as two of them jostle over a warm seep, their mineral-heavy carapaces knocking softly like pebbles in a pocket. Deeper in the warm cracks of the rock, unseen burrowers hum and shift, raising broods in the reliable kiln-heat rising from the engines below.

Above you, the darkness is populated too, though you only catch it when a passing wing blocks a stray blue spark on a ridge, or when a faint, dry whiff of warm leather brushes the air overhead. Thin-winged, dark-membraned gliders—wide as a man's outstretched arms, built almost entirely of stretched skin and hollow bone—hang effortlessly in the invisible thermal columns rising from the exhaust vents. They almost never flap. They circle for hours in vast, silent gyres high above the plateau, reading the faint electric and thermal distortions left by the herds below, peeling away into steep, soundless drops whenever a small crawler or a sick calf strays too far from cover.

And down on the flat stone, low to the grit, move the stalkers. They are lean, pale-gray, nearly eyeless predators with broad, ridged crests along their snouts that sense both body heat and the subtle warp a living creature bends into the plateau's ambient magnetic field. They can hold motionless beside a damp trickle-bed for hours, indistinguishable from a fallen slab of weathered basalt.

They know the schedule of the ground better than anything else alive.

Because the plateau is not merely running; it cycles. Roughly every three quarters of an hour, a vast load-shift rolls invisibly across this sector of the continent. You feel it first as a subtle drop in the deep hum under your heels—half a step lower, heavier, slower—followed by a faint, brief shudder in the bedrock as massive valves miles down redirect pressure from one subterranean trunk to another. For three seconds, the direct current flowing through the ore-seams reverses polarity.

In the dark, the change sweeps across the plain like a silent tide.

Along the far escarpment, a line of faint blue static ripples along the ridge-crests and dies. Every grain of magnetized sand gives a microscopic, dry *hiss* as it flips end-for-end. The fizzing in the shallow basins hesitates, then resumes at a slightly different pitch. Across the wide flats, every blind grazer freezes mid-step, lifts its broad muzzle to read the altered field, and turns its shoulders gently toward the new alignment.

And in that exact three-second shudder—when the bedrock vibrates just strongly enough to blur footsteps and the electrical field briefly scrambles every creature's senses—a lean shape breaks away from the edge of a damp pan. There is no roar, no outcry that carries far; only a brief, muffled flurry in the wet grit, the quick thump of hooves scattering outward into the dark, and then, within half a minute, the thin air swallows the disturbance whole. Scavengers are already sliding out from beneath the flat stones before the dust settles.

Miles downwind, where the shifted pressure has begun venting through a new cluster of rock blowholes, the warm, humid plumes climb higher into the dark ceiling. Distantly, a slow squall-line begins to gather itself—no loud thunder, only muffled, deep-chested rumbles muffled by the thin air and the occasional silent fork of blue discharge flickering high inside the cloud deck, briefly revealing an endless expanse of terraced stone, sparse dark scrub, scattered herds, and empty space curving away toward the edge of the world.

Rain will fall there in an hour, warm and slightly metallic, refilling the shallow pans, waking the crusts, feeding the scrub, cooling the stone by a fraction of a degree. Deep beneath the weathered rock, the engines hold their new cadence without effort or haste, and the wild, unbuilt surface settles smoothly back into its work in the dark.
