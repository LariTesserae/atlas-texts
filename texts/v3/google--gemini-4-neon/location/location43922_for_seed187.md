The hollow is no larger than a small village green—perhaps two hundred paces from one dark edge to the other—and at first glance its most striking quality is how thoroughly empty the air inside it is.

It sits low, sunken slightly below the surrounding flatlands, shaped like a shallow saucer with walls that rise abruptly on every side. Those walls are not stone or earth. They are solid, ancient vegetation: centuries-old hemlock, bog cedar, alder, and twisted yew grown so close and tangled with ivy, briar, and deadwood that they form a continuous, vertical curtain sixty feet high. Inside that living enclosure, the space opens out cleanly to ordinary, even daylight—a pale northern sky of thin cloud and washed blue, unobstructed from rim to rim. Nothing stands in the middle to break the view. A single gnarled dwarf birch, no taller than a man’s waist and plated in grey-green lichen, leans off to the northern quarter; fifty yards from it sits a low hump of fused roots wrapped in moss. Beyond those two minor presences, the entire interior volume is just cool, clear, hollow air.

You feel the coldness of the place first in your ankles, because cool air settles into this low pocket and stays there. It never freezes hard and never warms enough to feel dry. The air stays damp—not raining, not foggy, just heavy with cool moisture that beads patiently along the tips of every blade and needle.

Underfoot, there is no mineral ground at all. Whatever bedrock once lay beneath this plain was planed flat and dissolved into clay eons ago; not a cliff, ridge, or raw pebble remains anywhere in the region. Over millennia, plant matter swallowed the flat clay completely and became the terrain itself. When you step out onto the floor of the hollow, you walk on an unbroken, springy mattress of living vegetation—tightly braided sphagnum, coarse haircap moss, wiry mats of bog cranberry, low sedge, and beneath them, yards of compressed, fibrous peat still laced through with living rhizomes and ancient, water-preserved roots. Nowhere can you scrape down to bare dirt or rock. Even the small depressions where dark, tea-colored rainwater collects are lined completely with slick green moss and pale submerged rootlets. There is no stream, no pond, no open water—only these thumb-deep pockets of cold water held in the creases of the living floor, and the steady sheen of condensation across everything.

And yet, for a place with not a single wall, foundation, road, or shelter inside it, the hollow is shockingly loud.

The sound hits you the moment you clear the outer rim, filling the open bowl like water filling a basin. It is not wind—down in this low pocket, the air barely stirs. It is a massive, relentless, layered mechanical roar pressing straight through the thick green walls from every direction at once. Behind the impenetrable cedar and alder lies an unbroken expanse of heavy baseload industry: power generation, substations, cooling plants, continuous-process mills running on endless three-shift schedules. You cannot see a single brick, corrugated wall, or smokestack from the mossy floor. The ancient tree-wall seals them out completely, leaving only the occasional white plume of clean cooling vapor drifting slowly above the eastern canopy, pale and unremarkable against the ordinary clouds.

Still, the engines own the air. A deep, unvarying sixty-cycle transformer hum sits at the bottom of the register, so physical that you feel it through the soles of your boots—a fine, tireless tremor traveling straight through the water-clenched root-mat. If you kneel beside one of the dark pockets of rainwater tucked between the sedge tussocks, you can see it: microscopic, razor-sharp concentric ripples trembling continuously across the black surface, never growing and never smoothing out. Layered above that bass is the steady rush of immense cooling fans, the distant high whine of turbines holding a single pitch, and the muffled, rhythmic thump of heavy presses somewhere far behind the western trees.

Look straight up into the empty daylight, and the one visible trace of the industrial world cuts directly across the sky.

High above the hollow—strung between lattice pylons hidden well beyond the north and south tree-walls—run three thick corridors of high-voltage transmission lines. Bare steel-cored aluminum cables, bundled in pairs and spacers, sag gently across the open air in long silver-grey arcs. In this cool, damp air, the lines are never quiet. Condensation clings to the metal and triggers a furious, continuous corona discharge—a harsh, spitting, frying sizzle that crackles loudly down into the empty bowl, sharpest whenever a drift of damper air moves across the spans. Along with the noise comes a faint, clean, metallic tang of ozone and warm insulating oil, hovering subtly over the heavy green scent of wet moss, crushed sweet-gale, and cold peat.

Aside from those overhead wires—and perhaps a single forgotten cast-iron boundary pin half-swallowed by sphagnum near the southern edge—nothing built exists here. The reason is practical, not sentimental. The ancient, water-holding mat of roots and peat beneath the hollow is too deep, too soft, and too stubbornly unstable to carry concrete footings or heavy rail beds. When industry industrialized the flat plain around it, engineers simply paved and erected around the unbuildable pocket, leaving the natural tree-ring intact as a buffer and slinging the main trunk cables straight across its empty center.

Because no person ever has reason to step inside, the hollow belongs entirely to animals—and the wildlife here is neither sparse nor quiet.

Hundreds of birds treat the overhead cables as a permanent highway and roost. Starlings, crows, and jackdaws pack along the humming metal lines in long, shifting rows, ignoring the crackle beneath their claws, dropping in sudden noisy clouds toward the mossy floor or exploding back into the cedar walls. Their raucous chattering, scolding, and dry wing-clatter push straight over the turbine roar. Swifts and barn swallows scythe back and forth through the vast empty volume of the bowl, taking advantage of the unobstructed air to hunt the dense clouds of midges, craneflies, and gnats rising off the damp moss.

Closer to the floor, the sound is just as dense. Every wet crease in the ancient root-mat hides amphibians—chorus frogs, marsh toads, and peepers whose sharp, metallic trills cut cleanly through the lower industrial frequencies. They have been here for generations, their calls pitched high and steady, turning the mossy floor itself into a vibrating chorus. Beneath the surface mat, unseen, an entire labyrinth of dry-topped tunnels threads between the braided roots and peat fibers: voles, shrews, and water rails dart endlessly through the dark passages, occasionally rustling a tuft of sedge or slipping across a mossy gap before vanishing again. Wrens and song sparrows work the dense tangle of the living perimeter walls, shouting territorially from the ivy; now and then a fox or a mink follows the sheltered rim where the trees meet the moss, unbothered by the mechanical thunder beyond the leaves.

What holds the entire place together—what makes it feel strangely settled despite the sheer volume of noise—is that almost nothing here ever changes.

The geology finished its work ages ago; there is nothing left to erode, crumble, or silt over. The ecology long ago reached a locked equilibrium: the central mat is too acidic, cool, and tightly knitted with peat-roots for the surrounding trees to march inward, while the perimeter forest is too old and massively anchored to give way. The industrial complex beyond the leaves runs on steady baseload power, neither shutting down at night nor surging into sudden chaos; its pitch barely shifts from January to October. Even the weather holds a narrow, stubborn band—cool, damp, modestly lit, never parching the moss into dust and never drowning the hollow into a pond.

Standing in the center of that small, empty room of air, the impression is not of ruin or transition, nor of nature fighting back against machines. It is simply a finished state: an ancient, flattened bed of living green, roaring steadily with electricity, engines, frogs, and wings, holding its exact temperature, its exact ripples, and its exact note without pause.
