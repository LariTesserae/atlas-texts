You feel this place in your teeth before you see a single meter of it.

It is drowned—not beneath an ordinary sea, with an open blue ceiling and the slow heave of tide, but sealed inside a low, sunken valley of warm, heavy water that presses in from every direction at once. The water sits near body heat, thicker than it ought to be with dissolved iron, silt, and fine suspended fiber, and in the currents drifting off the machinery it climbs to the feverish warmth of an unvented boiler room. There is no quiet corner anywhere in the basin. Underwater, sound does not travel through air to the ear; it travels straight through bone, lung, and skull, and here the volume is punishing—a layered, continuous roar that leaves no seam of silence between its parts. Low-frequency turbine thrum sits at the bottom of the register, vibrating the ribcage like a stalled freight locomotive; over that rides the tearing, frying-fat hiss of cavitation from impellers spinning far past their tolerance; over that, the whip-snaps of torn kelp, the screech of young stone shifting on fresh faults, and every few seconds a massive, distant *THOOM* that hits your chest twice—once dull, once razor-sharp, and sometimes in the wrong order.

Light barely reaches down here. Whatever sky exists above the basin is buried under too much agitated water and suspended green mulch to send down more than a bruised, murky olive twilight, dim as the underside of a storm cloud at dusk. Visibility shrinks and balloons with every violent eddy. Most of the time you can only make out three or four body-lengths of crowded, shifting shadow; then a current shears past, clearing a sudden corridor down the valley, and for two heartbeats you catch sight of the basin's true scale—kilometers of sunken, jagged lowland stretching between unseen walls, punctuated far out in the gloom by dull orange pinpricks of submerged cage-lamps and the momentary, stuttering violet glare of electrical arcs jumping through salt-rich water.

The floor of the valley is newly made and brutally sharp. Nothing down here has had the patience of centuries to round off. The terrain consists of raw, fractured basalt shelves, stepped obsidian-black fault scarps, and upturned plates of slate-gray stone that jut out of the sediment like freshly snapped ribs. Edges are keen enough to part heavy rubber or skin on a careless drift. There are no cobbles, no smooth gravel bars—only angular scree that has barely tumbled from the cliff-faces above, already being wedged apart again by seismic twitches and the relentless pry of roots. Even the valley's topography refuses to stay settled: whole ridges groan and lift a hand's-breadth while you watch, spitting up curtains of black mineral dust, and here and there the rock repeats itself in ways geology never learned—three identical jagged ledges stacked like misfired impressions from a stamping die, down to the same diagonal quartz seam and the same chipped corner.

Every cubic meter above that raw rock is packed to suffocation with plants.

The warmth, the mineral load, and the stray voltage bleeding through the water have turned the sunken basin into a furious, layered jungle. There is almost no open water to swim through; you push, duck, and thread your way between near-touching strata of green, burgundy, and bruised yellow-brown. Broad strap-kelp thick as conveyor belts rises from the basalt cracks in dense palisades, whipping violently side to side whenever two currents collide. Beneath them stand cane-brakes of hollow, jointed reeds with crimson veins, snapping and regrowing so rapidly that splintered stalks swirl perpetually in the midwater like green confetti. Higher up, buoyant bladder-trees anchor themselves to sharp ridges with cables of pale root, lifting clusters of gas-filled bulbs the size of oil drums into the dim drift; when pressure waves roll down the valley, the bulbs pop in ragged volleys like distant gunfire. Across every gap hang matted webs of fibrous algae and long, pale curtains of water-moss that tangle around rock fins, snare drifting silt, and shiver constantly with the vibration of the water.

And nothing eats any of it.

You can drift through these thickets for hours and never see a flick of silver, a retreating shell, a burrowing worm, or a grazing snail. No barnacles crust the iron; no tube worms fringe the hot vents; no scavengers pick apart the tons of shredded frond that pile up in the lee of every boulder. The absence of animal life is so complete it feels active, almost surgical. Every motion you catch at the corner of your eye—a sudden lunge of a frond, a shudder in the reed-bed, a pale shape turning over in the murk—turns out to be hydraulic, botanical, or mechanical. Without mouths to trim it, the flora lives in a frantic, blind cycle of explosive bloom and violent mechanical shredding. Shoots uncoil at visible speed, bloat with gas, choke a channel solid, and are ripped to rags ten minutes later when an eddy spins up from the trench floor, churning the water into warm emerald broth until the next surge clears it.

Scattered widely across this drowned valley—never clustered into a town, never forming a continuous complex, yet dominating everything through sheer output—sit the industrial installations.

There are not many of them: a riveted iron pump-house bolted crookedly to a knife-edge basalt spine; a solitary switchyard half a kilometer down-valley, its forest of porcelain bell-insulators and corroded copper busbars humming so hard the water around them beads with fine hydrogen bubbles; a lone gantry crane leaning out over a black fissure, its hook swaying on nothing; a sunken condenser drum the size of a warehouse wedged into a narrow cleft; a stubby foundry stack jutting from the weeds, its mouth glowing a dull, angry cherry-red as it vents molten slag straight into the water in continuous, screaming explosions of steam and black glass beads. Between these isolated structures sag miles of thick, armored power cables and flanged steel conduits, draped across the kelp forests like telephone wires through a drowned woods, humming and thrumming so fiercely they shake loose clouds of trapped sediment.

Every machine is running at catastrophic, uncrewed load. There are no operators, no loading docks, no roads leading out of the basin, and no legible purpose to the scaled production grinding away in the dark. Inside the open louvers of the pump-house, massive bronze impellers roar as they suck warm water out of the valley and blast it back into the valley three meters away through a roaring white plume of cavitation, tearing strips off the nearby kelp and carving fresh pits into the rock. Farther down the slope, a solitary hydraulic press cycles every nine seconds—*hiss, groan, CLANK-THOOM*—stamping blank hexagonal plates out of raw basalt and iron-rich clay, shoving them off a rusted chute into the trench, where they sink into the reeds and, before they reach the bottom, quietly smear into reddish silt as if the water has decided iron plates are a grammatical error.

Because out here, reality has slipped its belt and is running off-track.

At first you blame the turbidity, the dim light, the pounding noise rattling your inner ear. Then you watch a column of denser water fall straight downward *through* the water around you—a roaring, vertical waterfall inside a submerged valley, complete with spray and a foaming plunge-pool where liquid hits liquid, parting the kelp in a thirty-meter circle before abruptly ceasing as though a valve closed in mid-nothing. You swim beneath an overhanging shelf of sharp slate and find an inverted pocket of bone-dry, whistling air trapped against the stone—not calm air, not trapped gas, but a miniature gale roaring sideways in dry darkness, smelling sharply of hot transformer oil and crushed mint, until the ceiling of water beneath it buckles and the whole pocket collapses with a concussion that rattles your jaw.

Distance refuses to stay honest. That lone transformer pylon with its pulsing violet arcs looks to be two hundred meters across the reed-flats; you kick toward it for twenty seconds and nearly smash your shoulder into its vibrating steel lattice, yet when you turn back to look at the ridge you just left, it has shrunk to a smudge far across the basin. Sound bends worse than sight. A steel chain snaps somewhere out in the murk, and the metallic whip-crack arrives in your skull a full second *before* the loose link whips past your ribs and shears a swath of reeds in half. Look down a narrow corridor between two walls of strap-kelp when an electrical surge flashes bright blue-white, and you see the same rusted sluice-gate standing twice along the trench, twenty meters apart, both gates vibrating in lockstep, both draped with the exact same torn ribbon of red weed—and when the flash dies, only one is there, though both echoes keep booming back from the walls.

The plants and the engines have grown careless about where one ends and the other begins. Near the condenser drum, thick pale vines sprout cleanly through the center of a spinning cast-iron flywheel, passing straight through solid rotating metal without snapping, without snagging, their leaves twitching in time with the sixty-cycle hum of the coils. When voltage spikes through the valley's sunken busbars, the sap inside the surrounding reed-brakes lights up from within—not biological glow, just raw electrical excitation turning a hectare of submerged stalks briefly incandescent pale green, illuminating every jagged rock edge and every drifting shred of detritus before the circuit trips and plunges the basin back into bruised amber-and-olive gloom. Somewhere near the basin's center, a thicket of bladder-trees grows in a closed vertical loop, roots knotting directly into its own upper canopy with no rock beneath it, spinning slowly like an untethered turbine in the crosscurrents.

Nothing holds its shape for long. A calm, glassy lane opens through the dense weeds—warm, almost still, letting you see a quarter-mile down the valley to where three distant pump-stacks glow dull orange through the murk—and then a lateral shear-current hits from nowhere, slamming a wall of turbid water, uprooted reeds, and boiling vent-plumes sideways across the open space. Pressure spikes hard enough to pop your ears. The cables overhead buck like struck bowstrings, throwing off brief white sparks that hiss and die. Beneath your boots, a newly cracked slab of basalt grinds two inches sideways and spits up a ribbon of superheated water that smells of sulfur and copper. Somewhere far off in the dark, a massive flywheel winds higher and higher in pitch, climbing past the threshold of hearing into a pure, sickening vibration in the chest, until the current folds over itself again and swallows the sound whole—only to return it a moment later, louder, heavier, and coming from directly below.
