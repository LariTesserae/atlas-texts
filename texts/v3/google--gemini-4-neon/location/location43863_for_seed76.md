The first thing that reaches you is the brightness, and the second is how little noise the world makes while moving so fast.

This is a continental crown lifted miles above any lowland—if lowlands still exist beyond the soft, pale curve of the horizon. In every direction, the stone runs out for eighty or a hundred miles without a single jagged peak to interrupt it. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground down ages ago. What remains is an architecture that has finished changing its mind: broad, whale-backed swells of pale dolomitic limestone, shallow concave basins, and mile-wide terraces stepped gently down from one elevation to the next. Every outcrop has been planed by dry grit into long, smooth, aerodynamic ribs pointing along the prevailing wind. Nothing overhangs; nothing threatens to fall. The rock is chalk-grey, bleached ivory, and faintly rose where seams of gypsum and talc run close to the surface, polished to a low satin sheen.

Overhead, the sky does not look soft. Because the air is thin and dry to the bone, the blue darkens overhead into a deep, polished indigo, almost slate-violet at the zenith, while the horizon burns white-gold with a faint, high veil of mineral dust. Sunlight arrives unfiltered and hard. It warms your face and hands wherever it strikes directly, yet the air itself stays crisp and cold, biting quietly at the inside of your nose. Step into the shadow of a low stone terrace and the warmth vanishes instantly, like walking into an unlit vault. The shadows themselves have edges as sharp as cut paper, dark charcoal laid cleanly against blinding stone.

There is no water here—no streams, no tarn tucked in a hollow, no dampness beneath an overturned slab, not even the memory of mud or rain-channels in the rock. The basins are dry stone pans dusted with fine silica flour and white crystalline salt. And yet, crossing these immense pale flats like dark brushstrokes laid down by a steady hand, the vegetation is astonishingly, unabashedly lush.

From a distance, the emptiness dominates. You can walk for hours over bare, fluted pavement with nothing around you but sky, cold wind, and the pale curve of the plateau, watching a single ribbon of deep jade and bronze shimmer ten miles ahead. Only when you reach the rim of a shallow geological trough—where the ancient weathering has exposed a seam of hydrated gypsum, clay-minerals, and alkaline rock—do you realize how rich the growth actually is.

The strip is a quarter-mile wide and runs forty miles across the tableland, following the buried vein of stone. Stepping down into it is like plunging from a sun-baked quarry into a dense, layered conservatory. Broad, pleated fronds rise past your shoulders and arch overhead in heavy, overlapping vaults—deep malachite, oiled emerald, dark spruce-green, with undersides burnished pale silver to reflect the ground-glare back up through the lower tiers. Beneath the main canopy grow thick, springy bolsters of velvet-textured foliage, coiled rosette-cushions, and tangled curtains of ribbon-like leaves that hang down into the cool dimness.

Nothing in this thicket is damp. If you press your palm against a leaf, it feels cold, supple, and smooth as waxed leather, coated in an airtight, mirror-gloss cuticle that refuses to yield a single molecule of moisture to the thirsty sky. There is no scent of loam or wet rot beneath the canopy; instead, the sheltered air smells sharply of cedar-resin, dry sage, beeswax, and crushed chalk. The forest floor is a deep, dry mattress of shed papery husks woven through with pale, wire-fine root-mats fused directly into the bedrock. Rather than drinking rain that never falls, these roots secrete slow mineral acids that pry bound crystal-water and magnesium salts straight out of the stone itself. Inside the stems flows a thick, slow, amber fluid closer to pine-oil and glycerine than sap. Snap a stalk, and a single glossy bead swells at the break, skinning over solid in seconds before anything can evaporate.

In the thin air, sound dies almost at the lip of whatever makes it. Your footsteps on the open stone make a small, dry scuff that vanishes two paces away. Inside the thickets, even when the midday wind leans hard against the canopy, there is no roaring of branches—only a low, hushed, papery rustle, like heavy silk drawn across a wooden floor, punctuated now and then by a crisp, quiet *tick* as a taut stem shifts under the sun.

Yet the plateau is never still. Its emptiness and its lushness are locked together in a vast, daily engine driven by light and temperature.

At dawn, the world lies glass-cold and motionless. Overnight, thermal contraction pushes microscopic blooms of white sulfate and carbonate out of the dry stone pores, coating the bare basins in a delicate, feathery "salt-rime" that glitters like frost though it would crumble to dry powder between your fingers. Along the green troughs, the giant canopies stand tightly rolled into overlapping silver-backed cylinders, hoarding the last of the previous day's warmth against the freezing dark.

Then the white sun clears the flat eastern rim. Light pours across a hundred miles at once. Within minutes, a faint, rippling crackle travels down the length of the groves as millions of wax-sealed joints warm and loosen. Over the next hour, the whole continental ribbon slowly unrolls. Silver cylinders unfold into vast, layered sails of dark oiled green; ruffled crowns lift and spread wide, turning steadily to follow the arc of the sun. Seen from a high terrace, the plateau seems to wake in long, silent bands of color.

By late morning, the sun-struck stone warms rapidly while the thin air above stays chilled, setting colossal rivers of wind sliding down from the highest domes of the tableland. You feel the push of the air against your ribs far more than you hear it—a cool, steady pressure accompanied only by a soft, high whisper around your ears. Across the empty stone pans, the wind shaves off the morning's salt-rime and lifts pale, translucent veils of gypsum flour and glittering mica dust, streaming them horizontally for leagues like dry smoke.

This is what the lush canopies have been waiting for. The upper leaves of every thicket are ridged with fine, resinous hairs and tiny honeycombed pockets. As the continental dust-streams sweep across the troughs, the broad fronds dip and sway in slow, coordinated swells, combing mineral powder straight out of the bright air. By late afternoon, the oiled green leaves carry a faint, powdery bloom of pale gold and white dust; toward dusk, specialized margins will curl inward, secreting mild digestive oils to draw the trace phosphorus, iron, and alkaline salts into the plant before the night-roll begins again.

In the heat of the bright afternoon, when the wind settles into its long, steady stride, the groves release their seed. Deep among the bronze-veined lower tiers, taut crimson pods split without a sound. Out of the shaded aisles drift thousands of translucent, gas-buoyed spore-bladders—light, iridescent spheres the size of plums, inflated with warm metabolic gas and trailed by fine silken filaments. They rise lazily out of the green trenches, catch the fierce sunlight, and lift into the indigo sky, scattering into glittering, sparse constellations that ride the high air for fifty or a hundred miles before settling into some distant, empty fissure where a new vein of stone waits.

Animals are rare here—so rare that you can cross three basins and two full groves without seeing a single living creature move. The distances are too wide, the air too thin, and the margins of energy too finely measured for crowds or herds. What lives here moves with startling patience and economy.

Deep in the green tunnels beneath the arching fronds, you might occasionally come upon a grazer: low-slung, heavy-shouldered, about the size of a mountain sheep, moving alone. Its broad back and flanks are tiled in overlapping plates of pale, chalk-white enamel—so reflective against the glaring sun that if it steps onto the open limestone, it vanishes into the rock the moment it freezes. It feeds with a narrow, mineralized beak shaped like pruning-shears, nipping the thick central ribs of the lower leaves and drawing them immediately into sealed cheek-pouches so not a drop of the resinous sap is wasted on the dry air. Its sides barely rise and fall as it breathes; when it senses you, it does not bolt in panic—running costs too much oxygen—but simply sinks low into the springy leaf-husk litter and pulls its pale plates shut like a boulder settling into moss.

Far above, where the sky deepens toward violet, the other half of the ledger drifts in complete silence. If you shield your eyes and stare upward long enough, you may catch the pale, cruciform flash of a glider turning lazily on a morning thermal—wings spanning twenty feet, built of hollow spar-bone and taut, semi-transparent membrane that catches the sun like thin sheets of mica. It never beats its wings once aloft. It rides the continental wind-rivers for days at a time, patrolling forty-mile loops over the bright stone and the dark green ribbons below.

When a grazer risks crossing a narrow neck of open rock from one depleted trough to another, the attack arrives without warning. There is no cry, no rushing roar of feathers—the thin air keeps the descent mute. One moment a distant bright speck wheels three thousand feet up; the next, a folded pale needle drops straight out of the sun, opening at the last instant with one sharp, taut *snap* of stretched skin as long, hooked forelimbs drive cleanly between the enameled dorsal plates. Minutes later, the glider rises clumsily on a fresh gust off the warm stone, carrying only the richest oily organs, leaving the rest for the flat, copper-shelled runners that emerge from beneath the root-mats at twilight to strip the carcass to dry, white bone before the frost-cold night seals the plateau.

Toward evening, the light lengthens and turns stark amber, throwing mile-long shadows off ridges no taller than a table. The continental wind slackens, and the glittering dust-veils settle back onto the polished flats. Down in the troughs, the great emerald canopies begin their slow, whispering furl back into silver cylinders, locking away their warmth and their harvest of mineral flour.

Across the vast, bright curve of stone, there is no sign that anything has ever built a wall, worn a road, or lit a fire here. Nothing is broken, and nothing is trying to conquer anything else. The dryness, the height, the fierce light, the rare quiet hunters, and the lush, oiled ribbons of green have been trading dust, salt, gas, and stone for so long that the whole high continent feels less like a wilderness than a single, settled instrument—cool, dazzling, nearly silent, and endlessly in motion under the dark blue sky.
