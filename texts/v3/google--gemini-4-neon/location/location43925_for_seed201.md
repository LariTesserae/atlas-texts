High enough that the sky stops looking blue and turns a hard, bruised indigo, a single crescent of rock has split away from the spine of a young ridge, leaving a steep-walled pocket no wider than a cathedral nave.

Three sides are raw, vertical facets of pale quartzite and splintered gray slate. Nothing here has had time to round off. Every ledge ends in a blade; every fallen block at the base of the walls lies with crisp, unstained fracture faces, sharp enough to score leather. The fourth side simply drops away—a clean lip of stone jutting over miles of thin air and the bright, undulating tops of clouds far below.

What strikes first is the emptiness of the volume and the ferocity of the light. Inside the bowl, there is almost nothing to break the air—no canopy, no mist-banked ceiling, just a vast, hollow shaft of blinding white brilliance pouring down from an unfiltered sun. The pale walls catch it and throw it back across the floor like tilted mirrors. Shadows here are not soft gradations; they are ink-black cuts with razor edges, and stepping from sunlight into the shade of a boulder feels like plunging an arm into meltwater. The air itself is bitterly cold, thin enough to sting the nostrils and pull moisture straight off the lips.

Yet the pocket is not dead. Wherever a horizontal seam interrupts the pale cliff, or a shallow trough catches the blown dust of crushed slate, the rock is draped in dense, shockingly lush pelts of dark vegetation.

Because the bare planes of stone between them are so wide and stark, these living bands stand out like wet ink on chalk. They are thick, springy mats of deep indigo-green moss, dark-leaved sedges, and densely packed cushion-plants whose hairs trap tiny beads of water until the whole surface gleams like oiled fur. Rising out of the mats, spaced cleanly apart so each claims its own patch of sky, are knee-high stalks wrapped in overlapping, paper-thin bracts—translucent as oiled parchment, pale amber and bone-white. They look like narrow lanterns built from dried petals. Inside each hollow hood, the fierce sunlight warms the trapped air twenty degrees above the freezing wind outside, turning every stalk into a miniature glasshouse whose inner walls bead with clear condensation.

There are no streams here, and no standing ponds. Water arrives in silent, rapid surges. Every half-hour or so, a tongue of white cloud rises up the sheer outer cliff, spills over the razor lip without a sound, and floods the pocket. For five minutes the blazing light turns to a dazzling white-out. Feathery hoarfrost blooms instantly across the sharp stone edges and the outer hairs of the moss. Then the air currents shift, the cloud tears loose and vanishes down the drop, and the sun slams back into the bowl. The frost does not melt into rivers; half of it sublimates directly into faint wisps of steam that curl upward and disappear, while the rest beads briefly on the stone and slides into the dark, damp humus beneath the moss cushions before freezing solid again in the shadows.

The entire bowl pulses to this rhythm. It is an intensely busy place, yet almost completely silent. The thin air swallows sound, and nothing here wastes breath on calls.

The moment the cloud clears and sunlight strikes the eastern ledges, the pocket wakes in a sudden, mute choreography. The papery bracts of the lantern-plants give a faint, dry crackle as they flex open toward the heat. Out of the dark crevices beneath them pour swarms of tiny, metallic-blue flies and pale, broad-winged alpine moths, stiff at first, then darting in glittering, soundless clouds from hood to hood. Right behind them come the grazers—plump, short-eared burrowers with dense white outer coats over pitch-black skin. In the harsh glare, their hollow guard hairs catch the sun so brightly that each animal seems rimmed in a thin halo of light. They never whistle or squeak; instead, they sit upright on the sharp blocks and communicate across the wide bare floor with rapid flicks of their black-tipped tails and sudden crouches.

Higher up, moving effortlessly along finger-wide ledges of unweathered slate, small pale-horned climbers pick at the dark cushion-mosses. Their split hooves are translucent, almost like horn-colored ice, and the only sign of their passage is the occasional dry *tick* of a dislodged splinter of rock falling down the cliff face.

Nothing grazes for long. Because the bowl is so open and the air so empty, the predators drop from the blazing zenith without warning. Narrow-winged harriers, pale as snow underneath so they vanish against the glare, fold and slice straight down through the hollow space between the walls, wings making only a whisper like torn cloth. A flick of a tail on the far ledge sends forty burrowers diving beneath the moss mats in a single synchronized ripple. A moment later, a cloud shadow races across the floor, the temperature plummets, the insects drop torpid into the plant hoods, and the whole cathedral-sized basin snaps back into crystalline stillness—until the light returns and the furious cycle starts again.

Even inside the warmth of the translucent plant-lanterns, there is movement: clusters of tiny, pale, long-legged spiders and thumb-sized warm-blooded mice share the damp, heated chambers beneath the papery leaves, darting out only during the brief sunny peaks to snatch torpid beetles off the freezing rock.

Tucked into the sharpest corners of the pocket, almost lost against the scale of the bare stone, are the scattered traces of people who know how to read these cycles.

There is no settlement here, only sparse, patient handiwork. Along the base of the north wall, where the reflected glare lingers longest, someone has stacked angular shards of raw slate into two low, crescent-shaped windbreaks, chinking the gaps with dried moss. Under a shallow overhang nearby sits a single tiny shelter—barely tall enough to crouch inside—its leaning roof built from flat slabs and stitched sheets of the dried translucent plant-bracts, lashed to a ridgepole of bleached horn with braided sinew. Sunlight floods through the stitched parchment roof, turning the cramped interior into a warm, amber-lit pocket just like the plants it borrows from.

Near the edge of the drop stands a slender stack of three pale quartz blocks balanced improbably on their sharp points—a marker, or a sightline aimed at a distant peak across the clouds. Tucked between the roots of the largest moss-mats are small, deftly set snares of twisted hair and bone toggles, and down in a dry crack covered by a weighted flat stone lies a cache pit lined with cold-hammered copper pins, a chert scraper, and bundles of dried, darkened plant stalks.

On a smooth, sun-warmed slab facing the open drop, someone has pecked dozens of neat vertical grooves into the pale mineral grain with a stone point—quiet tallies, worn pale at the edges. Sitting beside it, there is only the thin, freezing air, the faint *tink* of a frost-wedged slate flake snapping free high on the wall, and the bright, relentless sweep of light and cloud-shadow across a small, fierce, soundless world perched on the edge of nothing.
