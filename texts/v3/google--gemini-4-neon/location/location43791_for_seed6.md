High enough that the clouds drift below the floor-plates, the entire hall is full of warm water.

From the inside, its boundaries are always near. It is the size of a modest turbine-cathedral or a single brick-and-iron weaving shed—perhaps fifty paces from end to end, half that across, rising three narrow galleries to a pitched roof of thick, wire-reinforced glass. Nothing here belongs to the ground. Beyond the vertical panes of the outer walls, visible only in narrow, dazzling slits where the interior foliage parts near the mullions, there is nothing to rest the eye on except thin, pale, high-altitude sky and the flat white tops of distant cloud-banks far below. The sunlight that strikes the glass roof is sharp and unsoftened by valley haze; passing into the water, it stays bright, clear, and almost clinical, filling the flooded chamber with a steady, luminous green-white daylight.

There is no room to swim freely. The tank is packed nearly solid from its grated steel deck to the undersides of the roof trusses—a dense, interlocked volume of heavy industrial hardware and lush, unpruned aquatic vegetation, sharing every cubic inch without leaving a corridor between them.

Everything structural is startlingly new. No rust blooms around the bolts; no silt has settled into the corners. The riveted I-beams wear fresh coats of pale battleship-grey enamel, their corners still sharp enough to snag a fingernail. Brass valve-wheels gleam with crisp factory knurling. Black-and-yellow hazard stripes painted along the edges of the three cantilevered catwalks have not blistered or peeled, and the stamped serial plates riveted to the transformer housings read as sharply as the day they were punched. Rubber gaskets bulge cleanly and evenly from between flanged iron pipes. Even the water itself is free of murk, optically clear and warm as a drawn bath, heated by banks of ribbed copper coils bolted horizontally along the lower walls.

Out of this crisp, soil-less geometry, the plants erupt in extravagant, claustrophobic abundance.

Not a grain of sand, gravel, or mud exists anywhere in the chamber. The vegetation anchors directly to the built surfaces, and it does so with uncanny precision. Pale, immaculate mats of fine white roots weave tightly through the diamond perforations of the steel floor-grating, gripping clean metal without a trace of decay. Thicker, muscular rhizomes wrap around insulated copper busbars and clamp onto grooved white porcelain insulators, sending dense forests of vertical stems upward between the pipe-racks.

The species here obey both sunlight and voltage. Ribbon-bladed grasses with translucent emerald leaves hang in thick curtains from the overhead gantry crane, swaying almost imperceptibly in the slow thermal drift rising off the heating coils. Tightly coiled fronds of bronze-tinted moss upholster every bolt-head and pressure-gauge housing, following the crisp silhouettes of the machinery like tailored velvet rather than blurring them. Stranger still are the glass-stemmed reeds that grow in crowded batteries along the electrified conduits: stiff, jointed stalks that shoot straight upward and then turn at exact ninety-degree angles whenever they approach a live ceramic terminal, tracing invisible grids of current through the bright water. Between the larger plants, every remaining gap is stuffed with fine, lace-like tangles of silver-veined bladderwort and clusters of pale, bulbous float-sacs pressed flush against the undersides of the catwalks.

Where the foliage packs too densely for the roof-light to penetrate—underneath the central press-frames or behind the banks of vertical condensers—heavy incandescent work-lamps inside bolted brass cages burn with a quiet, localized amber. Their glass domes are clean, kept bare by their own gentle heat, creating small warm lanterns inside the green crush.

Nothing moves quickly. The place is working, though it takes minutes of watching to realize it.

Down the spine of the hall runs a row of four immense, slow-turning drum-looms, each framed in freshly milled steel and submerged to its crown. Their perforated bronze cylinders rotate at a speed almost indistinguishable from rest—a single revolution taking the better part of an afternoon. As they turn, they gently wind fine, glistening pale filaments directly off the tips of a dense hedge of fibrous reeds rooted in the troughs beneath them. The stalks do not tear; they grow at roughly the pace of the winding, extruding a translucent, slightly metallic thread that spools onto the brass drums in neat, shimmering layers.

Beside the looms, wide electrolysis frames hang from overhead rails, each fitted with horizontal trays of broad, waxy floating leaves held submerged beneath glass baffles. Slowly, over hours, thin hexagonal plates of pale, salt-like mineral precipitate onto the undersides of these leaves, fed by the warm, mineral-doped water and a steady low-voltage hum in the frames. When a plate grows heavy enough, its leaf tilts with infinite patience, releases the crystal disc, and lets it glide downward in a silent zigzag through the thick stems until it lands on an inclined stainless-steel chute and slides, over the course of another hour, into a sealed bronze hopper at the base of the deck.

Large hydraulic pistons along the northern bulkhead extend and retract with the same glacial composure, taking forty minutes to shift a valve-bank three inches, nudging the warm water through silent internal manifolds so that the temperature never drops and the dissolved minerals never stratify too completely. Only faint, glassy shimmers—clear horizontal planes where slightly warmer water rests against slightly cooler water—betray the gentle circulation, bending the straight lines of the pipes and stems like lenses whenever you shift your viewpoint.

And there is no animal life at all.

Not a minnow darts between the vertical conduits. No snail creeps across the clean interior of the perimeter glass. No shrimp picks at the white root-mats, no insect skims the tiny, trapped silver pockets of air flattened beneath the peaks of the skylight. Not even a swirl of microscopic motes clouds the sunbeams slanting down through the upper trusses. Because nothing here grazes, burrows, or nibbles, every single leaf in the tank is geometrically intact—unbitten, unspotted, growing smoothly to its exact limit until it meets a valve-stem, a glass pane, or another frond, and then folding politely around it. The lushness has a strange, immaculate tension, like a room filled wall-to-wall with living clocks.

The sound matches that stillness. Submerged, and slowed nearly to stasis, an industrial hall loses its voice. There is no clatter of gears, no hiss of steam, no splash of open water. Instead, there is only a deep, velvety quiet threaded with sensations barely crossing the threshold into sound: a soft, continuous sixty-cycle hum conducted through the steel deck and felt mostly in the jawbone; the rare, tiny *tink* of a single round oxygen bubble detaching from an illuminated frond, rising through three tiers of leaves, and tapping against the underside of a glass sight-tube; once every few minutes, the faintest velvet sigh as a heavy bronze rack-gear advances by the width of a hair along its lubricated track.

Up at the very peak of the hall, where the pitched skylights meet the ridge-beam, the water presses directly against the glass. Only in the sharpest angles of the roof-trusses do thin, flattened lenses of silver air cling like mercury pools, trapped above the highest tips of the reeds. Looking up through the crowded emerald canopy toward those bright, mirror-like slivers, and beyond them to the sharp, cold blaze of the high-altitude sun, the whole chamber feels sealed inside a jewel of glass and iron—warm, airless, freshly machined, impossibly full, and patiently manufacturing something silent out of light, water, current, and green tissue, miles above the nearest ground.
