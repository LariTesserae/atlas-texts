The air up here is thin, cool, and brilliantly lit, the kind of high-altitude daylight that leaves no softness on any edge. Yet there is no stone anywhere beneath it. What looks from a distance like an immense grey-green peneplain—the final, near-flat stump of a mountain range ground down over tens of millions of years—gives slightly under your boot. It is entirely alive. The ground is a continental mat of ancient, compressed alpine turf, root-felt, and dwarf woody cushion-plants woven so tightly and so deep that bedrock is only a rumor miles below. Press a heel down and clear, cold water beads up around the sole, then sinks back into the fibrous sponge as soon as you lift your weight. There are no rivers here, no ponds, no mud—only a steady, cool dampness held inside living tissue, replenished whenever the low clouds drift across the tableland at shoulder height.

Across this vast, gently rolling plateau sits a city, though it is laid out with an extravagant, unsettling emptiness. It is a planned place, gridded and zoned, with wide paved avenues, numbered distribution yards, viaducts, and towering industrial complexes—yet kilometers of wind-shorn living turf separate one district from the next. You can stand on a central boulevard forty yards wide, edged with low curbs of hardened pale burl, and see three miles down its straight length without a single moving figure in sight, only a line of humming transformer-boles spaced at exact half-mile intervals and, far beyond them, the silhouette of a refinery district rising cleanly against the bright, pale sky.

Every piece of the city is plant. Not stone overgrown by vines, nor iron wrapped in ivy, but urban infrastructure whose sole medium is vegetation. The broad avenues are paved in slabs of densely compacted, naturally interlocked heartwood worn smooth by ages of wind and weather. The factory halls are vast, ribbed cathedrals of petrified-yet-living xylem, their walls still thickening by fractions of a millimeter each decade, slowly swallowing the rivets of hardened thorn-pegs that hold their buttresses together. Overhead, high-voltage power lines run between tall, forked pylons—not bare metal wire, but taut, dark-green vascular cables rich with sequestered copper, sheathed in slick, damp bark that glistens in the sun and crackles faintly whenever a mist-bank brushes past. Skylights are panes of stretched, translucent leaf-membrane; storage silos are bloated, ridged tubers half-sunk into the turf; even the slag heaps outside the foundries are mounds of pale, dry woody char and compressed resin-cake, slowly putting out cautious shoots of orange lichen.

And the place is loud.

In spite of the open sky and the immense spaces between buildings, silence does not exist here. A deep, continuous sub-bass thrum comes straight up through the living ground—the vibration of colossal root-turbines turning deep in the fibrous substratum, drawing mineral sap up through miles of subterranean trunks to feed the surface mills. Above that sits a layered, mechanical din: the sharp, rhythmic *chuff-clack* of pneumatic sap-pumps cycling inside the pump-houses; the high metallic shriek of wind-driven dynamos spinning on tall louvered masts; the rattling clatter of elevated conveyor-vines hauling slatted crates of pressed pale amber across three-mile trestles; the sudden, ear-splitting hiss of pressure valves venting hot, sweet-smelling steam into the cold air.

Listen closely to that steam-vent, and the first hairline fracture in the world shows itself.

A refinery three miles to the east blows a three-second blast from its release stacks. The sound rolls crisply across the flat, open tableland and strikes you cleanly. Then, four seconds later, the exact same blast slams back from the north—hard, close, and sharp-edged, as if it had ricocheted off a sheer cliff face a quarter mile away. You turn to look north. There is nothing there. Only miles of flat damp turf, a single distant switching-yard, and bright empty sky. The ancient mountain range that once towered above this plateau eroded away eons ago, ground down to this serene tableland—yet its acoustic geometry never left. The echoes of the city still bounce violently off invisible granite flanks, funnel through vanished alpine valleys, and boom down from towering peaks that are no longer present to the eye.

The longer you walk the avenues, the more you realize that erosion here did not stop at rock. It wore down the rules of the world as well, leaving them thin, patched, and slightly slipped out of alignment.

Try walking toward the condensation towers of the southern works—massive, pale-wood lattice frames hundreds of feet tall, strung with fine filamentous netting that combs cool water straight out of passing clouds. They stand clearly in the crisp sunlight, dripping steady curtains of mist-water into hollow bamboo-like gutters. You walk toward them along the straight wooden causeway for twenty minutes, and they do not grow larger. The pylons pass beside you at a steady pace; your knees feel the distance; the wind stays cold on your left cheek—yet the towers remain fixed at the exact same apparent size, sharp and bright against the sky. Then you step over an ordinary expansion joint in the root-pavement, and with a quiet, stomach-dropping shift of air pressure, the towers are suddenly looming directly overhead, roaring with dripping water, casting a cold shadow across your shoulders. Look back the way you came, and the boulevard you walked for twenty minutes is only two hundred yards long.

Even the shadows have succumbed to the totality of the flora. Under the harsh, normal daylight, every pylon, crane, and gantry casts a crisp, dark-grey shadow onto the damp turf. If you kneel and run your hand along the edge of one, you feel a slight, cool ridge—maybe a quarter-inch high. The shadows have begun to photosynthesize, or to be colonized by a flat, pitch-dark lichen that moves in exact, silent lockstep with the sun. As the afternoon progresses, the dark tissue creeps imperceptibly across the wooden paving slabs, leaving faint damp rings where the noon shadows used to lie. If a cloud passes overhead and blots out the sun, the shadow-mats do not merely fade; they peel up slightly at the edges with a dry, papery rustle, curl inward, and vanish, only to snap flat again the instant the sunlight returns.

Inside the industrial yards—which are open, unfenced, and eerily accessible—the machinery runs without operators, driven by deep biological-mechanical schedules whose purpose has drifted past legibility. Great reciprocating presses built from laminated ironwood rise and fall with deafening regularity, stamping sheets of tough, damp leaf-felt into crisp hexagonal plates. Yet the rhythm is wrong. The heavy boom of the impact strikes your chest half a second *before* the counterweight drops, and the puff of woody dust blooms upward off the die-plate before the press makes contact. Along a sorting trestle, wooden hopper-cars rattle steadily from west to east, heaped with pale resin blocks, passing into a long windowless kiln-shed whose tall chimneys pour clean, cold grey smoke into the blue air. If you watch the chimneys for a full minute, your eyes begin to ache: the smoke is not dispersing. It is gathering out of the clear alpine sky, thickening into crisp plumes, and pouring *downward* into the stacks, while out the far end of the kiln roll the exact same hopper-cars, now carrying raw, wet bundles of unpressed moss.

The horizon itself refuses to settle into an honest curve. Because the plateau is so open and so sparsely built, you can see immense distances in every direction—fifteen, twenty miles of dull olive turf broken by scattered urban clusters and thin straight lines of power-trunks. At the farthest edges, the plateau appears to tilt gently upward, as though you were standing in the shallow dish of an enormous saucer. Worse, the districts do not quite agree on where they sit. To the northwest stands a distinctive triad of swollen fermentation domes, flanked by a single crooked chimney that leaks a thin thread of bright white vapor. Turn completely around and look southeast across twelve miles of empty upland, and the exact same triad of domes stands there too—the exact same crooked chimney, the exact same thread of vapor—except its bark is a shade paler, and its cooling-louvers are angled three degrees further open, as if you were seeing the same complex from an earlier Tuesday.

Life, in the animal sense, barely registers in this roaring botanical machine. The plateau is too cool, too loud, too thoroughly colonized by plant and industry to support much else. Once in an hour, you might catch sight of a pale, long-legged grazer—something roughly the shape of an alpine antelope, with a narrow head and a coat the color of wet birch bark—standing completely motionless in the middle of an empty six-lane intersection, cropping at the bright green seam between two wooden flagstones. It never seems startled by the thunder of the distant presses or the screech of overhead trolleys. If you walk toward it, it does not bolt; it simply steps calmly behind the narrow trunk of a signal-post, far too thin to conceal a creature of its size, and does not emerge on the other side. Closer in, tucked inside the warm, vibrating seams of the transformer-boles where the copper-sap current generates a steady low heat, tiny glossy-black beetles cluster by the dozen, riding out the cool mountain air on the hum of the grid. High overhead, caught in the wet mesh of the cloud-combs, the papery wings of rare white moths flutter weakly until the condensation weights them down and washes them into the collection troughs.

Every few hours, a cool weather-front rolls across the plateau—not a storm, just a broad, low belly of bright grey cloud dragging its underside along the ground. When it arrives, the entire city responds at once. Across miles of open turf, thousands of wooden louvers on the condensation towers and factory roofs crank open in a rolling wave, producing a vast, dry clattering sound like a giant deck of cards being riffled across the horizon. The air turns briefly dense and wet; visibility drops to fifty yards; the vascular power-cables sing a full tone higher as water beads along their insulated bark, throwing off faint, sharp snaps of pale blue static that smell cleanly of ozone and crushed pine needles.

In that brief damp whiteout, the reality of the plateau feels loosest. Through the mist, you can hear the rhythmic footsteps of crowds moving along the wide empty boulevards—hundreds of soles striking wet heartwood in brisk, purposeful unison—though the visibility clears to reveal nothing but damp, unmarked planks stretching away to the next humming substation. Sometimes, while the cloud is thickest, the faint vertical ribs of giant, vanished mountain slopes briefly darken the fog a hundred yards away, looming steep and impossible where flat turf ought to be, before the wind tears the cloud apart and bright, cold sunlight slams back down onto the open plain.

The louvers crank shut in another cascading rattle. Water drips steadily from every gantry, gutter, and conductive branch, soaking cleanly back into the endless living floor. Down in the deep root-turbines, the pitch of the hum shifts by a minor third—some unseen quota met, or some ancient shift changed—and across the bright, cool, empty miles, the factories keep running, loud and immaculate, manufacturing their quiet impossibilities under the sun.
