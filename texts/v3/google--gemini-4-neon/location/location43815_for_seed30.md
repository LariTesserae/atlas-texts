You stand on rock that has not yet learned how to be old.

The shelf is no wider than a small hamlet—perhaps two hundred paces from the sheer back wall to the jagged lip where everything drops away into cloud—and it feels even more contained than its measurements suggest, held in by curved, half-formed ribs of dark stone that rise like the unroofed apse of a cathedral. High up here, the sky should be thin and bitter, yet the air is heavy and warm, hovering in the damp heat of a greenhouse just after a storm. Thick, bruised-pewter cloud presses low over the rim, dimming the day to a perpetual late afternoon. Nothing casts a sharp shadow. Instead, light arrives from everywhere at once in a flat, reluctant sheen, catching only on wet edges and moving bodies.

And everything is wet, though it is not quite raining. Warm vapor rolls continuously up over the cliff lip from the abyss below, hits the cooler mineral geometry of the basin, and condenses almost instantly. Water appears out of the air directly on the stone—beading, fusing, running in glossy sheets across expanses of bare, dark basalt-glass. Where the rock has folded into angular troughs, the water pools two or three inches deep, clear as lens glass, shivering constantly with vibration.

The strangest thing about the ground underfoot is its utter lack of mercy or wear. There is no gravel, no sand, no silt, no rounded pebble anywhere. Every ridge is razor-fresh, every facet planar and crisp, as if the mountain were being cast in a mold that had only just been pulled away. In fact, the casting isn't finished. Every few minutes, somewhere across the wide open floor, a wet crack rings out—sharp as a rifle shot—and a fresh seam of obsidian-dark mineral pushes upward by the width of a thumb, pale blue sparks of triboluminescence winking along the fracture before the damp air swallows them. The rock sweats a faint, iron-sweet mineral brine as it cures. Most of the basin is open, bare, gleaming dark stone, great empty stretches separating one presence from the next.

Because of that emptiness, the scattered structures stand out immediately, though none of them is taller than a porch or wider than a wagon. There are seven or eight of them spaced irregularly across the terrace, looking less like buildings than like surgical pins driven into a healing bone.

Near the center, a slender hoop of matte, chalk-white material hovers a hand’s breadth above a shallow pool. It does not hum, it does not vibrate, and it refuses to get wet; condensing mist slides around it in a smooth, dry envelope two inches thick, as if the air itself remembers to step aside. Directly beneath the hoop, the water behaves wrongly—swelling into a steep, trembling dome six inches tall, held together by a surface tension that shouldn't exist, shedding fat, slow-falling droplets that sometimes drift sideways before they strike. Fifty paces away, wedged into the base of the sheer back wall, stands a low plinth of interlocked metallic leaves that shift their angles so slowly you only notice when you look away and look back. Wherever its leaves align, the air nearby develops a faint refractive kink, like heat-shimmer over summer asphalt, and the rock behind it visibly thickens over the course of an afternoon, growing new stepped terraces out of nothing more than vapor and pressure.

Further toward the drop-off sit three blackened tripod stakes, their legs braided from hair-fine filaments that vanish into the solid glass-stone without leaving drill holes or melt marks. Between them, a faint geometric grid is etched into the wet floor—not carved, just a difference in how the rock refracts light, as if the crystal lattice beneath was instructed to turn ninety degrees along a ruler. You get the distinct, unsettling sense that these scattered devices aren't *occupying* a mountaintop; they are *making* one, holding open a pocket of warm, dense pressure in the high cold sky and coaxing solid geology out of the gradient.

Despite the barrenness of the floor and the sparseness of the artifacts, the place is deafening.

Sound has nowhere soft to go in a bowl of wet, unweathered glass. The constant updrafts rushing past the jagged rim produce a low, hollow organ-note that shifts pitch whenever a heavier bank of cloud slides past. Against that drone, the curing stone snaps, ticks, and occasionally lets out a long, resonant groan as an internal stress relieves itself. Water hisses continuously as sheets of runoff pour off the outer lip into the void, only to be blown back upward in warm spray.

And layered above all of it—louder than the wind, louder than the rock—is the wildlife.

At first glance, the wide open floor looks sparse, almost austere. Then your eyes adjust to the dim light and the sheer velocity of what lives here. Life has not waited for soil or trees to colonize this newborn terrace. There are almost no plants: only the toughest pioneer smudges tucked into the sharpest right-angle creases where rock meets rock—tufts of damp, copper-green felt-moss no bigger than a palm, and occasional clusters of translucent, hair-thin reeds that vibrate visibly in the acoustic wash. Yet the fauna is staggering, packed into the vertical margins, the shallow pools, and the warm turbulent air between the sparse landmarks.

The loudest are the bell-crabs—fist-sized, translucent-shelled scavengers that cling in rattling clusters along the dry underside of fresh overhangs and dart in frantic sideways sprints across the slick open stone. Their shells are grown partially from the fresh, uncured silicates weeping out of the rock, making them brittle and glassy; when they communicate—or warn off rivals—they snap their flattened dorsal plates together with a sharp, percussive *clack* that echoes off the basin walls like castanets. Hundreds of them are snapping at any given second, waves of sound rippling from one end of the shelf to the other in rolling, syncopated crescendos.

Skimming just inches above the wet flats are clouds of pale, ribbon-bodied drifters, each about the length of a forearm, kept aloft by rapid undulations of two frilled membranes that catch the warm rising drafts. They graze directly on the mineral-rich mist and on microscopic blooms suspended in the condensing film. Where the water behaves strangely around the white hovering hoop, the drifters gather in dense, swirling knots, dipping their trailing mouthparts into the gravity-defying dome of water where dissolved minerals concentrate unnaturally thick.

Nothing here stays still for long because everything is being hunted.

From the angular fissures along the back wall launch the glass-hounds—sleek, six-limbed ambushers with wet, slate-colored hide and elongated, forward-facing acoustic funnels instead of external ears. They don't chase through cover; there is no cover. They wait until a wave of bell-crab clacking peaks, masking their own footfalls, then burst out across fifty yards of gleaming stone in silent, hydroplaning bounds, their splayed pads slapping water only at the last instant. A pack of three will herd a ribbon-drifter low against a wet rock step, snapping it out of the air with a wet, hollow *pop* before dragging it back toward the crevices.

Overhead, barely visible against the bruised gray ceiling of cloud, heavier shapes wheel on broad, leathery wings that never fully flap—only tilt and lock into the roaring updrafts along the cliff lip. Every minute or two, one folds its wings and drops straight into the dim bowl like a stone, braking violently just above the floor with a thunderous boom of trapped air, snatching a distracted hound or a cluster of crabs before banking back out over the abyss, crying out in a double-toned brassy shriek that sets the entire basin vibrating again.

Every ten or fifteen minutes, the whole micro-weather turns over. The ambient warmth spikes slightly; the scattered artifacts respond with subtle, synchronized shifts—a leaf-plinth clicks shut, the white hoop dims for a heartbeat, the air pressure drops enough to pop your ears. Instantly, a pulse of thick, hot fog rolls over the rim and fills the cathedral-sized shelf from wall to wall, cutting visibility to ten paces. In that sudden dim soup, the hunting rules flip. The winged predators vanish upward; the bell-crabs pour out of their crevices by the hundreds to graze the freshly weeping mineral brine; pale, blind worm-like grazers unfurl from the razor-thin fractures, sweeping fan-shaped gills through the rushing sheets of runoff.

Then, just as swiftly, a cold shear slices across the high rim, scouring the fog back out into the void. Light returns to its bruised pewter dimness. The water runs clear again over brand-new stone that is, by a fraction of a millimeter, larger than it was ten minutes ago. Across the wide, echoing floor, the snapping resumes, the drifters rise, and a fresh crack rings out from deep beneath your boots—another piece of a world arriving loudly in the warm dark.
