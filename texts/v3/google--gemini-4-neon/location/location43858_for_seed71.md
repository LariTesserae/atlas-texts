The plateau has no peaks left. Whatever mountain chain lifted it into the thin air did so far enough back in the earth's memory that the summits have long since surrendered—ground down, slumped, and planed by eons of ice and wind until the roof of the world is a single rolling expanse of worn-smooth bedrock. You can stand on a gentle swell of pale grey slate and see sixty miles in every direction, and beyond that, only more swells, each one a shade closer to the pale blue of the sky, until distance stops looking like geography and begins to look like curvature.

The light is ordinary and unforgiving. A clear, high-altitude sun, small and white-gold, casts sharp, honest shadows across the cold stone. There is no haze to soften things unless a cloud-bank happens to drag itself directly over the ground, which happens often at this height. You can watch three separate weather systems at once from a single terrace: a flat smear of sleet moving slowly across a basin twenty miles north, a low belly of wet fog snagging on a ridge to the east, and here, where you stand, dry pale sunshine and a wind thin enough to make your teeth ache.

Because the air is cold—always hovering just above or just below the line where water stiffens—moisture does not hurry away. It lingers in the worn depressions of the stone, beads on every horizontal surface, collects in dark, mirror-still puddles fringed with needles of shade-ice. And out of this thin, patient dampness rises a startling, heavy green.

The vegetation is thick, cold-blooded, and wildly prosperous. Vast cushions of peat-moss swell like wet loaves between the paving stones, pale chartreuse at their tips and bruised purple underneath. Broad-leafed frost-rhubarb unfurls waist-high in sheltered hollows, its leaves ribbed, waxy, and beaded with condensation. Dense tangles of alpine willow, rubbery evergreen sedge, and tall, dark-fronded ferns choke the lower slopes and spill across the open ground in dense mats. Everything grows close and thick to hold what little warmth the sunlight grants, so the moorland feels less like barren tundra and more like an enormous, chilled conservatory whose glass roof was removed ten thousand years ago.

And woven across this immense cold garden—thinly, loosely, at a scale that ignores human haste—is the city.

It is not a ruin, exactly, though parts of it are worn nearly level with the bedrock. Nor is it crowded. Between one district and the next lie miles of empty moss-plain, crossed only by straight, hand-laid stone avenues and low slate aqueducts. Yet the urban order is unmistakable. You come upon a crossroads where four cobbled ways meet around a sunken, octagonal cistern; half a league further, a cluster of forty low stone houses steps down a gentle hillside, their roofs shingled in overlapping slabs of hand-split shale, their eaves fitted with hammered copper gutters green with verdigris. Beyond them, across two miles of spongy fern-bog, stand a row of cylindrical stone granaries, a covered timber arcade, a terraced plaza built in three concentric rings of mortared fieldstone.

Everywhere, the craft is manual and patient. You can still see the dimpled bite of copper chisels on the lintels, the thumb-grooves in the kiln-fired drainage tiles, the tight herringbone weave of reed-matting tucked beneath timber rafters. Mortise-and-tenon joints are pegged with oak dowels. Heavy sluice-gates of blackened larch sit across the irrigation runnels, lifted by stone counterweights hung from braided flax ropes stiff with hoarfrost. And the city is deeply layered: newer dry-stone garden walls sit atop older lime-mortared foundations, which rest in turn on cyclopean blocks so ancient their corners have melted into smooth, blind pillows, almost indistinguishable from the native granite beneath them.

Yet nothing breathes here.

There are no birds threading the white sky, no crows perched on the granary ridges, no insects humming above the wet clover or skating across the dark water in the cisterns. Turn over a damp flagstone and you find only pale root-threads and cold black loam—not a single beetle, worm, or millipede. The granaries hold drifted leaves and ice-crystals, never droppings. The mud along the sluice-channels bears no prints.

The absence of animals leaves a profound, ringing hush across the leagues. Sound travels poorly in the thin cold air anyway, and what noise exists is small and intimate: the soft, wet brush of broad fern-fronds shifting in a draft; the slow, irregular *plip* of meltwater falling from a copper spout into a mossy trough; the dry whisper of wind working through frozen sedge. You could walk for a whole morning and hear nothing louder than your own boots on damp slate.

Which makes the first thing you notice wrong all the harder to dismiss.

You look down into one of the octagonal stone cisterns, expecting to see the sky reflected flat and true, and find the water tilted at a clean twenty-degree slant inside the basin—brimming right to the carved lip on the north side, sunk four feet down on the south, utterly still and unspilling, as though the earth beneath this single courtyard had quietly disagreed with the rest of the world about which way down lies. A thin rind of ice covers the tilted surface. When a drop of water falls from an overhanging fern, it strikes the sloped sheet and sends ripples out parallel to the slant.

Once you see that, the rest of the plateau begins to reveal what three eons of erosion have done not merely to the rock, but to the stitching of the place itself.

Walk down one of the straight stone avenues toward a cluster of red-tiled houses a mile ahead. Count your paces along the left curb: six hundred, seven hundred, and you are abreast of the first courtyard wall. Look across the twenty-foot width of the cobbles to the right curb, and you realize the stone parapet on that side has stretched—not visibly, not dramatically, just quietly—so that the opposite wall of the exact same street still lies half a league behind you, trailing off through a patch of pale sedge that doesn't fit inside the road's geometry. If you step across the avenue, your stride feels entirely ordinary, yet the wind changes direction mid-crossing, and the shadows of the cypress-like moss-shrubs point twenty degrees closer to noon than they did six feet ago.

The daylight stays clear and plain through all of it. Nothing shimmers or glows. A wooden roof-truss hangs suspended eighteen inches above the fieldstone walls of a long hall, the timber uprights that once supported it having rotted away into a soft ridge of wet brown peat along the floor; the roof simply has not received word that its pillars are gone, and its slate tiles continue to shed rainwater cleanly into gutters that lead into an empty square of air.

And the city is still active.

Stand beside a half-finished retaining wall on the lip of a mossy terrace, where a rough-dressed block of basalt sits next to a wooden mallet and a bronze chisel crusted with cold dew. Wait in the quiet. After three or four minutes, there is a small, sharp *tink*, and a crescent chip of grey stone pops neatly off the corner of the block and skitters into the ferns. Nothing swung the mallet. The blow landed somewhere far back in the worn-thin past of the terrace, and the vibration is only now finishing its journey through the grain of the rock. Five minutes later, another chip leaps free. Down the lane, a bundle of cut reeds lies on a low roof, and every hour or so a fresh handful is tucked smoothly beneath the eaves-poles, lashed tight by a twist of damp twine that tightens itself with a soft, fibrous creak.

Whole neighborhoods slowly revise themselves in this manner, at the pace of glaciers or growing timber. A terraced market-square slides sideways over the course of a week, its stone steps passing cleanly through a stand of dwarf-willows without bruising a single branch, merging with a courtyard that used to sit two hills over. Sometimes two eras of the same street occupy the same flagstones in alternating hours: at morning light, an open plaza paved in worn river-pebbles; by early afternoon, without a sound of grinding stone, the low walls of a clay-brick dye-works have risen knee-high out of the paving, their vats brimming with cold rainwater, their wooden drying-racks casting crisp shadows across the moss. By dusk they have thinned back into the damp slate, leaving only a few wet leaves lying where the walls had been.

The plants, with no animals to eat them and millennia to experiment, have made a living out of the broken seams.

They do not root merely in soil; they root across the fractures in distance and sequence. Pull gently on a thick, pale-veined runner of cold-ivy spilling from a cracked clay amphora on a high balcony, and you feel it tugged from somewhere far away—its root-bulb sits in a drenched peat-bog four miles down-slope, skipping the intervening miles of cold air entirely, drawing up dark acidic water through a pinch in space no wider than a thumb. You can watch a single flowering stalk of white snow-gentian bloom simultaneously in twelve different courtyards scattered across a province, its stems swaying in exact unison whenever a gust strikes any one of them.

Stranger still are the shade-mosses. Because the high sunlight is cold and sharp, and the shadows are consistent, a lush emerald fur of bryophytes has learned to colonize the shadows themselves rather than the stones beneath them. At mid-morning, a thick, wet carpet of moss covers the cool blue triangle cast by a watch-cairn. Watch closely for ten minutes as the sun climbs: the leading edge of the moss unrolls itself across bare frost-rimed slate at the exact pace of the moving shade, putting out tiny glistening spore-capsules in seconds, while the trailing edge dries, thins, and vanishes cleanly the instant direct sunlight touches it—leaving the rock behind completely dry, scrubbed clean, without even a smear of dampness. By evening, the same acre of moss has migrated cleanly across the entire plaza, grazing on the absence of sun.

Weather behaves with the same quiet, fractured insistence. Because the plateau is vast enough to brew its own climates, fronts and squalls move across it constantly—yet they snag on ancient property lines and topological rifts. A dense wall of icy drizzle will advance briskly down a terraced valley, drenching the slate roofs and sending dark runnels gurgling down the clay pipes, and then stop dead at a knee-high dry-stone boundary wall. On the wet side, ferns bend under heavy beads of water and fog blurs the far granaries; three inches across the wall, the flagstones are bone-dry in bright, cold sun, and the wind blows in the opposite direction. Only at the base of the wall does the dampness leak through—oozing sideways out of the mortar in slow horizontal threads of vapor that freeze into delicate horizontal icicles pointing straight out into the dry air.

In the wide, circular basins where several of the old avenues converge, the wind sometimes blows inward from every point of the compass at once—a steady, cold, silent draft converging on a single stone wellhead at the center, piling shed willow-leaves and fragments of pale lichen into a waist-high cone that shivers continuously without ever lifting into the sky or blowing away. Walk into the center of that basin and the air feels thick, almost heavy, pressing lightly against your chest from all sides, smelling sharply of wet limestone, crushed fern-stems, and old frost.

Even the water systems have surrendered to the slow unravelling of cause. A timber aqueduct carries a brisk, clear stream of snowmelt across a shallow gorge, emptying into a stone trough at the head of a terraced orchard of stunted, cold-adapted apple trees. Trace the runoff down the stone channels between the terraces, through the mossy culverts, under a low arched bridge, and you find it pouring smoothly into the exact hillside spring that feeds the aqueduct's intake—gaining height with every quiet bend, circulating endlessly in a closed loop that has neither source nor sea, keeping the gnarled orchard wet enough to hang heavy each autumn with small, hard, sour green fruit that drops into the moss, rots back into black humus, and feeds the roots of trees whose branches sometimes bud in the same week their fruit falls.

Nothing here feels violent. That is perhaps the most unsettling quality of the plateau: its breakdown is completely serene. There are no jagged rifts tearing open the sky, no roars of collapsing masonry. The world is simply very, very old—so old that the habits matter keeps, the agreements about straight lines and before-and-after and weight pulling downward, have worn as thin and smooth as the stone thresholds of the granaries.

Stand at the edge of an upper terrace as the pale sun drops toward the endless, gently rolling horizon. Far below—ten miles away, or perhaps two hundred paces along the right-hand curb—a broad band of cloud settles softly over a scattered cluster of copper-roofed halls, turning their mossy courtyards silver with condensation. Somewhere nearby, a wooden sluice-gate lifts an inch on its frozen flax rope with a quiet wooden *clack*, releasing a thread of cold water into a channel that winds uphill toward the darkening sky. Across the flagstones at your feet, the lush emerald shadow-moss lengthens rapidly, soft and wet and cold, racing the dusk across the worn stone while the thin air goes utterly still.
