The sound reaches you before the room makes sense to your eyes. It is not a single noise but a thick, wet pressure against the eardrums: a deep, hollow booming that rolls slowly in a circle, overlaid with the ceaseless clatter of water striking flat water, a dry submarine crackling near your boots, and sudden, sharp clicks that ricochet twice across the air before dying. The air itself is cellar-cold and heavy with mineral damp, tasting faintly of wet clay, lime, and pond-rot.

As your eyes adjust to the dim pewter light, the scale of the place settles around you—and with it, an odd vertigo. It is not vast in the way of a valley or a cavern; you can see its boundaries all at once. It is a single enclosed vessel, roughly seventy paces from curved wall to curved wall and perhaps half that to the crown of its domed roof. Yet it feels achingly hollow. Almost nothing occupies the middle. Between the flat, dark mirror of shallow water covering the floor and the arched ribs of the ceiling, there is only cold gray air, a handful of low weathered stone bollards protruding from the surface, and thirty or forty thin cords of braided rush hanging straight down from the upper beams, each weighted just above the water with a hand-formed lump of pierced clay or a notched spool of bone.

Nothing here is raw earth or untouched stone. Every surface you can see or touch was shaped by hands working with simple tools—chisels, adzes, wooden paddles, bone scrapers—and left to weather for centuries until craft and erosion fused into a single settled state. Beneath the shin-deep water, the floor is laid in concentric rings of hand-trimmed slate and thick terracotta tile, long since slumped into gentle dishes and troughs that guide the water inward toward a square central drain slotted out of solid limestone. The curved walls rise from a sunken bench of dressed blocks, built of flat river-brick and coarse mortar flecked with crushed shell. High overhead, darkened oak ribs—still ridged with ancient adze-marks and pinned together with green-crusted copper pegs—carry the weight of the dome toward a crooked roof-vent and a ring of narrow, slanted clay flues.

Through those upper flues comes all the light the chamber gets: pale, watery shafts of gray twilight that never quite reach the center of the floor. Only where that dimness brushes the upper masonry does anything green survive. A thin, bruised velvet of liverwort stains the wettest brick-courses; pale tufts of hair-moss cling to the white beards of lime that have leached out of the mortar joints; around the rim of the roof-vent itself, a ragged skirt of translucent fern-fronds hangs limply inward, trembling whenever a draft shifts outside. Everything else is wet ochre, pewter, bone-white mineral crust, and the glossy black of submerged stone.

Water is everywhere, though nowhere deep. It weeps steadily through the masonry as if the whole structure sits sunken below an unseen water table, beading along the lime-curtains, running in greasy threads down the bricks, and letting go from the timber ribs in heavy, irregular drops that slap the floor-mirror and send intersecting rings gliding out across the empty room.

It takes a second look to understand why the walls look so strange, and why the room sounds the way it does. The entire inner curve of the dome, from just above the waterline to the base of the upper ribs, is honeycombed with thousands of hand-coiled terracotta jars bricked sideways into the masonry, their open mouths facing inward. Thumbprints from whoever threw or coiled them still show under the translucent glaze of mineral lime; the lower rims have been worn slick and notched by generations of claws and wet bellies.

The center of the chamber is nearly void, yet the rind of it is crowded with life.

Wedged backward into the mouths of the clay jars are heavy, smooth-skinned amphibians the color of bruised tallow and wet ash. They are almost invisible until one inflates its throat-sac, pressing pale, veined membrane snug against the neck of its jar. When it calls, the terracotta vessel acts as a resonator. Small jars near the upper curve bark with a hollow, wooden snap; wide-bellied jars along the lower tiers boom with a brassy bass vibration strong enough to shiver the surface of the water. They do not call at random. One call trips the next along the curve, sending a thick band of sound chasing counterclockwise around the dome, rising and falling in pitch as it climbs and drops through the staggered tiers of pottery. When the band passes directly behind you, the hanging rush-cords near the center hum a faint, strained note in sympathy.

In the damp mortar seams between the jar mouths, flat-bodied, long-limbed things with pale, glistening chitin wait completely still, their legs splayed wide over the lime-crust. Whenever an amphibian thrusts its throat too far out, or a clumsy fledgling tries to haul itself from one jar lip to a wetter one above, the seam-hunters lunge with a crisp scraping of shell on baked clay. Sometimes the struggle ends in a soft plop forty feet below.

The shallow floor-water is waiting for anything that falls. Though dark from the dimness overhead, the water is clear enough to show a restless, crowded floor. Pale segmented crawlers with oversized, mismatched pincers pack every tilted seam between the flagstones; their constant snapping and jostling produces the dry, frying crackle that fills the gaps beneath the booming of the walls. Ribbon-thin, nearly blind fish with pale flared barbels slip in rapid flickers along the sunken tile channels, while broad, olive-plated lurkers—heavy and motionless as sunken cobbles—lie recessed in the troughs beside the low wall-sluices. When something drops from the upper jars, the flat mirror explodes for two violent seconds of thrashing water and echoes like a cracked plank, then heals back into a trembling dark sheen.

High up in the gloom, wedged shoulder to shoulder along the adze-cut oak ribs, hang hundreds of small, narrow-winged flyers with bare leathery hides, pleated ears, and long trailing whiskers that dangle like wet linen threads. Most of the time they merely shift and squeak in the rafters, dropping dry guano that hisses softly into the water.

Then the floor breathes.

Every quarter-hour or so—driven by some sunken, hand-built siphon of clay pipes and stone baffles beneath the foundation, or by the slow push of groundwater outside—a deep, wet sucking groan rises from the central limestone grate. The water level tilts subtly inward. Over the course of two minutes, the mirror drops by the width of a hand.

The change is tiny, yet the entire chamber snaps into a different gear. The high edges of the tilted flagstones break the surface, glossy and black with thin algal film. Along the exposed grooves, the crawling shell-backs scuttle frantically toward deeper troughs, their clicking doubling into a harsh roar. From the wet sheen of the uncovered stones, pale midges lift in shivering, smoky veils.

At once, the rafters empty. The naked-winged flyers peel off the timber ribs in tight, swirling ribbons and plunge into the hollow air, filling the empty middle with frantic, overlapping sweeps. They emit sharp, metallic clicks to steer through the dimness and around the hanging rush-cords, skimming so low that their trailing whiskers carve twin silver lines across the shrinking water. They scoop midges by the mouthful and bank steeply toward the brick walls, raking their claws across the mortar joints to pluck seam-hunters straight off the terracotta. Down in the lower tiers of jars, the steady counterclockwise bass song breaks into an agitated, overlapping stammer; the acoustics muddy into a deafening, wet thunder that shakes loose extra beads of condensation from the ceiling.

Below, the ribbon-fish dart in flashing silver knots toward the sucking grate, while the broad lurkers surge half out of the thinning shallows to ambush anything swept down the tile-channels. For several minutes the empty volume of the chamber is knotted with wings, splashes, falling prey, and reverberating shrieks.

Then, around the perimeter bench, the hand-carved stone sluice-mouths choke, belch air, and begin pouring cold, clear water back across the floor. The black tile-ridges vanish again beneath advancing ripples. As the sheet rises, the lowest hanging clay weights touch the water, ringing outward in slow, neat circles that cross and cancel one another across the wide floor. One by one, the flyers spiral back up into the dim timber crown, folding their wet wings and crowding back into the beams. The midges thin out. The scraping along the walls slows.

Within minutes, the center of the chamber is empty again—just cold gray air, hanging cords, and a dim expanse of gently shuddering water—while out along the weeping, jar-lined curve of the dome, a single low vessel booms once, a neighbor answers a beat later, and the great circling roar picks up its pace and begins turning around the room once more.
