The air here is thin enough to make the chest work for every breath, and cold enough to turn exhaled air into brief pale plumes that vanish into a wider, wetter gray. You are very high up—so high that the cloud floor lies somewhere below the lowest visible rock ledges, a slow, bruised sea of vapor that swallows the drop without ever revealing a valley bottom. Above, the sky holds no sun and no clean stars, only a dim, low-hanging lid of slate and pewter luminance, barely bright enough to read the riveted seams of an iron girder at twenty paces. Yet the view opens out for miles. Across the wide, sunken expanse of the basin, colossal ridges of stone rise like the knuckles of a hand still pushing through wet clay, spaced far apart with vast halls of empty air between them.

The rock itself looks freshly broken from the mold. Nothing here has been softened by centuries of wind or ground down by ancient rivers. The cliffs shear upward in sharp, crystalline facets, dark basalt and greenish slate cleaved along clean planes that catch the dim light like wet glass. On the steeper faces, the stone is ribbed with regular, parallel ridges—neither quite natural strata nor quite quarried marks—where whole slabs of mountain are still inching upward out of the mist below. A ridge that clears a viaduct by twenty feet in one decade will scrape the underside of the ironwork in the next, forcing crews to jack the spans higher on fresh timber cribbing and bolted steel collars. In a few places along the rawest ledges, the geology has not quite finished deciding to be rock: the edge of a promontory thins into a seam of matte, featureless gray that eats sound and bends the outline of whatever passes behind it, as though the air were thick glass poured unevenly.

Across these vast, raw heights, a city has grown in scattered, heavily stacked islands. Rather than sprawling continuously, it gathers in dense, vertical knots wherever a shelf of stone can bear weight—six and seven stories of dark brick tenements, corrugated iron workshops, cast-concrete retaining walls, and soot-stained boiler houses piled tightly atop one another, laced with exterior catwalks, chutes, and steep iron stairs. Between one district and the next lie half-mile gulfs of open air and unweathered stone, bridged by long, thin viaducts, single-track suspension railways, and sagging curtains of thick black power cable strung from solitary lattice pylons. From a parapet on the western ridge, you can look across the wide basin and see seven or eight of these dense industrial settlements clinging to separate peaks and ledges, their tiny amber windows glowing faintly through the cool haze, each island densely built to its very cliff-edge yet surrounded by immense, echoing emptiness.

Every surface is slick. It rarely rains in proper sheets, yet moisture is constant, pulled straight out of the low cloud as it drifts slowly through the towers and trusses. Water beads along every copper insulator, darkens the brick mortar, collects in shallow oil-filmed pools across flat tarred roofs, and runs in steady threads down the freshly cleaved cliff faces. Where that dampness meets the faint, lingering warmth radiating from steam mains, foundry walls, and humming substations, plant life erupts in thick, dark abundance. Because the light is so low, the foliage is intensely pigmented—deep bottle-green, black-emerald, bruised plum—and slick with waxy sheen. Broad, rubbery leaves fan out from cracks in the raw rock; massive fronds of wet fern arch six feet high along the drainage troughs beside the rail spurs; heavy carpets of sodden moss swallow the bolts of bridge abutments and hang in dripping beards from the undersides of overhead cranes. Vines thick as wrist-ropes braid themselves around the ceramic insulators of the power pylons and drape across unused gantries, turning whole flanks of the older brick factories into shaggy, dripping terraces of green.

It is not a quiet place. Across the wide spaces between ridges, sound travels far and doubles back in odd, heavy layers. The dominant voice is mechanical and deliberate: deep inside the terraced turbine halls, immense flywheels turn at a measured, ponderous tempo, sending a low bass throb through the rock itself, a pulse you feel in the soles of your boots every three or four seconds. Distant steam presses vent with long, wet roars that roll slowly across the gorge. High-voltage cables buzz loudly in the damp air, spitting faint blue sparks where wet fern-tips brush too close, and the suspension wires of the long bridges groan in long, cello-like swells as the mountain plates beneath their anchorages shift by fractions of an inch. Somewhere on the northern cliff, a drop-forge strikes once every half-minute, and the echo returns from the opposite crag twice—first as a sharp metallic ring, and a few seconds later as a dull, wooden thud that belongs to no material visible on either slope.

Through all this iron, steam, and dripping greenery moves an astonishing weight of animal life. The high air between the districts is never empty for long. Hundreds of massive, broad-winged birds—dark-feathered, oily against the damp, with heavy hooked beaks and throats that inflate like leather bellows—wheel in slow, wide gyres between the smokestacks. They roost in dense, noisy rows along the crane booms and inside the open ironwork of the viaduct arches; their calls are low, resonant, brassy blasts that blend so closely with the factory horns and pressure valves that newcomers cannot tell when a shift is ending and when a flock is merely settling onto a warmer girder. Below them, along the wet stone ledges and the mossy ballast of the rail lines, move herds of thick-fleeced, long-legged grazers. They step with calm, unhurried precision across razor-edged rock and narrow iron walkways, cropping the waxy ferns and broad-leafed cliff cabbages. Their hides steam faintly in the cool air, and they carry a strong, wet-wool smell that cuts right through the coal smoke and hot grease.

Closer in, every warm niche is packed with smaller presences. Transformer housings and steam-pipe joints are crusted with overlapping colonies of pale, ribbed, many-legged climbers no bigger than a palm; they huddle tight against the vibrating metal and click their shells in continuous, cascading waves of dry percussion that rise and fall like applause whenever the electrical load shifts. In the shallow puddles collected on concrete ledges and flat factory roofs sit broad, flat-headed amphibians with dull bronze eyes, motionless for hours until a slow mist-beetle drops from the overhanging vines. Lean, dark-furred scavengers with huge nocturnal eyes crouch along the brick parapets and ride atop the slow-moving ore cars as they creep along the suspension tracks, hopping calmly off at the sorting yards to raid grease traps and lunch tins.

The longer you stand on a catwalk and watch the place work, the harder it becomes to keep the geometry honest in your head. Nothing here breaks violently; there are no frantic flashes or torn skies. Instead, the rules of space and matter seem to have loosened slowly, settling into habits that the city and the animals have simply accommodated.

Take the great funicular that descends from the ironworks on the upper eastern peak to the damp switching-yard across the mist. You can ride its cable car downward for twenty minutes, watching the raw cliff walls rise past the wet glass and listening to the brake drums grind steadily as you drop into cooler, denser haze. Yet when you step out onto the cinder platform of the lower yard and walk to the railing, you can look across the gulf and see the smokestacks of that very same eastern ironworks drifting *below* the level of your boots, their slow gray plumes curling upward past your knees. The rail crews pay it no mind; they check the oil levels on the winches and chalk their tallies onto slate boards whose numbers sometimes drift slightly out of alignment overnight.

The overlap extends to the land itself. From certain bridge spans, two separate ridges across the basin occupy the exact same stretch of dim horizon without colliding, their silhouettes semi-transparent where they cross, like two photographic plates laid over one another on a light table. Yet heavy flocks of the brass-voiced birds fly smoothly out of one cliff's mossy crevices and alight on the brick chimneys of the other without ever altering their glide. In the older industrial blocks—if "old" means anything where the rock beneath the foundations is still freshly extruded—iron support columns do not merely rest on stone footings; halfway down, the riveted steel loses its seams and grades seamlessly into black, unweathered basalt, as though the mountain had grown upward through the blueprint and decided to finish the casting itself.

Even the herds on the rail-beds carry a quiet, patient strangeness. Watch a line of the shaggy grazers file across a narrow steel truss bridge through the mist: count them from the lead bull back to the last calf and you will reach fourteen, then count them back from tail to head and arrive at seventeen, though none have stepped off the bridge and the gap between them has not changed. The shadows cast by the dim sodium lamps along the freight platforms do not always point away from the bulbs; two workers walking side by side in thick rubber coats may throw shadows that lean gently toward each other, mingling on the wet flagstones like puddles joining across uneven mortar. And in the wide, cool pockets of empty air halfway between the high districts, where no viaduct reaches and only power lines sag through the gloom, condensed droplets of cloud-water occasionally stop falling altogether, hanging motionless in dense silver curtains for ten or fifteen minutes at a time before drifting sideways in a single slow sheet to soak the hanging vines on the next cliff over.

Everything proceeds at this unhurried, heavy tempo. The great flywheels turn, sending their slow thud through wet iron and fresh rock. Mist gathers on dark leaves and drops steadily into black puddles. Freight cars groan along high cables above unseen cloud-seas, their lamps smearing dim gold across the damp steel. Huge birds hang motionless on the thick, strange updrafts without a single wingbeat, watching the steam rise from chimneys that are still, millimeter by patient millimeter, being lifted higher into the dim, cool sky.
