The sky sits low over the plateau, the color of wet furnace-ash, giving off a dim, directionless light that never sharpens into shadows. Up here, high above the invisible lowlands, the air is thin enough to make a steady walk feel labored, yet so hot and saturated that every breath goes down like scalding steam. Nothing burns openly, and there is no sun to blame; the heat simply rises out of the rock and hangs beneath the heavy cloud-lid, pressing down with the patient, smothering weight of a boiler room left running for centuries.

Spread across the whole basin of the high tableland—kilometers of stepped terraces, long flat avenues, and sunken yards—is a city made almost entirely of empty space.

From the upper rim, you can see straight through it. There are towers here, dozens of stories tall, arranged in strict municipal grids along broad, curbed boulevards, yet they have no walls, no floors, no rooms. They are architectural skeletons: four slender piers of sintered basalt and blackened iron, cross-braced at wide intervals with thin ceramic struts, enclosing vast vertical shafts of warm, dim air. Between one skeletal block and the next lie paved plazas half a kilometer wide, utterly bare save for a single low transformer plinth or a widely spaced row of slender lamp-standards. Viaducts cross other viaducts on legs so thin and far apart that the bridges seem to hang on wet mist alone. Huge spherical cooling-shells—each the size of a cathedral dome—sit in the lower districts with their sides cut away into wide louvered ribs, hollow dark cavities you can read right through to the blurred horizon of the plateau's far wall. It is unmistakably urban: there are storm drains, stepped flights of public stairs, numbered porcelain plaques bolted to corner piers, elevated rail beds, pedestrian landings, drainage kerbs. Yet the volume of solid matter per acre is vanishingly small, as if a dense industrial metropolis had been stretched outward until ninety-nine percent of it became void, leaving only its geometry behind.

Everything that does exist is streaming wet.

Because the rock beneath the plateau is fiercely hot and the high air is trapped under its dark cloud ceiling, water never rests here. It rises as invisible vapor through grated trenches cut into the lowest streets, climbs through the hollow towers, and condenses heavily on the upper ironwork and ceramic cross-beams—surfaces just a few degrees cooler than the breath rising from below. Fat, hot drops fall steadily from the skeletal ceilings onto the bare plazas, sizzling softly where they strike an unshaded transformer hood, spreading into thin, gleaming sheets across the gently dished paving tiles before draining back into the gutters and vanishing into the dark cisterns beneath. Nothing pools for long; the heat drinks it back up too quickly. Every stone surface wears a permanent, slick sheen, dark with moisture, reflecting the bruised slate of the sky.

Look closely at the masonry, though, and the water is not obeying gravity alone.

The entire city carries a low, quiet electrical charge—a steady direct current bled through copper-inlaid kerbs, overhead catenaries, and the riveted iron spines of the empty towers. Wherever hot, mineral-heavy condensate meets that voltage, the water creeps *upward*. Thin, glassy films climb the sides of basalt pillars against their own weight, drawn along the charged stone by slow electro-osmosis, pooling into trembling beads along the undersides of energized wires. Where positive and negative struts pass near one another, the wet current precipitates strange, brittle crusts out of the steam: delicate lacework of pale silica, iridescent bismuth-colored scale, and powdery turquoise verdigris that grows along the rivets like frost in an oven. In the dimness, these wet mineral blooms catch the faint light and make the hottest joints of the ironwork look rimed with ice.

There are no plants anywhere. No moss clings to the weeping curbs; no weed splits the miles of wet slate; nothing green has ever survived this dim, scalding twilight. The closest thing to flora lives only in the hottest drainage seams, where scalding runoff glides over blackened grates: slick, rubbery skins of ochre and charcoal slime, thin as wet paint, feeding directly on sulfur, iron, and heat. They do not look alive so much as like an oil slick that has learned to stay put.

Down at street level, walking the wide, empty avenues between the hollow frames, the age of the place makes itself felt in the polish of its surfaces. Nothing here is newly built, and nothing is collapsing. It has reached a long, weathered equilibrium. Centuries of hot, slightly acidic rain have rounded the corners off concrete steps and worn smooth channels down the centers of stone ramps; the cast-iron sluice gates and riveted girders have rusted down to a dense, dark hematite skin as hard and sealed as kiln glaze. Even thermal fractures do not linger—the same electrolytic mineral crust that frosts the struts slowly fills hairline cracks in the cistern walls and viaduct piers, healing the city's masonry at roughly the same pace the heat tries to split it. What was erected here has settled completely into its own physics, neither growing nor quite decaying.

For long stretches, the only sound is the hushed, damp cadence of water: the faint patter of drops falling through thirty meters of hollow scaffolding into deep tiled sumps, the whisper of sheet-runoff gliding into brass catch-basins, the occasional crisp *tick* of a long iron tie-rod adjusting to a shift in heat. Beneath all of it sits a hum so low and even that it barely registers in the ears at all—you feel it instead in the soles of your boots and the roots of your back teeth, the quiet pulse of buried induction coils and submerged substations working somewhere below the pavement.

Then the plateau exhales, and the quiet city moves.

There is no wind to announce it, only a sudden thickening of the heat. From the long iron grates running down the medians of the lower boulevards, dense banks of white, scalding mist begin to well upward, smelling strongly of wet chalk, hot copper, and faint sulfur. Within minutes, the vapor fills the avenues to waist-height, erasing the paving stones so the slender piers seem to wade in bright steam. As the warm front climbs into the hollow superstructures, the wet insulators high overhead begin to spit hair-thin halos of pale-blue corona light, barely visible against the dark sky.

Across a whole district at once, a slow mechanical wave wakes up. Bimetallic louvers built into the ribs of the huge hollow cooling-spheres begin to groan and pivot open, driven by nothing more than the expansion of their own layered metals. Farther down the slope, hidden counterweights descend inside hollow corner-columns with a muffled, rhythmic *shhh-clack*, opening sluice-gates in the sub-streets to take the sudden surge of condensate. Somewhere in the mist, a cistern float-switch reaches its mark and trips a relay; a minute later, a lone maintenance carriage—an open iron flatbed with no seats, no cab, only a heavy ceramic counterweight and a brass collector arm—glides silently out of the haze along an elevated monorail. Faint blue sparks hiss and die where its shoe wipes water off the live rail. It rolls smoothly past an empty concrete station platform, crosses a kilometer of open void on slender stilts, and disappears into the dim skeleton of the next quarter, leaving only a thin wake curling in the mist.

If you stand still long enough after the carriage has passed, and watch the vast empty spaces between the towers rather than the ground, you may eventually see something that is not a machine.

Life here is scarce enough to feel like a trick of heat-shimmer. Once in an hour, perhaps, a pale shape detaches itself from the underside of an upper viaduct and drifts out across two hundred meters of empty air. It is wider than a spread coat yet thin as oiled vellum—a translucent, diamond-shaped membrane supported on slender, glassy struts that look disturbingly like the city's own cross-bracing. It does not flap. It rides the rising thermal plumes inside the hollow towers, banking subtly on the warm currents, trailing six fragile, hair-thin legs that comb water droplets directly out of the mist. When the vapor thins, it settles silently back onto the shadowed underside of a high ceramic beam, folding itself flat against the wet mineral crust until it vanishes.

Closer to the ground, rarer still, are the small grazers that hide beneath the warm lips of the sunken transformer hatches. No larger than a cupped hand, their backs are armored in dull, kiln-pale plates grown from the very silica and calcium they scrape off the electrified gutters. They emerge only when the mist-tide ebbs and the avenues fall silent again, inching along the copper-inlaid kerbs with tiny wet clicks, leaving narrow clean trails through the turquoise mineral frost. If a distant shutter clacks shut, or a heavy drop strikes the puddle beside them, they clamp down hard against the hot stone and become indistinguishable from bolted junction caps.

At the far western edge of the basin, the gridded streets come to an abrupt end. There is no wall, no railing—only a long terrace of weathered stone piers and projecting iron sluices jutting out over nothing. Beyond the rim, the plateau drops away into a bottomless gulf of churned, dim vapor, lit from somewhere unimaginably far below by the faintest possible suggestion of dull amber warmth. From the ends of the city's drainage channels, steady streams of hot water spill out into the open air, breaking into silver plumes that evaporate completely before they have fallen a hundred meters, curling back upward as mist to drift over the edge and re-enter the avenues.

Looking back from the brink, the whole urban expanse lies open to the eye: miles of wet slate, nested wireframes of blackened iron, faint amber filament globes glowing weakly behind salt-fogged glass every half-kilometer, pale blue sparks prickling along the high wires where the steam rolls through. There is no trace of whoever laid out these avenues, numbered these piers, or poured these sweeping empty plazas. If there was once a purpose beyond the weather itself—something distilled from the rising vapor, some charge harvested and banked in the deep stone, some immense slow accounting kept in rising floats and turning louvers—it has long since folded seamlessly into the mountain's own heat. The city simply stands high in the dark, scorching and drenched, holding its vast hollow spaces open to the steam, letting the hot air pass through its bones and turn to water and rise again.
