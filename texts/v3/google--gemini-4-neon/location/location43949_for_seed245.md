The heat arrives as a damp press against the face, smelling of slaked lime, wet terracotta, and boiled iron. Above, the light is the color of soiled tallow—dim, sourceless, evenly smeared across a low haze that never clarifies into sky. Whether a sun burns somewhere behind that vapor, or whether the glow is merely the residual warmth of the ground itself reflected back from an unseen vault, there is no way to tell. The light is just bright enough to read the grain of wet clay at your feet, and just faint enough that anything half a mile off dissolves into warm pewter mist.

There is no dirt beneath you. There is no bedrock, no soil, no silt brought down by rivers. As far as the dimness permits sight, and far beyond it, the ground is laid.

It stretches out low and almost level, a continental floor of hand-struck brick, tamped marl, and broad slabs of dressed mudstone fitted together with pale, weeping mortar. Every edge is raw. Nothing here has had time to weather; the corners of the plinths are sharp enough to nick skin, the troweled seams between flagstones still carry the bright ridges of a blade drawn across them moments ago. In shallow depressions—acres wide, sunken only by the thickness of two or three courses of tile—hot water stands in flat, pewter-colored sheets. Steam lifts off them in thin, sluggish veils, too heavy with heat to rise far before curling back down.

The expanse is mostly empty. Across miles of slick, glistening pavement, structures rise only at wide intervals, isolated like dry-docked hulls or half-remembered monuments. A low curving retaining wall runs for four hundred paces, buttressed every ten yards by timber props, then ends abruptly in a toothed edge of staggered bricks waiting for a continuation. Farther out, a cluster of dome-like kilns squats in a mirror of ankle-deep water, their mud-plaster skins blistered and sweating. Beyond them, a single aqueduct of narrow, hand-molded arches steps out across the flats, carrying a trough of scalding trickle from nowhere into the haze, its farthest piers leaning at angles that should have brought them down.

Everything is made by hand, or by the ghost of hands. You see it everywhere: the shallow hollows of thumbs pressed into the frogs of bricks; the criss-cross weave of cordage impressed into damp plaster; the crooked adze-marks along the pale shoring beams; the coarse, hammered copper staples pinning cracked lintels together. Yet there is no timber-yard, no quarry, no reed-bed, no clay-pit. No plant grows anywhere on the plain. Not a tuft of sedge pokes through the wet joints, not a scrap of moss clings to the shaded cistern walls. Where green would be, there is only efflorescence—fragile, feathery blooms of white salt and sulfur-yellow crust that sprout along the damp masonry in minutes and dissolve back into brine when the mist thickens. In the hottest standing pools, an iridescent film occasionally skins the water, oily and slick, trembling at the threshold of being something alive without ever crossing it.

No animal moves here either. No insect ticks in the warmth, no bird crosses the murky air, no crab or worm disturbs the shallow channels. Even the water holds no swimming things. Yet the plain is strewn with the unmistakable residue of labor: a wooden float resting beside a heap of wet lime, its handle still slick; a plumb-bob of cast lead hanging from a knotted linen string, still swinging in a tight, dying circle; a shallow pan of gray clay slip whose surface carries fresh ripples, as if a heel just jarred the floor beside it.

And the place will not stay still.

Despite the hushed atmosphere—a heavy, kiln-room quiet broken mostly by small, intimate sounds—the landscape is in a state of violent, continuous upheaval. You only realize how turbulent it is when you watch a distant pier long enough, or when you look away from a courtyard and look back.

Half a mile across the steaming tiles, a terraced cistern is swelling upward out of the flat floor. Course after course of wet brick extrudes itself, Mortar beading bright along every joint as whole walls flex like wet leather, climb thirty feet into the dimness, buckle inward, and slump back into a steaming slurry of clay and broken tile—all in the span of twenty breaths, and almost entirely without noise. There is no roar of collapsing masonry, no grinding avalanche. The air swallows catastrophe the way wool swallows a bell. What reaches you across the hot water is only a muffled, sub-audible pressure against the eardrums, a faint wet *shuck* of sliding stone, and then the soft hiss of steam as the ruined heap drinks itself back into the pavement.

Even up close, the chaos is weirdly polite to the ear. A drainage channel beside your boot silently unzips along its mortar seam, widens into a steaming trench, sprouts three narrow stone bridges in rapid succession—each built in a different bond, one of herringbone brick, one of rough-hewn flags, one of woven mud-and-wattle—and then pinches shut again, leaving only a ridge of damp lime and a line of tiny popping bubbles. You hear the faint *tick-tick* of terracotta cooling too fast, the soft suck of wet mud parting from wood, the steady drip of hot condensation falling from an archway that was not overhead a minute ago.

It is not merely that things are being built and unbuilt at storm-speed. The rules that govern a wall, a measure, a straight line, are themselves coming undone.

Pick up a hanging plumb-line and let it settle: it hangs twenty degrees off the vertical, pointing stubbornly toward a low, windowless turret three hundred yards away. The bricks of the nearest wall have obeyed the plumb-line rather than gravity, stacked cleanly along that impossible tilt, while the puddles at its base obey a third rule entirely, pooling thick on one side of a level slab as though the floor were pitched like a roof. Step across the threshold of an open doorway standing alone on the wet flats, and the light shifts by a fraction of a shade; turn around, and the doorway is now bricked shut from the other side, its mortar dry and crusted with salt, while the wet footprints you left behind continue straight through the solid wall.

The weirdness speaks entirely in the dialect of primitive craft. Nothing shimmers with strange radiance; instead, knots tie themselves wrong. A hemp line lashed around two scaffold poles loops once, twice, and passes through the center of the wood without breaking the grain. A wooden level lies on a damp coping stone, its trapped air-bubble split into four separate beads that drift in opposite directions whenever you breathe near it. Scratched into the wet clay of a retaining wall is a column of tally-marks—four strokes crossed by a fifth, repeated down the face—and if you trace them with a finger, the number of arches in the aqueduct behind you quietly adjusts itself to match your count, an extra span easing out of the mist with a soft sigh of dripping water.

Time itself refuses to cure evenly here. One end of a long parapet is still liquid slip, sagging between its wooden shuttering boards; ten paces along the same unbroken course, the clay is leather-hard; ten paces further, it is vitrified, glazed dark purple by kiln-heat that left no ash and no scorch marks on the timbers still braced against it. Water behaves with the same fractured memory. Condensation runs upward along certain pillars in narrow, trembling beads, gathering under the capitals before breaking free and drifting off as warm fog. Elsewhere, rain falls in brief, localized curtains—never from clouds you can see, only out of the featureless dimness overhead—scalding at first, tasting of chalk and copper, vanishing before it cools the tiles.

Because the plain is vast enough to hold its own weather, these instabilities gather into slow, continental fronts. Far off on the horizon, where the dim ochre air bruises to a darker soot-gray, an entire district is in the grip of a building-squall. You cannot hear it, except as a low thrumming felt through the soles of your shoes, like the distant thump of a mallet driven into wet earth. Through the haze, shapes rise and erase one another in rapid, overlapping drafts: stepped pyramids of unfired mud, sprawling labyrinths of waist-high stalls, immense vaulted roofs that dome up like blisters and burst into drifting curtains of wet plaster dust. Wind rarely blows across the flats, yet when one of those distant storms turns inward and unmakes a few square miles of brickwork at once, a warm, sluggish draft rolls out across the basins, smelling sharply of freshly broken clay and hot river-stone, ruffling the shallow sheets of water into intersecting grids of ripples that linger far longer than ripples should.

Walk long enough across this endless, low construction, and you begin to sense that the ground is not resting on anything at all. Here and there, where two shifting sections of pavement have pulled apart and not yet been mortared back together, a narrow seam opens—no wider than a handspan, raw-edged and steaming. Peer down into it and you do not see earth, nor water, nor darkness. You see only the undersides of other bricks, other damp laths and knotted cords, faintly lit by the same tallow glow, fitted together at angles that cannot meet the floor you stand on, dripping hot water downward—or upward—into a dimness that smells of fresh lime.

Then the slabs ease back together with a quiet, wet kiss. The seam closes. A bead of pale mortar squeezes up along the join, smooths itself flat as though stroked by an unseen thumb, and begins to steam in the heavy, patient heat.
