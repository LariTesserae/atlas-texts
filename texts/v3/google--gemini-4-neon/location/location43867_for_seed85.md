The first thing you notice is that the cold does not move. It sits against the skin at an even, snowmelt chill, identical near the jagged stone floor and forty meters higher among the upper arches. There are no warm pockets rising from below, no drafts sliding off the walls, no current to turn your body if you let yourself hang loose. The water is stripped of everything that usually clouds a deep place—no silt, no suspended ash, no pale snow of dead organisms, not a single thread of algae or green veil. It is so pure that, were there light enough, you could see clear across the whole basin.

There is almost no light.

Only after minutes of stillness do the shapes detach themselves from the dark. Far overhead, where the water ends against the thin sky, filtered starlight slips through in narrow, lensed bands and casts pale silver-indigo webs across the upper roofs and terraced slabs. In any ordinary sea those caustics would shiver and fold; here they do not move at all. They lie across the dark masonry like frost-etched lines on black glass, fixed in place because nothing above them ripples. Lower down, where the starlight fails, the settlement marks itself with pinpricks of dim violet and bruised amber set flush into corners, landings, and thresholds—never bright enough to throw a shadow, only enough to measure the empty dark between one presence and the next.

Beneath the settlement lies a mountain saddle so newly broken it still looks wet with fracture. The bedrock is black volcanic glass and raw, split basalt, thrust up in knife-edged fins and sharp conchoidal scars. Not a single corner has been rounded. If you drift low and brush a palm against a ridge, the edge is crisp enough to score skin. There is no sand in the clefts, no mud in the hollows, no lichen or velvet of marine growth softening the seams. Nothing green has ever taken hold here, and nothing seems to decay into anything that could feed it. Every rock face looks as though it were cleaved an hour ago and immediately sealed in cold glass.

Rising from and hovering above those sharp crags is a small, orderly district—no larger than a hamlet, or a cluster of cathedrals spaced wide apart. It is unmistakably urban, yet oddly spacious, built in three dimensions with generous gulfs of empty black water left between every structure. Slender, matte-dark towers rise thirty or forty meters without taper, their corners machined to the same severity as the rock beneath them. Between them hang layered civic terraces, narrow footbridges that break off cleanly into open water, transit rings aligned along invisible axes, and long colonnades whose flat roofs hold nothing but frozen strands of starlight.

The buildings do not seem to respect weight. Several wide, multi-tiered halls hang twenty meters clear of the fractured saddle beneath them, tethered only by two or three hair-thin dark filaments—or by nothing visible at all, locked cleanly to coordinates in the cold fluid. Their surfaces show no mortar, no rivets, no vents, no sign of toolwork or repair. Run a hand along a wall and it feels dry even underwater, strangely slick, neither stone nor metal, absorbing the faint indigo glints rather than reflecting them. Inside the open galleries, rooms stand flooded and immaculate: low benches, numbered plinths, recessed alcoves holding shelves of pale, unmarked plates, staircases that wind upward through empty dark and end at flat landings with no railing. Everything has the crispness of recent completion, yet nothing stirs to indicate anyone just left.

Moving through the streets reveals the quieter rules of the place. As you sink from the high colonnades toward the narrow basalt floor, your ears never tighten; the pressure here refuses to stack with depth, holding steady and gentle from top to bottom. When you kick forward, the water parts cleanly and closes behind you without swirl—turbulence dies out a handspan from your heels, as if the liquid remembers stillness too strongly to stay disturbed. Cross certain broad avenues between the terraces and your vision briefly snags: the distant amber markers jump a fraction of a degree sideways, and the sharp line of a tower bends for half a second before snapping straight again, as though you have just swum across an invisible seam where two slightly mismatched slices of space were stitched flush.

In the wider public plazas, shallow rectangular basins have been cut directly into the terrace floors. Each holds a second liquid, heavier and even clearer than the water around it—a pooled, silent lens resting beneath the water with its own mirror-flat boundary. A dim pinprick light from a corner pylon reflects cleanly off these sunken pools, creating tiny inverted ceilings underfoot. Near the archive vaults lower on the ridge, heavy mineral doors sit flush in the rock with no handles or hinges; if you strike one sharply, it rings dull and hard as granite, yet if you press an open palm against it slowly and hold still, the stone yields like cold gelatin, parting smoothly around a shoulder and sealing seamlessly behind without a bubble or a scrape.

The silence is almost complete, heavy and close against the eardrums. Underwater, sound ought to carry from far away, but here vibrations seem to wear out quickly in the damped cold. Minutes pass with nothing at all—only the faintest pressure-hum that is not quite audible, felt instead behind the teeth whenever you drift within arm's length of a civic anchor-pylon. Every so often, from somewhere high near the roof of the water, a single tiny *tick* travels down—sharp and pure as a needle touching quartz—where the containment adjusts infinitesimally against the thin air outside. Once in a long while, a dry microscopic click rises from the raw rock below.

That click belongs to the few living things that share this basin. There are no schools of fish, no drifting clouds of larvae, no swaying forests to shelter them. Life here is rare, solitary, and stripped of hurry. Down along the razor-crested basalt, pale, spindly crawlers no longer than a finger pick their way across the glassy facets on needle-fine legs. They never swim; they step with uncanny precision from crystal edge to crystal edge, pausing for long stretches to scrape trace minerals from the unweathered stone with pale, scissor-like mouthparts.

Higher up, along the thin anchor-filaments and the dim edges of the suspended terraces, drift long translucent ribbons of living tissue—half a meter long, jointed like spun glass, faint enough to vanish completely unless they cross in front of an amber beacon. They do not hunt or flee. They anchor one tapered tip near the faint electrostatic bleed of a pylon and hang motionless for hours, drinking whatever whisper of field-energy leaks from the architecture, occasionally sending a slow, pale blue pulse down their length that dims before it reaches the tail.

Rarer still, if you wait long enough in the open dark between the upper towers, something wider passes through the negative space: a flat, chalk-pale mantle two meters from wingtip to wingtip, blind and featureless, folded gently at its margins. It glides across the empty avenue without beating, without stirring the water behind it, as if its own skin obeys the basin's law against wakes. In twenty heartbeats it crosses the dim light of the star-caustics and is swallowed again by the dark toward the perimeter.

Swim outward from the plazas for five or six quiet minutes and the sparse towers thin behind you until you reach the edge of the world.

There is no wall of glass. No bulkhead, no cliff, no dam. The water simply ends.

Push a hand forward and it meets a cool, smooth, elastic resistance—yielding half an inch under your palm like a taut membrane of nothing at all, faintly slick, humming almost imperceptibly in the bones of your wrist. Beyond it is no longer water, only the thin, freezing black air of extreme altitude.

Through that darkened, optically dimmed skin, the true position of the hamlet finally declares itself. The razor-sharp basalt ridge that cradles the settlement does not descend into an abyssal plain; it drops cleanly away on every side into miles of empty night. Far below, barely distinguishable from the dark, sheer mountain flanks plunge downward into a veil of distant, unmoving cloud-tops, while above and around, crisp, unblinking stars hang in cold thin air. The entire drowned district—its silent plazas, its hovering halls, its immaculate fractured crags, its rare pale drifters—is a single bounded volume of motionless cold sea pinned to a high mountain crown, holding itself together in the dark, untouched by wind, untouched by seasons, waiting in cool equilibrium for nothing that ever arrives.
