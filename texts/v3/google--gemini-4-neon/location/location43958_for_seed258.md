The first thing that meets you is the noise, and the second is the emptiness.

You are high up—high enough that the air feels thin in the lungs despite its steady warmth, and at the outer rim of the basin, where the terraced floor ends in a clean lip, the pale tops of clouds drift lower than your knees. Between those distant rims stretches a valley several miles across, open to a bright, ordinary sky. Clear daylight pours down without haze or glare, lighting up an immense expanse of pale, wet green.

There is no stone here, and no soil. Every yard of ground is a continuous, deliberate pavement of living plant-matter. Broad hexagonal and rectangular plates of firm, waxy tissue—the color of bleached jade and new reed-pith—fit together across the valley in dead-level terraces, stepped down in crisp right-angled drops and joined by straight, sunken seams of darker cambium. Nothing grows *on* the floor; the floor *is* the plant, laid out with the severe, sweeping geometry of a plaza meant for a capital city that was abandoned in the first hour of its sprouting.

Across this vast, glistening expanse, structures rise far apart from one another, separated by hundreds of yards of bare, open pavement. It is a remarkably sparse place. From any terrace you can see straight across the basin to the far rim, taking in the whole scattered arrangement at once: here a half-circle of smooth, bone-pale columns standing in a shallow sheet of water; a quarter-mile away, a broad stair leading up to an unfinished platform whose cantilevered ribs stop cleanly in midair; farther still, tall hollow towers pierced with neat oval apertures, and low rectilinear basins brimming with clear runoff. The space between these presences is wide, airy, and uncrowded—nothing tangles, nothing encroaches, no stray vine ever dares to cross a walkway.

And yet nothing here was cut, laid, or fastened with tools. Get down on one knee and examine a corner where a wall meets the terrace floor, and you will find no chisel marks, no mortar, no woven lashing, no trace of metal or handcraft. The right angle is a biological weld—millions of turgid plant cells instructed to divide strictly along orthogonal planes, forming smooth, seamless fillets of pale callus tissue. The stairs are ridged root-stock whose tops bear a fine, velvety skin of microscopic hairs to grip a wet foot. The drainage channels are not carved; they are folded directly into the epidermis of the floor, lined with hydrophobic scales that shear sheets of water cleanly away from the raised causeways. Whatever intelligence conceived this geometry did not build with hammers; it wrote architecture into sap and meristem, and let the valley extrude itself whole.

What makes the place deeply unsettling is that it never finished—and never moves on.

Every single structure in the basin is poised in the exact posture of early emergence. Arches curve upward from opposing piers and terminate two feet short of meeting, their severed ends capped in glossy, swollen buds of translucent pale-green tissue—raw growth-tips that look five minutes old, taut with the pressure of imminent division. Wall-leaves stand unfurled to thirty degrees, stiff and crisp as freshly snapped celery. Latticework vaults stop mid-pattern, their exposed ribs gleaming damply in the sun. Look closely at any edge and you see the sharp, glassy margin of embryonic flesh, untouched by a single nick, crack, or blister. There is no weathering. There is no dead wood, no shed leaf, no puddle of brown silt, no smudge of mold or lichen anywhere in the warm, wet air. In a climate this humid and mild, everything should be choking in rot and secondary growth; instead, the entire valley is held in an immaculate, unchanging equilibrium—a miles-wide embryo frozen permanently on the morning of its first cell-burst, neither ripening nor decaying.

If you drag a fingernail hard across a low parapet, leaving a pale groove, clear fluid immediately beads along the score. Come back an hour later and the groove is gone—not scarred over, not calloused, merely restored to the exact smooth contour it held before you touched it. The stasis here is active, stubborn, and quiet.

Everything else is loud.

Beneath the broad living floor runs a pressurized vascular system of monumental scale, pumping immense volumes of water upward from unseen depths beneath the plateau. The taut, turgid plates of the pavement act like the soundboard of a drum; even standing still in the middle of an empty plaza, you feel a steady, low-frequency thrumming travel up through your ankles. Above ground, the water escapes everywhere. Warm air condenses continuously on the cooler vascular skin of the architecture, and far more water is forced directly out through pores, seams, and unfinished growth-tips in heavy, tireless guttation.

Every scattered structure is an involuntary hydraulic instrument. Water sheets in thick, glass-clear curtains down the fluted sides of half-grown walls, hammers without pause into taut, drum-tight leaf-basins, and pours into the open mouths of hollow vascular chimneys. Those tall, perforated stalks catch the rushing torrents and turn them into sound—a deep, resonant, wet booming layered with reedy, pitched whistles wherever the falling streams compress air through the oval vents. Because the valley is so open and the surfaces so smooth and turgid, the sound carries effortlessly from terrace to terrace, overlapping into a continuous, bright, reverberant roar. There is no wind to shift it, no weather to swell or hush it. It is the same volume at every hour, a warm acoustic flood that fills the empty spaces between the scattered pavilions until silence feels like something from another world. To speak beside someone requires shouting into their ear; after an hour, the roar settles into the bones like the sound of blood inside a shell.

Under your feet, a thin, unbroken film of water glides across the pale hexagons, constantly replenished and constantly draining toward the straight spillways that drop from terrace to terrace in neat silver ribbons. The water is lukewarm, faintly sweet, and startlingly clean—carrying dissolved minerals and trace sugars straight from the plant's internal circulation rather than washed-out dirt. Because the daylight stays steady and clear, the wet architecture shines everywhere, turning the open valley into a bright, glaring study in pale greens, translucent whites, and moving silver.

Animal life is scarce, and what exists here feels carefully edited to fit the rules of a place that never rots. There are no scavengers, no gnats hovering over stagnant pools, no burrowing things breaking the floor. You can walk a half-mile across the thrum of the plazas without seeing anything move except falling water.

Then, far off across a gleaming terrace, you catch sight of a thin vertical shadow that does not belong to a column. It is a tall, pale-ochre grazer, built almost entirely of long, smooth legs and a narrow, keel-shaped torso, stepping with slow, deliberate precision across the flooded pavement. Its feet are wide, pale rings of cartilage perforated with open holes so they pass through the film of water without splash or suction. Moving from one weeping arch to the next, it lifts a slender, trough-like lower jaw to catch the heavy, metronomic streams of sweet exudate pouring from the frozen meristematic tips. Its flanks quiver faintly in sympathetic vibration with the booming floor—or perhaps it navigates entirely by the unchanging standing waves of sound that map the valley like invisible walls. It pauses, drinks for minutes on end, and walks on into the bright distance until its thin silhouette vanishes behind a distant colonnade.

Closer in, if you crouch beneath the lip of a half-formed lintel where the mist hangs thickest, you may find small, translucent snails with shells like clouded quartz clinging to the waxy ceiling, rasping away the microscopic film of sugar-water without ever marring the plant-skin beneath. Now and then, high in the bright, steady air between the outer rim and the tallest acoustic chimneys, a pair of broad, skin-winged gliders—pale as wet parchment, completely silent—bank in wide, effortless circles on the warm updrafts rising off the sunlit floor, never flapping, never landing, drifting across the vast open gulf like scraps of the same unfinished blueprint carried loose on the air.

Stand near the center of the valley, on one of the widest empty terraces, and the scale of the place settles over you all at once. Around you, spaced wide across miles of warm, bright daylight, the embryonic bones of a grown city gleam wetly and pour water into the air. Beneath your boots, the living pavement hums with the relentless transit of unseen rivers. A single heavy drop swells on the translucent green tip of an arch overhead, catches the clear sun, detaches, and strikes the taut floor with a hollow *tok* immediately swallowed by the grand, unvarying roar of the basin. Another identical drop swells in its place. Nothing will ever finish the arch; nothing will ever wear it down. The valley simply holds its breath, wet and bright and deafening, forever poised at the edge of becoming.
