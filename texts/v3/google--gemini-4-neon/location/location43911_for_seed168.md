At this elevation, the sky is a deep, clean blue that holds no cloud and no haze, and the sunlight comes down hard and clear, the familiar white-gold of a bright noon that never seems to tilt. Below it lies a wide, stepped basin carved out of newly uplifted rock—miles of broad mineral benches dropping in clean, angular terraces toward a flat central floor, bounded on the far side by fault-scarps so sharp their silhouettes look cut with shears.

Nothing here has been worn down. The rock is raw and freshly broken, its cliff faces gleaming with flat cleavage planes, jagged quartz seams, and razor-edged fractures that have never met rain, frost, or blowing grit. Angular slabs of dark slate and pale pegmatite sit exactly where they cracked loose during the plateau’s rise, balanced on crisp points, dusted only with a dry, crystalline flour. There is no soil, no mud, no dry riverbed cutting a furrow down the valley. Even the air feels completely wrung out—thin, warm, and so transparent that ridges ten miles off show every individual fissure.

Yet the valley is lush.

Across the stepped benches and sweeping down onto the basin floor, vegetation flourishes in broad, rich swaths of deep graphite-green, oxidized teal, and warm bronze. From a high ledge, the growth looks abundant enough to rival an upland meadow or a terraced orchard: wide canopies of corrugated fronds, tiered thickets of branching stems, low velvety mats crowding the pale mineral ledges. It is only as the eye adjusts to the bright, unmoving light that the strangeness of the spacing comes clear.

Nothing touches.

Even in the thickest stands, every frond holds a crisp, uniform margin of empty air between itself and its neighbor. Tall, slender stalks rise straight out of bare rock fissures, unfurling wide parasol-like leaves that hover cleanly apart, never tangling, never brushing, leaving vast hollows of bright warm light beneath and between them. Across the wider basin, whole colonies of foliage sit separated by broad aprons of gleaming, bare stone. The landscape feels simultaneously luxuriant and hollowed out—a vast volume of still, empty air occupied by delicate, wide-spaced presences.

Walk down onto one of the middle terraces, and the reason makes itself felt in the fine hairs on the back of your hand. The air carries a dry, steady charge. It smells faintly of warm wax, heated pennies, and the clean metallic tang of ozone—the scent of an electrical room left humming in full sun.

There is no water here for plants to drink, and no wet sap inside them. Instead, the freshly fractured bedrock beneath the plateau acts as an immense dry battery. Strained quartz veins, layered sulfide beds, native copper seams, and lithium-rich strata lie freshly exposed and unweathered, baking under constant light above a warm, deep-seated thermal gradient. Steady direct current moves naturally along the mineral faults, and the flora of this basin evolved to feed on the voltage itself.

Up close, the leaves are unmistakably botanical—veined, lobed, faintly fibrous, shedding papery outer husks as they mature—yet their anatomy follows the logic of industrial refinement. The broad upper surfaces of the canopy fronds are dusted with a matte, powdery sheen of photovoltaic selenium and dark graphitic pigment, angled precisely to the light. Beneath, their midribs and branching veins are drawn threads of pure copper and pale silver, gleaming softly through translucent, waxy skin. Where terrestrial plants pump water up from the ground, these circulate dry ionic salts and thick, warm, non-aqueous electrolytes through hollow hexagonal stems.

Because they carry live current, proximity is lethal. A brushing leaf would arc and scorch. So the plants grow by strict electrical etiquette, sensing the field of every nearby stem and halting their outward reach a finger’s breadth short of contact. The result is an uncanny botanical architecture: miles of lush, feathery, interlocking silhouettes separated everywhere by narrow ribbons of bright, empty daylight.

The scale of what they do becomes plain wherever a fresh rock ledge drops away into shadow. Hanging down into the dry, echoing clefts beneath the newly lifted slabs are long, lacy curtains of pale root-filaments. They do not clutch at earth; they suspend themselves in the empty air of the fissures, bridging opposing faces of raw ore. Slow, dry electrolysis runs through them continuously. Dry acidic exudates at the root-tips dissolve raw mineral faces molecule by molecule; the current draws the loosened ions down the filaments, stripping them clean and plating them out according to voltage potential.

Beneath every lush terrace, the unweathered stone is tiled with the clean output of this living refinery.

Under one sweeping band of bronze-fronded scrub, thin, mirror-bright wafers of pure electrodeposited copper peel silently off the lower bark and stack in neat, gleaming shingles along the rock lip. Across a fault line, where the bedrock shifts to pale spodumene and sulfur-veined shale, a broad carpet of low, mossy foliage exudes crisp drifts of snowy white carbonate and bright, unmixed sulfur crystals—sorted so cleanly by chemistry that the valley floor reads from above like a map of raw industrial feedstocks. Nothing is smeared or mixed by runoff. Every pile lies dry, sharp-edged, and pure, accumulating in slow geometric aprons at the foot of the ledges.

Closer to the center of the basin, the plants take on forms that feel almost mechanized, though no tool has ever touched them. Thick, fluted stems—gray-black and smoothly insulated in hard resinous bark—grow straight along the main fault lines, fusing end-to-end into long, branching conduits that equalize voltage across miles of valley floor. From their upper nodes hang plump, ribbed gourds the color of old amber: natural capacitors, taut with stored direct current, their leathery skins slightly warmer than the surrounding air. Beside them rise clusters of tall, slender stalks topped with interlocking metallic bracts. Inside each bloom sits a tight bimetallic coil and a dry, pressurized spore-cylinder. There are no insects here to carry pollen, no birds to eat fruit, no animals of any kind to brush past a seedpod and drag it away. In fact, there is not a single animal anywhere in the basin—no wing-shadow crossing the bright stone, no burrow, no skittering claw, no thread of silk between the stalks.

Reproduction belongs entirely to charge and tension. When the subtle internal pressure and voltage of a mature cylinder reach exact equilibrium, a tiny metallic latch gives way with a single crisp *tick*. A measured puff of dry, conductive powder jets out into the still air, each grain carrying the same electrostatic charge as its parent stalk, repelled outward in a slow, silent halo until it settles on an uncolonized fracture yards away.

Look up from the fronds and sweep your gaze across the entire high valley, and the mind keeps trying to find the builders. The order is too vast, too clean, too unmistakably productive. The bands of lush metallic greenery follow the ore seams with surveyed precision; the stepped mineral terraces gleam with sorted heaps of refined tin, copper foil, white salts, and yellow sulfur; the thick conduit-roots trace straight lines across the open hollows; the air itself hums with scaled, electrified work. You look automatically for a retaining wall, a service track, a glass insulator, a distant chimney, or at least the foundation of some abandoned works.

There is nothing. Not a cut stone, not a welded seam, not a single artifact or footprint anywhere between the jagged horizons. The whole immense operation is raw geology and wild flora locked in an untouched marriage.

And it is almost completely silent.

With no wind to stir the fronds, no water to trickle, no engines burning fuel, and no living throat to call across the terraces, the basin holds a vast, ringing stillness. Stand motionless on a warm slate ledge and the quiet deepens until the smaller sounds surface from beneath it: a faint, sub-audible hum felt more in the jawbone and teeth than in the ears, radiating from the big resin-sheathed conduit stems; the rare, glass-sharp *tick* of a cooling metal flake detaching from a leaf margin and falling onto bare stone; a whisper-thin crackle of corona static where a sharp quartz point leaks excess charge into the dry sky. Minutes pass between each sound, and each one hangs cleanly in the hollow, high-altitude air before vanishing without an echo.

Above all, the place feels held in a tireless, motionless balance. The warmth never climbs to scorching and never drops to chill; the radiant heat of the sun matches the gentle resistive warmth of the stems and the steady loss into the thin blue sky. The newly emerged rock does not crumble, because nothing wears it down. The lush, wide-spaced canopies do not sway, do not wilt, do not rush. Current flows smoothly out of the freshly split mountain, rises through miles of copper-veined leaves, plates another micron of gleaming metal onto the ungathered heaps below, and returns to the dark bedrock—a vast, sun-lit, unbuilt engine idling forever in the quiet, dry height of the world.
