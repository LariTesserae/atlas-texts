The first wrongness is the light.

You are buried—sealed entirely beneath an unmeasured weight of living mass, with no shaft, no skylight, no gap to any surface—yet the space around you is full of clear, ordinary afternoon daylight. Not the sickly green glow of luminous fungus, not the dim wash of light filtered through leaves, but plain, crisp, slightly warm daylight, bright enough to pick out every frayed cellulose fiber on the floor and throw hard, dark shadows across the pale walls. There is no source for it. Look straight up and there is only a taut, arched ceiling of pale green-white membrane ribbed with thicker stalks, seamlessly closed. Yet the light falls at an unmistakable slant, as if a high window stood open just beyond your peripheral vision.

The second wrongness is the noise.

A sealed pocket underground ought to be muffled. This place roars. Behind the thin, translucent walls, fluid is moving in staggering volume—a deep, booming, rhythmic surge that hits every few seconds like heavy surf breaking behind a locked wooden door. Between surges comes a constant layered racket: the wet, sucking rasp of gigantic sheaths sliding against one another somewhere outside the cavity; the shrill, papery hiss of thousands of microscopic pores flexing open along the upper ridges; and, at unpredictable intervals, a crack like a rifle shot as a fresh seam of living tissue tears open an inch wider. You cannot hear your own footsteps over the pulse of the walls. To speak here is to shout into a drum.

There is no stone in this place. There is no dirt, no sand, no bedrock, no silt. Every surface is plant—not plants growing *upon* a floor, but living vegetable architecture forming the entire substance of reality. The floor beneath your feet is freshly exposed pith, pale ivory-ochre and faintly springy, scored with razor-clean vertical ridges where it recently split away from what used to sit against it. The walls rise in smooth, sweeping curves of taut vascular bundles and layered fibrous ribs, green-white near the bottom and deepening to a translucent jade higher up, like the inside of a monstrous, freshly snapped stem.

Nothing here is old. Nothing has rotted, slumped, or softened into humus. Every edge where one layer peels back from another is crisp as newly folded parchment, sharp enough to slice a knuckle. The smell is warm, raw, and startlingly bright—cut celery, crushed willow bark, heated grass, and the faint metallic tang of fresh sap.

By any plain glance, the cavity is small: a hidden pocket, an intimate subterranean room perhaps six paces from wall to wall, tucked away inside a vast buried body of growth. You can take in the whole chamber without turning your head. Yet the interior is shockingly hollow—stripped of clutter, bare of vines or thickets or fallen litter—and that emptiness does not behave. There are only a handful of distinct presences in the entire volume, and the air between them feels thinned out and impossibly wide. Start walking from one curved wall to the opposite rib and the room stays close at your elbows while your stride eats up distance that should not fit inside it. A single sharp snap at one edge echoes back as though crossing an empty warehouse, or a dry canyon folded inside a gourd.

The warmth here is the steady, pressing warmth of a greenhouse or a feverish palm, never quite muggy, never truly dry. It runs on a cycle driven by the hydraulic boom behind the walls. For several minutes the air stays hot and crisp, vibrating with dry friction as the chamber subtly narrows—the curved ribs bowing inward by a finger's width, groaning as the plant tries to heal its own hollow shut. Then the deep thump doubles in speed. The translucent walls flush a richer, darker green as pressure floods the vessels just beneath the skin. With a sudden collective *shhhhk*, the upper ridges flare open their stomata and sweat.

It is not a true rain, just an abrupt, warm beading across one half of the ceiling and walls. Heavy clear droplets gather along the razor-sharp flutes, race downward in bright silver lines, and spill into narrow, freshly split troughs along the floor's perimeter. The air thickens with green steam for sixty seconds, then the pressure drops, the pores snap closed, and the warm draft drinks the dampness right back up. Within minutes the pith is merely cool to the touch, the wet seams drying down to a faint chalky crust of mineral salts until the next pulse.

It is during the quietest part of that cycle—still loud, just lowered from a roar to a heavy, pulsing hum—that the broken rules of the place become hardest to ignore.

Watch a bead of moisture trembling on an overhead rib. A sharp, resonant *tok* rings out from the damp trough below—loud as a pebble dropped in an empty bowl—half a second *before* the droplet actually lets go and falls. When it strikes the wet pith, it lands in total silence.

The shadows do not agree with the room. Your own shadow stretches long and neat across the pith floor, angling away from that nonexistent afternoon sun—except when you raise your hand, the shadow's hand lifts a beat too late, or bends at a joint your wrist does not have. Near the far wall, a thin, pale shadow of a tall, wind-tossed stalk sways lazily back and forth across the smooth green rib, even though nothing stands in front of the wall and there is no wind here at all. Once in a while, a fresh fibrous crack splits open along the curve with a deafening gunshot report, flashes bright wet yellow-green along its raw interior, and then seamlessly zips closed in reverse—only to appear, already split and weeping clear beads of sap, a foot to the left.

No one has built anything here. There are no cut chambers, no stacked supports, no woven roofs, no hearths or paths. The pocket is a raw biological rupture, untouched by architecture.

Yet it is not untouched by hands.

Driven deep into three of the vertical seams where the walls strain hardest to grow back together are crude, unmistakable wedges: fire-hardened billets of dense heartwood and thick splinters of pale bone, battered flat on their exposed butts by heavy pounding. They are jammed sideways across the fresh splits, groaning loudly under the lateral pressure of the living walls, holding this small hollow pried open against the plant's relentless urge to close. Around a severed vascular nub near the floor, someone has tied a length of braided bast-fiber cord with a neat, tight slip-knot. The cord stretches all the way across the empty air to a notched peg hammered into the opposite seam. Because the walls never stop trembling, the taut fiber vibrates constantly, droning out a low, clear, continuous bass note that threads through the hydraulic roar.

Near the center of the barren pith floor—isolated by too much empty space—lies a single hand-tool: a leaf-shaped flake of pale grey chert, roughly knapped along one edge and lashed with dark, glossy resin and dried plant-sinew to a short grip of curved root. Beside it curls a single pale ribbon of freshly shaved cambium. The shaving is still wet at its cut edge, green and supple, never wilting, caught permanently a few seconds after the blade passed through it. Look closely at the stone head of the tool and your eyes ache; its grey mineral edges look faintly soft, almost smudged, as if this pure-plant world cannot quite maintain the idea of rock and keeps trying to resolve the flint into hardened shell or dry thorn.

Animals are scarce here. You can sit through ten pressure-cycles and see nothing move except the flexing walls, the trembling cords, and the stray shadow of a stalk that does not exist.

When life does appear, it comes through the seams like a spark from flint.

A pressure-surge forces a thick, glistening blister of sweet amber-white sap out from beside one of the wooden wedges. Almost immediately, a hairline crack below the wedge widens by a millimeter and a solitary crawler slips out—flat as a pressed seed, translucent white, legs thin as hairs. Its tiny claws click startlingly loud against the resonant pith as it hurries toward the sap-bead, mandibles working furiously. High above, clinging to the humming bast-cord where it looks indistinguishable from a curled sliver of torn bark, something else unhooks itself. It drops without a flap or a cry—except that its impact on the floor cracks loudly through the hollow like a dropped stick. A dry-shelled, long-legged ambusher, pale green and razor-jointed, folds over the sap-crawler in one stiff jerk, hauls it backward toward the wedged seam, and vanishes into the fibrous gap. The seam shifts a fraction of an inch with a wet creak, and the floor is empty again.

Rarer still are the ones who own the wedges.

They are almost never seen in the full glare of the sourceless daylight. Mostly you know them by an interruption in the rhythm: a sudden, deliberate *thock—thock—thock* echoing from behind a wall before any breach opens, striking out of time with the vascular pulse. If you happen to be there during the dry trough between pressure surges, a vertical rib near the floor may peel back with a wet tearing sound, revealing a dark, tight crease that leads nowhere sensible. Through it squeezes a solitary figure—pale, hunched, broad-knuckled, with ears pressed flat against its skull against the chamber's roar and eyes narrowed to wet, glossy slits. It carries no light and wears nothing but a sling of knotted reed-cord across its shoulder holding a smooth river-pebble, a bundle of charred pegs, and a bone awl.

It does not linger in the wide, stretched emptiness of the middle floor. Moving with quick, economical gestures, it checks the groaning wedges along the seam, sets a fresh wooden pin into a narrowing crack, and drives it home with three sharp blows of the pebble. Each blow rings out twice—once from the stone, once a half-beat earlier from the opposite wall. It bends to scrape a quick handful of mineral-crusted pith from the damp gutter into a folded leaf-pouch, tests the tension of the humming bast-cord with a calloused thumb, and pauses—just for a second—near the chert knife lying on the floor, without picking it up, as if the tool belongs to a slightly different version of the room that has not finished overlapping with this one.

Then it slips backward through the narrow fibrous tear. The edges snap flush behind it with a loud, wet pop, leaving not even a scar—only a smooth, freshly parted rib gleaming in the bright, steady, impossible afternoon light, booming with the rush of hidden rivers, held open by a handful of battered wooden pins against a whole buried world trying to heal.
