You arrive at the air first: thin enough to make your pulse sit higher in your throat, yet unexpectedly warm, thick with the scent of wet rust, heated machine oil, and crushed fig leaves. Above, the sky is a hard, pale cobalt, so bright it bleaches the tops of the highest girders white. Below, there is no ground—not a ridge of bedrock, not a slope of gravel or a smear of clay. Everything under your boots is built: perforated iron decking, poured concrete cracked into dry mosaics, courses of vitrified brick braced by riveted steel trusses that drop away, tier beneath tier, into a luminous haze miles deep.

The place opens out like a bowl or a broad highland valley, several miles from rim to rim, except the valley walls are terraced walls of infrastructure. Stepped galleries, aqueducts, gantry towers, and cantilevered walkways curve around a vast central gulf, linked by sagging suspension spans and bundles of insulated pipe as thick as old trees. You can see far across the basin—clear to the opposite rim where giant skeletal derricks stand sharp against the glare—yet there is very little empty space within reach. Everything crowds everything else. Catwalks run a shoulder's breadth beneath sagging electrical conduits; brick cisterns are wedged between diagonal cross-braces; iron staircases switch back through thickets of trellis-work and dripping troughs.

At this altitude the air ought to bite, and at the outer edges, where the wind shears past the perimeter masts, it does carry a cold edge. Inside the basin, though, the superstructure holds warmth like a kiln. Unfiltered sunlight beats down on acres of dark masonry, slate, and oxidized iron, while from deep below—far down in the unseen foundation decks—a steady, invisible tide of waste heat rises through the grates. Stand over any of the broad ventilation shafts set into the walkways and you feel it on your shins: a slow, humid breath smelling faintly of hot copper, damp limestone, and sulfur-free coal smoke, drifting upward without haste, turning the entire high city of trusses into an open-air conservatory.

Water does not fall here so much as it gathers. There are no rivers, no lakes, and storms pass mostly beneath the lower cloud deck. Instead, stretched between the tallest masts along the windward rim are colossal curtains of tarnished copper mesh, half a mile wide and sagging gently in their iron frames. When the afternoon cloud-banks drift up against the superstructure, the gauze drinks them. You can watch the silver mist comb through the fine wire, bead, darken, and run down in ten thousand slow threads into lead-lined gutters. Elsewhere, condensation sweats along the cold-return pipes and pools in the recessed channels of I-beams, dripping steadily from deck to deck. The air is never drenched, only perpetually damp in the shadows—cool beads hanging from bolt heads, dark slicks across the shaded brick, shallow pans of rainwater sitting warm and tea-colored in the hollows of abandoned machinery housings.

Out of that meagre, faithful moisture, a staggering weight of greenery has taken hold.

Without a scrap of natural earth anywhere, the plants have invented their own. Centuries of fallen leaves, rotted cordage, windblown dust, bird guano, and flaking iron scale have packed into every trough, planter, drain, and collapsed skylight, forming a dark, fibrous mulch rich in minerals. Out of it spill wild figs, thick-stemmed climbing gourds, tangled bougainvillea, and cascading curtains of maidenhair fern that hang thirty feet down into the sunlit voids between walkways. Broad-leafed palms and gnarled citrus trees lean out from sunken concrete bays, their roots braided tightly around steel stanchions and wedged deep into masonry expansion joints, holding cracked walls together even as they slowly pry them apart. Moss coats the north faces of the boiler housings in thick, bright velvet; creeping thyme and pale lichens crust the bolt-plates where boots don't tread. In the glare of noon, the upper canopy glows a fierce, translucent green, while twenty feet underneath, beneath the overlapping decks and tangled vines, the shadows drop into a cool, damp emerald twilight.

Nothing here looks new, and nothing looks ruined. It has the settled, stubborn poise of a structure that stopped changing shape generations ago and entered a long pact with weather and roots. Paint has weathered down to chalky blues and ochre ghosts in the sheltered corners; exposed iron has rusted to a deep, dry chestnut that seals the metal beneath; brass fittings have gone matte brown except where some swinging cable or passing creature rubs them bright. Concrete steps are dished smooth in the center. Even the newest-looking repairs—riveted patches over split pipe-elbows, steel bands cinched around bowed brick piers—carry their own coats of lichen and lime crust.

And it is loud—not with the sudden violence of a storm or a crowded factory floor, but with a dense, overlapping wall of sound that never quite drops to silence.

From the depths comes a low, continuous baritone thrum, half heard and half felt through the soles of your shoes: massive turbines or reciprocating engines buried far below, turning at a slow, inexorable cadence. Closer at hand, the basin is full of patient industry. Enormous spoked flywheels, thirty feet across and streaked with grease, rotate behind iron safety cages at one revolution every nine or ten seconds, their leather drive-belts slapping softly against polished pulleys. Along the sun-facing tiers, rows of vast glass-and-iron louvers—some panels cracked, others green with algae along their seals—creak and groan as weighted cable-drives shift their angle by a fraction of a degree to track the sun across the sky. Every minute or two, somewhere along the viaducts, a tall brass pressure-governor reaches its limit and vents a brief, singing plume of white vapor—*hssss-clack*—before settling back into silence while its float-chamber refills. The wind adds its own voice, thrumming across miles of taut steel guy-wires in a shifting metallic chord that rises and falls over half an hour at a time.

Layered over the iron and steam is the racket of the creatures that have made this vertical jungle theirs.

Birds own the upper air and every hollow girder. Swifts and pale, long-winged kestrels wheel in hundreds across the glaring central gulf, riding the warm industrial updrafts without beating a wing. Smaller, glossy-feathered flocks—deep blue-black with copper-colored throats—nest inside defunct junction boxes, broken glass insulators, and the dark undersides of crane jibs. Many of them have folded the basin's acoustics into their songs; from a tangle of flowering vines you will hear a clear, liquid trill break cleanly into a sharp, mechanical *tink-hiss* indistinguishable from a bleeding steam-cock.

Down among the dense girders and wet cable-trays, the fauna moves in thick layers. Slender, long-tailed climbers with rough grey coats and pale grasping paws travel the overhead pipe-bundles in chattering troops, pausing to crack hard-shelled nuts against rivet heads or strip fruit from the fig branches overhanging the abyss. Metallic-green lizards and thick-bodied skinks crowd the sun-baked copper manifolds, motionless until a shadow crosses them, then vanishing into the gaps behind riveted plates. In the damp, shaded cisterns and flooded cable-conduits beneath the walkways, small amber frogs call steadily through the afternoon, their clicking chorus competing with the dry, vibrating roar of cicadas clinging to the bark of cantilevered trees. Heavy carpenter bees and iridescent beetles drift thickly through the bright air, bumping lazily against sun-warmed glass panes before vanishing into clusters of white trumpet-flowers.

The oddness of the place does not announce itself all at once; it settles in only after you have walked a mile along the tiered galleries and noticed what isn't there, and what keeps happening without anyone hurrying it along.

Drop a loose nut through a floor grate and lean over to listen. It strikes a beam twenty feet down with a bright ring, bounces to a lower pipe, ticks faintly off a distant catwalk, and then the sound is swallowed by the steady warm rush of rising air—never a final thud, never an ending. Look closely at the great mechanical assemblies—the counterweight towers, the slow-pumping cistern rams, the endless overhead bucket-chains crawling along greased cables at the pace of a walking beetle—and you realize they are linked in long, self-regulating loops. Water condensed on the upper windward mesh fills a high balance-tank over the course of four hours; when its weight tips the beam, a valve opens, flushing a lower tier of irrigation troughs, turning a heavy brass escapement wheel by three teeth, and winding a tension cable that will, sometime near sunset, tilt a bank of collector mirrors toward the dying light.

Everything operates on long gradients. Rust creeps by millimeters a decade, sealing one pinhole leak while opening another a yard away. A banyan-like strangler fig wraps a cast-iron crane mast over eighty years, holding the rusted boom upright long after its anchor bolts have sheared through. Clouds take half an afternoon to bruise the outer copper nets and dissolve into the bright warmth of the interior. Even the light shifts with a heavy, theatrical slowness: as the sun crosses the zenith and declines toward the far skeletal rim, the massive sun-louvers groan into their evening angles, throwing broad, sharp-edged shafts of gold through the steam vents and hanging ferns, turning drifting pollen and fine condensation mist into glowing, suspended curtains.

By late afternoon, the warm drafts from below strengthen slightly against the cooling upper air. The steam plumes hang longer before thinning out. The glossy birds gather in noisy, swirling ribbons along the high tension wires, the lizards slip down into the warm crevices beside the boiler ducts, and the vast iron valley settles deeper into its own hum—bright, damp, overgrown, deafening, and entirely unhurried, suspended between a sky that never darkens completely and a floor that no living thing has ever seen.
