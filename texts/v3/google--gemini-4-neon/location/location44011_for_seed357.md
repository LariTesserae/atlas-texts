At first glance, it looks like an unfinished capital laid out on the roof of a mountain range—vast, terraced, and impossibly clean-edged under an ordinary, bright noon sky. Miles of flat, pale esplanades step gently down toward distant cloud-rimmed escarpments, punctuated here and there by solitary colonnades, wide sunken basins, and sharp-cornered towers spaced so far apart that a traveler could walk for twenty minutes across open, gleaming ground between one structure and the next.

Then the heat reaches you through your soles, and the pavement flexes.

There is no stone anywhere on the plateau. There is no soil, no gravel, no iron, no mortar. The ground beneath your feet is an immense, continuous slab of living tissue—interlocking hexagonal plates of dense, pale-celadon bark-wood, smooth as planed linden and slick with a thin sheet of warm water. Between the plates run hairline expansion channels of glossy, dark-green cambium that pulse faintly when you stand still and stare at them. Beneath the tiles, you can feel a heavy, liquid vibration, as if whole rivers are being pumped through hollow beams just under the skin of the plaza.

The warmth is not merely the sun, though the high-altitude daylight is crisp and direct. The floor itself is feverish. This entire highland floor runs hot—thermogenic, fermenting, metabolizing at a pitch that turns the thin upper air into an open greenhouse. Rising plumes of vapor lift lazily off the shallow water sheets, drift a hundred yards across the empty courts, and suddenly slam into the cold shear-winds blowing in off the surrounding abyss. Where hot vapor meets high air, the sky curdles in localized pockets, dropping brief, violent curtains of warm rain that hammer across a single terrace while the next terrace over stays bright and mirror-calm.

Everything here is built, yet nothing has been touched by a hand or a tool.

Walk up to one of the isolated structures—say, a three-story pavilion rising out of a broad reflecting deck half a mile from its nearest neighbor—and the strangeness sharpens. Its proportions are austere and deliberate: fluted pilasters, crisp right-angled lintels, recessed stairways descending into flooded inner courts, clean drainage runnels scored along every cornice. Yet every angle is a cellular cleavage plane. The pale ivory pillars are giant, turgid stalks of compressed pith wrapped in taut waxy cuticles; the flat roof-slabs are laminated, fused leaves thickened to the rigidity of slate; the beveled doorways are simply where two opposing growth fronts met, recognized one another chemically, and halted with mathematical precision.

Nothing has weathered. There are no rounded corners, no crumbling ledges, no lichened ruins. Every edge is fresh as sliced celery, sharp enough to catch the daylight in wet silver lines.

You understand why before you have finished crossing the courtyard.

A sound like a rifle shot cracks across the open expanse to the east, followed immediately by a deep, wet, fibrous groan. Half a mile away, a long aqueduct-like viaduct—forty feet high, ribbed like the underside of a bridge, carrying a shining ribbon of runoff across an empty plaza—is buckling. It does not crumble like masonry. It swells along its central span, pale tissue turning translucent under enormous internal water pressure, until a vertical seam rips open from parapet to base. A torrent of clear, steaming water bursts sideways into the air in a roaring arc. With a slow, heavy shriek of tearing cellulose, two entire bays of the viaduct shear off cleanly and crash onto the tiled floor, throwing up a wave that rolls outward for hundreds of yards.

Almost immediately, the fallen arches begin to soften. The fractured pith weeps a frothy, sweet-smelling enzymatic liquor that smells of scalding grass, crushed fennel, and warm bark. By the time the splash-wave has settled back into a mirror-flat sheet, the edges of the wreckage are already sagging, dissolving back into the hungry, absorptive pores of the plaza floor.

And fifty paces to the left of the ruined span, the pavement is already bulging upward.

That is the rule of this place: nothing is old because nothing is allowed to last. The landscape is an open drafting board in constant, loud revision. Beneath the calm, minimalist geometry—the wide bare promenades, the sparse monuments, the tranquil sheets of shallow water—the entire highland is in a state of hyperactive hydraulic turmoil.

Stand still for ten minutes anywhere on the upper terraces and you can watch the skyline change. A stepped plinth extrudes smoothly out of a sunken basin, lifting a wide rim of water with it until the excess spills over all four razor-sharp sides in loud, even curtains. Across the plain, a cluster of hollow, chimney-like towers opens a series of neat vertical louvers along their flanks—giant, fleshy valves flexing on swollen hydraulic hinges—venting a whistling rush of hot vapor that instantly condenses into mist and patters back down as a private rainstorm. Nearer at hand, a wide staircase shifts its pitch with a series of wet *pop-crack* snaps, the risers widening an inch at a time as the supporting rib underneath stretches toward the noon light.

It is loud here, in a way that feels entirely alien because there is no voice in it. No birds cross the bright sky between the escarpments. No insects buzz in the steam. No frogs call from the warm, clear cisterns; nothing ripples the water except wind, falling rain, and the slow peristaltic heave of the floor itself. There is almost no animal life at all—no watchers, no scavengers, no wood-borers to soften the fresh timber. The noise is purely structural and hydraulic: the constant subterranean rush of pumped sap, the crisp percussion of snapping fibers, the resonant booming of collapsing vaults echoing cleanly across miles of bare, flat space, the hiss of thermal vents, and the endless chorus of water pouring from newly grown spouts onto taut leaf-decks.

Why does it build like this? Look across the whole sweeping basin from one of the higher, newer ramparts, and a logic begins to show through the strangeness.

Without animals to graze it or carry its spores, and perched so high that the thin air swings violently between fierce direct sunlight and bitter upper-atmosphere drafts, the flora here has become pure engineering. To regulate its ferocious internal heat, it must raise cooling towers, shaded arcades, and broad evaporative plazas. To capture and distribute the water wrung from its own breath, it extrudes basins, stepped spillways, angled roofs, and long elevated conduits. To cast its microscopic spores into the high crosswinds, it shoots up solitary, razor-edged spires and cantilevered terraces, pushing turgor pressure to the absolute edge of structural failure.

Because there is no stone to anchor to and no slow hardwood to wait decades for, it builds fast, wet, and huge—and because it grows so fast, every structure is born already racing toward its own collapse. A colonnade reaches optimal height, vents heat, sheds spores into a passing gust, grows too heavy for its own water-logged pith, and shears apart in a wet thunderclap, returning its sugars and minerals to the tiled deck in hours so another geometry can rise a quarter-mile away.

Walking through it requires an uneasy alertness. The vast, open emptiness makes it feel safe at first—you can see for miles across the glistening flats, under normal clear daylight, with only an occasional solitary pavilion or distant wall breaking the horizon. Yet the ground you cross is an active machine of cellulose and water. A wide, dry walkway will suddenly tilt two degrees as an underground cistern fills, sending a shin-deep sheet of hot runoff gliding silently across the tiles. A perfectly symmetrical doorway you passed through an hour ago may now be pinched shut into a tight green seam, or split open into a gaping, weeping cleft with raw white fibers dripping sap into the pool below.

Here and there, you encounter moments of eerie architectural grace that vanish almost as soon as you notice them: a broad circular amphitheater of pale jade steps filled to the brim with steaming mirror-water; a freestanding arch spanning eighty yards of empty space, translucent along its crown so the sunlight glows lime-gold through its veins; a narrow causeway lined with perfectly spaced buttresses that ends abruptly in mid-air because its far abutment dissolved before noon.

Then a cold gust shears in over the plateau’s edge. The rising steam flattens and bursts into rain. Somewhere across the bright, sparse expanse, three distinct cracks ring out in rapid succession—the sound of something new pushing up through the floor, wet and sharp and fever-hot, already beginning its brief, loud tenure against the sky.
