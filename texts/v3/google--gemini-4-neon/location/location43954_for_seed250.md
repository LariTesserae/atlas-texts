You are suspended in daylight, miles above the floor of the world, and there is no air anywhere around you.

The water is bright, glacial, and violently alive. It has the sharp, stinging clarity of alpine meltwater, lit from above by a sun that barely has to cross any atmosphere before it strikes the churning roof of the sea. Far overhead, that surface never settles into a plane; it is a torn, foaming ceiling of white and silver, battered by thin-air gales that fold bright curtains of bubbles deep into the upper strata. Through the breaks in the foam, high-altitude daylight plunges straight down in racing, brittle spears, turning kilometer after kilometer of open water into a cold, luminous slate-blue.

Nothing holds this sea up that a hand could touch. No dam rims it, no walls contain it, no submerged towers hum beneath its troughs. Yet the ocean sits heaped high over a rising continental dome—drawn miles upward into the thin sky by a sculpted bend in gravity itself, an invisible swell of potential that holds an entire pelagic plateau aloft like water clinging to the back of a curved mirror. Near the upper crests, the air above the foaming surface is so thin that the water behaves strangely: wherever a fierce current whips past a sharp edge, pressure drops just enough for cold vapor to tear open inside the liquid. Silver ribbons of cavitation hiss into existence, run along the rock like liquid mercury, and collapse with crisp, stinging pops.

For leagues at a time, there is almost nothing here.

You can drift through clear, bracing cold and look in every direction across immense, hollow volumes of water without finding a single landmark. No reefs rise to clutter the view. No ships, no struts, no sunken cables, no ruins—not a single fabricated angle anywhere in the blue. And no animals at all. Nothing darts out of the corner of your eye. No shadows wheel above you, no tiny pulsing transparent bodies drift in the sunbeams, no shells cling to the stone, no jaws or gills or nervous systems exist anywhere in this vast, roaring cube of light and cold. When something moves in the distance, it is never alive with intent; it is a wall of current, a falling slab of newborn rock, or a cloud of mineral dust caught in an invisible eddy.

Then, far across the empty blue, a spine of dark stone emerges from the glare.

It does not look like a mountain worn down by ages of rain and ice. Nothing here has had time to grow blunt. The ridge is raw, narrow, and impossibly sharp—a staggered comb of black basaltic glass, pale milky silicate, and iron-grey plates rising straight out of an abyssal drop that falls away into cold indigo. Its faces are flat, stepped, and serrated, cleaved at crisp, unforgiving angles, with not a single rounded pebble or patch of sand anywhere along its flanks.

Wait beside it for an hour, and you can watch it being born.

The water carries an immense, invisible charge of dissolved stone. Across the empty gulfs between ridges, heavy mineral silt does not simply sink; it streams sideways and even upward along unseen filaments, moving like slow, inverted rain through the bright water. Where these streams converge upon a nascent crest, the water turns faintly milky. Fine crystalline grains lock directly onto the bare rock faces, building razor-thin horizontal shelves, fluted ribs, and brittle glassy fins out of the rushing cold. You feel a faint, brief thread of warmth only inches from the active seam—just a few degrees above the surrounding chill—where fresh mineral bonds snap shut and release their heat into the tearing current.

This is engineering without machinery, architecture without builders. Somewhere in the local fabric of space and fluid, fields that have no visible source shepherd the raw matter of a continent into place. You only ever glimpse their shape by accident. When a dense plume of pale carbonate dust sweeps through a clear gulf, the suspended particles will sometimes catch for a few heartbeats and snap into vast, ghostly geometries—immense standing-wave lattices, rippling parabolic curtains hundreds of meters across, luminous nodes suspended in the empty blue—before a turbulent surge scrambles the dust back into a blind grey fog. Elsewhere, two walls of water rush past each other in opposite directions at storm speed along a knife-sharp seam with scarcely a swirl between them, held apart by an unfelt shear-plane until the balance shifts and the two currents slam together in a mile-wide detonation of vortexes.

Nothing stays finished for long. The place is in a state of frantic, unhurried violence—building, testing, tearing apart, building again. A pale silicate escarpment half a kilometer long will precipitate over the course of a turbulent afternoon, growing thin and high like a blade thrust into the cross-current. As the unseen field retunes itself, the surrounding current reverses in minutes. The new ridge begins to shudder. A low groan rolls through the water, followed by a sound like giant sheets of ice splitting across a frozen lake, and whole sections of the fresh crest shear clean away. Razor-edged slabs the size of houses tumble slowly, silently in appearance yet deafening in the water, sliding off the shelf into the vast, hollow blue below, already dissolving back into stinging plumes of suspended grit before they vanish from sight.

Because of this, the silence you might expect from an empty, creatureless sea does not exist. The world is relentlessly, overwhelmingly loud.

Underwater, sound does not come from a direction so much as it fills you, vibrating in the bones of your skull and the hollows of your chest. At the bottom of the noise is a vast, continuous tectonic bass—the planetary groan of an uplift settling beneath trillions of tons of moving water, and the deep, heavy thrumming of the elevated sea straining against its own gravitational slope. Over that rides the constant rush of currents tearing across serrated stone, the dry hiss and clatter of glassy talus avalanching down unweathered slopes, and the muffled artillery of the high-altitude storm hammering the foaming surface above. Higher still come the bright, uncanny sounds: the crackling static of cold cavitation tearing open along every ridge; the sudden crystalline pings and singing-glass cracks of newborn rock cooling under tension; and, at unpredictable intervals, a pure, immense harmonic chord that sweeps through hundreds of kilometers of water at once. When that tone passes, every sharp mineral fin rings like a struck bar, and even the sparse plants tremble in unison.

Life exists here only as a stubborn, fragile afterthought.

No meadows, no tangled forests—just sparse pioneer flora clinging wherever a jagged fracture offers a handhold out of the worst of the shear. Tough, translucent ribbons of silver-green and pale olive anchor themselves directly to the bare, glassy stone with pale mineral holdfasts, streaming straight out horizontally like taut wires in the rushing cold. They are thin, stripped-down things, built to drink the crisp high-altitude sunlight and endure scouring blizzards of suspended quartz. Miles of barren ridge separate one small cluster from the next. Sometimes a whole ledge gives way and sends a clump of fronds tumbling out into the empty pelagic void, where they join occasional gossamer drift-veils—loose, tattered sheets of pale photosynthetic filaments floating through the sunbeams, slowly being shredded by the turbulence. Yet wherever a tuft manages to survive on a sheltered shelf for a few weeks, its microscopic exudates subtly alter the chemistry of the water around its base, softening the razor-edged precipitate into a thin, porous crust—the first hesitant rumor of future soil in a world made only of water and raw stone.

Weather moves here in three dimensions. Above the foaming ceiling, high-altitude cloud decks race across the sky, dragging vast, abrupt corridors of shadow across the sunlit depths at bewildering speed. Inside the water, internal fronts act like mountain squalls. A wall of pale, ice-cold silt will sweep across fifty kilometers of luminous blue, dropping visibility to arm's length and filling the water with a biting, metallic tang; minutes later, a clear downdraft from the boiling surface knifes through it, scattering needle-thin flashes of frazil ice that melt almost as soon as they form, and throwing the vista open again to dazzling, empty distances.

When the sun sinks low toward the curved rim of the heaped ocean, the light enters the churned ceiling at steep, flashing angles. The roof of the world turns to hammered silver and cold copper, sending frantic lattices of bright gold racing down the raw black ribs of the emerging peaks, while the immense, hollow gulfs beneath them deepen into a ringing, turbulent indigo. In the fading glare, the mineral rain keeps rising through the dark blue water, the stone keeps crackling as it grows and shatters, and the empty, roaring sea continues its long, violent work of conjuring highlands out of nothing.
