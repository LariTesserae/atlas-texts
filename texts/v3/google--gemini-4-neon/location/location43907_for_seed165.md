At this height, the air has almost nothing left in it to carry breath, yet it carries a sound immense enough to bruise the ribs.

It does not blow. There is no wind up here—not a draft, not a curl of mist drifting across a ridge. The atmosphere hangs dead still over the wide, saucer-shaped basin, thin and dry-cold against the nostrils, and yet the entire valley roars. It is a flat, unvarying wall of noise: a low subterranean grinding pressed tight beneath a high, metallic ringing, like the resonance of a vast bronze bowl struck once at the beginning of things and never allowed to decay. It never swells, never drops, never catches its breath. Walk for two hours across the open slate and the pitch changes only where the terrain itself shifts the standing waves, moving from a teeth-loosening thrum in the hollows to a dry, hissing shriek along the broken rim.

The basin is perhaps a day’s hard walk from rim to rim, perched so high that the world beneath it has simply ceased to participate. Where the outer crags fall away, there is no timberline, no foothills, no cloud-deck far below—only an opaque drop of bruised pewter, the same dull, directionless twilight that presses down from above. No sun crosses this ceiling. No moon or stars replace it. The light is permanently that of a frozen evening twenty minutes past dusk: enough to pick out every fracture in a distant cliff, never enough to give anything a warm color or a sharp shadow.

In fact, shadows here have largely given up their allegiance to light. A jutting pillar of pale gneiss may cast two faint smudges in opposite directions, or a single dark pool directly beneath itself as if lit from a zenith that does not exist. Farther out on the flat stone aprons, you will sometimes walk through a band of deeper gloom cast by nothing overhead at all—a cold, sharp-edged silhouette of a ridge that does not stand anywhere near it.

Because this place has been broken for a very long time, the breakage itself is weathered and settled. Nothing flickers or sparks. There is no violence left in the failures of space here; they have eroded into equilibrium, smoothed down by ages of cold and vibration until they feel as native to the mountain as scree.

Halfway down the western talus slope, a slab of black shale the size of a cottage rests four inches above the gravel bed it calved from. It does not bob or drift. Its underside has been worn satiny-smooth, though nothing passes beneath it, and the damp air clings to the gap in a permanent, unmoving haze. Across the northern floor of the valley, a seam runs straight through the bedrock for half a mile: a ribbon of ground about three paces wide where distance has been quietly omitted. Viewed from a rise, the rock looks continuous, only faintly blurred along the line like glass with a flaw in it. Step into it, however, and your front boot lands on dry lichen forty yards closer to the rim, while the roar of the air shifts down half an octave without transition. No loose stones fall into the seam; whatever was once unstable along that hinge fell out of the world long ago, leaving only a clean, worn threshold.

The cold is steady and biting, sitting just above the threshold where flesh stiffens, yet almost nothing here is frozen solid. Water exists everywhere as a thin, reluctant dampness. The dark grey stone is slick with a permanent film of condensation that never beads up enough to run and never dries away. In the shallow depressions worn into the granite flats, pockets of black water sit motionless—or almost motionless. Look closely at any puddle and its surface is etched with a razor-fine geometric lattice of ripples, crisp and stationary, driven by the ceaseless acoustic vibration of the valley. The trembling seems to keep the ice at bay; the water stays supercooled and heavy as oil, cold enough to turn fingers white in seconds, refusing to lock into crystals.

Sometimes, crouching beside one of those dark pools to look for your own face, you find instead a clear reflection of the sky as it is not—a sharp noon-blue dome with a small, blistering sun—or else the puddle shows the underside of the stones you are kneeling on, seen from deep below, as though the rock were transparent from the wrong side. The water does not stir when you draw back. It keeps its fine, vibrating grid and its quiet contradiction.

The valley is overwhelmingly empty. Most of its volume is simply vacant, deafening air and long, scoured expanses of bare stone—grey phyllite, bone-pale quartz veins, shattered basalt—broken into wide shelves and dry gullies that have held their shapes so long even the scree feels cemented in place. You can stand on a low spur and see miles of open terrain without a single vertical interruption taller than your knee, only the distant jagged teeth of the outer rim leaning slightly inward, as though the basin were a cracked cup trying to close around itself.

What grows here does so sparingly, tucked into the acoustic lee of boulders or ringing the damp depressions. There is an ink-black moss, spongy and saturated, that forms low cushions tough as wet felt; press a thumb into it and the water that wells up smells faintly of iron and wet ash. Around the flatter slabs, pale grey-green lichen spreads not in ragged blotches but in precise, concentric rings, echoing the invisible nodes of the standing waves—thick where the vibration nulls out, bare stone where the resonance is fiercest. Here and there, spaced tens of yards apart across the gravel flats, rise solitary tufts of a stiff, colourless sedge. Its blades are hollow and coated with minute beads of cold dew that never drop. Because there is no wind, the stalks never sway, yet if you touch one it buzzes sharply against your skin, humming in exact sympathy with the valley’s chord.

Almost nothing moves, which makes the rare things that do seem impossibly stark against the stillness.

High in the dim gulf over the centre of the basin, two or three pale shapes sometimes hang suspended—wide, thin-bodied creatures like bleached skates or folded paper kites, their wing-membranes ribbed with cartilaginous struts. They do not flap, and there are no thermals for them to ride. Instead they rest motionless on the invisible pressure shelves where the basin’s roaring harmonics stack one atop another, drifting only inches over the course of an hour when they tilt a wingtip to slide from one acoustic ledge to the next. If they eat, they do so rarely; mostly they simply remain aloft, pale silhouettes against the pewter ceiling, indistinguishable at a glance from high shreds of cloud that forgot how to blow away.

On the ground, separated by miles of bare rock, move the hollow-horned grazers. They are gaunt, long-legged animals the color of wet slate, their hides thin and hairless in patches where cold moisture beads on the skin. Their skulls are disproportionately large and strangely sculpted—broad, fluted crests of porous bone sweeping back over their shoulders, perforated with narrow slits. They are completely deaf to ordinary sound, or perhaps the valley’s roar is all the world they have ever heard; their perforated crests act as baffles, breaking the ambient wall of noise into quiet internal chambers where any small distortion—a footstep on wet gravel, the shift of a predator’s bulk, the strange deadening of an approaching spatial seam—registers as a sudden change in pressure. They move with an unnerving, glacial economy, taking one slow step, lowering their narrow muzzles to scrape the ring-lichen from the stone, then standing utterly rigid for twenty minutes at a time, looking like weathered boulders until an ear-slit twitches.

Smaller things inhabit the trembling black pools: flat, translucent salamanders with blunt heads and too many toes—or rather, the right number of toes when viewed straight on, though out of the corner of the eye their limbs appear doubled, trailing faint, lagged outlines of where their feet were a heartbeat earlier. They crawl along the siltless stone bottoms without disturbing the geometric ripple-grid overhead, feeding on microscopic films in the dampness, cold-blooded as the granite they cling to.

Now and then, crossing the wide emptiness between ridges, you come upon evidence that hands once worked the stone here—nothing ambitious, nothing cut by metal or bound with mortar, only bare rock piled upon bare rock.

A single dry-stone wall, waist-high and roughly stacked from local slate, runs straight across an open gravel flat for thirty paces and simply ends, enclosing nothing, sheltering nothing. Half a mile south, a ring of flat boulders barely large enough for two people to crouch inside sits beside a damp hollow; the inner stones are worn smooth on top, as though arms rested there for long seasons, though there is no hearth, no ash, no trace of wood ever having burned in this treeless height. Farther toward the western rim, perched on a spur where the sound drops abruptly to an eerie, muffled hum—a rare pocket of relative quiet where your own breathing suddenly sounds deafening—stands a solitary cairn built of pale river-rounded cobbles that do not exist anywhere in the basin. The stones are stacked seven high, leaning at an angle that ought to topple them down the cliff, yet they sit firm and slick with cold dew, locked into one of the valley’s quiet local refusals of gravity.

There are no paths linking these scattered marks. Whatever built them left no tools, no carvings, no metal pins or woven scraps—only these spare, unhurried arrangements of weight and placement, most of them sited right beside the invisible seams where space folds short or where the roar thins out enough for a mind to hear itself think.

Stand beside one of those low walls and look back across the basin. Nothing shifts. The dim twilight does not deepen toward night and does not lighten toward morning. The water in the black hollows keeps its crisp, trembling lattice without freezing and without warming. Far away, a single pale kite-creature holds its exact notch in the empty air, while three hundred yards off a slate-colored grazer stands bowed over a ring of lichen, motionless as the cairn beside you. The roar presses evenly against both ears, solid as cold stone, unchanged since before the rocks were stacked, unchanged as you turn away—as if the entire high valley were a single note held past the end of the music, too cold to decay and too far above the world to ever be released.
