Opening your eyes changes nothing. The air simply touches them—warm, heavy, and wet enough to leave a bead of condensation on the lash before you blink. There is no faint glow overhead, no phosphorescence in the mud, no spark where metal strikes metal. Light is not merely absent here; it is not a currency the world trades in. Everything that exists announces itself through weight, temperature, chemistry, static, and sound.

The sound comes first, and it never stops. It is wide and layered and strangely vertical. Down below your boots, water threads through grooved stone in a dozen overlapping pitches—gurgles, thin hisses, the steady bell-like *plink* of heavy drops falling into deep rock pockets. Around your knees and shoulders, wet foliage rubs, drips, and drums. Higher still, the air itself is moving in great, warm rivers that press upward from somewhere inconceivably far below, rushing past unseen cliff edges and blowing across the perforated stone like breath across the mouth of a thousand bottles. A low, shifting chord hangs permanently in the back of your teeth.

You are high up. You know it not from any vista, but from the way the wind arrives from beneath you, lifting the hems of sodden fronds and carrying up a dizzying, hollow acoustics—an emptiness so deep that if you kick a loose pebble over a lip of rounded stone, you never hear it land. The air feels faintly thinned in the lungs, yet saturated to the point of drowning, as if an entire warm ocean far below is constantly boiling off into mist and climbing miles up the sides of a continent-wide shelf just to dump itself back down here.

This highland stretches for thousands of leagues. It is old country—settled, smoothed, long-finished with violence. There are no sharp edges left on the bedrock. Ages of warm, slightly acidic rain have worn the plateau into broad, undulating ribs, terraced basins, and honeycombed gorges where every corner has been rounded to the slickness of river glass. Underfoot, the stone sweats even between squalls. Water seeps from fine horizontal seams, slicks the rounded ledges, and pools in shallow, ankle-deep marshes clogged with fibrous roots before spilling smoothly over worn stone lips into the next canyon down.

Nothing here grows toward a sky. Without a sun, height offers no reward except wind and electrical charge, so the flora spreads sideways, downward, and inward, crowding every cubic yard in dense, overlapping tiers. You cannot take two steps without brushing against three wet surfaces at once. Overhead, ten or twenty feet up, broad, pleated fronds interlock like folded hides, angled to intercept the rising mist and funnel it down grooved stalks. Beneath them hang tangled curtains of slick aerial roots, velvety with mineral moss, brushing your face and neck with warm, clinging drops. Around your thighs press thickets of hollow, segmented reeds that quiver and groan whenever a gust slips through the understory. Every fallen stem is swallowed quickly by thick, spongy mats that squelch warm, metallic-tasting water around your boots.

The sap here burns sharp on the tongue—copper, iron, bitter salt—and the larger growths hum. As immense warm air masses slam into the high escarpments and condense, they build vast, continuous static fields between the racing cloud-deck and the wet rock. The flora has learned to drink it. Tall, gnarled grounding-trunks thrust crowns of fine, damp, carbon-rich filaments up into the rushing vapor, bleeding silent current straight out of the sky and conducting it down through braided root-webs into the saturated bedrock. Put your palm against their wet, ridged bark during a rising wind and you feel it immediately: a dry, steady prickle beneath the slick skin, a faint tremor running straight up the bones of your forearm. Even the air around a dense stand of them smells sharp and tinny, like rain falling on hot copper.

And the thickets are packed with life. Nothing in the dark is quiet for long, because silence here is either sleep or an ambush.

Out over the gorge rims, wheeling on the relentless warm updrafts, vast flocks of leathery gliders sweep the air with rapid, bone-dry curtains of tongue-clicks. Their sonar bounces back off the fluted cliff faces, the falling rain, and the moving canopy in a dense, rattling hail of sound; when one banks low to snatch a fat, drifting spore-sac, you feel the sudden tepid slap of its wing-wash against your cheek and catch a sharp musk of ammonia and wet hide. Closer in, through the crowded middle canopy, multi-limbed climbers move by suction-pad and feathery antenna, their soft bodies brushing wet leaves as they comb the pleated fronds for slow, shelled raspers. They speak to one another in rhythmic wet scrapes and throat-ticks. When a slender, long-limbed hunter drops silently onto a taut root-line, feeling for footsteps the way fingers read a vibrating wire, an entire hillside of climbers freezes at once—a sudden, rolling pocket of quiet moving through the din of the rain.

Down in the eroded stone flutes and weeping rock crevices live the bellow-colonies: bloated, muscular sacs wedged permanently into the honeycombed limestone. They gulp warm mist and force it back out through cartilaginous reeds in deep, booming groans that swell and fade across leagues of wet terrain, harmonizing with the wind-chords of the canyons until the rock itself seems to be breathing. In the warm terraced shallows below them, heavy, armored grazers trudge noisily through the mud, wrenching up metallic tubers with grinding, stony jaws, trailing clouds of stirred sulfur and warm peat.

There is no day here, and no night. Time is kept by the sky’s weight. Across the vast expanse of the plateau, planetary weather rolls in long, majestic swells—barometric tides that take tens of hours to crest and slacken. You feel a surge coming hours before it arrives: your inner ears pop, the canyon-chords drop half an octave, the air grows feverishly close, and the hairs along your neck lift as atmospheric charge climbs toward saturation. Then the rain changes from a perpetual dripping mist to a roaring, solid drumbeat against the pleated canopy. Creeks swell from trickles to thrashing torrents. The bellow-colonies go mad, booming in overlapping waves to be heard over the water.

And it is usually then—or in the brief, dripping lull after a surge has passed—that you first catch the other sound.

It comes from miles away, carried along the wet stone and through the humid air beneath the roar of the gorges. It is too patient to be thunder, too stubbornly even to be an animal. *Thump—chug—hiss. Thump—chug—hiss.* One beat every four seconds, faint enough at first that you only register it in the soles of your feet when you stand still on bare rock.

You could travel for two wind-tides across the plateau and touch nothing made by hands. The built things here are scattered thinly across an enormous continent—a solitary station bolted to a high stone rib, a rusted steel cableway strung across a mist-chasm fifty miles further on, a lone inspection hatch half-buried under root-mats where a subterranean conduit surfaces for a hundred paces before diving back into the shale. There are no roads between them, no cities, no clusters of roofs. Yet when you come close to one, its presence is immense.

Half a mile out, the jungle changes. The flora grows denser, thicker-stalked, and noticeably hotter, leaning inward toward the invisible center of vibration. The tangled root-webs underfoot grow taut and warm, tracing hidden lines of buried cable like vines following a stream. The wild smell of wet mud and crushed greenery gives way to something harsher and older: hot mineral grease, wet cast iron, boiled limestone, scorched copper varnish, and an overwhelming, throat-drying richness of ozone. The static prickles hard enough to make your damp clothes cling to your skin.

Then your outstretched hand meets riveted steel, slick with warm condensation and vibrating like the flank of a huge, feverish beast.

It is a solitary engine-tower—cast iron, bronze-alloy, ceramic, and vulcanized rubber—bolted directly into the ancient rounded bedrock at the lip of a mile-deep drop. No lamps hang from its flanks; no dials wait to be read. It was built to work blind, and it has been working long enough that mineral dripstone has cemented its iron baseplates seamlessly to the cliff, while heat-loving tendrils have braided themselves through every cooling fin and bolt-flange. Inside its bolted housings, something enormous is turning—a weighted flywheel whose greased bearings sing a high, steady whine beneath the sledgehammer stroke of reciprocating hydraulic pistons. Deep-bore pipes wide as full-grown trunks plunge down through the floor of the station into the hot crust below, shuddering under relentless pressure. Overhead, thrusting up into the rushing, saturated cloud-deck, bristles a tall lattice of copper collector-masts and ribbed insulators. They hiss violently as they harvest megawatts of silent, dark discharge straight out of the storm-front—no sparks, no glow, only a ferocious crackling whisper and the biting taste of bruised metal on the tongue.

Every ninety seconds, a weighted pressure-valve snaps open with a sharp metallic crack and vents a scalding plume of steam into the dark canopy. Nearby fronds thrash wildly in the hot blast; flocks of clicking gliders wheel away in frantic, overlapping bursts of sonar; and from somewhere deeper in the bolted bowels of the machine, banks of sealed mechanical relays clack briskly in sequence, adjusting governors and shunting load without a single mind watching over them. Whatever the station is condensing from the high-altitude storms, cracking from the geothermal brine, and pumping away through buried continental trunk-lines, it sends it somewhere unimaginably distant—down into the unseen abyss below, or across thousands of leagues of wet plateau to a terminus no creature up here will ever touch.

Yet the wilderness does not treat the machine as an intruder. It is far too old for that. Shelled grazers pack themselves shoulder-to-shoulder into the warm grooves of the heat-exchangers, rasping iron-oxide slime off the wet plates. Blind web-hunters anchor their sensing-filaments directly to the humming steel guy-wires, using the steady industrial vibration to cloak their own faint tremors. Colonies of stone-crevice bellows perched along the nearby cliff have pitched their booming calls right into the rhythmic gaps between the piston strokes, turning the engine’s thunder into the bass-line of their chorus. Occasionally, a small, heavy-shelled maintenance mechanism clatters out through an iron flap on articulated legs, smelling sharply of cold solder and oil, tapping its way along a weeping flange to tighten a seam while the local predators step aside, long having learned that it tastes foul and breaks teeth.

When the next great barometric surge rolls in off the unseen ocean far below, the whole highland rises to meet it at once. The upward wind doubles until the hollow reeds shriek and the canyon-chords swell into a chest-rattling roar. Warm rain hammers down in sheets. Across hundreds of miles of pitch-black tableland, separated by endless roaring leagues of wet forest and weeping stone, the scattered towers sense the climbing voltage and spin up together—flywheels climbing from a groan to a howl, pistons hammering faster, buried trunk-lines humming beneath miles of sodden roots, and the whole dense, blind, warm world surging louder and faster in the dark.
