High above anything that could still be called lowlands, the world consists of old bone-dry rock and an unbearable abundance of living things, all held in the clear, cool light of an ordinary afternoon that does not end.

The land is built at a dizzying vertical scale, yet nothing about it is jagged. Whatever tectonic violence lifted these pale limestone plinths, slate blades, and terraced amphitheaters into the thin upper air happened so long ago that even the rubble has ceased to exist. There are no scree slopes, no loose pebbles, no drifts of sand in the hollows. Ages of dry scouring—now utterly finished—have pared the range down to its hardest internal ribs, leaving sweeping, open basins ringed by mile-high walls smoothed to a matte, chalky satin. Canyons drop away in stepped, pale-grey tiers into cold, dry depths where no river runs and no mist gathers. The air is cool, crisp, and parched, carrying neither dampness nor scent beyond a faint, clean trace of powdered feldspar and dry keratin.

Under the steady, unremarkable daylight, you might take a moment to realize that almost none of the rock is bare.

Every ledge, cornice, cleft, and flat terrace is packed with animals. Not scattered in herds, not moving in migrations, but layered onto the architecture of the cliffs with the density of barnacles on a pier—except these are dry-land beasts of staggering variety, pressed shoulder to flank, wingtip to horn, shell to hide. Along a single sweeping basin rim, thousands of tall, narrow-ribbed browsers stand motionless on slender, multi-jointed legs, their pale dust-colored coats brushing one another. Beneath their bellies, tucked into the shallow flutes of the bedrock, huddle dense drifts of plate-backed runners, long-snouted burrowers, and papery, moth-winged crawlers the size of hounds. Higher up, where the rock rises in sheer vertical fins, broad colonies of owl-faced, dry-scaled climbers cling to the polished stone, filling every recess until the cliff looks upholstered in muted ochre, ash-white, and dull slate-blue.

Even the open air between the peaks is occupied. Look out across a three-mile gulf and the clear space is stippled with tens of thousands of winged, finned, and ribbon-bodied creatures suspended at every altitude, hanging in layered shoals across the chasm. Their membranes are spread, their slender tails trailing, yet no wing beats. They hang there in exact, effortless poise, casting tiny, sharp-edged shadows onto the distant valley floors far below.

What makes the sheer volume of life unsettling is the silence.

There is no wind in the gorges, no roar of drafts rising from the abyss, no chorus of cries, buzzes, or trampling hooves. The air carries sound poorly, as if the vast congregation of bodies drinks vibration before it can travel more than a few paces. Walk along a high ridge threaded between packed flanks and you hear almost nothing—only the faintest, driest intimacies if you stop and hold your breath: the microscopic tick of a chitinous plate warming slightly in the cool sun; a whisker scraping half a millimeter against chalky stone; a slow, papery exhalation from some hidden spiracle, like dry grains settling inside a closed reed. Then long stretches of complete, ringing stillness.

They are alive. Warmth radiates faintly from their pressed hides into the cool air. Their eyes—glossy, dark, or pale amber—are open and clear, though an eyelid may take a quarter of an hour to close and lift again. Nothing here sleeps, nothing panics, and nothing rushes. In a shallow amphitheater halfway down a canyon wall, a lean, dry-fanged predator stands with its jaws closed gently around the throat of a broad-horned grazer. Neither bleeds; neither strains away. Their ribs rise and fall with glacial slowness, locked in an equilibrium where the act of hunting has shed its urgency and become simply a posture, as permanent as the arches of stone behind them.

Nothing decays here, either, and nothing grows in any way that can be watched. In the rare fissures where animals do not quite touch, sparse scraps of vegetation hold fast: tough, silver-grey scrub with woody stems like twisted wire, dry lichen crusts flaking like old mineral enamel, and tiny rosettes of brittle needles that seem to draw their sustenance from cold light and stillness alone. There is no water anywhere—no seepage on the cliff faces, no dew in the shadowed alcoves, no mud, no clouds overhead. The biology of this place has forgotten moisture entirely, operating on some dry, nearly stalled chemistry that requires neither drinking nor waste.

The longer you look across the open vistas, the more clearly you see that stillness is not the only rule that has slipped loose and reset itself.

Reality here has eroded alongside the mountains, worn down until ordinary laws of space and identity only apply out of loose habit. Along a narrow cornice scarcely wide enough for a boot, two dozen bulky, wool-coated beasts stand abreast—not falling, simply overlapping, their pale flanks passing cleanly through one another where they press too close, like layered chalk drawings made solid. Near a terraced promontory, five slender, long-necked creatures perched on separate ledges share a single long spine that threads invisibly between them, visible only where it bridges the cool air in pale, translucent segments; when the highest one shifts its gaze a fraction of a degree over the course of an hour, the lowest one blinks at the exact same instant.

Shadows do not always consent to match the forms that cast them. Three folded, insectile creatures huddled in a sun-lit basin throw one long, branching shadow shaped like an animal that is not present. Gravity, too, has become a local agreement rather than a universal law. On the sheer underside of an overhanging shelf, heavy-hoofed grazers stand upside down, their coarse manes hanging upward toward the stone as if the ceiling were ground; ten feet out into empty air beyond the lip of a spire, a gnarled clump of dry thorn-scrub floats intact, its exposed roots gripping the contours of a ledge that is no longer there—or has not finished arriving.

Even distance refuses to stay honest. Across the wide central valley, a towering pale massif rises beyond a nearer ridge of slate spires, yet where their silhouettes cross, the far wall quietly passes in front of the near one. Creatures clinging to the distant face appear the same size as those ten paces away, and now and then—if you watch for half a day—one will step unhurriedly from a near outcrop directly onto a ledge that ought to be a mile across the gulf, without shrinking or crossing the gap between. Perspective has flattened and folded without losing its sense of vast, open room, leaving the whole region feeling like a landscape that remembers three dimensions but no longer insists on them.

Scattered thinly across this immense, crowded quiet are the modest marks of hands that once worked stone, bone, and soft metal.

They are easy to miss at first, tucked beneath overhangs or half-absorbed into the press of living bodies. On a broad, flat shoulder overlooking a dry chasm stands a low, single-room shelter built of flat shale slabs stacked carefully without mortar, its roof patched with bundles of brittle scrub weighted down by curved, pale rib-bones. Beside the doorway sits a cold hearth of arranged stones cradling a neat mound of white ash that never scatters. Inside, on the smooth floor, lie a hammered copper awl, a flint scraper lashed with dry sinew to a pale wooden grip, and two shallow clay bowls containing nothing but fine mineral dust.

Elsewhere, there are simpler traces: a lone cairn of balanced stones standing shoulder-high on a knife-edge ridge, surrounded closely by motionless horned beasts that neither knock it down nor avoid it; faint spirals and rows of vertical tally-marks pecked laboriously into polished limestone with a harder stone; a hand-lashed ladder of bleached poles and hide strips leaning against a sheer face, ascending to a ledge packed so solidly with resting, dry-winged creatures that there is nowhere left to step. In one dry alcove, woven fiber snares hang slack from wooden pegs driven into cracks, looped gently around the motionless ankles of small, six-legged beasts that have never pulled them tight.

Whether the makers left long ago or simply became part of the equilibrium is difficult to tell. Far along a sun-lit terrace, half-hidden among a dense crowd of pale, crouched forms, a low stone wall shelters a hunched figure wrapped in stitched dry hides, a bone needle held half-drawn through a seam. Its hands do not tremble; its shadow merges seamlessly with the flank of a broad-backed creature resting beside its knee.

Above it all, the pale sky remains clear and cool, holding the same steady, unremarkable brightness hour after hour. No shadow lengthens. No cloud forms. Across miles of ancient, polished verticality, millions of living eyes remain quietly open, packed shoulder to shoulder in the dry air, poised forever in a world that finished happening a very long time ago and never bothered to empty out.
