The heat is the first thing, and it never changes. It sits everywhere at once, dry and enveloping, the warmth of a kiln brick long after the fire has been raked out, or the inside of a sealed clay jar left in the sun. There is no source to turn toward and no shadow to step into for relief. Above you are miles of stone you will never see; below you, more of the same, descending until the rock stops breaking and becomes solid crust. You are inside the skin of the world, not in a hollow cave but in a continental fracture-zone—a planetary expanse of shattered strata stacked so tightly that the vastness is felt not as open air, but as endless, near-touching repetition.

Hold out your hands in the dark and you can almost always touch something. A slab of warm shale hanging at shoulder height; a vertical fin of dark basalt thin as a knife blade; a low shelf of banded gypsum pressing down from above, leaving a crawlspace no deeper than your ribs. Yet if you walk for a year, threading through the narrow horizontal galleries and sidling along the vertical seams, the labyrinth never ends. It runs beneath what might once have been an ocean or a desert, thousands of miles of layered plates, tilted wedges, and interleaved mineral curtains. Now and then the stacked sheets part into a sudden cleft—a slot barely three paces wide that drops straight down into utter warmth, or opens laterally into a low, pillared hall where a hundred flat stone ceilings hang one above the next like the shelves of an infinite press. Even there, the space is crowded with rock. Nothing has room to echo.

Every edge is still sharp.

No water has ever run through these strata—not a seep, not a drip, not even a memory of dampness in the air. The rock was cracked open once, cleanly, by some ancient planetary tension or cooling, and since nothing flows here and no wind blows, every fracture looks as fresh as broken glass. The cleavage planes of the slate are honed fine enough to slice a palm. Splinters of volcanic glass and crystalline salt jut from the seams at precarious, impossible angles, never rounded, never slumped. Beneath every overhanging lip lies a neat, triangular apron of stone flour—the exact dust shaken loose when the fissure first split, resting undisturbed in the same crisp geometric slope it settled into ages ago. Brush your boot against one and the powder slumps with a faint, dry *shhhk*, puffing upward in a warm cloud that takes an hour to settle again in the motionless air.

Because there is no water, there is nothing green, nothing soft with sap, nothing that rots. The smell is pure dry chemistry: warm iron, crushed chalk, a sharp undertone of elemental sulfur, and something fainter and stranger—the warm, slightly sweet odor of heated paraffin and ozone. The air itself carries a quiet, persistent static charge. Dust does not simply fall; it chains together along the razor edges of the stone, growing outward into delicate, motionless beards of pale mineral soot that lean all in the same direction, following the slow electric grain of the rock.

To eyes freshly arrived from any lit place, the dark seems absolute. Only after a long stillness does the darkness reveal its faint, dusty grain. Some of the deeper veins of fluorite and doped calcite hold a dim, milky afterglow—so pale it vanishes if you look straight at it, visible only at the corners of your vision as a ghost-pale stripe along a ceiling rib. More common are the sparks. Across the dark ledges, tiny pinpricks of cold violet light blink into existence for a fraction of a second and vanish. They are not fire and they are not stars; they are the stone itself giving up light under pressure, scraped awake by the things that live here.

For the dark is full. Packed tightly into the warm, crowded geometry of the rock is an abundance of life that owes nothing to sun, leaf, or water.

You know them first by the texture of the stillness. What feels like a smooth stone ledge beneath your fingers turns out, when your warmth lingers on it, to be a stack of flat, hinged bodies wedged between two plates of shale like pages in a closed ledger. They are pale as salt, articulated along crisp geometric creases, thin as dried parchment. Without lungs or wet blood, they absorb dry chemical traces from the air and siphon tiny electric currents from the stressed quartz grains beneath them. Disturb them and they do not scramble in panic; with a single, hushed *snick* like a fingernail flicking dry paper, the topmost one unfolds an inch and slides sideways into a crevice too thin to admit a coin.

Hang your hand above a fissure and you may brush against a curtain of pendants—hundreds of spindle-shaped creatures the length of a finger, suspended motionless from the sharp overhangs by single extruded hairs of brittle mineral wax. Their shells are dry and chalky, ribbed like tiny bleached bellows. They have no eyes, only long feathery combs of conductive salt-bristle fanning outward from their undersides. When you pass, the electric field of your body bends the air around them, and the whole curtain turns silently in unison to face you, a hundred blind faces tracking the faint static wake you leave behind. They feed on the slow dry rain of the upper layers: shed scales, drifting sulfur motes, and microscopic mineral grains drawn onto their bristles by static pull.

On the razor edges of the quartz veins inch the spark-lickers—blind, rubbery things the color of smoked tallow, no larger than a thumb. They carry rows of flint-hard denticles along their undersides, and as they drag themselves across the freshly fractured crystal faces, they grind the strained lattice, waking those brief, cold-violet flashes of triboluminescence and consuming the raw, broken chemical bonds exposed in the fresh silica. Across a vast, low gallery, dozens of them work in silence, their tiny flashes winking on and off like static in a dry wool blanket—the nearest thing to weather or sky this world possesses.

Moving among these fixed populations are the larger scavengers. They are knee-high, low-slung, armored in overlapping plates of warm ochre gypsum that blend seamlessly with the dust. They walk on six long, stiff, many-jointed legs tipped with broad pads of fibrous dust-felt, so that even a creature the weight of a hound passes within a yard of you without a footstep—only a faint displacement of warm air, a breath of paraffin scent, and the soft brush of a mineral plate against a low ceiling. Inside them is no liquid blood, only a thick, warm waxy oil and dry crystalline marrow. When any creature in the labyrinth dies, it does not swell, soften, or stink; it was already dry, and simply stops moving, perched on its ledge or hanging from its wax thread like a finished artifact. Sooner or later the gypsum-plated striders arrive, and with patient, copper-hard rasps, they dismantle the dry husk piece by piece until nothing remains but a neat smudge of pale flour.

Everything moves inside an ancient, tight equilibrium. There are no seasons to hurry anything along, no floods to clear the floors, no cold snaps to cull the crowded shelves. Sound rarely rises above a dry rustle, a soft click of jointed horn, or—perhaps once an hour, from miles away through the solid rock—a single muffled *tock* as some deep geological seam settles a millimeter closer to rest.

It is in this quiet, unchanging density that you find the marks of hands.

They are sparse, scattered leagues apart, never forming a town or even a village—just careful, primitive interventions tucked into the folds of the rock. A low sleeping blind built from stacked slabs of raw slate wedged tightly between a floor and a ceiling ledge, leaving an opening just wide enough to crawl through, blocking out the tallow-scented scavengers. A line of small notches chipped into the sharp lip of a basalt rib, each notch rubbed with crushed phosphor-mineral so that a dim, ghostly dot marks the safe handhold across a bottomless seam.

What unsettles you most about these works is that you cannot tell their age. Below a single chisel-groove cut into a gypsum shelf sits a neat, tiny cone of white stone powder—the dust from the blow that carved it. Because no breeze has ever stirred it and no drop of water has ever washed it flat, the mark could have been struck half an hour ago or three thousand years in the past. A small mortar ground smoothly into a flat boulder holds a pestle of dense ironstone, still ringed by a bright yellow halo of pulverized sulfur and dried wax-paste. Beside it rests a snare made of beaten native copper wire and notched bone, strung across a narrow vertical chimney where the parchment-thin creatures slide during their slow, generational shifts.

Whoever walks these warm darks works with what the rock and its dry beasts yield: chipped volcanic glass, hammered soft metals—copper, lead, tin—pounded cold with stone hammers; needles carved from the dense chalk-bones of the striders; cords braided from dry extruded wax-threads and mineral-tanned sinew. Occasionally you find a small cache tucked into a blind alcove: a bundle of split-bone spark-strikers bound with wire, each clasping a tooth of strained quartz to throw half a second of violet glare when scraped; a flat hammered-copper tag hung from a stone tooth, incised with ridges meant to be read by bare fingertips rather than eyes; a stoppered hollow bone packed with dense, warm, edible tallow rendered from the dry creatures of the ledges.

You rarely meet the makers. In a place this vast and this densely baffled by stone curtains, two travelers could pass within twenty paces of one another, separated by three thin walls of warm shale, and neither would hear a breath. Only once in a long while does a copper tag sway faintly on its tendon before your hand reaches it, or a fresh footprint interrupt the pristine geometric slope of a dust apron—sharp-edged, unblurred, destined to stay sharp until another foot crosses the same narrow shelf centuries from now.

And yet the vast labyrinth does have a single, continental pulse—so slow it almost escapes notice. Across hundreds of miles, the planetary crust holds a quiet electrical tide. Over decades, the deep charge shifts imperceptibly from one side of the fractured continent to the other. No wind blows when it turns, and the temperature does not waver by a fraction of a degree. Only the hanging dust-beards along the razor edges of the rock lean slowly, by a single degree, from north to south; the curtains of blind, salt-bristled pendants pivot together in the warm dark; and somewhere, miles deeper in the dense, layered silence, the parchment-thin creatures unfold one by one and begin to drift along the warm stone, following a grain in the rock that has never hurried, never cooled, and never once been broken by rain.
