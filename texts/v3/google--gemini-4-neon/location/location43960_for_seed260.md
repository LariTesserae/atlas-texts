Before the eye can make sense of the height, the sound takes hold of the body.

It is not a roar like water—there is no water anywhere here, not a smear of dew, not a stain of old mud, not a trace of vapor in the cool air—nor is it the frantic lash of a storm. It is a single, immense, dry chord held without breath or interruption, so loud it stops feeling like noise and becomes a kind of weather, or a weight strapped to the ribs. It lives in the jawbone and the collarbone, a slow, grinding harmonic like two iron plates the size of provinces sliding past one another at the speed of a growing fingernail. Underneath the low, crushing hum runs a high, dry shriek, thin as stretched wire, vibrating at the edge of hearing. Nothing interrupts it. Over hours, it shifts by perhaps a quarter-tone, descending with terrible patience, as though some buried instrument were slowly losing tension across the span of centuries.

In that acoustic pressure, the landscape opens wide and vertiginously high.

It is a vast, elevated basin—an open highland of stepped shelves, raw mesas, and sheer, unrounded ridges suspended at an altitude where the cool air smells only of struck flint, chalk, and cold copper. Everything here has the sharp, unfinished geometry of stone that has never been touched by rain, wind-grit, or time. Cliff faces do not slope; they jut in fresh, razor-edged planes, stepped like newly cleaved slate. Talus does not tumble into smooth aprons at the bases of the crags; instead, angular blocks sit where they emerged, half-extruded from the floor of the basin, their corners crisp enough to slice skin. The whole expanse looks roughed out and abandoned mid-stroke—or still in the slow act of pushing itself upward out of nothing, millimeter by glacial millimeter, still deciding what shape a mountain ought to take.

And it has not fully decided.

Across the miles of open, dim terrain, the rules of space and solid matter sag and fray at the seams. Look across the broad trough toward the far terraced wall, and the ridge you are standing on is unmistakably higher than the plateau opposite; walk the rim to that far wall, look back, and you are looking down at the very shelf you left behind. Elevation here is not a simple ladder. Drop-offs plunge from the broken edges of the basin into a pale, dry verticality where distance refuses to behave: stones kicked over the lip shrink too quickly, hang for a long, unhurried minute at the apparent size of peppercorns, and then vanish sideways rather than downward, as if the abyss had a grain to it that they slid along.

The light offers no help in sorting out what is near and what is far. It is dim and sourceless, a permanent tarnished-pewter twilight, cool and shadowless in the broad stretches yet strangely sharp along the raw edges of newly formed rock. Some crags glow along their fracture lines with a chalky, muted luminance, no brighter than wet bone—though nothing here is wet—as if the seam where matter meets un-matter leaks a faint, cold residue. Shadows appear anyway, detached from any overhead sun: dark, velvety wedges that lie across the flat mineral benches, pointing in three different directions at once. A few of them cast from rocks that are not there. If you drag the edge of a slate shard across one of those orphaned shadows, it offers a faint, dry resistance, like scraping soot from dry paper, before resettling intact behind the blade.

Even the stone itself is only mostly committed to being stone. Along the taller spires and the raw steps of the basin floor, the outlines of boulders double and triple, faint ghost-facets extending a hand's width past the solid face—earlier drafts of the same rock, or adjacent versions that failed to fully separate. Press a palm to the cool mineral, and it is utterly hard, dry as an attic beam; yet from the corner of the eye, a ledge five hundred paces off seems to breathe outward by an inch and draw back, too slowly to ever catch in the act.

Across this sparse, ringing emptiness—widely spaced, separated by acres of bare slate-dust and raw fractured benches—sit islands of astonishing, exuberant vegetation.

Without a drop of water in the soil or the sky, the flora has made another bargain entirely. It drinks the dry mineral powder, the cold static of the high air, and above all the relentless vibration of the world. Where a hairline fissure lets the basin's deep chord resonate most fiercely, life erupts in thick, baroque, waterless abundance.

Huge, billowing rosettes of chalk-fern unfurl to the height of a person's chest, their fronds dry as parchment and intricately feathered in shades of tarnished verdigris, soot-violet, and ash-silver. Each frond is stiff and finely ribbed, trembling continuously in sympathy with the deafening hum. When the slow pitch of the landscape shifts, whole groves of them tilt a fraction of an inch in unison, tuning themselves to the pressure wave. Through these thickets twist dense domes of hollow-stemmed briar, whose papery bark peels into thousands of narrow curls; the curls shudder and flute, adding a papery, rasping overtone to the surrounding roar.

Beneath the tall fronds lie knee-deep cushions of bone-pale velvet-ash—a dry, fibrous moss that feels cool and oddly silken to the touch, crumbling like dried tea if crushed hard, yet alive and tangled with pale, papery blooms shaped like cupped ears. None of it has sap. Snap a thick stem and the interior is packed with dry, iridescent dust and tight chambers of honeycomb pith, like the inside of a wasp's nest, vibrating audibly when held near the cheekbone. The pollen these plants release does not drift randomly; there is scarcely any true wind, only sluggish drifts of cool air. Instead, the fine, chalky spores hang suspended in visible curtains and neat geometric ribs along the acoustic standing waves, shimmering faintly in the dim twilight until they settle like gray frost onto the un-eroded rock.

Like the terrain, the vegetation does not always obey ordinary continuity. A thick, lush tangle of indigo-black ribbon-vines spills exuberantly out of a bare vertical cliff face, its heavy foliage swaying in the sound—yet its thick, pale roots are anchored across a hundred-yard gulf of empty air on an opposing spire, gripping the dry scree there with no stem bridging the space between. Elsewhere, broad, glossy-dry spade-leaves of deep mineral green grow in lush clusters that vanish completely when viewed edge-on, possessing width and intricate veining but no thickness whatsoever, flickering in and out of sight as you shift your weight from one foot to the other. In a sheltered hollow, a solitary grove grows upward from the underside of an overhanging shelf, its dry plumes reaching down toward the empty drop, shedding pollen that drifts stubbornly up toward the stone ceiling.

Between these rich botanical islands lie long, bare stretches of open country, empty and still. There are no animals here. No bird crosses the pewter sky; no beetle works the dry pith; no burrow breaks the powdery scree; no bleached bone of any beast lies among the ferns. The sheer acoustic pressure would shred any ordinary ear, collapse any lung that tried to keep its own small rhythm against the continent-sized drone—or perhaps animal life simply requires a settled world, one where ground stays underfoot and time runs in a single, reliable channel. Only the dry plants endure, patient and deaf, metabolizing the trembling stone.

Yet the basin is not entirely unmarked by hands.

Scattered far apart across the vast, sparse distances are small, humble works of primitive craft—modest, painstaking, and hauntingly out of scale with the fractured immensity around them.

Tucked into the lee of a raw basalt overhang sits a single low shelter, dry-stacked from angular slabs of unweathered slate and roofed with tightly bound sheaves of hollow briar-stalks, weighted down with sharp stones. Its doorway faces away from the broadest stretch of the basin, shielded by a curved baffle wall of layered shale—an attempt, crude and touching, to carve out a pocket of slightly quieter air. Inside, the floor is swept clean of pollen. A few hand-knapped flint blades lie beside a shallow stone mortar containing ground bone-moss and chalk-dust, along with a bent pin of soft, cold-hammered copper. There is no hearth; nothing here has been burned, though the cool air bites gently at bare skin.

Out on the open shelves, separated by half-miles of bare mineral ground, stand other small traces. Cairns of five or seven un-mortared stones, stacked with extreme care so that each flat slab balances on a razor edge. Stretched between pairs of driven horn-pegs or hammered copper stakes are lengths of tightly braided plant-fiber cord, knotted at regular thumb-width intervals. Some of these cords hang slack now, looping lazily onto the dry dust; others are pulled taut as bowstrings, humming with their own bright, frantic pitch inside the larger roar, as though the distance between the two stakes has quietly widened since whoever drove them walked away.

Along the sheer face of an emerging terrace, hundreds of neat tally-marks have been scratched into the stone with a harder rock—groups of four vertical lines crossed by a fifth. The sequence runs waist-high along the ledge for thirty paces, then breaks abruptly where the rock has ghosted into a double-exposed step, and resumes twenty feet higher up on a sheer, unreachable overhang, upside down, the scratches looking freshly cut. Wedged deep into a resonant crack nearby is a hand-forged prong of brittle bronze, greenish with dry tarnish, vibrating so fiercely it looks blurred around the edges, singing a steady, lonely note that neither matches nor surrenders to the basin's chord.

Whoever made these things is gone—or perhaps they are never here at the same time as the observer, slipped into a slightly different fold of the slow, smeared hours. Now and then, a freshly dropped pebble skitters across a slate shelf with no foot near it; minutes later, higher up the crag, a tiny notch of raw stone silently detaches from the rim, its fall already over before it began.

Nothing hurries to resolve this. The world here moves on a vast, patient calendar. Over seasons that have no rain or snow to mark them, a raw bench of slate inches upward, lifting a dry-stone cairn along with it until the upper stones silently part company with their shadows. A lush thicket of chalk-ferns slowly extends its papery runners across a seam in space, blooming simultaneously on a ridge and in the sunken trough a mile below. The dim pewter sky neither brightens into morning nor deepens into true night; it only breathes, across weeks, from cold lead to tarnished tin and back again.

And beneath it all, filling every hollow, shaking the dry fronds, holding the pollen in pale, trembling curtains across the towering, unfinished basin, the deafening chord continues—patient, cool, dry, and immense—the sound of a high place still straining to become real, or slowly forgetting how.
