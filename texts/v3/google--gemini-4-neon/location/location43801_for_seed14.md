There is no empty space here, though almost nothing lives in it.

Far below the reach of waves, the valley lies completely drowned—a broad, sunken basin several miles across, sealed beneath fathoms of salt water cold enough to stiffen joints in minutes. Whatever light survives the descent arrives as a dead, pewter dimness, already nearly spent before it meets the clouds of silt rising from the floor. There is no color left at this depth, only degrees of slate, soot, and bruised grey. You cannot see the far slopes of the basin, nor the surface overhead, yet you can feel the scale of the place in the way the currents travel: long, unimpeded surges that gather momentum over miles of submerged ground before colliding over the low ridges and shallow bowls of the valley floor.

The bedrock beneath it all is ancient—so old that every sharp edge surrendered epochs ago. Ice sheets ground this trough smooth before the sea ever claimed it, and the sea has spent millennia finishing the work. There are no cliffs, no spires, no fractured crags. The basin is made of low, whaleback domes of dark gneiss and flattened pans of shale, worn down to the quiet geometry of things that have nowhere left to fall.

Yet the floor itself is never bare, and never still. Every inch of that ancient stone is packed tightly under a dense, overlapping skin of matter that the basin has collected and cannot easily expel. Cobbles worn smooth as eggs sit wedged shoulder-to-shoulder in compacted beds, their seams choked with crushed mussel shell, black peat-clods, coarse river grit, and slick grey clay. Above the shingle, the water column is just as crowded. There is no clear blue here. The cold sea is thick with suspended glacial flour, fine organic ash, drifting flecks of rotted weed, and heavy, rolling sheets of hyper-saline brine that refuse to mix cleanly with the slightly fresher meltwater pressing down from the drowned tributaries. Layers of different density stack and shear against one another, filling every cubic foot with weight and grain.

And all of it is in constant, violent motion.

A storm is always happening down here, though no wind touches it. Tidal bores spilling over the distant submarine sill crash into cold gravity-currents sliding down the valley's flanks, turning the basin into a chaotic engine of underwater weather. Whole fronts of dense, mud-choked water—turbidity plumes thick as smoke—race across the floor, curling upward in turbulent vortices before collapsing back onto the stones. A low ridge of silt that takes an hour to accumulate will shear away in thirty seconds, lifted bodily into the murk and flung across the next gravel pan. When the cross-currents lock together in the deeper bowls, the packed cobbles themselves begin to shudder, then lift and roll, entire pavements migrating inches at a time in a slow, grinding avalanche.

The strangest truth of the basin is how quiet this violence is. Deep water swallows high sound; soft clay and suspended silt muffle the rest. For all the churning weight in motion, there is no roar, no thunder of surf. The tumult registers mostly as pressure against the ribs and a low, hushed hiss—like coarse cloth dragged steadily across wet slate. Now and then, when a surge is strong enough to tumble fist-sized stones across a bare shale shelf, a dull, dry *tock* travels a short distance through the cold, followed by the faint, papery rustle of shifting shell-grit. Then the muffled hush closes back over it.

Vegetation barely holds a claim in this dark, scouring broth. On the sheltered lee side of the largest embedded boulders, where the direct shear of the grit-laden currents breaks slightly, thin crusts of leathery, near-black red algae cling flat against the stone. Nothing tall can root here; the currents would strip it clean. What other plant matter appears is only passing through—tattered ribbons of coastal kelp and knotted bladderwrack ripped from unseen shallows miles away, dragged down into the trough to tumble endlessly through the eddies until the silt grinds them into dark fibers and buries them in the ooze.

Fauna is just as scarce, and built for patience rather than grace. Wedged tightly into a pocket between two worn cobbles, a mottled sculpin presses its broad belly against the gravel, pectoral fins flared like wedges to keep the current from peeling it loose, its gills pulsing slowly in the grit-heavy water. Elsewhere, a pale, long-legged spider crab hunkers in a clay hollow, legs folded tight against its carapace, waiting for a brief slackening of the surge to pick a shred of dead fish out of the passing drift. When a dense mud-plume settles, thin, blind hagfish slip out of the soft pockets to probe the fresh deposits, coiling straight back into the murk when the next cold sheet of brine sweeps through. Once in a long stretch, high in the dimness where the silt thins slightly, the blunt silhouette of a deep cold-water shark drifts past—slow, scarred, scarcely moving its tail, vanishing into the grey haze before its full length has cleared the gloom.

Nothing has ever been built in this valley. No piers descend into it, no walls cross its floor, no sunken hulls break the smooth sweep of its ridges. The basin belongs entirely to rock, water, and sediment. Yet it is not wholly untouched by hands. Far above, along the cold, unseen margins where the sea meets ice and shore, people work the water with simple gear, and the deep trough is where lost things eventually slide.

Caught in the tight shingle beside a shale lip lies a heavy river stone with a neat, pecked groove worn around its waist, still wrapped in three turns of blackened, swollen sinew—a fishing weight parted from its line and rolled miles downhill by the bottom currents. A few yards away, wedged upright between two flat stones like a splinter of bone in a jaw, is a hand-carved toggle of antler, notched cleanly with a blade and polished smooth by decades of passing grit. Sometimes a storm in the upper world tears wicker fish-traps off the high ledges and sends them tumbling down the slopes; by the time they reach the basin floor, they are only snapped, waterlogged strips of peeled willow and frayed hemp cord, snagging briefly on the cobbles before the silt swallows them. Deeper in the packed gravel, mixed indistinguishably among ordinary pebbles, lie older traces still: a stubby chert scraper, a bruised hammerstone, a leaf-shaped point—tools eroded out of ancient riverbanks that drowned millennia ago, now rolled smooth and dull by the same cold currents that carry the freshly lost bone hooks of the living.

Nothing stays uncovered for long. As the tide shifts far overhead, a fresh wall of freezing, silt-thick water pours silently over the valley's western lip, flattening the small eddies and erasing the tracks of the crabs. The antler toggle disappears beneath a sliding curtain of grey mud. Three ridges over, an older pocket of shingle is scoured open, briefly exposing a tangle of rotted cord and a bed of pale shells before the cross-current swings back and buries them again. The ancient bedrock beneath never yields an inch, while everything resting upon it is endlessly lifted, sorted, collided, and laid down anew—a packed, restless world churning in muffled darkness, too cold to rot quickly and too turbulent ever to clear.
