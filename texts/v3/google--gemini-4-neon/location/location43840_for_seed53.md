You drop into the basin over a lip of shattered dark stone, and the wind cuts off all at once. Above the rim, the gale tears straight across the roof of the range, carrying wisps of dry snow off neighboring peaks that sit lower than your boots. Down here, ten paces below the crest, the air is suddenly still, thin, and painfully cold—the kind of cold that dries the back of the throat in two breaths and leaves a metallic taste on the tongue.

The place is small, no wider than a cathedral nave and cupped tightly inside jagged walls of frost-riven rock. From the rim it looks like a shallow bowl of gray rubble and blinding snow. Once you step inside, the floor vanishes. The whole interior is packed tight with upright fins of dark phyllite and iron-stained slate, tilted at a single steep angle like the pages of a waterlogged book stood on its spine and frozen solid. Between the stone slabs—crowding them, leaning against them, filling every gap—stand dense thickets of blade-like ice spires, waist-high to shoulder-high, carved by sun and dry wind into fluted knives that all point unanimously toward the high midday sun.

There is no open ground to cross. You move sideways through narrow aisles, brushing wet rock with one shoulder and serrated blue-white ice with the other. Nothing grows here. Not a crust of orange lichen, not a thread of black moss, not even the tough alpine cushion plants that cling to scree fields miles below. The air is too thin, the light too punishing, the stone too raw and salt-bitten.

That light is the first thing that makes the place feel wrong. The sky overhead is a deep, bruised indigo, almost black at the zenith, yet the sun burns through it with a ferocious, colorless glare. Every slanted face of ice throws it back; every wet slate fin flashes like a dull mirror; tiny specks of mica embedded in the dark stone prickle in the corners of your eyes. You squint until your forehead aches, and still the basin is split into two incompatible worlds separated by half an inch of shadow. In the direct beam, the dark rock drinks the radiation fiercely, warming just enough on its outer skin to sweat a thin, glossy film of meltwater. Turn your hand an inch into the shade behind a slab, and the temperature plunges back into a stillness that bites through wool and leather.

Because of this split, the basin is never truly frozen and never truly dry. It is damp in a strange, gleaming, precarious way. Thin sheets of water creep down the sunlit faces of the slate, catching dust and dark grit, only to hit the shadow-line at the base of a fin and lock instantly into black glaze. Off the tips of the ice blades, invisible plumes of vapor rise directly from solid to air, drifting two feet sideways before condensing on the shaded undersides of overhanging stone as thick, feathery fur of hoarfrost. Look closely into a narrow cleft and you can watch the place working: a single drop swells along a dark quartz vein, hangs clear as glass, and falls—striking a pocket of meltwater no bigger than a cupped palm, ringed by a fragile collar of needle-ice that thickens from the edges inward while the center stays wet.

Every few minutes, the basin speaks to itself. Mostly it is quiet—the deep, flattened quiet of extreme altitude, where thin air refuses to carry sound and the crowded fins muffle the roar of the wind overhead into a faint, hollow sigh. Nothing flies through the aisles; nothing burrows beneath the scree. Once in a great while you might find a single desiccated moth wing or a bleached barb of a feather plastered to an ice blade, carried up on a freak thermal from valleys leagues below and preserved like a speck in amber. Yet the silence is never dead. A sun-warmed wafer of slate springs loose from its parent slab with a sharp, ceramic *tink*. Deep inside a shadowed pile of talus, trapped ice shifts and groans softly—a dry, contracting tick, like cooling stove-iron. A miniature slide of dark shale chips slithers down a trough with a hushed papery hiss, then stops.

It takes time, moving slowly through the glare and the tight corridors, to notice the stranger crust growing along the dampest seams. Where the dark rock has been ground finest by millennia of freezing and thawing, a pale brine seeps out—copper-bearing, chalky, bitter. Wherever this seepage meets the fierce midday light, mineral blooms sprout directly from the stone. Pale turquoise crusts blister along the fractures; delicate white hairs of gypsum and salt curl outward like frost-flowers, catching the bright air until they stand an inch thick in sheltered crevices. They look uncannily like pale moss or bleached coral, thriving in the exact places where water sweats and vanishes fastest. By mid-afternoon, when the sun shifts and the rock cools, whole clusters of these salt-needles dry out, snap at their bases, and collapse into faint blue-white powder drifts at the foot of the slabs.

And only after your eyes adjust to the clutter of fins, ice blades, and mineral crusts do you begin to pick out the marks of hands.

They are scattered thinly through the basin, weathered half back into the geology, built by people who never stayed long and brought almost nothing with them. Wedged into the mouth of a narrow cleft, propped on two hand-knapped chips of quartz, sits a square plate of hammered copper no larger than a boot sole. Its surface is scratched and tarnished green at the corners, but its center has been rubbed bright with fine silt; angled carefully, it bounces a harsh rectangle of midday glare deep into a shaded rock pocket where no direct sun ever reaches. Below that lit patch, a shallow, thumb-wide channel has been patiently pecked into the slate with an iron chisel, guiding a slow, glistening thread of mineral-rich meltwater into a blackened clay saucer half-buried in scree.

Farther along the eastern wall, tucked beneath an ancient overhanging slab where frost-wedging has peeled a flat shelf into the rock, someone has stacked a low windbreak of dry slate—barely waist-high, roofed with three overlapping stone plates weighted down by heavy cobbles. It is not a house; it is a crouch-hole, built for one body to huddle out of a storm or wait out a bitter night. Inside, the floor is swept clean of sharp shale, leaving a bed of fine gray dust. Resting on a ledge of dark rock sit three things: a curved scraper carved from thick bone, its edge stained faint verdigris; a coil of coarse braided hair-cord; and a palm-sized slab of smooth river stone, clearly carried up from far below, worn glossy on one face from burnishing metal. Along the upright stone framing the low entrance, rows of neat vertical scratches have been ground into the slate—hundreds of them, some sharp and pale, others softened almost to invisibility by centuries of frost-spalling.

Scattered through the tight aisles are other small, deliberate contraptions, primitive and oddly exact. Splintered stubs of twisted juniper wood—rare, precious timber hauled up from days below the snowline—have been lashed with rawhide into tiny tripods wedged between the ice blades. Across them rest hand-split panes of cloudy, translucent mica, tilted like miniature skylights over the richest turquoise seams. Underneath each pane, shielded from the dry air while trapping the brutal sun, the damp rock steams faintly and the pale mineral needles grow twice as thick, dense and glassy as spun sugar. Near the center of the basin, a shallow basin has been ground directly into a flat boulder; a thin ring of bright copper wire lies submerged in its half-frozen puddle, crusted thick with crystalline rime that glitters blue-white under the clear skin of ice.

Nobody is here now. The footpaths between the fins are faint—just places where the sharpest shale edges have been kicked flat and the ice blades snapped off at shin height—and already the basin is quietly reclaiming them. Frost-heave has tilted one of the copper reflectors off its aim so it throws its bright square uselessly against empty ice. A slow creep of scree has half-buried a hollowed soapstone bowl, leaving only its dark rim poking through the gravel. High up in the air, faint ice crystals—diamond dust formed straight out of the clear, freezing sky—drift lazily across the bowl, sparking like tiny silver sparks whenever they cross the beam of the sun, piling a barely visible sheen across the abandoned cords and weathered stone.

Toward late afternoon, the whole small world turns at once. The sun drops toward the saw-toothed western rim, and the shadows of the slate fins stretch rapidly across the narrow corridors, swallowing the copper plates and mica panes one by one. You can hear the change before you feel it. The faint, occasional *plip* of water into clay bowls stops. A wave of tiny sharp ticks ripples across the basin as the wet films on the rock flash-freeze into glossy black armor. The mist stops rising from the ice spires; the hoarfrost on the undersides stops growing and hardens into brittle glass. Above the rim, the high wind shifts pitch, humming a fraction lower over the dark crest. Down inside the crowded labyrinth of stone and ice, the light goes thin, sharp, and silver-gold, catching the tips of a thousand leaning blades before vanishing entirely, leaving the small basin locked tight, bristling, and waiting for the morning glare to start it working all over again.
