You hear the basin long before your eyes can agree on what fills it. It is a continuous, physical wall of noise—like a logging camp operating inside a thunderhead, or a packed market shouting through a gale—yet when you crest the low rim of the depression, the sky above is plain, warm, and bright, and nothing that breathes or walks moves anywhere in the sprawl below.

A city occupies the whole valley floor, low-lying and wide, stretching from one shallow rise to the next in dense grids, terraced quarters, winding avenues, and sudden, sun-struck plazas. There are four- and five-story blocks, cantilevered balconies, arched viaducts, narrow stepped lanes, and steeply gabled roofs. None of it is stone. None of it is brick, mortar, or cut lumber. The entire settlement—pavement, foundation, load-bearing wall, window frame, gutter, and spire—is living plant tissue, grown seamlessly into urban form without a single nail, joint, chisel mark, or trace of a tool.

Down at street level, the light is ordinary and candid, a warm afternoon sun throwing razor-sharp shadows down canyons of braided wood and pleated frond-walls. It is hot here—not the damp swelter of a jungle, though the green mass is total, but a dry, feverish warmth radiating both from the sun-baked upper roofs and from the furious metabolic heat of trillions of dividing cells. The air tastes of fresh-split pine, crushed green stem, hot resin, and chalk-pale pollen that hangs in every slanting sunbeam.

Water is hoarded rather than plentiful. You walk on interlocked slabs of flattened, sun-warmed root-flesh—fibrous, slightly springy, dusty with curled bark shavings—and only in the deep seams between them do narrow root-gutters glisten with dark, lukewarm trickles. Moisture lives in pockets: cupped inside the leathery axils of giant buttress-leaves that act as open cisterns on the third-story ledges; beading in warm, sticky drops along the eaves of papery bract-shingles; sweating through pale, spongy pith-walls in the shaded underpasses where condensation gathers and drips into shallow basins grown directly into the avenue floor. Every few minutes, a local pressure-shift triggers a sudden squall of guttation—an entire block weeping fine warm spray from its upper foliage into the dry street below, darkening the fibrous cobbles for a few yards before the heat drinks it back up.

Nothing here looks old. Even the broadest, thickest walls—columns of pale ironwood and knotted bark-plates—have the raw, violent crispness of something that emerged ten minutes ago. Edges are knife-sharp, unweathered, freshly unfolded like stiff heavy paper. Along the corners of buildings, outer bark splits cleanly down the grain to reveal wet, cream-colored sapwood and weeping amber gum. Splinters jut cleanly from lintels that have never been worn down by wind or foot traffic.

And the noise is relentless, almost unbearable inside the tighter quarters. With no animals and no machines, the city manufactures a deafening uproar out of pure cellulose, hydraulic pressure, and geometry. Overhead, massive hollow trunks swell as they haul water up from the sunken water table, emitting a low, chest-rattling boom that repeats down the avenue like a bass drum. Across the street, a newly forming facade stretches too rapidly for its outer casing, and the bark rips apart with a series of pistol-cracks that echo off the opposite walls. Millions of stiff, dry-ribbed leaves and papery husks chafe violently against one another in the hot updrafts rising from the valley's core, raising a coarse, continuous hiss like surf dragging over gravel. High on the cornices, melon-sized woody seed pods dry in the sun until their seams fail, detonating with sharp concussions and hailing hard, rattling buckshot down onto the frond-awnings below.

Worse than the raw volume is the way the architecture shapes it. Hollow support pillars, vaulted root-arcades, and tightly spaced alleyways act as resonant chambers and organ pipes. The groaning of shifting heartwood, the pneumatic hiss of sap cavitating through strained vessels, and the clatter of dry fronds bounce through the street grid until they braid together into the unmistakable acoustic texture of a crowded metropolis: distant hammering, slamming shutters, the overlapping babble of ten thousand muffled voices in a bazaar. You turn a corner expecting a throng and find only a narrow lane of twitching bamboo-like balusters, hot sunlight, and drifting pollen.

Nothing holds its shape for long. This is not an overgrown ruin frozen in stillness; it is a city in a state of frantic, turbulent construction and demolition, churning with anthill speed.

Stand at the edge of a sunlit plaza and watch. The root-pavement at the center begins to heave, creaking loudly as pale fibrous plates buckle upward at sharp angles. Within minutes, raw green structural ribs spear out of the seam, thickening visibly as you stare, browning and furrowing into rough bark with loud tearing pops. Horizontal cross-braces shoot sideways from trunk to trunk, grafting cleanly on contact without seam or scar. Pleated fan-leaves unfurl across the gaps, drying almost instantly into stiff, corrugated wall-panels. Windows open where the tissue simply ceases to divide along crisp rectangular boundaries; braided creepers draw themselves taut across the openings like louvered blinds.

Meanwhile, half a block away, a tall gabled tenement abruptly loses its structural conviction. Its sharp right angles soften; its dark heartwood pales and slumps into wet, steaming pulp. With a drawn-out, splintering roar, three stories fold inward in a cloud of golden spores and shredded pith. Before the dust settles, thick lateral roots surge up through the wreckage, drinking the collapsed mass back into the street-mat, while fresh, razor-edged shoots already pierce the rubble to lay out the floor plan of a different building—perhaps an arcade, perhaps a flight of cantilevered stairs leading nowhere.

If you stay inside the city long enough to look past the sheer speed of the botany, you realize the foliage is not merely growing fast. The rules of space and sequence are coming apart in the cambium.

Start down a straight, sunlit alley bordered by three-story walls of tightly woven cane, its far end plainly visible forty paces away where it opens onto a bright square. Walk thirty paces and the square is no closer. Walk fifty, and the alley has bent gently around a curve you never saw from the entrance, its walls now six stories high, the shadows thrown by the eaves slanting northeast on your left and due south on your right. Step through a vine-framed doorway in the side of a narrow wedge-shaped building—barely ten feet wide from the street—and find yourself in a vast, open-air courtyard surrounded by tiered galleries of pale shelf-wood, bathed in the same ordinary warm daylight, with a sky overhead that holds slightly different clouds than the one outside the door.

The plants seem to grow along folded dimensions, stuffing three rooms into the footprint of one, braiding stairways of overlapping woody scales that descend two full stories below street level only to emerge onto a sun-baked rooftop three streets west. Sometimes causality slips along a single beam: you see fresh green buds, deeply furrowed mature timber, and sun-bleached hollow deadwood occupying the same three-foot length of lintel, flickering between states as if the tissue cannot decide what decade it belongs to. Across a square, a tower begins shedding heavy chunks of dry bark-masonry *before* its upper stories have finished extruding, the debris stalling mid-air in the thermal drafts or snapping cleanly back into the rising rafters like a tear closing in reverse.

And everywhere, there is the eerie, corrupted mimicry of civic purpose. The vegetation has no inhabitants—no birds wheel in the clear blue sky above the roofs, no beetles scuttle beneath the peeling bark, no flies gather around the sweet, sticky pools of weeping sap, no voice answers the deafening roar—yet the plant-mass behaves as though it is frantically drafting a city from a damaged blueprint. You find broad, flat slabs of pale bark mounted at street corners, their surface veins branching into crisp, parallel rows that almost resolve into lettering before splitting into frantic, meaningless bifurcations. Inside newly grown ground-floor rooms, root-wood rises cleanly from the floor into knee-high benches, counter-ledges, and narrow alcove tables, only to lose the thread halfway through and twist the tabletops into impenetrable cages of thorns or fuse the whole chamber into a solid plug of raw timber. Doorways are consistently scaled to the height of absent shoulders. Drainage grates of woven twig-work cover deep, dark cellar-shafts that lead down into a dense, hot underworld of root-tunnels, where vast pitcher-like cisterns hang from the vaulted ceilings, digesting fallen bark-lint and shed leaves in warm, sour broth and venting damp, acidic steam up through the street-seams.

Without animals, the flora fills the vacuum of motion itself. Along the warm, dry stretches of avenue, fist-sized clusters of barbed, papery seed-cases crawl steadily across the fibrous cobbles on long, jointed awns that curl and uncurl with every microscopic shift in humidity, gathering in dry, clicking drifts at street corners like scurrying crabs before exploding into drifts of fluff. Whip-thin tendrils lash sharply across narrow alleys whenever a passing shadow crosses their stems, snagging blowing husks and hauling them into fissures in the walls.

From the basin's low rim, the whole valley appears to simmer under the bright, normal sun—a vast, tight-packed urban expanse of olive, ochre, fresh sap-green, and raw splinter-white, visibly rippling as whole neighborhoods surge upward, shear apart, and fold back into the living substrate. The sky stays calm and pale blue. The deafening roar of splitting wood, hammering sap, and phantom street-clamor rises straight out of the depression like heat off an oven, announcing a metropolis furiously building itself for nobody, out of nothing but light, hoarded moisture, and broken space.
