From the surrounding flats, the place does not exist. The land there is low, scrubby, and almost level, a plain of pale silt and stunted grass lying barely above the water table. You could walk within thirty paces of the rim and see only a shallow crease in the earth where a ring of dark, tightly matted juniper hugs the ground. Only when you step to the edge and look straight down does the earth open into a pocket no wider than a courtyard and perhaps twice as deep—a single collapsed sinkhole in ancient pale limestone, stuffed from floor to lip with a city.

The first thing that strikes you is the light. The sinkhole should be a shadow-pit, yet it is painfully, evenly bright. The vertical walls of the collapse are composed of chalk-white karst streaked with broad, mirror-smooth veins of translucent selenite, scoured flat by epochs of slow frost-heaving. Sunlight dropping into the hollow hits those pale walls and bounces back and forth across the narrow span until every underside of every arch, every narrow stairway, and every recessed doorway is flooded with clean, glare-white radiance. There are no true shadows here, only pale blue-grey tints tucked beneath eaves. It looks like a bright room with its roof removed, though the brightness carries no warmth at all.

Cold pools permanently in the hollow. Because the depression sits so low and its rim is tightly skirted by dense scrub, the wind slides cleanly over the top without dipping in. The heavy, chilled air settles to the bottom and stays there, locked in a thermal dead-zone just a few degrees above freezing. Step over the lip onto the first carved stair and the temperature drops cleanly across your face, crisp and motionless, smelling of wet chalk, crushed evergreen, and old wool. Your breath hangs in a neat white puff right in front of your mouth and takes long, unhurried seconds to thin out, because there is no draft to carry it away.

And there is no sound.

Not the ordinary quiet of an empty place—the hollow is anything but empty—but a thick, physical hush that swallows vibration almost at the source. Every surface that is not bare, weathered stone is muffled under layers of swollen greenery, felted cloth, or damp moss. More than that, the cold dense air inside the pocket seems heavy enough to blunt whatever small noises occur. A person stepping onto a flagstone wears soles stitched from layers of boiled wool; the footfall makes nothing at all. Water never runs fast enough to babble or falls far enough to splash. Even the wind across the plain above is cut off the moment your head dips below the juniper rim. Inside the city, you can hear the faint clicking of your own eyelashes if you blink hard, and almost nothing else.

Look closely, and the sheer crowdedness of the pocket makes the eye ache to sort it out. There is not a hand's-breadth of wasted ground anywhere in the basin. The settlement is built directly into and against the limestone walls in five or six stacked, interlocking tiers, with a dense central cluster of three-story houses rising from the floor of the sinkhole like a tightly packed tooth-root. Alleys between the buildings are barely wide enough for two bundled shoulders to slip past each other sideways. Where a lane cannot fit, a stone staircase climbs straight over a neighbor's roof; where a roof narrows, a timber footbridge pegged together with oak dowels spans the gap to an upper window across the way. Every wall leans slightly against another wall, braced by squat stone arches and thick cedar beams whose bark was adzed off centuries ago.

Nothing looks newly made, and nothing looks ruined. The erosion here has reached a long, slow truce. The limestone stairs are dished smoothly into shallow cradles where generations of soft-soled feet have pressed the exact same spots. Door-lintels carved from slate have lost every sharp chisel-edge, their corners softened to the polish of river stones. Lime mortar has dissolved slightly back from the joints of the masonry, and in its place, roots and mineral crust have locked the stones together more tightly than mortar ever could. Copper rain-gutters and dew-pans—hammered thin by hand, showing the dimpled marks of stone mallets—have oxidized to a thick, matte sea-green that never flakes further. The whole settlement has the settled, slightly slumped permanence of a pile of river pebbles that has already finished shifting.

Over and through every built surface, the vegetation surges in an astonishing, cold-bright lushness.

By ordinary rules, a pocket this cold and bright should be sparse and scrubbed bare. Instead, because the air is never scoured by dry wind and moisture clings constantly to every chill surface, plants have colonized the masonry until architecture and botany are hard to tell apart. Every roof is bowed beneath a mattress of emerald sphagnum and plush haircap moss half a foot thick, glowing neon-green in the bounced limestone light. Retaining walls are quilted solid with overlapping rosettes of waxy saxifrage, dark leathery leaves the size of a palm, and trailing curtains of pale jade stonecrop that hang across lintels like beaded blinds. Dwarf birches and twisted prostrate willows—gnarled as old orchards yet barely waist-high—root directly in the mortar of the upper terraces, their tiny glossy leaves packed tight together. Lichen hangs from the wooden bridge-rails in stiff, pale-gold lace.

What makes the foliage quietly strange is its stillness and texture. Touch a leaf and it is shockingly cold, stiff and turgid as carved wax, swollen with a clear, slightly viscous sap that resists frost. Nothing wilts quickly here, and nothing shoots up fast. The lushness is not the frantic growth of a jungle, but the patient, accumulated wealth of centuries where almost nothing rots and nothing dies back for winter. A cushion of moss on a stair-wall holds the neat three-toed dent of a bird's foot from months ago, slowly filling back in by fractions of a millimeter. Fallen leaves do not turn brown and slimy; they bleach into delicate, papery white skeletons that sit tucked in crevices until they crumble silently into pale dust.

Water is everywhere as a presence and nowhere as a force. There is no stream in the hollow, no fountain, no muddy puddle. Instead, the entire city is permanently damp. Every night, as the pocket cools another degree toward freezing, moisture condenses out of the still air onto the bright limestone walls, the waxy leaves, and the wide hammered-copper dishes angled along every parapet. By morning, every surface is slick with a uniform film of cold dew. Narrow channels no wider than a finger are chiseled along the stone ledges, sloping almost imperceptibly so that beads of water slide along them without a murmur, creeping downward from terrace to terrace into covered stone cisterns sunk beneath the lowest alleys. Each cistern is fitted with a heavy wooden lid swaddled in moss; lift one, and the water inside is black, mirror-flat, and painfully cold, tasting faintly of chalk and green stems. Between the flagstones, the dampness stays locked in the peat-rich moss, yielding a quiet sheen under a pressing heel before springing back dry-looking on top.

Packed into the crevices of this bright, silent greenery is an unexpected abundance of animal life, all of it shaped by the cold hush.

Plump, thick-downed birds the size of clenched fists—white-bellied with moss-grey backs—crowd the eaves and wooden rails in tight rows, puffed up to double their size. They never sing, and when one launches off a stone arch to cross an alley, its flight feathers are fringed with such soft down that it glides through the heavy air without even a whisper of wind. Along the sunny white ledges, fat, short-eared hares with broad furred pads sit motionless beside clay storage jars, chewing waxy saxifrage leaves with slow, soundless sideways motions of their jaws. They barely shift when a person steps right over them.

Look closer into the masonry and you see smaller presences everywhere: sleek, pale-furred voles threading the gaps between mortarless stones; thumb-sized salamanders with translucent, slate-blue skin clinging upside down to the damp undersides of copper dew-troughs; broad, velvety moths with cream-colored wings resting completely flat against white plaster walls, virtually invisible until you notice their feathered antennae trembling slightly in the still cold. Slow, heavy beetles with shells the exact iridescent green of wet moss crawl along the cedar bridges, their feet making no tick against the damp wood. Even the predators fit the pact of quiet—now and then, a long-bodied white stoat slips out of a drainage niche, glides across a mossy roof-ridge like a ribbon of milk, and vanishes into a wall-hole before a single leaf moves.

The people of the hollow are just as tightly fitted into the space, and just as absorbed by its stillness.

Because the basin is so small and so fully built, you are never more than a few paces from another inhabitant, yet there is no bustle. Everyone moves with a measured, unhurried economy, wrapped in thick layers of undyed grey and lichen-green felt, knit leggings, and heavy hooded shawls fastened with carved bone pins. Their hands are reddened by the cold and remarkably steady. On a narrow terraced landing no larger than a tabletop, two women sit knee-to-knee on folded rush mats, scraping the pale mineral crust from the rim of a copper condensation-dish with flint knives; a foot above their heads, on an overhanging balcony, a man carefully thins a mat of edible wintergreen with iron shears, laying each clipped sprig into a woven willow basket without letting a stem drop to the stairs below.

They rarely speak. When they need to coordinate—passing a heavy clay water-jug up a ladder, trading a bundle of dried roots for a spool of sinew—they use crisp, economical hand-signs, slight tilts of the chin, or a gentle tap on the back of a gloved wrist. If words are spoken at all, they are breathed softly at a distance of inches, swallowed immediately by the thick air and the plush walls. Even the children, bundled like round felt bundles on the mossy steps, play silently, arranging pale snail-shells and translucent selenite chips in tight geometric patterns along the damp stone ledges.

Heat is handled with the same careful restraint. There are no roaring fires and no smoke-stained chimneys; rising smoke would hang stagnant in the windless sinkhole and sour the bright air within an hour. Instead, deep inside the thick-walled stone rooms—whose windows are small, recessed, and fitted with thin, hand-scraped panes of horn or clear selenite rather than glass—slow-burning clay vessels hold densely packed bricks of dried moss-peat. They smolder without flame, emitting almost no fumes and no crackling sound, warming the stone benches built around them just enough to take the fatal edge off the chill while leaving the outer alleys crisply cold. Above the doorways hang tiny ceramic vents stuffed with charcoal and dry moss to filter out even the faintest wisp of haze.

Everything in the pocket-city runs on manual craft and patience. Ropes are braided from willow-bark fiber; water-dippers are hollowed from dense birch burls; baskets are woven so tightly from soaked rushes that they can hold dry grain without leaking a single seed. There are no wheels—there is nowhere flat or wide enough to roll one—and no beasts of burden. Everything moves on human backs, or travels vertically up and down the stacked tiers on simple wooden pulleys greased with pale rendered fat so the axles never squeak.

Stay on the steps for an hour, watching the light crawl by inches down the opposite limestone wall, and the strangest quality of the place settles into your bones: nothing feels as though it is on its way to becoming anything else.

Outside, on the flat plain above the rim, wind tears across the grass, storms roll in from the horizon, mud freezes and thaws, and seasons slam into one another. Down inside the bright white pocket, none of it reaches. The sun moves across the narrow disk of sky overhead, shifting the bright glare from the western selenite veins to the eastern ones, and that is almost the only change the day brings. The dew beads at dusk, creeps down the carved stone grooves before dawn, and vanishes slowly into the stone cisterns and the thick green cushions of moss. A pale moth stays on the same square inch of plaster from morning until evening. A hare sleeps in a niche warmed only by its own breath. A wool-wrapped figure lifts a copper pan, tilts a thin silver thread of cold water cleanly into a clay jar without a splash, sets the pan back in its worn ring on the parapet, and sits still again.

If you climb back up the dished limestone stairs and step over the ring of dark juniper onto the open plain, the sudden roar of an ordinary breeze across dry grass feels deafening, almost violent. Turning back, you see only the pale flat earth, the low seam of scrub, and nothing at all to suggest that a few feet below your heels, an entire bright, green, crowded city sits locked in cold perfection, breathing quietly in the glare.
