You know the size of this place long before you ever see an inch of it.

You know it by the wind, which does not blow across a flat surface so much as it pours through a continent-sized instrument. It hits your face thin, dry-cold, and smelling faintly of bruised pine, wet slate, and sulfur, carrying a sound so layered and immense it presses against your breastbone. Low down in the register, there is a hollow, booming drone like air blown across the mouths of a thousand stone wells. Above that runs a shimmering, discordant chord—tens of thousands of taut strings humming in the black air, rising in pitch as the gusts climb, shivering into a high, raw whine whenever frost-load pulls them tighter. And through it all come the sudden, ragged cracks: the snap of ice sheathing breaking loose somewhere half a mile off, the sharp whip-report of a severed cable recoiling across an unseen chasm, the wet slap of enormous membranes shifting in the dark.

There is almost no light to put a face to the noise. The sky overhead is a permanent, high-altitude bruise—an indigo-slate smear thinned by freezing haze, occasionally pale enough along one distant rim to suggest a sun that never quite climbs into view, never bright enough to cast a shadow. What little you can see is mostly silhouette and edge: the dull pewter gleam of rime-ice coating a windward curve, the darker stroke of a colossal arch cutting across the slightly paler void, the faint, cold wink of pale green or sulphur-white along a freshly strained seam of bark where living fibers stretch under a sudden gust.

And there is no ground.

What stands beneath your boots is a curved, pleated ridge of dark, frost-seamed plant tissue forty feet across—one rib among millions in a continental lattice suspended thousands of feet above the earth. If you walk to the edge of the rib and lean out over its lip, looking down into the roar, there is nothing there. Not canopy, not underbrush, not river-gleam. Just a cold, hollow abyss of moving air, dropping away until another giant arch ghosts dimly across the blackness a quarter-mile below, and another far beneath that, like the spars of a sunken cathedral stretched across an entire mountain range.

Almost the whole volume of this world is empty air. Yet every solid thing within it is alive.

### The Architecture of the Void

Far down at the roots of the darkness—sometimes visible only where a razor-edged crest stabs straight up through the lower lattice like a broken tooth—lie the mountains that made this place. They are geologically newborn: raw, unrounded ridges of black shale, fractured basalt, and sharp-edged granite still groaning under slow uplift. Nothing has had time to wear them smooth. Frost gets into every fissure, pries slabs apart, and sends rockfalls clattering unseen into the deep troughs.

No ordinary forest could live on these raw, freezing knives in near-perpetual twilight. Instead, one vast, cold-hardy lineage of mega-flora colonized the rising peaks from the cracks up—and rather than hugging the stone, it vaulted over the emptiness between them.

Its great holdfasts grip the jagged peaks like frozen talons, driving pale, acid-weeping taproots deep into the newly torn crust. Down there, miles below the screaming wind, the young fractures still bleed mineral warmth and sulfurous, iron-rich fluids from the earth’s settling interior. The roots drink that dark chemical heat and pump it upward through pressurized vascular cores, turning the entire aerial expanse above into a sprawling, continent-wide scaffolding fed from below rather than from the sky.

From those anchored peaks, the **great bows** leap outward—hollow, chambered cylinders of tough, resin-dark fibrous wood, some thick enough to house a village inside their segmented cores, arching cleanly across chasms a mile or more wide before fusing into the ribs rising from the next ridge. Between the bows stretch the **guy-vines**: pale-gray cables of braided vascular cordage, some thick as a torso, some thin as a finger, pulled guitar-string tight across the empty air. They propagate by casting sticky, gossamer lead-filaments out on the gales; when a drifting strand snags a distant rib in the dark, it anchors, thickens, lignifies, and contracts, hauling the whole immense web into tension.

Because the plant grows mostly as hollow struts and slender tension-lines, ninety-nine percent of the space it claims remains utterly open. You can hike half a day along the crown of a single bow and feel the sheer, dizzying vacancy on every side—vast cubic miles of freezing night threaded only by the occasional diagonal cable vanishing into the ink.

Yet the structure is never still. The wind leans against miles of braided fiber and sets whole spans heaving in slow, sickening swells of twenty or thirty feet. You learn to walk with your knees bent, feeling the rhythm of the flex rise through your soles. And because the young rock beneath is still restless, the tension is never permanently settled. Once or twice a season, a peak-tooth shears under frost-heave or a tremor; a holdfast loses its purchase, and an entire square mile of arches drops abruptly with a thunderous, rolling *crump-crack*, snapping a dozen secondary guy-vines whose severed ends lash back through the blackness like giant whips until the neighboring bows absorb the load and groan back into balance.

### Water, Frost, and the Breath of the Lattice

There are no lakes here, no streams, and very little true rain. The air is too high and too cold; moisture arrives on the move, carried in thick, drifting banks of freezing mist that spill up from lower valleys or condense where katabatic drafts collide along the upper spans.

The lattice is built to comb that dampness straight out of the wind.

Along the flanks of the great bows sit folded tiers of **mist-sails**—enormous, parchment-thin membranes of dark violet-gray tissue covered in stiff, microscopic bristles. During dry gales, the sails stay furled flat against the bark, locked down by fibrous ribs to spare the bows from wind-shear. But when a wet front rolls in, the humidity swells the hinge-cells along the edges, and mile after mile of sails unfurl in a rolling, deafening wave—a sound like ten thousand wet hides snapping open in unison.

For hours, the darkness turns slick and heavy. The bristled sails rake droplets straight out of the passing fog. Water beads along their ribs, merges into cold trickles, and slides down grooved bark-channels to collect in **cistern-galls**: leathery, melon-sized bladders clustered wherever two giant bows intersect. The water inside is icy, faintly bitter with tannins and mineral trace, often topped with a crunchy skin of slush.

Then the dry wind returns—razor-cold air pouring down off the highest, barest ice-spires. The sails snap shut with hydraulic groans and dry, papery clatters. Everything the mist left wet flash-freezes into a bright, brittle crust of rime. As the wind climbs, the taut cables accumulate thick fins of windward frost, growing heavier and louder until a violent flex shakes the ice loose in cascading sheets. You hear those shards falling for eight, ten, twelve seconds—a bright, tumbling hiss dropping away into the hollow dark—before they shatter against some unseen lower span.

Inside the hollow bows, though, the rules soften slightly. The thick, pleated cellular walls insulate the chambers against the raw gale outside, and the slow upward pulse of mineral-rich sap keeps the interior air a few degrees warmer than the freezing void—still bitingly cold, still smelling sharply of damp resin and iron, but calm, humid, and dripping gently from the inner ceiling. Long, dark wind-tunnels run for miles inside the ribs, punctured every few hundred yards by oval vent-slits where the wind roars past outside and turns the whole chamber into a vibrating, low-throated organ pipe.

### The Active Dark

Empty as the air looks, nothing in this landscape is dormant. The ecosystem moves at the pace of the wind-cycles, frantic during every fog-surge, hunkered down and watchful during the singing gales, threaded through with constant, roaring predation.

Because light is nearly useless, everything that hunts or flees here reads the world through vibration and sound.

Along the leeward flanks of the bows move troops of **clatter-backs**—lean, long-limbed climbers the size of hounds, armored along their spines in overlapping, frost-shedding plates and tipped with curved, split iron-hard claws that bite cleanly into fibrous bark. They sprint upside down or sideways along taut guy-vines in the pitch dark, tapping the hollow ribs rhythmically with toughened knuckles to listen for the muffled hollow pockets where **pipe-grubs** chew through the sweet inner pith. When a troop finds a seam, they chatter and bark in sharp, metallic bursts that echo across the void, tearing open the bark with hooked incisors.

That noise draws the **shroud-gliders**.

They are the true lords of the empty volume: broad-winged, thick-furred predators with leathery flight membranes stretching between elongated ribs and hind legs, and huge, mobile ear-funnels tufted with insulating bristles. They do not flap much in the thin air; instead, they hang folded like dead sails beneath the upper arches, listening to the vibrations humming down the guy-vines. When a clatter-back missteps, or a flock of heavy-downed **vent-birds** pours screaming out of a rib-slit to catch a hatch of woolly sap-moths during a fog-surge, the gliders simply let go.

They drop into the black void silently except for the sudden *shhhk* of wind across stretched hide, ride the upward eddies between the bows, and bank around invisible cables with short, rapid tongue-clicks that bounce back off every taut strand. A glider can sweep in from below a guy-vine, snatch a climber clean off its perch with long, grappling hind-talons, and vanish back into the ink before the rest of the troop finishes its warning shriek.

Lower down, huddled around the junction-joints where cistern-galls swell, cling heavier, slower things: thick-matted, four-eyed **rime-grazers** with flat, chisel-like teeth for cracking the ice off water-bladders and scraping pale, bitter frost-fungi from the damp bark-seams. When a dry gale hits, they clamp all four hooked feet into the bark, tuck their snouts beneath their shaggy bellies, and become indistinguishable from frozen burls until the air softens again.

And every few weeks, when pressure builds inside the upper chambers after a series of wet fronts, the lattice itself erupts into motion. Miles of upper arches split open their vent-crests simultaneously, blasting great, pale-gray clouds of oily, cold-resistant spores into the rushing dark. The air turns thick and gritty; visibility drops to arm’s length; the guy-vines grow treacherously slick. Within minutes, the void fills with the frenzied, whistling swoop of blind filter-wings—flat, translucent-finned drifters that spend dry months dormant inside hollow dead-ends, now tumbling wildly through the spore-fog with wide, bristled mouths, pursued in turn by the clicking, diving gliders.

### Scattered Marks in the Roar

Even here, across this freezing, howling emptiness, small traces of hands and intent persist—sparse enough that you can travel for days without meeting another soul, yet unmistakable once you know how to feel and listen for them.

The people who move along the spans—**Chime-walkers**, sap-tappers, glider-trappers—leave no roads and build no towns. Against a continent of swaying, creaking voids, permanence is a slow way to die. Everything they make is light, lashed, and read by touch or ear.

Where two massive bows cross out of the worst wind-shear, you may suddenly hear a sound that does not belong to the plant: a sharp, rhythmic, three-note clatter cutting cleanly above the deeper groan of the wind-harp. Follow that sound in the near-dark and your fingers will brush a line of split bone-plates and chipped slate slivers suspended from a taut gut-cord—a **way-chime**, tuned to carry half a mile through a howling mist-front. Different pitches mean different things to those who walk the ribs: a double-clack for a tapped, unfrozen cistern; a rapid, rattling trill warning of a frayed or rotting cable ahead; a slow, hollow wooden knock marking shelter.

Tucked deep into the crotch of that junction—or slung *inside* a widened rib-chamber behind a lashed hide baffle—sits a **saddle-hut**. It is small, teardrop-shaped to shed the gales, framed from split cane and dried bow-ribs bent under steam, tied exclusively with braided phloem-cord and pinned with carved bone toggles or the occasional hammered spike of dull, cold-worked copper. The walls are stitched from cured glider-hide, heavily caulked along the seams with blackened sap-pitch and stuffed with matted fur. Inside, suspended by three cords from the roof so the heaving of the bow will not tip it, hangs a shallow stone-lined pan holding a lump of slow-burning resin-peat—rarely flamed openly, just smoldering with a sharp, acrid smell and a tiny, shielded smudge of orange glow barely bright enough to inspect a frayed harness or sharpen a flint scraper.

Along the leeward crown of the major bows, away from the scouring rime, run faint **hand-tallies**: sequences of deep, deliberate notches gouged into the tough outer bark with bone chisels so a traveler in thick mitts can read the distance to the next junction without ever needing to see. Here and there, where a sheer drop separates two unbridged ribs, a thin, hand-knotted rope of twisted vine-fiber and sinew hangs slackly across the gulf, coated in frost, swaying violently in the gusts.

And sometimes, as you edge along a humming bow in the dim indigo-black, you hear a way-chime ringing unevenly—out of rhythm, swinging wildly, fading in and out as the wind twists it. If you lean out carefully over the abyss and squint down into the cold dark, you may just catch the ghost of a shadow turning slowly at the end of a single snapped cable: an old saddle-hut whose anchor-rib broke during some long-ago mountain tremor, now hanging askew over three thousand feet of empty air, its lashed bone-slats still faithfully knocking in the dark, warning nobody, while the great bows overhead groan, flex, and sing on into the cold.
