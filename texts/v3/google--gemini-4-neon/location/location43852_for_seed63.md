You feel the place long before you can make out its shape: a dry, pressing kiln-heat that leaks through the narrow cleft in the mountain's shoulder and strikes the face like air from a brick oven whose embers have died down to ash. Outside, the ridge is high and bare, the air thin enough to make every breath work for its keep. Inside, the air is no thicker, only hot—baked, mineral-sharp, and so utterly stripped of moisture that it pulls the damp straight from the corners of your eyes and dries the tongue in a dozen breaths.

There is almost no light. Forty or fifty feet above, where the eroded stone dome tapers into wind-worn slots, a few hairlines of bruised mountain sky leak through—never enough to reach the floor, only enough to suggest a vaulted ceiling the size of a small cathedral, ribbed with ancient basalt and hardened tuff. After an hour in the dark, the eyes begin to catch a second, even dimmer rumor of light from below: deep inside the narrow fissures splitting the floor, the rock holds a dull, ember-colored smudge so faint it vanishes the moment you look straight at it. Between those two thresholds—the sliver of thin sky and the buried red of the cracks—the chamber is pitch-black, weighed down by heat and crowded to its very seams.

At first it seems silent. Thin air does not carry sound eagerly, and the stone has been rounded and softened by millennia of dry convection until no sharp echo remains. Only when you hold your breath does the true soundscape surface: a vast, continuous, papery rustle, like dry grain sliding inside a clay vessel, layered over the soft, steady sigh of hot air rising from the floor-cracks. Now and then comes a crisp, tiny *tick*—a dry carapace splitting, a salt crystal snapping free from a hot ledge—or the faint whisper of something weightless brushing past your cheek in the dark. Nothing calls. Nothing buzzes. Nothing cries out when it is caught.

You reach out for the wall to steady yourself and discover that there is no bare stone to touch.

Every surface of the vault—walls, overhanging ribs, floor-ledges, even the undersides of the highest arches—is tiled three and four layers deep in living bodies. The rock beneath is completely hidden under overlapping plates of flat, palm-sized grazers with leathery, scalloped backs dusted in gray mineral flour. They pack their beveled edges tightly together like roof shingles, forming a warm, shifting hide over the entire interior of the mountain. Under your palm they do not scatter in panic; they simply press harder against one another, their undersides clamping down with a faint dry friction, reluctant to expose even a millimeter of soft skin to the parching air.

Nothing green grows here. No moss clings to the dark stone, no fern or root threads the cracks, and there is not a drop of open water anywhere in the chamber. The only non-animal growth is a chalky, rust-and-ochre mineral rime—half crystalline salt-bloom, half heat-loving crust—that sweats directly out of the baking basalt along the lips of the floor-fissures, fed by thin volcanic gases rising from below. It tastes bitter, iron-heavy, and sharp as lye. That dry frost is the bedrock of everything alive in the dark.

The shingle-backed grazers move in a slow, relentless vertical tide across the walls. Those in the lowest ranks scrape the fresh salt-crust from the scalding rock with hard, file-like mouthplates, taking in mineral energy and microscopic wisps of chemical film until their bodies approach lethal heat. Then they inch upward, climbing over the backs of their neighbors toward the cooler stone near the high arches, while cooled grazers from above slide downward to take their places. The upper layers shade the lower ones from radiant heat; in return, the lower layers weep tiny, guarded beads of metabolic fluid through dorsal pores, which the riders above siphon dry before the oven-air can steal it.

Water, here, is not a feature of the terrain. It is a currency locked strictly inside flesh.

Because nothing can afford to spill a drop onto the hot stone, the chamber's ecology runs on theft, thrift, and silent precision. Picking their way across the living mosaic of the walls are slender, blind hunters balanced on eight needle-thin legs tipped with pads of dry velvet. They move without a whisper, their broad, concave heads tilting constantly to read the subtle thermal map of the dark. They are hunting for cold. When one of the tiled grazers outgrows its shell and splits its dry seam along the back, the pale, wet tissue exposed beneath loses a fraction of a degree to instant evaporation. In the oven-heat of the vault, that microscopic drop in temperature shines like a beacon. Within seconds, three or four of the stilt-legged stalkers converge silently over the backs of the unmoving tiles, drive hollow, grooved spines into the fresh seam, and drink the creature hollow before its new hide can harden. What remains is a crisp, translucent shell that detaches and drifts downward.

The floor is knee-deep in those shells.

Over centuries, the basin of the chamber has filled with a warm, talc-fine meal of powdered chitin, mineral ash, and hard, chalk-dry pellets. It feels like wading through heated flour, and it never stays level. Slow ridges swell and collapse across the dust as heavy, wrist-thick burrowers swim just beneath the surface—long, articulated tubes of matte, horn-colored segments that plow endlessly through the drift. They have no eyes and no legs, only flared, grinding collars at their fore-ends that scoop up the dry meal, along with any sleeper or fallen grazer unlucky enough to touch the floor. Inside them, stone-lined gizzards crush the husks to squeeze out the last chemically bound moisture, turning the floor into a slowly churning mattress of dry bone-meal and dust.

Even the open air of the nave is packed. Look up—though looking tells you nothing—and lift a hand into the rising thermal column, and your fingers will brush dozens of soft, papery bodies suspended in the dark. They are weightless ribbons and scalloped umbrellas of pale, smoky membrane, fringed with down as fine as soot. They never beat their wings; they simply angle their dry surfaces against the rising heat and ride the convection current in slow, tangled spirals from floor to vault and back again. Thin sensory filaments trail beneath them, tasting both rising heat-plumes and the faint chemical signature of exposed moisture. When two drifters tangle mid-air, there is no struggle you could hear. The larger folds cleanly around the smaller, presses it flat with a dry rustle like turning a page, drains its meager fluid through mouth-slits along its ribs, and releases the spent hull to spin upward toward the ceiling flues.

Anchored high along the stone ribs, wedged into eroded pockets where the basalt arches meet, hang colonies of pale, folded bladders the size of lanterns, textured like dried gourd-skin and tethered by tough, sinewy stalks. They inflate slowly with hot, gas-heavy air drawn from narrow wall-cracks, hold it inside chambers dusted with catalytic iron-powder, and then exhale it in a soundless collapse that ripples out of phase from one arch to the next. In the dark, if your hand rests against a rib, you can feel the whole ceiling pulsing gently against the stone, slow as a sleeping lung.

Yet this is not an untouched wilderness, though whoever came here left almost nothing behind—no metal, no mortar, no trace of any tool harder than struck rock. Just inside the narrow entrance crawlway, three slabs of dark basalt stand wedged upright in the dust, angled to break the outer mountain wind so it will not chill the throat of the chamber. Their edges have been rubbed smooth by eons of draft and shifting hides. Farther along the western wall, half-submerged beneath the migrating carpets of shingle-backs, a line of shallow, thumb-worn footholds has been pecked into a rising rib, leading up to a small shelf where four knee-high cairns still keep their rough, stacked outlines beneath a three-inch crust of living shells. Near the center of the dust-basin, the curved rim of a dry-stone ring juts an inch above the shifting floor-meal, and beside it lies a hollowed block of tuff worn smooth inside like a mortar—filled now to the brim with warm ash and coiled sleepers. On one low pillar, where a seam of hard, glassy rock resists the salt-bloom, a row of vertical grooves has been scraped with a sharp stone, faint and half-erased by centuries of rasping mouths. No fire was ever lit here; the rock is unblackened, and the heat already belonged to the mountain. Whatever brought hands into this hot, crowded dark—to shelter from the high-ridge storms, to gather shed husks, to stack stones in silence beside the breath of the earth—ended long ago, and the chamber has calmly tiled over the memory.

The whole place lives by a single vast pulse, dictated by the sky it barely sees.

During the high-altitude day, the sun hammers the bare black dome of rock outside until the mountain's skin grows nearly as hot as its belly. The temperature gap between the deep floor-fissures and the upper wind-flues narrows, and the chimney stalls. Inside the vault, the rising draft weakens to a sluggish shimmer. Heat pools thick and motionless between the walls. Across the nave, the ribbon-drifters lose their lift and settle in dense, hanging curtains along the stone ribs, hooking themselves shut like folded rags. The tiled grazers lock their scalloped edges tight against the rock, sealing every seam to hoard their moisture; the burrowers sink deep toward the outer edges of the basin where the stone holds a fraction less heat. For hours the chamber holds its breath, locked in an arid, stifling hush broken only by the slow, staggered sigh of the hanging bladders and the faint contraction of baking rock.

Then sunset strips the warmth off the outer peak. Up at this height, the outside air drops toward freezing within an hour while the fissures deep in the floor stay fiercely hot. Across the narrow ceiling slots, cold thin wind begins to shear at speed, pulling hard at the mouth of the dome. Suddenly the pressure catches. The chimney wakes.

A steady, dry rush of superheated air surges up from the floor-cracks, carrying fresh sulfur-dust and painting crisp new beards of bitter white salt along the lower stone. Without a single cry, without a hum or wingbeat, the dark erupts into motion. Hundreds of thousands of drifters unfurl from the ribs at once and rise into the central column in a silent, swirling blizzard of pale membranes. The tiled walls ripple from top to bottom as the grazers unlock their margins and surge downward toward the blooming salt-cracks. Across their crowded backs, the velvet-footed stalkers glide at startling speed, reading every tiny drop of cooling moisture in the rush of air. Beneath your boots, the deep flour of the floor begins to heave and roll as the horn-bodied burrowers climb back into the churning drift.

You stand in the middle of an absolute, stifling dark, breathing air so thin and dry it burns the throat, surrounded on every side, underfoot, and forty feet overhead by more life per square inch than a lowland forest—a whole frantic, ancient, waterless city turning on the breath of a hot mountain, and all of it moving no louder than dust falling through an hourglass.
