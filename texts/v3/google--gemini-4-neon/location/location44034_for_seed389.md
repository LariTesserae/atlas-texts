At this altitude, the sky loses its softness. Overhead it is a hard, clear blue—not dark enough for stars, not bright enough to blind, just steady, ordinary daylight stripped of all haze and kindness. Far below the outer retaining walls, a floor of clouds sits like dirty wool stuffed into a canyon no one can see the bottom of. Between the clouds and the ridge, the air is thin enough to make every breath feel narrow and deliberate. Yet the place is fiercely hot.

The heat does not come only from the sun, though the sun bakes every flat iron roof and ceramic louver until the air above them trembles in clean, vertical ribbons. It rises straight out of the mountain itself. The whole highland—a sprawling, multi-crested saddle three miles across, cradled between two jagged rims—is new rock, pushed upward out of the crust so recently that it has not yet learned how to crumble. Nothing here is rounded. Where raw stone shoulders up through the foundations of the city, its faces are gleaming black-green obsidian and striated grey basalt, scored with long vertical drag-marks like clay squeezed from a die. The talus piled in the corners of freight yards is not gravel, but fresh, razor-edged splinters that ring like broken plates if a boot nudges them. In the deepest seams between the retaining piers, the rock is still too hot to lay a bare hand against.

Clamped across this raw, rising highland is a city of dense, shoulder-to-shoulder industry.

From the upper switchyard rim, the whole basin opens out in a single, dizzying sweep—miles of terraced infrastructure packed into every fold of the emerging terrain. It is layered five, eight, sometimes twelve levels deep: stacked kiln-houses of dark slag-brick, narrow iron stair-streets bolted directly to sheer rock faces, bundled copper mains as thick as waistlines suspended from chains of pale porcelain insulators, and riveted cylindrical stills packed as tightly as jars in a pantry. Numbered viaducts leap the interior ravines in double and triple decks, carrying rail-guided ore sleds and insulated pipe-trains from one ridge to the next. Everywhere you look, structures lean close enough to share shadows, braced against one another with cross-struts, catwalks, and expansion joints. There is barely an arm’s length of empty wall that does not carry a conduit, a pressure gauge, a ceramic baffle, or a chalked tally mark.

Yet when you stand on a high walkway and listen, the city is almost silent.

There is no roar of furnaces, no hammering of pistons, no shrill whine of turbines. In the thin, high air, sound dies quickly, and the porous, freshly cooled basalt beneath the streets drinks up vibration like ash. More than that, the machinery itself does not hurry. This is an industry built on expansion and patience.

Running the length of the sun-facing ridges, resting in oiled stone troughs, are blackened iron stretch-beams hundreds of yards long. Across the hot, dry morning, they drink the light and lengthen by inches; as the afternoon shadow crawls over them from the western crag, they shrink back. That imperceptible creep drives the city. At the ends of the beams sit colossal, silent ratchets and compound levers. Every ten or fifteen minutes, somewhere in the tight tangle of alleys below, a heavy iron pawl drops into a new tooth with a single, dry *tock*. A winch turns a fraction of a revolution. A rail-car loaded with annealed glass blocks edges three feet closer to the sorting house. Giant counterweights of cast lead hang inside hollow brick towers, descending the thickness of a coin per hour to turn the paddles inside covered electrolytic vats. The vats themselves emit only a faint, papery fizz, audible only if you press your ear close to the stoneware rim.

The strangest thing about the heat and the altitude is the dampness tucked inside them.

Out on the upper gantries and sun-struck terraces, the air is scorching and parched; your lips dry out in minutes, and exposed iron blisters bare skin. Drop down two flights of bolted stairs into the shaded under-galleries, however, and the climate flips. Beneath the viaducts and inside the cramped canyons between boiler walls, the air is heavy, warm, and slick. Great vertical curtains of fluted zinc plates hang from the undersides of bridges and over geothermal fissures, catching the mineral-rich steam venting from cracks in the new rock as well as the thin wisps of moisture that drift upward from the cloud-floor far below. Water beads continually along these dark, shadowed fins, gathering into warm, sluggish threads that drip into lead-lined gutters running along the curbs.

The masonry of the lower streets is permanently dark with sweat. Puddles sit in the recessed bolt-holes of deck plates, tepid and faintly oily with dissolved silica. Because the water is so heavily freighted with minerals, it behaves strangely as it dries: wherever a leaky flange drips steadily onto a hot pipe or a ledge, pale hollow tubes of glassy salt and gypsum grow downward overnight like fragile icicles. Now and then, a thermal shudder runs through the ironwork, and half a dozen of them snap at once—a tiny, distant shower of crystalline *tinks* echoing down a dim corridor.

There are no plants anywhere in the basin. Not a tuft of dry grass in a mortar crack, not a smudge of lichen on the dampest brick, not a single root or green leaf across the whole three-mile breadth of the city. Where moisture lingers on warm stone, the surfaces bloom instead with delicate mineral growths: fuzzy white carpets of alum needles, brittle fan-shaped crusts of blue copper sulfate, hair-thin whiskers of sulfur that sway faintly in the rising convection currents. In the warmest drainage sumps beneath the condenser blocks, a slick, colorless film coats the submerged lead—perhaps a simple thermophilic slime, perhaps only an oily precipitate; nothing moves in it, and it leaves no smell beyond hot wet slate and pennies.

Beneath all this iron and brickwork, the ground is not finished moving.

Every major building in the city rests not on fixed foundations, but on massive threaded collars of cast steel—screw-jacks as wide as millstones seated directly into sockets drilled into the young rock. The mountain is still extruding, lifting the basin floor upward by a hand’s-breadth each season, unevenly, with a slow, glacial insistence. Every few weeks, the great collars are turned a quarter-rotation—some by silent, pendulum-triggered gears, others by solitary attendants who appear briefly in the alleys wearing wide tin-brimmed hats and pale, mineral-stiffened linen coats, moving slowly to spare their lungs in the thin hot air.

And the rock coming up out of the earth is not ordinary stone.

Across the central floor of the basin, where the newest extrusion pushes up between the refining blocks in raw, stepped domes, enormous braided copper grounding cables plunge straight into fresh magma-warm fissures. The city does not merely harvest the mountain; it runs a continuous, low-voltage direct current straight down into the plastic stone miles below. In response, the emerging basalt rises already half-organized. Split open a fresh slab from the rising floor and you find the stone riddled with precise hexagonal cavities, veined with natural filaments of pure bright tin and clear, bubbleless glass—as if the earth’s interior were slowly being drawn through an electrical loom, learning geometry before it ever sees daylight. The factories do not mine with explosives; they wait for the ground to lift new ribs of patterned mineral up into the reach of their slow, cable-drawn saws.

Animal life is sparse enough that you can walk six terraces and see nothing move but the faint twitch of a pressure needle and the drift of shimmering air. Yet the place is not completely empty of creatures.

Look up past the ceramic insulators and the forest of brick vent-stacks, into the clean, unblinking sky, and once in an hour you might catch a slow shadow sliding across a sun-baked walkway. High above the basin, riding the silent pillars of hot air rising off the cooling towers, wheel thin, four-winged gliders the color of oiled vellum. Their bodies are little more than slender grey ribs stretched inside translucent, papery membranes. They never flap; they simply tilt their wingtips by fractions of an inch, circling effortlessly for hours in the thin air, nearly invisible until they bank against the deep blue zenith and catch the daylight like mica. At dusk, or when a venting plume turns too dense, they fold themselves flat and slip into the mouths of idle chimneys to roost.

Lower down, in the warm, sweating dark beneath the viaducts, live rarer, slower things. Clamped into the damp angle where a steam-return pipe meets a cast-iron elbow, you may notice a flanged bulge that does not match the bolts beside it: a flattened, palm-sized animal covered in overlapping plates that look remarkably like glazed grey stoneware. It moves at the city’s own speed—an inch every few minutes—inching across the wet metal on a pale, rubbery underside, rasping away the freshly precipitated alum needles and sulfur crusts with a hard, metallic ridge inside its mouth. If a footstep vibrates along the iron catwalk above, it pulls its plates flush against the pipe and goes motionless, indistinguishable from a mineral-encrusted fitting until the stillness returns.

As the sun arcs cleanly across the middle of the sky, the light shifts across the tiered city with surgical clarity, turning one face of every brick stack brilliant ochre while dropping the alley beside it into cool, damp slate. Somewhere across the three-mile basin, a mile-long stretch-bar finishes an hour’s silent lengthening. A counterweight settles a fraction of an inch. A distant valve breathes a short, white plume of vapor into the thin sky, where it vanishes almost before it can drift clear of the roof. Beneath the bolted stairways, beneath the sweating zinc fins and the humming electrolytic vats, the hot, jagged, unborn stone inches upward toward the daylight, carrying the whole hushed metropolis with it, millimeter by patient millimeter.
