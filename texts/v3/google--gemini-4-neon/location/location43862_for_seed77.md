Everywhere you set a foot, the ground was laid there.

Beneath the sole there is no bedrock pushing through, no wild loam, no old root-mat—only tamped silt, graded clay, and thin skimmed crusts of pale lime-slurry, spread flat and left to bake. The basin stretches out so low and wide that the horizon reads like a drawn line, faintly softened by heat. It is all floor: a continental plain built up in shallow lifts, layer over layer, still in the middle of curing.

The newest ground is the color of wet river-bottom, dark umber and glossy where water still beads on the surface. A hundred paces over, yesterday’s work has already turned the shade of raw linen, spiderwebbed with hairline shrinkage cracks. Beyond that, the older aprons—older by a season, perhaps, no more—have bleached to chalky buff and bone. You can trace the progress of the basin’s making purely by these broad, rectangular bands of color shifting slowly toward the sky’s pale glare. Nothing has had time to weather. Every corner on a brick is still razor-crisp; every low embankment still carries the long, parallel drag-marks of wooden screeds and the faint, scalloped prints of wet mud patted down by hand.

There are no machines here, no iron, no wheels, no towers. What rises above the flat does so only to waist-height or shoulder-height, and only at wide intervals—miles of bare, leveled apron separating one low presence from the next. Out in the distance, a solitary stepped cistern squats like a pale slab floating above a band of heat shimmer. Farther still, a long retaining dike runs arrow-straight until the curve of the air swallows it. Between them lie huge, unhurried vacancies: acres of smooth clay-bed, empty save for an occasional shallow mixing-pit, a heap of crushed shell-lime waiting to be slaked, or a loose grid of sun-bricks set on edge with finger-width gaps between them so the dry air can draw their moisture out.

To keep a plain this immense true without instruments, the ground is scored at vast intervals with water-leveling trenches—narrow, hand-cut troughs no wider than a palm and deep as an ankle, lined with dark compacted clay. Filled with thin standing water, they glint across the pale flats like fine silver threads, revealing by the slightest lap or dry margin where a terrace sits half a thumb too high. In the midday heat they slowly shrink away, leaving damp dark ribbons of mud that crack before dusk.

The heat is steady, heavy, and close to the ground. Sunlight—ordinary, clear daylight, neither harsher nor dimmer than anywhere else under a wide pale sky—strikes the buff surfaces and bounces straight back up into the face. Shadows tuck themselves tightly beneath the lips of unfinished brick courses and inside the sunken catchment basins. There is nothing tall enough to make real shade; the only shelter from the glare is where bundles of cut reeds have been woven into loose mats and pegged flat over freshly poured lime-plaster to keep it from blistering before it sets.

Yet for all its baking breadth, the basin is not truly dry. Moisture moves through it constantly, in small, deliberate pockets and brief, sweeping cycles.

Because the leveled plain is large enough to build its own weather, the heat does not simply sit still. By early afternoon, silent columns of warm air lift off the pale clay, and thirty or forty miles away—visible from the waist up over the ruler-straight horizon—tall, slow cumulonimbus clouds begin to tower. Sometimes they drift off without breaking. Other times, dark gray aprons of rain detach themselves and sweep briskly across the low ground.

When a squall crosses a stretch of curing flat, the transformation is immediate. The pale chalky floor darkens to deep chocolate within seconds. A thin, fragrant mist rises straight off the hot brick and lime, smelling sharply of wet terracotta, chalk dust, and warm mud. Rainwater does not puddle randomly here; the whole plain has been graded to almost imperceptible slopes, dropping inches to the mile. As soon as the surface saturates, a bright, milky sheet of runoff slides smoothly across the open aprons, gathering into shallow chamfered runnels and pouring silently over low clay lips into sunken rectangular settling-pans. Within an hour the storm has moved on, the sun returns, and the open expanses begin steaming dry again—leaving behind full, opaque cisterns and broad damp halos around the feet of every wall.

Night brings a second, quieter wetting. The vast low basin loses its heat quickly under clear darkness, and before dawn the air cools past its dew-point. The porous bricks and rough-faced lime walls sweat heavily. Beads of condensation run down shaded vertical faces and collect in narrow drip-troughs cut along their bases, trickling down into covered sumps. Where water lingers and evaporates day after day along the lower brick courses, delicate white crusts of salt-bloom crystallize overnight, looking like frost until the next afternoon shower dissolves them back into the clay.

It is in these damp seams—along the weeping toe of a dike, in the shaded inner corners of a catchment basin, inside the shrinkage cracks that never quite dry out—that life manages to arrive. Sparse, low, and stubborn, the vegetation belongs entirely to the margins. Pale, fleshy glasswort creeps out from mortar joints; wiry tufts of salt-grass and tough little sedges push up along the edges of the leveling-trenches; in the permanent shadow beneath an overhanging brick lip, a thin velvet of olive-green moss and dark algae coats the wet clay. Occasionally, where reed matting was laid over damp slurry and left too long, a few dormant stalks have struck root straight into the ground they were meant to shade, sending up thin, dust-coated shoots before the next dry week withers them back to pale wire. Nothing grows taller than the knee. Across miles of open apron, a single low rosette of creeping spurge, pale green against acres of buff clay, can draw the eye from a hundred yards away.

Nothing moves among the plants. There are no birds crossing the wide sky, no lizards slipping into the stacked bricks, no beetles or flies hovering over the warm standing water. The rain-filled cisterns are eerily clear once their silt settles—pale mineral pools undisturbed by a single darting shadow or surface ripple. Walk along the soft margin of a freshly screeded mud-bed and you will find no tracks of small feet, no burrow-mouths, no shed skins.

Without animals, without trees to catch the wind, without machines, the basin holds an enormous, breathable silence. When air moves across the flats, it travels almost soundlessly over the smooth grades, making only a dry, papery whisper where it brushes a solitary clump of sedge or lifts the frayed edge of a reed mat. Inside that stillness, small mechanical sounds of earth and water carry far: the tiny, crisp *tick* of a sun-struck brick cracking along an internal seam; the soft hiss of the first raindrops striking hot lime; a slow, spaced *plip* of dew falling from a shaded ledge into a half-full basin. When thunder rolls from one of the distant horizon storms, it arrives so muffled by twenty miles of warm air that it is felt less in the ears than as a faint weight against the ribs.

Nowhere does the basin feel abandoned, and nowhere does it feel finished. A brick course ends cleanly mid-row, its last mortar bed still cool and slightly yielding to a thumb pressed into the shade. A stack of fresh clay loaves waits under damp matting, darkened with moisture that has not yet escaped. Far off across the flat pale miles, a fresh band of dark umber clay lies gleaming in the sun—spread thinly, graded to the horizon, slowly lightening toward bone as the afternoon clouds begin to gather again at the edge of the sky.
