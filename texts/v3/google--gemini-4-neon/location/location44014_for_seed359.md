You feel the heat first, before your eyes adjust to how crowded everything is—a dry, holding warmth, like stepping into a baker’s courtyard an hour after the ovens have been drawn. Every surface gives it back. Terracotta tiles, lime-washed brick, pale cedar lintels, stacked clay planters: all of it is warm to the palm and utterly bone-dry. There is no moisture in the air, no dampness in the mortar, not a smear of dew or dark stain of seepage anywhere. Even the air in your nostrils tastes of chalk dust, toasted flax, and the sharp resin of freshly adzed timber.

The place is small, held within a perimeter wall of hand-molded brick no more than a hundred paces across in any direction, yet it refuses to feel open. It sits low, sunk into a tight, terraced bowl of courtyards, narrow walkways, covered loggias, and stepped platforms that crowd against one another until you must turn your shoulders to pass between a plastered column and a wooden trellis. There is no raw earth beneath your feet, no bedrock, no natural slope. Every inch is laid, pegged, mortised, plastered, or knotted by hand. Pry up a hexagonal floor tile and you will not find soil; you will find a neatly screeded bed of fine gypsum sand over an older course of brick, and beneath that, the flat beam-work of a lower ceiling. Everything looks startlingly new. The brick edges are still razor-sharp, dusted with pale kiln-flour. The lime plaster bears the crisp crescent sweeps of wooden trowels and the clear whorls of thumbprints pressed into it when it was wet—though nothing here has ever been wet.

Out of this parched masonry pours an extravagant, tangled abundance of plant life.

It hangs in heavy curtains from pegged eaves, erupts from mortar seams, coils thick as a wrist around hand-carved railings, and roofs over the narrow alleys in layered canopies. Because the light is ordinary—a clear, steady, mild-afternoon brightness falling evenly from a pale parchment-colored sky—your eye expects the sheen of watered leaves. Instead, everything that grows here is dry to the marrow. Broad fronds of a matte sage-green feel like oiled vellum between your fingers; when they brush one another, they give off a faint, papery whisper. Thickets of jointed reeds, pale as bleached bone, sprout directly from the dry gaps between paving stones, their hollow stems filled not with sap but with a warm, cinnamon-scented air. Vines bearing leaves like hammered copper foil spill out of wheel-thrown clay urns, heavy with clustered blossoms made of stacked, wafer-thin mineral petals that shed slow plumes of ochre dust. Snap a thick stem and nothing bleeds. A few dry seeds shaped like miniature wooden wedges rattle out onto the flagstones, and the broken end seals itself within minutes into a neat, bevel-cut joint.

For a place so tightly packed with foliage and living things, it is extraordinarily quiet.

There is no wind to speak of, only slow thermal drifts rising off the warm tiles. The silence is thick and powdery, cushioned by the porous clay walls and the dense mats of dry leaves. Yet nothing is still. Look closely at any ledge, cornice, or tangle of stems and the whole place is teeming.

Dozens of flat-bodied lizards—no longer than a thumb, scaled in overlapping plates that resemble tiny cedar shingles—bask motionless along the warm plaster. They do not dart when you pass. Instead, one will slowly flatten itself against the wall until its shadow disappears, its shingle-scales paling to match the limewash, and then it will slide seamlessly *into* the plaster, leaving only a faint, painted outline that creeps an inch to the left over the course of an hour before puffing back out into three dimensions beside a wooden door-latch. In the mortised rafters overhead, small dust-colored birds nest by the hundreds, weaving their hollows from unraveled hemp cord and dry reed-shavings. They never sing. Every so often one tips its head back, opens a pale horn beak, and exhales a small, crisp ring of white chalk dust that drifts downward through the sunbeams, holding its geometry until it settles onto a leaf.

Across the floor tiles, pale ceramic-shelled beetles the size of walnuts trundle slowly through the dry leaf-litter, gathering fallen splinters, copper pins, dried blossom-wafers, and bits of knotted string. They cement these onto their own backs with a dry, resinous paste until each beetle resembles a tiny, ambulatory basket or a moving tile-shard. When two meet in the shadow of a bench, they pause face to face and patiently trade parts—a splinter for a bead of baked clay, a chipped wing-case for a bone toggle. Occasionally one trades a leg for a dry reed-joint and walks off on it without breaking stride, the reed flexing neatly at the knee.

Larger things move through the deeper thickets, though always with a languid, sun-drugged discretion. Slender browsers the size of lean dogs step silently along the stepped parapets on padded, felt-soft hooves; their hides have the taut, sunlit translucence of stretched sheepskin, and through their ribs you can see bundles of tightly lashed twigs instead of organs, rising and falling in a single breath every minute or two. They graze on the tips of shadows. Where a trellis casts a dark diamond across a bench, one of these creatures lowers its narrow muzzle and nibbles the corner away, leaving the light intact while the shadow stays ragged until afternoon wears on.

This is where you begin to notice that the place does not hold together the way a place should.

At first it seems like small tricks of craft and perspective. You walk down a flight of six brick steps into a sunken court, turn through a low wooden archway draped in parchment vines, walk down six more steps, and step out onto the upper terrace you just left—not above it, not beside it, simply *onto* it, your own footprint in the gypsum dust still crisp a yard ahead of your boot. The perimeter wall is always close, never more than a stone’s throw away, yet the alleys between the central courtyard and that wall contain more rooms, alcoves, and trellised galleries than could ever fit inside the masonry ring. Space here is not stretched; it is *pleated*, folded like thick linen, so that two courtyards can occupy the same patch of warm afternoon without colliding, separated only by a woven reed screen you can hear a beetle ticking behind.

And the categories of things have gone soft at the edges.

In one narrow lane, a row of freshly thrown clay amphorae sits along a bench in the full light. The furthest one is ordinary terracotta, wheel-ridges sharp and clean. The next has sprouted two curled, leathery tendrils from its handles, clutching the mortar of the wall behind it. The third has softened into a ribbed, dry-skinned gourd while keeping its fired-clay lip and painted ochre band; and the fourth has split cleanly down the seam, spilling out not seeds, but a clutch of neatly knotted hemp coils and three half-formed wooden spoons that shift slightly when the light warms them. Across the alley, a pile of mortised cedar beams—stacked neatly as if waiting for a roofer who just stepped away—has begun to bud. Tiny secondary struts emerge from the chisel-cut slots, branching into smaller, mathematically precise joinery twigs that end in flat, square leaves of planed pine, each pierced by a miniature peg-hole.

No builder is ever seen, yet the work continues with excruciating slowness. If you stare long enough at a bare patch of brick wall, nothing appears to move—but return an hour later and a fresh course of mortar has been laid along the top, still stamped with the smooth swipe of a wooden trowel, though the air remains dry as ash. Shadows arrive before the objects that cast them. On a sunny flagstone terrace, the sharp dark silhouette of a three-legged stool lies flat against the clay tiles, empty air above it; dry vines creep toward the edges of the shadow, climbing an invisible rung, and over the course of days the air inside the silhouette thickens, growing dusty, then fibrous, then faintly grain-lined, until a rough hand-turned stool stands there solidly, warm to the touch, its wood smelling newly shaved.

Even cause and sound have drifted slightly loose from one another. Watch a pale clay pot get nudged off a high sill by the slow tail of a shingle-lizard: it tumbles through the dry leaves, strikes the hexagonal tiles below, and bursts into seven sharp, crisp shards in absolute silence. Nothing flinches. The dust settles. Four minutes later, from the cool shade inside a hanging reed basket across the courtyard, comes a soft, delayed *clack-tink*, gentle as a knuckle tapping dry bone.

In the very center of the lowest court stands a waist-high well-head built of curved bricks, capped with a mortised wooden frame and a rope of hand-twisted flax that hangs down into the shaft. If you lean over the warm brick rim, there is no darkness inside and no water. The shaft is only ten feet deep, and at its bottom is another identical well-head, upside down, opening onto a bright, clear sky of pale parchment light. Dry copper-leafed vines from that lower courtyard grow upward into this one, tangling tightly with the vines descending from here, so that halfway down the throat of the well it is impossible to say which direction is down. A chalk-breathing finch drops off the rim, glides three feet into the shaft, flips casually onto its back without flapping, and perches upright on the underside of the far lip, peering up at you with calm black eyes.

If you climb one of the steep, narrow brick staircases to the highest parapet—ducking beneath trellises so heavy with paper-leaves that the warmth feels almost liquid—and look out over the outer wall, there is no wasteland beyond, no forest, no sea, no distant ridge. Directly outside the brick rim lies the exact same small, dense, terracotta-and-timber courtyard, rotated a quarter-turn, bathed in the same steady afternoon light. You can see the well-head, the stacked cedar beams, the bench of amphorae turning into gourds. On the far parapet of that outer copy stands a figure leaning forward on its elbows, looking out over the wall—only it is facing the wrong way, or perhaps it is looking five minutes ago, before you climbed the stairs.

Nothing hurries here. The sun does not visibly travel across the pale sky, though over long hours the light shifts a shade warmer, leaning from parchment-white toward toasted straw and back again in a cycle so gradual it feels like breathing. The fresh plaster never ages into ruin; instead, old courses of masonry slowly forget they are stone and loosen into mats of woven cane, while dry roots thicken and square their corners until they are joists supporting a new balcony. Shingle-lizards sink into the walls and emerge on the far side of courtyards they never crossed. Chalk dust hangs in the still, warm air, settling with infinite patience onto leaves that are almost clay, onto clay that is almost bark, onto timber that remembers how to grow—everything hand-fitted, everything bone-dry, everything quietly coming undone and stitching itself back together in the warm, crowded hush.
