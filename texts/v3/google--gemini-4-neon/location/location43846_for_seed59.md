You hear the basin before you see it: not a single roar, but a wet, grinding uproar that rises out of the mountain flank like steam from a kettle—falling water, a thousand wooden pulleys groaning under load, the heavy thump of water-driven mallets, and over all of it a frantic, shifting racket of birds and voices.

Then the trail drops over a lip of moss-furry slate, and the whole town is below you, wedged tight into a steep-walled stone bowl no wider than a couple of bowshots across.

It is high country here, cool enough that the air bites at your knuckles and hangs in pale ribbons off the breath of anyone hauling a load uphill. Clouds snag constantly on the jagged rim above, rolling in fast grey sheets that break open ten minutes later to throw sharp, clean mountain daylight straight down into the hollow. When the sun strikes, the entire bowl flashes wet. Nothing in it is dry, and almost nothing in it is bare.

From rim to mire, the basin is choked with living plant mass. Centuries ago—long enough that the stone walls have weathered smooth beneath their green pelts and the oldest cedar trunks are as thick around as cottages—a pocket of temperate cloud-forest took hold in this sheltered spring-head. Now the settlement does not sit *in* the woods; it is woven *through* them, six and seven stories deep, filling the cathedral-sized hollow like a hornet’s nest built inside a living bush.

There is almost no flat ground to build on. At the bottom of the bowl, where the seepage from a hundred rim-springs collects, the floor is a cold, dark braid of root-knots, gravel bars, and sunken peat-pools. So the town climbs. Heavy pilings of charred oak and bog-cedar are driven down between the living buttress roots, wedged tight with river cobbles and lashed with wet rawhide that shrinks like iron as it dries. From those footings rise tiered galleries, narrow planked alleys, cantilevered workshops, and overhanging sleeping-lofts, all bolted into the living trunks with mortised beams or slung between boughs on thick ropes of braided willow and hemp.

The plants do not submit to the carpentry; they swallow it and become part of it. Railings are made of live hazel and alder saplings, bent and braided together while young so they keep growing in place, sprouting tight pale leaves along the banisters every spring. Roofs are pitched steep and buried under thick, overlapping mats of living sedge, moss, and creeping thyme—heavy enough to keep out the wind and cold drizzle, and so thoroughly colonized by wild ferns and mountain sorrel that from the upper rim the town looks like a tumbled hillside of shaggy green humps pierced by crooked chimneys and flapping woollen door-curtains. Even the dry-stacked slate retaining walls along the cliff-sides are furred two inches deep in emerald liverwort and dripping saxifrage. Exposed dead wood lasts only a few seasons in this damp; anything that isn't kept greased with tallow, smoked over peat, or alive with sap softens, swells, and turns into substrate for shelf-fungi and moss.

Water runs through the entire place like blood through veins. It pours off the cliff rim in dozens of thin, cold falls that never stop, only thicken when a squall blows across the pass. Half of those falls are caught mid-air in hollowed cedar troughs and split into an intricate wooden plumbing network that zigzags across the tiers. Water rushes down open flumes right past people’s elbows, splashes over overshot paddle-wheels wedged into the narrow alleys, fills stone-lined holding troughs for dyeing wool and soaking hides, and drops down vertical bark spouts to drive grain-stamps and fulling-hammers down in the lower mire. Everywhere you walk, water drips from the bridge above onto your shoulders, beads along the underside of every lintel, and runs in shiny silver threads down the grain of worn floorboards. People cut cross-hatched grooves into the planks with adzes every few months just to keep boots and hooves from skating off into the gap below.

And the place is *crowded*—not just with timber and foliage and people, though you can barely stretch both arms in the market-lanes without knocking a basket of smoked fish off a hook or brushing someone’s wet cloak, but with animals.

Fauna doesn't inhabit the margins here; it swamps the settlement. The hollow acts as a windbreak and a larder at this elevation, and every species in the range knows it. Hundreds of thousands of cliff-swifts, slate-backed starlings, and noisy mountain jays pack the upper canopy and the eaves of every loft. They nest inside the sod roofs, in hollow beam-ends, in clay pots purposely hung under the walkways to collect their eggs and feathers. Every time a goshawk cuts across the sunlit circle of sky above the bowl, a huge chunk of the town seems to detach itself and explode upward—a roaring, swirling dark cloud of wings that blocks the light for five seconds and rains wet droppings and shaken water down through all seven levels.

Down in the dimmer, cooler tiers, the animal traffic is just as relentless. Shaggy, short-legged mountain goats pick their way up and down the slatted ramps with absolute composure, climbing straight over sleeping dogs and nudging past porters to strip fresh shoots off the living willow-railings. Ducks and geese paddle through the sunken drainage gutters and flap heavily between the lower decks, quarrelling over spilled barley and slugs. Thick-bodied snails the size of walnuts plaster the damp undersides of every bridge. Whenever you lift a coil of wet rope or unlatch a shutter, cold-skinned highland salamanders and little mottled cloud-frogs scatter out of the moss-chinks. Sleek, dark, river-wet martens slip along the water-flumes in broad daylight, chasing rats that are nearly as bold as the cats; and in the swirling, moss-rimmed catch-pools at the bottom of the bowl, where the town’s kitchen scraps wash down through woven reed screens, cold-water trout lie stacked shoulder to shoulder like dark stones in the current. In the warmer afternoon breaks, clouds of midges and long-legged crane-flies dance in the bright shafts cutting between the cedar boughs, keeping the swifts darting three feet over people’s heads with an audible *snap-snap* of beaks.

Because of all this—the tight space, the relentless moisture, the speed at which roots heave and wood rots, the sheer press of mouths and hooves and wings—the town never settles into stillness. It runs on anthill energy, a perpetual, jostling turbulence.

Look down into the central well on any ordinary midday and there is no pause anywhere. At the third tier, four carpenters in greased leather aprons are hanging from hemp harnesses to pry out a split spruce girder that has gone spongy with bracket-fungus; they haul a fresh adzed trunk into place with wooden block-and-tackle while pedestrian traffic refuses to wait, squeezing single-file past their boots along a swinging, three-plank bypass. Twenty feet below them, a rope-hoist groans as two men crank a slatted crate of squealing piglets and peat-bricks up from the gorge-gate, swinging it past an awning where a woman is scraping damp sheepskins with a bone blade and shouting down at a boy whose goat just ate half a bundle of dried nettle-fiber. A sudden gust slams down over the northern lip, whipping the high cedar crowns sideways; a shower of cold stored rainwater dumps off the canopy all at once, making everyone duck and curse, slamming oiled-parchment window flaps shut, and sending a wave of startled pigeons bursting out from beneath the miller’s chute.

Everything here is made by hand, with simple iron, stone, bone, wood, and cord, and everything shows the constant friction of use. You see the adze-marks on every railing, the shiny polished dip in the middle of every stone step, the thick layers of repairs—wooden cleats nailed over cracked joints, fresh green willow withes twisted right over blackened old bindings that are starting to fray, stone weights hung from leather thongs to keep swinging sluice-gates tight against the current. There is no smooth metal machinery, no glass, no dry plaster; where a window needs light without wind, greased hide is stretched over a bent-twig frame, glowing a warm amber when a tallow lamp burns behind it.

Smells layer as thickly as the walkways: cold crushed fern and wet peat; sharp cedar resin from the saw-pits; woodsmoke—alder and bog-turf—curling out of crooked stone-and-clay flues and hanging low when the mist presses down; the sour tang of oak-bark tanning vats; wet goat wool; hot barley-mash; and underneath it all, the unmistakable earthy funk of guano, wet feathers, and rich, black leaf-mold.

Life in the bowl follows strict, unspoken rules dictated by the wetness and the crush. You do not block a flume or let silt back up a gutter, because ten minutes of backed-up cold water can wash out the peat-footing under three neighbors’ kitchens. You prune your share of the living framework every week—hacking back the vigorous alder suckers and creeping brambles before their thickening stems pry apart a doorway or snap a dry-stone corner—and the trimmings go immediately to the goats, the basket-weavers, or the smoking-fires. You store grain and dry wool only in the highest, draftiest lofts up near the sunlit upper limbs, hoisted onto stone-capped staddle-posts greased with pine tar so neither the damp nor the climbing voles can reach them. And you never throw waste down without checking the bell-rope or shouting, because there is always another roof, another laundry-line, or another face twelve feet beneath your boots.

When the mountain weather turns truly sharp and a cloud-bank sits right down on the rim, the bowl pulls shut like a room. Mist seeps through the high branches in slow, cold eddies, dampening the bright daylight to a flat silver glow. The birds cram themselves tight under the mossy eaves, still muttering and rustling by the thousands; the braziers in the tiny workshops brighten, throwing orange patches across wet green planks; the wooden water-hammers keep up their steady *thud-clack, thud-clack* down in the fog at the bottom of the well; and the whole shaggy, dripping, overcrowded little city keeps creaking, growing, leaking, and hammering on, held snugly in the cold green fist of the mountain.
