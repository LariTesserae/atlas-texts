The ward sits at the bottom of a narrow, steep-walled basin, sealed off from the open sea by shelves of dark concrete and older basalt, no wider across than a cathedral nave and its cloisters, though it drops nearly as deep. Overhead, far above the tallest brick chimneys, the water thickens into a lid of turbid grey silt and seasonal shelf-ice that admits almost nothing of the sky. By the time daylight reaches the upper gables it has been spent; down among the cobbles and archways there is only a heavy, saturated darkness, cold enough to stiffen rubber and slow the blood.

The water here is held just below the freezing point of fresh water by salt and depth. It feels dense on the skin, almost oily in its chill, and it fills every volume without exception—every stairwell, workshop, cistern, and furnace flue. Only in the highest vaulted rooms and beneath the iron awnings of the narrow lanes do small lenses of ancient gas remain trapped, pressed flat against the masonry like puddles of silver mercury, quivering whenever a pressure wave moves through the basin.

Because the basin is small and walled in, the builders long ago packed everything tightly together, and the drowning did not scatter it. Tenements of dark, vitrified brick lean close across alleys scarcely two arm-spans wide. Overhead, the lanes are roofed in layers of iron infrastructure: steam conduits now full of brine, catwalks of perforated plate, sagging trusses, switchboxes bolted directly onto window frames, and thick braids of lead-sheathed cable looped from eave to eave like winter vines. Nothing stands alone. A gantry crane's foot rests on the roof of a boiler house; an iron staircase wraps around a stone aqueduct that pierces straight through the second story of a foundry. To drift through the ward is to thread a needle continuously, brushing past porcelain insulators, shutter hinges, and the rusted brackets of streetlamps at every turn.

Whatever catastrophe or deliberate abandonment let the water in happened far in the past. Nothing here looks freshly broken. Collapsed lintels have long since settled into the silt and been cemented there by pale mineral crust; shattered panes have worn smooth at the edges, their frames lined with a thick, velvety black fur of manganese and iron-stained mud. Exposed timber—doorframes, wharf pilings, the heavy oak beds beneath old presses—has cured in the cold salt into something dense, pickled, and glossy as dark horn. Even the corrosion has reached a slow truce with the place. Sharp iron corners have rounded into bulbous, soft-edged silhouettes, draped in fine grey flocculent that lifts in slow ghosts if disturbed and settles back down without hurry.

What makes the silence so unnerving is that the ward is still running.

Deep beneath the lowest courtyard slabs, some tidal-pressure engine or buried geothermal line still feeds current and hydraulic force up into the grid. Yet there is no roar of industry here, no clatter, no steam-hiss. Over decades, the cold brine and the thick mats of silt-mucilage have acoustic-deadened every surface, turning brick and iron alike into soft, sound-swallowing baffles. The machinery itself operates at a glacial, heavily damped pace, driven by pressure differentials, sealed oil-baths, and magnetic clutches rather than dry gears. In the central press-hall—a three-story space whose roof-trusses are draped in hanging sheets of pale mineral slime—a cast-iron flywheel twice the height of a person turns continuously, completing one revolution every four minutes. It glides through the near-freezing liquid in absolute hush. Only if you press your bare knuckles against its housing do you sense, through bone rather than ear, a low, steady thrum far below the threshold of hearing.

Throughout the drowned alleys, the industrial routines continue in their quiet, disjointed choreography. Along the terraced facades, row upon row of iron storm-shutters hinge slowly open and closed on a six-hour cycle as the buried sluices answer the tide beyond the basin walls. Inside a flooded switch-house, relays still trip behind thick, algae-dimmed sight glasses; each shift produces a bruised violet spark that flares for half a second, lighting up the crowded pipes and brick arches across the street before the dark closes back in. Along the underside of an exposed copper busbar running the length of an arcade, a faint, steady corona of pale greenish-blue light clings to the metal, bleeding tiny streams of hydrogen bubbles that gather along the lintels and creep upward toward the trapped gas mirrors.

This continuous, silent electrolysis has remade the chemistry of the ward. The electricity does not merely rot the ironwork; it sculpts it. Around the active induction coils and transformer tanks—still half-submerged in their heavy, insulating mineral oils—strange crystalline architectures grow and shed in cycles. Fine needles of pale salt-glass and green copper-salts sprout from bolts and valve wheels like frost on a pane. When a circuit cycles warm, the sudden thermal plume—visible as a clear, oily shimmer rising through the dark brine—loosens the accretions, sending a silent snow of translucent shards drifting downward over the cobbles.

Even colder than the ambient water are the intake manifolds near the upper rim of the ward, where hyper-saline brine seeps in from beneath the surface ice. From these riveted spouts, hollow icicles of pale salt-ice—brinicles—grow slowly downward through the liquid, crooked as dead roots. When one touches a stone parapet or a tenement roof, a pale lacework of ice spreads quietly across the masonry for a few yards, cementing loose silt in place, until a shifting current or the distant warmth of a cycling transformer melts the slender neck above. The detached ice-spire floats sluggishly upward a few feet, catches under an overhead pipe-bridge, and dissolves without a sound. Sometimes, out of the coldest conduits, brine so dense and frigid pours forth that it refuses to mix with the surrounding water at all. It spills down the stepped cobblestone lanes in silent underwater waterfalls, grey-white with suspended salt, pooling in the sunken basement courtyards as distinct, mirror-flat lakes beneath the water, whose surfaces ripple slowly whenever a heavy valve turns nearby.

In the dark, crowded interstices of this cold machine, a sparse vegetation holds on. It bears little resemblance to sunlit kelp forests. Tufts of tough, thread-thin black sea-moss anchor themselves in the recessed mortar of the brick facades, leaning all one way when the tidal sluices reverse the drift. Out of cracked gutters and the silt-filled sills of factory windows grow small, brittle clusters of pale, papery fronds—colorless as wet bone, slightly translucent—that seem to draw sustenance from the mineral-rich runoff of the corroded pipes. The densest patches appear nowhere near the dim upper walls, but deep down around the lukewarm iron casings of the submerged transformers, where thin carpets of grey-green film and glassy, hair-like filaments sway in the gentle convective updrafts.

Animals are rarer still, and move with the same economies of stillness as the machinery. You can drift through three narrow lanes and across a sunken square without seeing anything stir on its own. Then, coiled tightly around a copper intake pipe whose slow current radiates a fraction of a degree of warmth, you notice a pale, blind fish no longer than a forearm, its skin so clear that its coiled gut glows faintly like a strip of damp amber. Pale, tiny amphipods, almost invisible against the grey silt, pick methodically along the mineralized fur of a slowly ratcheting conveyor belt. Once in a long while, a solitary, dark-skinned ray glides vertically down the narrow cleft between two tenement rows, its wide wings rippling once to slip beneath a tangle of overhead cables, vanishing into an arched workshop doorway without lifting a single grain from the stones below.

The strangest feature of the ward is not that the machines survived the flooding, nor that the cold brine turned them into quiet chemical engines, but that they still appear to be making something—and keeping a schedule no living hand remembers.

At the low center of the basin stands the old galvanizing yard, bordered on four sides by tiered brick workshops whose glassless windows stare inward like dark hives. A broad conveyor of woven steel mesh emerges from a flooded tunnel, climbs a shallow ramp across the yard, and ends abruptly over a stone pit. Every twelve minutes, a magnetic clutch engages with a faint tremor in the soles of your feet, and the mesh belt inches forward by the width of a hand. Resting upon the steel links, spaced at even intervals, are fist-sized, sharply faceted polyhedra of pale turquoise salt and precipitated copper, grown slowly in the cold electrified vats inside the tunnel. When the belt reaches the lip, the foremost crystal tips silently off the edge and drifts down through the heavy brine—taking several seconds to fall—before settling onto a low, pale pyramid of identical forms that has been accumulating in the pit for generations.

Directly above the pile, an iron gantry crane hangs from tracks bolted to the surrounding facades. Once every hour, triggered by some buried clockwork whose face has long since vanished under a dome of grey lime, the crane's trolley glides two meters along its rail with zero vibration, lowers its heavy, barnacle-free hook into the dark water until it hovers a foot above the apex of the crystal heap, pauses there in the dim violet spill of a nearby arcing switchbox, and then draws the empty hook slowly back up into the shadows of the overhead pipes.

And around it, in the sunken streets, the iron-rich silt on the cobblestones shifts subtly every time the municipal clockwork pulses—aligning itself into faint, concentric rings and fine spoked geometries across the lanes, wiped out an hour later by the silent sweep of the tidal brine, and drawn cleanly again in the dark.

The only true sounds in the entire basin arrive minutes apart: the tiny, glass-sharp *tink* of a salt-ice needle fracturing under its own weight, or the brief, muffled snap of a wet electrical relay firing behind thick cast iron—each swallowed so swiftly by the cold water, the crowded brick, and the velvet silt that the silence afterward feels heavier than before, pressing inward from the dark walls of the pit.
