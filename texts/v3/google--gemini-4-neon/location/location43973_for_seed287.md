The first thing you know of the vault is that it is too hot to draw a deep breath, and too loud to hear yourself cough.

It lies well beneath the crust, a bell-shaped cavity roughly the breadth of a small cathedral and not much taller, though its ceiling disappears behind layers of hanging growth and iron rigging. There is no sky here, and no wind from outside—only a relentless, vertical draft rising from hairline fissures in the basalt floor. The rock itself radiates a dry, kiln-baked ferocity, the sort of heat that sits just below the threshold of open flame, turning every exposed piece of metal into a branding iron and baking the air until it smells of scorched copper, wet chalk, and boiled oil.

Yet the vault is not barren. It is choked with life—crowded, layered, near-touching—packed so tightly from floor to dome that moving through it requires pressing sideways between slick leaves, braided stems, and sweating cast-iron pipes. Nothing here is green in the ordinary way. Without sunlight, the flora has given up on chlorophyll and turned instead to heat, vibration, and the faint stray currents bleeding from the old machines bolted into the stone.

The light is barely enough to read the shape of a hand held before your face. Down in the cracks of the floor, a sullen, cherry-brown luminescence pulses from deeper veins of superheated rock, casting upward shadows that stretch and bend across the dense canopy. Scattered throughout the chamber, suspended from iron brackets or half-swallowed by black bark, a handful of industrial cage-lamps still burn. Their thick amber glass globes are crusted with mineral rime, and their heavy carbon filaments glow no brighter than dying coals. They draw their trickle of power from rows of ancient thermoelectric piles—slabs of riveted bismuth and cast iron clamped directly onto the hottest seams of the bedrock, quietly converting the temperature difference between the scorching floor and the slightly cooler upper vault into a weak, steady voltage. After centuries, the wiring is swollen with verdigris and wrapped tight in roots, yet the lamps remain dimly lit, pooling bruised orange glow across the undersides of the foliage.

The noise is immense and relentless, an enclosed roar that presses against the eardrums from every side of the curved stone shell. Part of it is mechanical and geological: somewhere behind the western wall, an inaccessible turbine or pressure-head groans with a deep, rattling vibration that travels through the basalt and sets every loose bolt and hanging chain in the vault humming in sympathetic fury. Part of it is the plants themselves. Rising in thickets from the floor grates and mineral mounds are forests of hollow, segmented stalks—tall, pale, and translucent as smoked glass, stiffened with silica pulled from the rock. Perforated with rows of slit-like pores along their upper lengths, they act as flutes in the searing updraft. Thousands of them blow at once, producing a hoarse, brassy, overlapping drone like a vast pipe organ jammed open and fed by a furnace. Over this continuous wall of sound comes the dry, rattling clatter of thousands of heavy leaves knocking together in the rising currents, punctuated at irregular intervals by the sharp, flat rifle-crack of expanding iron or the sudden, violent *hiss* of water striking a hot manifold.

Moisture here is scarce and fiercely contested. The vault is mostly parched by its own heat, yet it stays stubbornly damp in pockets, seams, and layered microclimates. Far above, invisible through the tangled canopy, a single cold fracture in the limestone cap weeps a slow, mineral-heavy trickle. Beneath it, the original builders hung a scattering of tall, fluted iron condensers and broad copper trays—industrial artifacts now fused with stone and vegetation into an ancient, settled equilibrium. Water drips from the corroded fins, flashing halfway into scalding vapor before settling onto the surfaces below.

Wherever that dampness collects, the chamber grows heavy and sodden. Great curtains of silver-gray wool—a fibrous, asbestos-like moss that feels like damp spun glass—drape from the suspended chains and the underside of a warped iron gantry that spans the middle of the vault. These curtains drink the passing puffs of steam, swelling slowly over days and weeks, growing darker and heavier as they hoard every trace of condensation. Beneath them sit the broad, shingled leaves of the heat-palms: thick, leathery plates colored like oiled slate and veined with dull metallic copper. Their upper surfaces are baked dry and dusted with white salt crust, while their undersides sweat a cool, sticky brine that shields their inner flesh from the scorching air.

There are no animals here. Not a single beetle crawls the dark bark; no silverfish darts beneath the rusted flange-plates; no web hangs between the porcelain insulators. No creature calls, burrows, or breathes. The absence gives the deafening roar of the vault a strange, hollow purity. Everything that moves does so by convection, thermal expansion, gravity, or growth. If a frond trembles violently beside your cheek, it is only the draft; if a heavy amber blister hanging from a cinder-vine ticks loudly in the dark, it is only the dense oil inside expanding as a fresh pulse of heat rises from the floor.

The scattered industrial remains do not look ruined so much as digested. A colossal spoked flywheel leans at a steep angle near the center of the chamber, its lower half buried in layered travertine and gripped fast by braided trunks of slag-vine—black, charred-looking creepers that coil tightly around live copper busbars and hot steam-headers, feeding directly on the trickle of current and warmth. High on the curved walls, riveted pressure-vessels hang from massive forged hooks, their paint long since blistered away into a uniform dark patina of magnetite and dried sap. A row of brass gauges mounted to a stone pier has had its glass faces cracked and clouded by heat, the needles fused permanently against their stops, while thin glassy roots have forced themselves into the casings to drink the tiny beads of moisture trapped within.

Nothing in this place hurries. For all its deafening volume and blistering intensity, the vault operates on a deeply patient clock. The great tilted flywheel is not entirely dead; linked by corroded shafts to some slow geothermal piston buried beneath the floor, it inches forward by the width of a single rusted gear-tooth every two or three days, emitting a slow, agonizing screech that gets swallowed inside the broader drone of the singing stalks. The wet silver curtains hanging from the gantry take a full fortnight to gather enough condensed water to tip their balance; when one finally gives way, it slumps slowly sideways, emptying a single dark torrent onto a rusted iron hopper below. For ten seconds, a blinding plume of scalding mist blooms upward through the layered canopy, rattling the tin-hard leaves and momentarily dimming the amber lamps. Then the hopper dries, the salt crust thickens by a fraction of a millimeter, and the wool curtain begins its long, quiet work of drinking the air all over again.

Deep in the dense, hot tangle near the floor, translucent wax-bulbs swell over seasons along the roots, holding pockets of clear, pressurized fluid that eventually split with a soft pop, spraying mineral-rich spores into the updraft to settle on warm iron and damp stone alike. The stone wears smooth; the iron thickens with rust-skin; the flora weaves tighter around the manifolds, holding up the sagging walkways that once held it back. Bound inside its dim, roaring shell of rock, the vault continues its slow, scorching commerce—an engine that no longer manufactures anything except heat, noise, salt, and the stubborn, strange thicket that has learned to live inside the furnace.
