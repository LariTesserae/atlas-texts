High above the lower clouds, cradled inside a jagged ring of newly thrust mountain peaks, lies a submerged valley that has never known a current.

Seen from beneath, the surface of the water is a bright, taut skin stretched miles wide between raw cliffs of dark stone. The air above that skin is thin and mountain-crisp, but the cliffs rise so steeply around the basin that they wall out the wind. Down here, the water is deeply, languidly warm—sun-steeped from above by the fierce, unfiltered high-altitude light, and heated gently from below by a fractured crust that still remembers the violence of its own rising.

The valley itself is vast and freshly broken. Looking out across the open expanse, the water is so startlingly clear that entire drowned ridges and stepped plateaus remain visible for miles, receding into a luminous, saturated blue-green haze. None of the rock has been softened. Without waves to scour it or rivers to grind it smooth, the bedrock keeps the razor edges of its first splitting: stacked slabs of dark slate, sawtooth hogbacks of volcanic glass, angular screes of tumbled shards resting at steep, precarious angles where they fell. Every seam and cleft is crisp, unrounded, geologically raw.

Yet almost none of this naked stone is bare. The basin is choked with a lush, heavy, near-touching abundance of life that thrives in the warm stillness.

Because there is almost no movement to mix the water, the valley has sorted itself over centuries into distinct horizontal strata. Dense, mineral-rich brine weeping slowly from the deep fissures settles into the lowest trenches; above it sit successive layers of progressively lighter, slightly cooler—though still bath-warm—water. The boundaries between these layers are sharp enough to see. They hang across the valley like immense, invisible panes of glass, shimmering faintly where the strong sunlight bends across the shift in density.

The vegetation has learned to build its world upon these liquid shelves.

Thick, buoyant stalks—flexible as green cane and studded along their lengths with pale, amber-tinted bladders—anchor themselves in the sharp cracks of the valley floor and rise straight upward for thirty, fifty, eighty feet. When their crowns meet an upper water-layer too light to bear their buoyancy, they stop ascending and spread sideways instead, flattening into broad, interlocking canopies that span acres. In the open expanses between the drowned ridges, three or four of these suspended forests hang stacked one above the other, separated by narrow, glowing corridors of clear water. Their fronds are broad, papery, and astonishingly delicate—veined like translucent stained glass in shades of olive, deep jade, and warm copper. In any moving sea they would shred in an hour; here, untouched by surge or tide, single leaves grow wider than a man's outstretched arms and hang unbruised for years.

Beneath each floating canopy, dense curtains of pale, hair-fine rootlets dangle into the space below, never touching rock. They drink directly from the shimmering boundaries between the water layers, where drifting pollen, shed leaf-scales, and tiny beads of oxygen come to rest. Looking sideways through the middle depths, the world appears tiled with faint horizontal planes of suspended gold dust and silver pearls, held aloft on nothing visible. Closer to the jagged stone terraces, thick pelts of velvety moss-ribbons and rigid, lime-crusted fans crowd every ledge and overhang, overlapping until the sharp geometry of the mountain floor is half-buried in layered, breathing green.

The light here is neither dim nor watery-pale. The high mountain sun strikes the flat surface almost vertically, pouring down in bright, crisp sheets that break into slow, heavy caustics across the upper leaves. As the light descends through the stacked canopies and refractive water-skins, it thickens and saturates, splitting at the edges of stems into faint fringes of violet and chartreuse. Even deep in the clefts, where the jagged walls pinch close together, a warm, jade-colored twilight glows against the dark rock.

It is an almost entirely quiet world. Without surf, wind-chop, or rushing streams, the water holds a thick, drowsy hush. Wait motionless among the upper fronds, and the only sounds that reach you are tiny and far apart: the faint *tick* of an oxygen bubble detaching from a sun-warmed leaf and tapping against the underside of the canopy above; the dull, distant *creak* of a heavy mineral crust cracking along a deep geothermal seam; a soft, muffled hiss when a sudden rain squall passes over the thin air miles above, stippling the ceiling of the valley into hammered silver for twenty minutes before stillness returns.

Animals are scarce enough to seem like afterthoughts. The basin belongs to stone, water, and plant. Now and then, a solitary ribbon-shaped grazer—no longer than a hand, translucent except for a faint blush of rose along its ruffled gill-edge—rows with agonizing slowness along the underside of a density-plane, sweeping up the settled film of spores. Far below, along the razor-edged scree, a lone heavy-shelled snail the color of tarnished bronze edges across a fracture in the black glass, leaving a faint pale track through the mineral silt. There are no darting shoals, no sudden flashes of silver, no startled scatterings. Nothing here hurries to eat or flee.

Time itself moves at the pace of thermal drift. When a great bladder-stalk finally dies and rots free of its holdfast, it does not simply fall. It drifts downward over the course of days, pausing for hours at a time on each invisible water-skin as its chambers slowly flood and grow heavier. When at last it slips through the lowest boundary and sinks into the dense, crystal-clear brine pooled in the sharp troughs of the valley floor, decay almost ceases. Down there, in the warm, still salt, whole fallen thickets lie intact upon the unweathered stones—amber leaves, delicate hair-roots, hollow stems—preserved in glassy perfection, waiting beneath layers of quiet green light while the mountains around them slowly age.
