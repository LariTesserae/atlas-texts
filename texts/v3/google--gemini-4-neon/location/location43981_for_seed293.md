The basin is no larger than a walled village green—four hundred paces, perhaps, from rim to opposite rim—and flat as a saucer left out in the rain. You can see the whole of it at once under the ordinary, bright afternoon sky. A low, rounded berm circles the perimeter like the lip of an ancient pond, worn down so softly by time that it barely rises above waist height. Nothing beyond that lip intrudes. Inside it, everything is wet, warm, loud, and in the middle of changing its mind.

There is no stone here, and no dirt. The floor of the entire basin is a single continuous plate of living tissue—dense, springy, pale olive and bruised umber, composed of root-fibers fused so tightly over eons that they have the density of old teak yet still weep pale sap when pressed. A millimeter-thin coat of velvet bryophyte and slick translucent algae skins every horizontal surface, holding a permanent sheen of warm, amber-clear water that pools to the ankles in the shallow depressions and thins to a mirror-film across the raised walkways. Everything smells of steeped tea, crushed reeds, warm rain on old cedar, and a faint, clean tang like wet flint struck together.

What makes the place immediately uncanny is the emptiness between things. For all its frenzy, it is not cluttered. At any given moment there are perhaps a dozen distinct structures standing inside the bowl—a tiered pavilion here, a slender ribbed pier eighty paces north, a curving arcade of pale lignified arches far off toward the eastern rim—separated by wide, generous expanses of open water-sheet and flat vascular lawn. You could walk across the center of the basin in bright daylight with nothing above your head but drifting cumulus clouds, hearing the city work around you at a comfortable distance.

It *is* a city, or the deeply eroded ghost of one that never received the order to stop. Beneath the translucent skin of the floor, visible whenever the wind stills the surface water, run the sunken geometries of ancient infrastructure: concentric avenues of pale, calloused xylem-brick; circular plazas whose edges have softened into gentle swells; branched drainage veins pulsing rhythmically with dark fluid; recessed conduit-grids worn so smooth by centuries of water and cellular turnover that they look like the fossilized markings on a turtle’s shell. Whatever rose high here in the primordial era has long since slumped, weathered, and flattened into this lowest stable dish. Even the few permanent anchor-plinths scattered across the floor—knuckled stumps of petrified cambium as wide as threshing floors—have been rounded into pillowy, water-slick domes.

Yet out of those ancient, nearly erased foundations, the city keeps rebuilding itself in real time.

Watch one of the open stretches of flat wet moss for two minutes. First the surface water begins to shiver in crisp concentric rings, though no breeze has touched it. A low, vibrating groan rises through the soles of your feet—like a ship’s hull flexing in a swell—followed by a rapid series of wet, woody snaps. The living floor bulges upward. Without tearing, the vascular mat extrudes a cluster of fluted columns made of pale, fibrous cellulose, rising thirty feet in fifteen seconds with the sound of heavy linen ripping underwater. Ribs fan outward from the capitals, interlacing into a vaulted civic canopy; tiered steps unfold along the base like the petals of a giant aquatic bud; drainage gutters slit themselves open along the eaves and immediately begin pouring warm water that was not there a moment before.

Strange as the speed is, the geometry is stranger. The newly extruded pavilion does not look fresh. Its cornices emerge already softened and beveled, pitted with old drip-marks, draped in trailing beards of damp moss, as though the blueprint itself has weathered over millennia and can now only generate ruins of itself. Worse, the physics holding it together do not belong to any ordinary carpentry. Half of the upper gallery cantilevers twenty yards over empty air with nothing beneath it, thin as a wafer of dried kelp, utterly rigid against the gusting air. Beneath its archway, the daylight subtly bends—not like heat-shimmer, which blurs, but like a clean seam stitched through space. Stepping through that arch from the south puts you out forty paces to the west, emerging from a low stair-head that did not exist until your foot crossed the threshold.

And across the basin, even as this pavilion finishes settling with a loud hydraulic sigh, an older arcade near the southern rim begins to give up its form. Its arches sag smoothly, lose their bone-like stiffness, and collapse downward into a frothing pancake of warm fibrous slurry that is reabsorbed into the floor within a minute, leaving only a ring of floating foam and a sudden, heavy slap of displaced water that rolls outward across the quiet flats.

This is the rhythm of the place: a relentless, hyperactive civic bazaar with no citizens, where buildings trade places across sparse open water. At any second, two structures are rising, three are holding steady while executing subtle internal adjustments, and one is unspooling back into the substrate. Because the air is warm and saturated, every burst of morphogenesis vents heat and moisture, sending up ragged curls of mist that evaporate quickly in the clear afternoon light.

The water itself does not merely sit or fall; it is woven into the engineering. Warm rain condenses directly out of the humid air in localized patches, pattering loudly onto a single plaza while thirty feet away the floor stays calm. Along the underside of a newly grown vine-bridge, a thick ribbon of water flows briskly *uphill*, clinging to the pale bark in defiance of weight, channeled by invisible gradients embedded in the vascular walls. In the center of a wide, empty lawn, a wall of water twelve feet high and clear as blown glass stands upright with no tank to contain it, quivering softly, holding suspended twigs and pale seed-pods in its belly—until an unseen switch trips in the roots below, and the entire curtain drops straight down with a thunderous *whump* that throws spray twenty yards in every direction.

In certain dry-looking alcoves beneath the shifting towers, the rain simply pauses. Droplets hang motionless in the warm daylight at eye level for three or four heartbeats, glinting like beads on invisible threads, before their momentum is abruptly returned and they strike the moss together in a single sharp crack. Nobody built pipes for any of this; the matter of the plants themselves is woven at a scale far beneath the cell, bending inertia, pressure, and local distance the way ordinary roots bend around a stone.

All of this makes the basin remarkably loud. There is never true silence here. The ambient floor of sound is a busy, echoing clatter carried cleanly across the flat open water and cupped gently by the low perimeter berm: the deep hydraulic thumping of the sub-floor veins, the crackling chorus of emerging columns, the hiss of pressurized sap venting through pinhole stomata along the piers, the sudden percussive slaps of collapsing water-curtains, and the constant trickle of a hundred temporary gutters.

Layered right over the machinery of the city is the racket of the animals—abundant, bold, and completely at home in the chaos.

Perching along the trembling cornices of a rising tower are dozens of stilt-legged waders with long, curved, chalk-white bills and translucent bladder-wings that catch the daylight like oiled paper. They do not flee when a building begins to dissolve beneath them; they wait until the very last second, riding the downward rush of air and launching off in shrill, rattling flocks to claim the next pavilion rising across the water. Their nests are woven into the temporary pockets of flattened gravity under the cantilevers, where their speckled eggs float gently in loose clusters of damp moss until the structure's cycle ends—by which time, somehow, the hatchlings are always ready to tumble into the warm shallows below.

In the wide mirror-flats between the structures, swarms of porcelain-shelled amphibious runners—no larger than a human hand, with six spindly legs and inflated amber throat-sacs—skitter effortlessly across the water-film. They race toward every fresh dissolution site, congregating by the hundreds around the foaming slurry to graze on the rich, warm starches released when a pier unravels. As they feed, they inflate their throats in staggered, clicking choruses that sound like a room full of castanets, loud enough to carry halfway across the basin.

Through the standing sheets and uphill ribbons of water glide pale, ribbon-bodied lung-eels, their fins trailing like wet silk. When a vertical curtain of water abruptly loses its containment and collapses, the eels flop unbothered onto the slick moss-plaza, undulating briskly across fifty paces of open ground to throw themselves into the nearest sunken transit-channel, where the current whisks them down into the glowing-pale xylem veins beneath the floor.

The only unhurried presences are the broad-backed grazers—low, heavy-limbed beasts about the size of calves, their hides plated in overlapping damp scales colonized by the same velvet moss as the ground. Four or five of them wander the sparse open intervals at any given time, heads down, cropping the pale spore-buds that erupt along the eroded anchor-plinths. They seem to read the basin’s buried vibrations far better than the eye can. A grazer will pause mid-step on an utterly flat expanse of shallow water, turn its massive damp head, and amble ten paces to the left; four seconds later, a ribbed stair-tower bursts upward from the exact patch of algae where it had been standing, showering the animal’s mossy flanks with warm spray while it continues chewing without lifting its gaze.

Wait long enough on the low perimeter berm, watching the sun move slowly across the bright, ordinary sky, and a faint grammar begins to surface beneath the turbulence. The basin is not merely thrashing at random. It is trying to balance an equation whose variables vanished ages ago. Whenever a tall pier rises in the northwest quadrant, a sunken amphitheater must open in the southeast within thirty seconds, as though the total volume of civic space inside the rim is strictly conserved. Whenever two distant archways link their thresholds, the water level across the eastern flat drops by half an inch, drawn away to cool or weight the folded seam. Every hour or so, a low, resonant chord—rich and woody, like a massive marimba struck with a felt mallet—pulses up through the floor from all four quarters at once; for ten seconds, nothing rises and nothing falls, the curtains of water hang perfectly still, even the clicking swarms quieten, and the whole sparse little wetland city holds its breath in the warm afternoon light.

Then a gutter coughs loudly near the northern berm. A bench unfurls out of a puddle where nobody sits. A flock of bladder-winged waders bursts upward with a sharp clatter of cries, banking bright against the clouds, and the ancient, half-erased city goes right back to rearranging itself in the wet.
