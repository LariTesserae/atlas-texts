The first thing the place does is take your eyes. You are miles beneath the surface of the world, yet the glare is worse than noon on a salt flat—a clean, surgical white that pours from overhead and rebounds off every surface until shadows thin to pale blue ghosts and lose their edges. If you look straight up, squinting through the sting of cold air, there is no sky, only a fractured ceiling of ancient glacial ice three or four kilometers thick, vaulted across a sunken basin the size of a small sea. Sunlight drives down through that colossal lens, scattered by trapped air and pressure-cracks into an all-encompassing brilliance, with occasional raw, unblunted shafts piercing straight through the deeper rifts like white blades.

Beneath your boots, there is no rock. There is no gravel, no clay, no true dirt anywhere you can reach. Everything solid in this vast hollow is alive—or was alive until yesterday.

The floor is a pale, ribbed expanse of dense botanical plate: colossal, interlocking scales of waxy celadon and bleached ivory, slick with a thin film of rime. It feels like cold horn under weight, faintly yielding where the newest shoots are pushing up from below, ridged with sharp, unweathered seams. If you kneel and scrape away the frost with a fingernail, the outer skin is chalky and reflective—an armor evolved to throw back the merciless light—and just beneath it lies a taut, translucent green pith, cold as wet glass and smelling faintly of crushed pine needles and raw turnip.

Step to the edge of the terrace and the true scale of the cavity opens out, emptying the stomach. The space is almost entirely nothing: tens of kilometers of freezing, luminous air stretching toward distant walls that look like pale chalk cliffs fading into a bright mist. There are no clustered forests here, no crowded canopies. Instead, the flora behaves like geology. It coats the colossal subterranean bowl in sheer, folded sheets, builds sweeping buttresses that arch miles out over the void before snapping under their own weight, and throws up jagged, blade-edged ribs that stand alone in the glare like shattered sails. Everything is freshly made and sharply cornered. Nothing has had time to wear smooth.

Because nothing stays still for long.

Watch the far wall—fifteen kilometers away across the empty middle—where a bright shaft from a ceiling fissure is crawling slowly down a cliff of pale, overlapping bracts. You can see the turbulence before you ever feel it. Where the concentrated light strikes the frozen plant-skin, the temperature jumps a few crucial degrees. Dormant sap surges through hollow vascular tubes; frozen pockets thaw and expand; cells divide in a frantic, brittle rush. Within minutes, a shelf of living foliage half a mile wide begins to buckle outward, swelling into a white blister before shearing cleanly off the cliff face.

It falls in complete silence.

A whole landscape of shattered stems, curled fronds, and severed pith-blocks tumbles down through the bright air, breaking apart as it drops, trailing a luminous veil of frost crystals and pale spores. You watch the entire collapse unfold in miniature across the gulf—the slow-motion violence of a botanical avalanche—and wait for the thunder. When the sound finally arrives, thinned by distance and swallowed by miles of fibrous, porous walls, it is nothing more than a soft, dry *whump*, like snow sliding off a roof. Then the quiet settles back in, wide and ringing.

That is the unsettling trick of this place: it is in constant, churning upheaval, yet it rarely raises its voice. The air is too vast and cold, the surfaces too spongy beneath their glassy crust to carry echoes cleanly. Up close, the world speaks only in small, sharp notations—the crisp *tick* of frost tightening along a leaf-vein, a low, stressed creak under the soles of your feet as a buried rhizome shifts, the sudden glassy *snap* of a tensioned tendril parting somewhere behind a ridge.

Water here is a shy, intermittent thing, never enough to form rivers or lakes, yet woven into every surface. Across the open void, the air is parched and biting, freezing the moisture in your nostrils. Yet wherever a roving beam of light warms a patch of living cliff, the plant-mass sweats. Thin curtains of condensation drift off the heated faces, swirling upward in silent, chaotic eddies before the surrounding cold flashes them into sparkling clouds of diamond-dust ice. These dry squalls spin across the hollow like weightless storms, vanishing as abruptly as they form. Down in the tight crevices where huge, folded leaf-plates meet—and in the deep hollows of freshly split stems—meltwater and thin, sweetish sap collect in dark, freezing puddles. Occasional slow drips fall from the vaulted ice miles overhead, arriving as scattered bursts of hard sleet that patter briefly across the waxy ground and freeze into clear beads.

Where a massive trunk or plate has sheared away recently, the wound is shocking in its color: bruised jade, wet amber, and deep violet-black, steaming faintly in the cold air. Already, sharp pale shoots are spearing out from the raw margin—curved, serrated, aggressive, racing to claim the newly opened light before a neighboring plate curls over and chokes them out. The terrain is a slow war of geometry. A flat ridge you walked yesterday may be tilted twenty degrees by morning, split down the middle by a clean, foot-wide trench exposing pale fibrous strata like freshly split timber.

Life that moves on its own is scarce, and built for the glare and the void. Hours can pass without a single living motion other than the writhing of the plants themselves. Then, far out over the central drop, you might spot a glider—a lean, long-winged thing the color of frosted parchment, riding the silent thermal updrafts that peel off the sunlit cliffs. Its eyes are shielded behind dark, slotted lids; it never flaps, only tilts its translucent membranes to slip from one rising column of cold mist to the next, scavenging the bruised pith exposed by fresh collapses.

Closer to the ground, you find their signs more often than the creatures themselves: long, winding welts raised just beneath the waxy skin of the floor, where blind, thick-furred burrowers tunnel through the warmish, antifreeze-rich cambium. If you stand very quiet beside a fresh welt, you can sometimes see the pale surface twitch, and watch a tiny bead of steam puff from a breathing-slit before the tunnel goes still again. Once in a great while, along the jagged vertical ribs, a solitary clamberer appears—spindly, pale-pelted, moving with nervous precision on hooked keratin toes, pausing to lap condensation from a curled bract before leaping effortlessly across a fissure that has no visible bottom.

And scattered across the immensity—so rare you could travel for days without meeting one—are the marks of people who know how to read the restless ground.

Nothing they make is meant to last. You cannot lay foundations on a floor that grows, swells, and splits beneath you. Instead, you come across a lone shelter tucked into the crook of an older, thicker heartwood knuckle where the plant-crust shifts more slowly: a low, dome-shaped frame of bent ribs lashed tight with braided phloem-cord, covered in layered sheets of cured, darkened bark to block the relentless glare. Heavy pegs of hammered bone and cold-worked copper pin the guy-lines straight into the living tissue; around each peg, the plant has already begun to swell in a pale, irritated callus, slowly pushing the metal back out.

Outside the shelter stands a tall marker-pole spliced from three dried stems, flying a single tattered strip of cloth dyed deep rust-red—the only color that refuses to disappear into the white-and-celadon haze. Beside it, a wooden rack holds strips of salted cambium and tough, pale hide freeze-drying in the sharp air. On a flat ledge nearby lies a discarded pair of goggles carved from dark, smoky inner-pith, their eyeholes reduced to razor-thin horizontal slits to save a traveler from snow-blindness.

Farther along the cliff, someone once notched a careful flight of footholds and lashed a hand-rope down a sheer, waxy wall to reach a damp basin of melt-pools below. Halfway down, the wall has split. A new seam has opened six feet wide, severing the rope clean and hoisting the lower half of the carved staircase sideways into empty air, where it juts out over a two-kilometer drop, coated in fresh rime.

No one has come back to fix it yet—or perhaps whoever built it has already moved on, following the slower ridges, reading the creak of the ground under their feet the way a sailor reads the pitch of a deck. Overhead, the great glacial ceiling groans once, impossibly high and faint, shifting a fraction of an inch under the weight of the unseen world above. A pale shaft of light slides off one distant cliff and settles onto another. In the bright, cold hush, miles away across the empty gulf, a new shelf of white-green earth begins to swell, preparing to break.
