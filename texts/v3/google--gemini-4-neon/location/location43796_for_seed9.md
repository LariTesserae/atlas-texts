The floor is stamped steel grating, brand-new, its diamond ridges still sharp enough to bite through soft soles. Beneath the mesh, three feet down, warm water sits in a pale concrete cistern that has not finished curing; thin milky trails of lime still weep from the formwork seams, clouding the water where it curls around submerged copper heating coils.

Everything in here is warm—a close, stove-side, shirt-sticking warmth—and everything just beyond the glass is empty sky.

The whole place is no larger than a village church, a narrow, high-shouldered vault of riveted steel ribs and tall plate-glass bays bolted tight against the thin mountain air. Outside, the altitude is unmistakable. Daylight arrives hard and crisp, bleached of lowland haze, pouring straight in through the upper clerestory before the wet foliage breaks it into shifting slabs of green and silver. Now and then a cloud-top drifts past below the sill line, or brushes the exterior panes like cold wool for half a minute, dimming the interior to a soft, pearled shadow before peeling away into pale violet void. You can hear the high wind only as a distant, flattened pressure against the mullions, muffled almost to nothing by the double-glazed seals.

Inside, there is no ground. No soil, no bedrock, no fallen timber—nothing that was not extruded, fired, poured, or bolted. The plants grow out of perforated porcelain baffles, wired troughs of expanded grey clay, and thick mats of spun mineral wool slung in galvanized frames. Every surface within reach is slick with condensation. Because the mountain air chills the outer glass while the floor coils keep the chamber steamed, the tall walls run continuously with clear distilled water. It beads, joins, and slides down in wavering curtains into riveted copper gutters, carrying the faint, clean scent of boiled rain, wet putty, and warm ozone.

It is startling how new it all is. Nothing has rusted; nothing has settled. White soapstone assembly marks and stenciled flange numbers—*4-B*, *FEED*, arrows pointing upward—still sit crisp on the pale-grey steel arches. Along the second catwalk, a span of iron decking ends mid-air, its next section hanging ready from a silent overhead trolley, plumb-bobs still dangling from the beam-ends on taut waxed twine. Coils of bright unused copper wire rest on trestles beside buckets of pale sealant that has barely skinned over. Yet the vegetation is already shoulder-high, dense, and pressing inward from every tier, as if the plants were unspooled from the same crates as the piping and switched on before the roof bolts cooled.

You cannot see from one end of the vault to the other. The space is packed tight in three dimensions: stacked catwalks, suspended trays, vertical irrigation fins, hanging root-curtains, and bundles of insulated conduit woven so closely together that moving down the central aisle means ducking beneath heavy brass unions and parting broad, glossy leaves that leave cool streaks of water across your forearms.

The botany here obeys the architecture with an odd, quiet literalism. Broad-leaved climbers with translucent, silver-veined fronds wrap themselves not randomly, but in tight, even spirals around the low-voltage copper busbars, their tendrils thickening wherever a mild current hums through the metal. Thick carpets of bright, rubbery liverwort have already colonized the freshly grouted joints of the cistern walls, drinking the alkaline seepage before it can dry. Hanging from the higher steel trusses are dense pale curtains of aerial roots, each tip ending in a glistening bead of mineral water that swells, trembles, and drops into the slatted ceramic troughs below. Where two bolted flanges seep warm vapor, clusters of waxy, translucent pitchers cling directly to the hex nuts, swollen heavy with condensation and faintly warm to the touch.

For a place driven by engines, pumps, and live wiring, it is remarkably quiet. The machinery was built to move slowly and without clatter. Beneath the grating, transformers emit a low, steady vibration you feel mostly in the arches of your feet. Steam vents through porous ceramic sleeves that turn a hiss into a soft, breathy sigh. All the joints and pulleys run on felt-baffled bearings greased with heavy amber oil. The dominant sounds are small and dampened: the muted *plink* of heavy drops striking broad leaves, the soft wet tick of an expanding brass thermostat, and the steady, almost imperceptible rustle of water slipping down glass.

Still, nothing holds still for long. The vault runs on a handful of overlapping mechanical and biological clocks, each nudging the others.

When a cloud slides across the exterior sun, the drop in glare cools the long copper expansion-rods rigged along the roof ridge. Within seconds they contract, drawing weighted chains quietly downward and tilting a bank of frosted glass louvers open to gather what light remains. As the light shifts, the broad fronds along the middle tiers respond visibly—not over hours, but over minutes—unfolding their cupped blades with tiny, jerky adjustments of turgor, shedding collected pools of water onto the lower trays.

Every eleven minutes, a high copper cistern tucked under the gable fills to its tipping point. There is a single soft wooden *clunk* against a rubber stop, and a warm sheet of nutrient-rich condensate spills into a stepped porcelain weir, trickling down through four stories of mesh trays, mineral mats, and root-nets. A fine mist rises immediately from the floor grates where the runoff hits the warm coils, thickening the air until the far glass wall dissolves into bright milky glare, then slowly clearing again as the cold panes pull the moisture back out of the air.

Overhead, the work of finishing the place continues by itself. Along a greased I-beam near the ridge, a brass-and-steel gantry crawls at the pace of a minute hand, its electric motor nearly silent. It stops above an empty steel cradle, lowers a fresh tray of dry, bone-pale mineral wool on four braided cables, releases its latches with four tiny metallic clicks, and glides back toward the end wall. Before the cables have even retracted into the carriage, the dry mat has begun to darken at its edges with ambient moisture, and nearby tendrils from the adjacent tray are already leaning subtly across the gap toward the fresh, warm dampness.

Animal life is scarce enough that at first the chamber seems purely botanical and mechanical. Only if you stand still on the middle catwalk, letting the warm vapor settle on your skin, do you notice the few small citizens that have somehow established themselves in this brand-new sky-box.

Pressed flat against the circular glass face of a brass pressure gauge sits a small, pale amphibian no longer than a thumb, its skin so thin and watery you can see the faint dark smudge of its organs and the tiny red pulse in its throat. It does not blink; it simply absorbs the heat radiating off the brass bezel, shifting one splayed translucent foot only when the needle behind the glass trembles upward by half a mark. Lower down, along the freshly poured concrete curb where white lime-bloom crusts the seam, a solitary snail with a delicate, matte-white shell—almost indistinguishable from the porcelain insulators beside it—glides steadily along the mortar line, rasping a neat wet track through the curing salt. And once, if you look straight down between your boots through the diamond grating, a pale, ribbon-finned fish turns lazily between two glowing immersion coils, completely blind, nosing through the cloudy lime-water before slipping back under a concrete baffle.

Near the far gable, where the vaulted glass curves down to meet the steel floor-plate, the last row of panes has only just been set in fresh linseed putty. The smell of it is sharp and oily against the rich green steam. Through that spotless corner glass, the mountain light enters cleanly, striking a freshly bolted manifold where three copper pipes meet a ceramic manifold box. Already, a hair-fine filament of green moss has crept across the bright brass thread of the pressure valve, and a single translucent root-tip has found the tiny gap between two steel washers, anchoring itself into the warm metal seam as if the building and the garden had never been told they were separate things.
