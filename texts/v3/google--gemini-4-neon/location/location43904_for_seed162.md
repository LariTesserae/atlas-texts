Up here, miles above whatever sea or floor the world keeps hidden beneath its lower cloud-decks, the air should be thin and brittle with frost. Instead it lies warm and heavy against the skin, carrying the close, fevered smell of wet iron, crushed fern, and ozone. The sky is a low ceiling of bruised pewter that never brightens into day and never quite settles into night; it gives off a sullen, diffuse twilight just strong enough to pick out the wet sheen on a ridge twenty miles off, while leaving the deep gullies in a velvety near-darkness.

And it is almost entirely quiet.

You feel the violence of the place before you hear anything of it. A kilometer across a narrow, jagged chasm, a wall of black rock half a mile high detaches itself along a seam as straight as a knife-cut and begins to rise. It climbs steadily out of the mist, bearing a thick, dripping mane of dark jungle on its shoulders, grinding upward past a neighboring cliff with showers of pale blue static crawling along the friction line. Thousands of tons of fresh basalt shear, lock, and heave upward—and the only sound that crosses the gorge is a soft, dry whisper, like a palm drawn lightly over damp wool, followed by a brief, dull pressure in the inner ear. Whatever law governs acoustics elsewhere has grown thin and porous here. Shockwaves die a few feet from their birth. Thunder never finishes forming. The whole continental expanse churns in a thick, cottony hush.

Nothing here is old enough to have softened. The terrain is raw, razor-edged, freshly extruded—stacked plates of glassy black stone, fluted shale, and knife-crested ridges that have had no centuries of wind or river to blunt them. No rivers exist here anyway. Water arrives only as a lukewarm, persistent dampness: fat droplets condensing along the cooler edges of metal-veined stone, thin curtains of warm mist unspooling from fissures, brief sullen drizzles that fall out of the pewter sky and pool in sharp rocky hollows as dark, mineral-slick puddles. Everything is perpetually slick to the touch, sweating under the internal heat that rises from somewhere far beneath the massif.

Yet for all its rawness, the stone does not behave like ordinary mountain rock. There are no buildings anywhere on these heights—no roads, no walls, no rivets driven by a hand, no trace that anything ever came here to mine or construct. The landscape simply produces industry the way other continents produce karst or glacial valleys.

Look closely at a cliff face and the illusion of simple geology breaks down. Freshly thrust-up ridges emerge with evenly spaced cooling fins along their flanks, radiating heat into the damp air. Where two tectonic plates rub past one another in slow, restless cycles, their edges have grown interlocking stone teeth, turning a three-mile fault line into a sluggish, mossy rack-and-pinion that shifts a valley floor fifty feet to the east before reversing an hour later. Fissures do not merely vent geothermal steam; they inhale and exhale on steady, four-beat rhythms, opening and closing along naturally vulcanized lips of dense rubbery mineral that seal tight with a barely audible *thup*. Deep in the narrow clefts, pockets of raw ore smelt themselves in spontaneous pockets of white-hot pressure, extruding long, gleaming ribbons of pure copper and dark iron-nickel that slide out onto slick chutes of wet lichen, cool into segmented bars, and tumble noiselessly into warm sinkholes to be drawn down and reworked again.

There is no purpose to the output, no warehouse waiting at the end of the line. Production here is just a kind of weather.

Packed tightly into every fissure, ledge, and shifting crest is a lush, crowded tangle of vegetation. The warmth and constant condensation feed a dense, dark-green upholstery that clings to the moving rock with furious tenacity. Broad, rubbery ferns with tarnished silver undersides unfurl across the razor edges, cushioning the stone; thick-stemmed vines loop between adjacent spires, stretching and slacking as the crags slide past one another. Many of the plants have grown straight into the landscape’s electrical and thermal veins. Hollow, translucent reeds sprout around the rhythmic exhaust vents, swelling and collapsing with each puff of warm vapor; flat, waxy canopy-mats stretch across the gullies like taut tarps, trapping pockets of humid air underneath while their upper surfaces gather static charge from the drifting mist, glowing at their serrated margins with a faint, cold violet haze. Cut one of the thicker lianas and it bleeds not sap alone, but a warm, coppery mucilage that arcs with tiny pale sparks before knitting itself shut again.

The crowding is vertical and intimate. You cannot reach out a hand without touching three different kinds of growth, a sweating edge of striated ore, and at least a dozen living things.

Fauna swarms through every layer of this dim, wet tangle—abundant, densely packed, and eerily mute. Along the broad ribbed leaves crawl dense herds of flat, many-legged grazers with velvety gray hides and soft, spongy pads that make no tick against the stone; they feed on the ionized dew and the thin film of dark mineral oil that weeps from the rock seams. In the narrow air between the canopy and the razor-crags hang great, slow-drifting curtains of translucent, ribbon-bodied flyers—not beating wings, which would fail in the strange eddies, but riding the warm updrafts and electrostatic gradients like living kite-tails, thousands of them brushing past one another in total silence.

Larger creatures move through the lower gloom where the warm vents glow a dull ember-orange. Heavy, segmented browsers the size of oxen pick their way effortlessly across fresh fracture-blades on split, leathery hooves, their jaws built like cushioned reciprocating shears that nip cleanly through soft extruded copper shoots and fibrous fern-trunks without a clink. Suspended beneath the overhangs hang colonial nests that look at first glance like snarls of braided wire and wet black moss; approach closer and the whole mass shifts, adjusting its weight with faint ripples of body-heat, passing silent electrical pulses from member to member like slow thoughts traveling down a nerve. Nothing calls out. Nothing screeches, roars, or sings. The crowded forest communicates in heat-flushes, tiny shifts in local static, ripples of bioluminescent pale amber along damp flanks, and the soft, incessant brushing of bodies against wet leaves.

And beneath the hush and the crowding, the whole vast platform is never still for a single minute.

Stand on a high spur and look out across the continental reach of it, and the scale refuses to behave like stable geography. Far away, whole mountain ranges are in the middle of rewriting themselves. A massive terraced massif thirty miles across slowly sinks into a boiling bank of mist, its sharp ridges softening into a luminous, churning slurry of raw ore, torn jungle, and half-formed mechanical strata, while five miles to the left a brand-new range pushes up through the gloom in smooth, hydraulic surges—already jagged, already hot, already sprouting rapid thickets of silver-backed fern from the warm condensation along its flanks. High in the bruised sky, slow dark cloud-banks rotate around localized updrafts of pure electrical tension, flashing internally with noiseless sheet-lightning that briefly silhouettes miles of shifting pistons, spires, and tangled greenery before dropping everything back into dim indigo.

The longer you stay, the harder it becomes to trust the boundaries between things—not because your eyes are tired in the low light, but because the place itself has stopped strictly enforcing them.

A puddle of lukewarm water pooled in a basalt hollow does not reflect the pewter clouds above; it shows the underside of the rock you are standing on, glowing with deep amber veins and tangled with roots that descend into empty sky. A cluster of dark ferns beside a warm vent suddenly enters a rapid eddy of its own time—uncurling, shedding a fine cloud of damp spores, blackening, and calcifying into slotted, heat-finned stone in the space of three breaths, while ten feet away a heavy drop of condensation hangs detached from a vine-tip, trembling motionless in an invisible electrostatic pocket for twenty minutes before finally dropping.

Shadows lag half a step behind the creatures that cast them, or stretch toward a light source that is not there. Along the active fault lines, distance itself develops seams: a distant volcanic stack exhaling a neat, steady pulse of dark steam appears close enough that you can count the velvet mites crawling on its mossy intake-rim, and then with a faint tug behind your eyes it snaps back into ten miles of haze and intervening chasms. You pull back a mat of wet moss expecting stone and find a smooth, warm sheet of dark brass humming faintly under your fingertips, veined with pulsing roots that are half-plant and half-conduit; look away to step past a silent herd of ribbon-flyers, look back, and the brass has fractured into cold, razor-sharp slate, already beading with fresh warm dew.

Nothing here collapses into pure chaos, though. That is perhaps the strangest rule of the place. For all its frantic unmaking and remaking, its blurred borders between flesh and foliage and ore and engine, the landmass keeps an intense, feverish momentum—like a colossal factory floor that dreamed itself into a mountain range, forgot what it was supposed to manufacture, grew thick with dark jungle, filled up with silent, teeming life, and simply kept running in the dim heat, forever extruding new sharp worlds into the quiet dark.
