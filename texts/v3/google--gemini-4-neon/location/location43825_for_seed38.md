Up here, the sky has no sun—only a broad, low ceiling of warm cloud that leans directly on the roof of the world and never quite lifts.

This is a continental crown, raised miles above whatever oceans or lowlands lie beyond its distant, unseen rims. It has been raised for so long that time has finished with its stone. Every peak that once stood here was ground away epochs ago; every gorge filled, every fault smoothed into the shallowest possible curve. Beneath what you walk on, the bedrock is an ancient, nearly level dish of dissolved basalt and pale limestone, settled into its final posture. Yet you will never see a hand’s breadth of it. Between the dead stone and the cloud-deck lies a living country dozens of yards thick, grown so old and so complete that geology has been replaced by botany.

There is no soil. What holds your weight is a continuous, felted crust of fused roots, swollen horizontal stems, and generations of compacted, half-living moss—a continent-wide mattress saturated with warm water. When you take a step, the ground does not crunch; it yields with a slow, cushioned give, depressing an inch or two around your sole while dark, clear tea wells briefly up through the pale velvet and sinks back in behind you. Below this springy skin, unseen and miles across, older layers of dead plant-matter are pressed together in the dark, fermenting with immense, glacial patience. It is this buried hearth, trapped beneath the thick cloud-blanket, that keeps the high altitude fever-warm—never hot, never cool, always the steady, cradling warmth of a closed greenhouse or a cupped palm.

The light is dim and shadowless, the color of pewter strained through bruised fruit. High overhead, the cloud-sea glows with an even, milky pallor that brightens vaguely for half the day and fades to a deep ash-violet for the other half, without ever declaring an east or a west. By the time that light filters down through the layered architecture of the canopy, it has passed through three or four tiers of living tissue.

Nothing here is the bright green of sunlit forests. In this permanent twilight, the flora has turned toward darker, stranger pigments: oxblood, wet slate, olive-black, smoky plum, and the pale wax-yellow of things grown in cellars. Nor do the plants look like ordinary trees. There are no sharp twigs, no fluttering small leaves. Instead, immense pillar-trunks—smooth, barkless, sheathed in a damp, ribbed hide covered in fine silver cilia—rise out of the root-mat and arch seamlessly into one another, fusing where they touch until the whole landscape forms a single interconnected vaulting of living bridges and buttresses. From these arches hang vast, translucent membranes, stretched tight across branching cartilage-like ribs like oiled parchment or the wings of enormous bats. Veined in deep crimson, angled gently downward, they do not catch wind—there is almost no wind here—they catch the dim light and the drifting cloud.

Everything weeps. It does not rain in the fierce way of lower worlds; the air simply sits at the edge of saturation and lets go wherever it touches a surface. Mist moves through the dim aisles in slow, ragged ribbons, combing itself against the cilia of the trunks and beading along the undersides of the burgundy membranes. Drops gather with unhurried precision, swell until they gleam like mercury in the twilight, and detach. All across the vastness, water falls in a soft, irregular, muffled patter—onto stretched leaf-skins, onto cushioned moss, into dark standing pools—never loud enough to become a roar, just a quiet, perpetual ticking that measures no urgent hour.

Because the ancient tableland beneath is almost flat, water does not rush. There are no rapids, no scouring streams. Instead, water migrates across the continent in immense, lazy sheets beneath the root-crust, surfacing wherever the living mat dips into a shallow basin. These pools are startlingly still, stained the dark amber-black of steeped bark, rimmed with thick white feeder-roots that dangle like pale fingers into the depths. Looking down into one is like looking through a pane of smoked glass into a deeper twilight. Higher up, water never reaches the floor at all: the giant fused forks of the canopy hold bowl-shaped leaf-axils and hollowed, living cistern-stems, each brimming with warm, pale rainwater—an entire archipelago of aerial ponds suspended forty feet in the mist.

At first, the place feels empty, hushed by its own density. Then your eyes adjust to the dimness and the slowness, and you realize the space is crowded.

The silence is not an absence of animals; it is a way of living. Sound travels poorly through wet felt and hanging curtains of moss, and nothing here is in enough of a hurry to shout. On the waxy underside of an overhead membrane, a dozen flat, translucent sheet-slugs the size of spread palms glide over the crimson veins, scraping invisible films of moisture-mold. When two drift near each other, a faint, cool-green luminescence ripples across their mantles—a brief, silent flare no brighter than wet glass catching moonlight, fading before you are certain you saw it.

Wait long enough beside one of the dark pools, and a section of the mossy bank uncurls and rises. It is a felt-walker—a tall, narrow-bodied grazer standing on four long, supple legs that end in wide, splayed pads like webbed snowshoes, built to walk the floating peat without breaking through. Its coat is long, damp, and faintly green with symbiotic algae and tiny thread-ferns rooted directly in the fur. It has no external ears, only smooth depressions behind small, liquid-dark eyes. Moving with excruciating delicacy, it lifts one padded foot, holds it suspended for ten quiet seconds while droplets fall from the hairs, and sets it down without a splash. Its pleated, soft muzzle lowers to the black water—or reaches upward, neck extending like a pale gourd-stem, to sip from a suspended leaf-bowl. A herd of five or six may be standing thirty paces away in the bruised twilight, completely visible yet nearly indistinguishable from the arching stems until one blinks or shifts its weight.

Higher in the dim vaults, pale clusters hang beneath the arches like folded scraps of damp paper. These are veil-drifters—broad-winged, moth-like creatures with feathered antennae and trailing hind-ribbons that hang limp in the humid air. Once every few hours, prompted by some subtle shift in barometric pressure or warmth, a cluster lets go all at once. Hundreds peel away from the ribbed hide of a trunk and drift outward through the misty aisles, their wings so thick with velvet scales that their flight makes not even a whisper. They pass within inches of your face, smelling faintly of sweet rot and wet chalk, and settle again a hundred yards away, folding shut like eyelids.

In the still pools and aerial cisterns live smooth, flattened amphibians with branching external gills that fan out around their necks like pale lace. They hang suspended just beneath the surface film for hours at a time, motionless, waiting for some tiny drifting larva or fallen droplet-rider to disturb the meniscus. Deeper down, tucked inside the spongy folds of the floor, blind, segmented burrowers turn the warm peat over millimeter by millimeter, keeping the continent aerated, their presence betrayed only by tiny chimneys of pale mud and occasional, long, low sighs of gas venting slowly from the fermenting dark beneath.

That is almost the whole of the soundscape: the slow, scattered *plip* of condensation; the faint, subterranean sigh of warm vapor escaping a peat-vent; now and then a deep, muffled groan as an immense living arch—centuries old, heavy with epiphytes and water-bowls—settles half an inch closer to the floor.

If you walk far enough through these tiered aisles—choosing your footing along the firmer root-ridges, parting hanging curtains of silver bladder-moss—you will eventually find things the plants did not make alone.

They are rare, separated by miles of untouched tangle, and so deeply folded into the living mass that you can walk right past one before the geometry catches your eye. No metal was ever brought here; no blade has cut this tissue, no kiln has hardened brick, no chisel has touched stone. Whatever hands worked here worked the way the landscape itself works: slowly, softly, without breaking the skin of things.

Here, beside a shallow basin where warm mist rises steadily from a break in the peat-crust, stands a braided hollow. Centuries ago, someone coaxed dozens of supple aerial roots downward from an overhead arch, weaving them together and binding their intersections with strips of shed stem-skin until they fused into an egg-shaped dome about the height of a crouching person. Generations of untended growth have thickened the walls since then, swelling the braided ribs into fused, muscular pillars and pinching the low entrance into a narrow, tear-shaped cleft like a healing wound. Inside, the floor is dry—relatively dry, packed hard with dried moss and pale ash-like lime—and the ribs of the ceiling still hold the unmistakable, patient rhythm of an over-under weave.

Farther on, stretched at chest height between two immense pillar-trunks, hangs a length of dark, rot-resistant vine pulled taut and tied in a series of deliberate, swollen knots. Threaded onto the cord at wide intervals are small, naturally perforated river-pebbles—worn utterly smooth, brought from somewhere far beneath the root-mat or carried up from the forgotten rims of the plateau—alternating with bleached, hollow seed-husks and tiny rings of bone. Follow the line, and it leads you safely across a deceptive expanse where the peat-crust thins over a deep, dark water-vein, terminating at a low ledge of flat, unworked shale flakes stacked by hand at the edge of a mirror-pool. The stones are never fitted or mortared, only chosen for flatness and laid one upon another, now half-swallowed in oxblood velvet.

High in the forks of the oldest canopy-bridges, you sometimes spot sagging woven cradles—platforms of dead, interlaced stems smeared with pale bog-clay, half-reclaimed by hanging ferns. In one, a pair of thick, hollowed gourd-shells sit upright, brimming with clear condensate, their rims worn smooth by fingers or lips. Nothing looks freshly abandoned, yet nothing looks entirely dead either. Once in a great while, miles from any dome or stacked ledge, you come across a pale, supple root-tendril that has been bent around a neighbor and knotted once, the bend still faint and bruised where the skin stretched—perhaps a week ago, perhaps a season, impossible to tell in a place where wounds heal in decades and decay takes a lifetime.

If you climb—ascending the broad, gently sloped backs of the fused trunks, stepping from rib to mossy rib through layer after layer of dripping parchment-membranes—you can eventually breach the upper roof of the canopy.

Up here, there is no vista of peaks or skies, only a vast, rolling sea of velvet-dark domes and pale translucent sheets stretching outward in every direction until the horizon simply dissolves into warm white cloud. The scale of it is disorienting; you are standing on the back of a single, unbroken, continent-sized organism—or a parliament of organisms fused so long ago they have forgotten where one ends and another begins.

From above, you can watch the whole plateau breathe. Over the course of a long, dim afternoon, warm mist rises from the deep fermenting valleys beneath the canopy, thickening the cloud-layer overhead until it turns heavy and pewter-dark. Gradually, almost imperceptibly, the cloud settles back down into the domes, feeding the weeping membranes, refilling the aerial bowls, soaking down into the dark peat to keep the buried warmth alive. There are no storms, no droughts, no sharp seasons—only this vast, unhurried, self-contained tide of warm vapor rising and sinking across an ancient roof of the world that has reached the end of its changing, and settled into the quiet work of simply continuing.
