Below the earth, there should be darkness. Instead, the cavity is blinding.

It is not a large place—roughly the size and proportions of a small cathedral nave, or the inside of a broad, sunken bell—yet its emptiness gives it an unnerving sense of scale. Almost the entire volume is nothing but warm, brilliant air. The walls curve inward smoothly from a flat, chalky floor, meeting overhead in an unbroken dome sixty paces up, with no columns, no hanging stalactites, no fallen rubble to interrupt the sightline. Because every surface is pale and the light arrives from everywhere above at once, depth flattens out. Standing near one edge, the opposite wall looks at once close enough to touch and miles away through a haze of white glare.

The light comes through the ceiling itself. Over ages too slow to measure, the stone roof of this hollow grew into a dense, fused cap of fibrous mineral—millions of hair-thin, translucent crystals packed vertically side by side, like frozen silk or stacked glass reeds. They act as a vast, crude lens, drinking the sun from whatever arid plain lies overhead and piping it straight down into the chamber. Looking up hurts the eyes. The dome glows a uniform, ferocious milk-white, pulsing only when the weather shifts on the unseen surface above. Now and then, a soft grey smudge drifts languidly across the chamber floor: a cloud passing overhead, its shape blurred by yards of crystal grain into a bruise of dimmer white.

Because the light pours down in tight vertical threads, almost nothing casts a sideways shadow. Whatever rests on the floor holds only a small, sharp pool of pale violet shade tucked directly beneath its belly.

The chamber is old, long past the violence that carved it. Whatever underground river once hollowed this limestone and gypsum pocket vanished epochs ago, leaving behind walls worn to the smoothness of river-pebbles, sculpted into broad, shallow scallops that hold the light like hammered silver. The air is comfortably warm, trapped beneath the glowing cap like heat inside a white linen tent, and mostly dry. Most of the floor is bare stone dusted with fine chalk flour.

Yet moisture persists here, patient and intermittent. Wherever cooler veins of rock thread behind the ceiling’s crystal crust, condensation gathers in slow, invisible films. It creeps down the smooth wall-curves in hair-thin silver tracks, darkening the pale gypsum for an hour before evaporating again. Higher up, on the slight ledges where crystal seams meet, water beads until its own weight breaks it loose. Every few minutes, a single heavy drop falls straight through fifty feet of glaring empty air, catching the vertical light like a falling spark before striking the floor.

Where these drops have landed for centuries, they have worn shallow, saucer-shaped basins into the pale rock. A dozen or so of these pockets sit scattered across the broad emptiness of the floor, each ringed with a delicate, frosty lip of re-deposited mineral salt. Some hold a finger's depth of clear, tepid water; others are merely damp, darkened chalk waiting for the next slow drip.

Green life exists only as a sparse punctuation around these dampnesses and along the hairline cracks where the walls meet the floor. Wide expanses of blank stone separate every plant from its neighbor. Beside the saucers grow small, isolated tufts of pale chartreuse fern, their stems almost translucent, their fronds stiff and slightly glazed with mineral crust. Flattened rosettes of papery yellow-green lichen cling to the cooler north wall like dry fish scales. Here and there, a wiry, leafless creeper threads its way out of a seam, sending hair-fine pale roots across the bare rock to drink the wall-sweat, blooming rarely in tiny, parchment-colored cups that smell faintly of warm dust and wet chalk.

At first glance, the hollow looks utterly uninhabited—sterile as the inside of a bleached shell. The glare hides things, and the vast, empty air swallows small presences whole.

Only when your eyes narrow against the brightness and settle on the margins do you realize the chamber is quietly crowded.

High in the warm, luminous void, thin clouds of nearly transparent midges hang in the rising thermals, so widely spaced and pale they read as floating dust until one crosses a brighter crystal-beam and flashes silver. Inside the shallow water-saucers, translucent snails the size of fingernails glide over the mineral sediment, their shells thin as onion skin, sharing the tepid water with pale, thread-like shrimp that freeze whenever a cloud-smudge passes overhead. Along the curving warmth of the lower walls, dozens of small, alabaster-skinned lizards rest completely motionless, plastered flat against the scalloped stone. They have heavy, milky eyelids that shield their eyes from the relentless glare, and they stay still for hours at a time, waiting for a midge to drift within tongue’s reach.

And then you notice the objects lying scattered on the chalk floor.

There are no buildings here—no stacked walls, no hearths, no shelters, no carved reliefs or gathered settlements. The stone of the chamber is completely untouched. Yet resting beside a damp saucer lies a splinter of clear gypsum, carefully pressure-flaked along one edge into a needle-sharp blade. Ten paces away, near a dry crack, a piece of grey chert has been chipped into a small, crescent-shaped scraper, its grip worn smooth by oil and skin. Dangling from a seam halfway up the lower wall is a braided cord of pale creeper-fiber, knotted around a tiny limestone weight so that condensation runs down the thread and drips directly into a hollow chipped into a loose rock below.

The makers of these things are here, too—dozens of them, though spaced so generously around the perimeter and tucked so deeply into the gentle scallops of the floor that the great hollow still feels empty.

They are slender, forearm-sized creatures, pale as wet clay, with hairless, faintly translucent skin that shows the pale blue lace of veins across their ribs. They sit hunched on folded hind legs, their long, five-fingered hands busy in their laps. Heavy bony shelves jut above their eyes like natural visors, casting permanent shadow over dark, vertical-slitted amber pupils. They do not cluster together or build roofs; there is no rain to flee and no predator large enough to enter the narrow fissures that feed this chamber. Each claims a wide circle of glaring chalk and works in solitary, unhurried silence.

One sits near a dry basin, turning a palm-sized flake of flint over and over, striking its edge gently with a sliver of shed bone. *Tink.* A long pause—perhaps half a minute—while it inspects the edge, blows the white dust away with a soft puff of breath, and strikes again. *Tink.* Another kneels beside a wet saucer, using a sharpened gypsum needle to pry the tough mineral crust from a snail’s shell before drawing out the meat. A third sits entirely still near the base of a creeper, rolling stripped pale fibers against its thigh until they twist into a fine, strong thread, its head tilting occasionally to track the slow drift of a shadow-smudge across the ceiling.

Because the dome is smooth and empty, sound behaves strangely here. The chamber is mostly hushed—a dry, warm stillness where you can hear the blood in your own ears—yet any small noise travels cleanly across the full width of the bowl. That single dry *clink* of bone striking flint forty paces away arrives sharp and intimate, as though it happened beside your elbow. The wet *plip* of a ceiling-drop hitting a water-saucer rings out with a faint, glassy echo before dissolving back into silence. Now and then, a lizard’s dry toes whisper across the chalk, or a fiber snaps, and then five minutes pass with no sound at all.

Everything here obeys a patient, low-burning rhythm. The makers do not hoard their tools or carry them far; when a blade dulls or a wick clogs with salt, they simply set it down on the floor and move a few paces away to knap another. Over years and decades, the intermittent dampness and mineral-rich dust settle over the abandoned craft. A dropped chert scraper slowly grows a frosted, crystalline rind; a forgotten fiber snare turns brittle and calcifies into a pale, stony twig, fusing gently into the floor.

Standing in the center of the hollow, bathed in the vertical, shadowless glare of a sky you cannot see, you are surrounded by the quiet accumulation of centuries of small hands—flint flakes, bone awls, salted cords, all slowly turning back into the white stone they came from, while around the bright, echoing rim, the pale makers sit far apart in the warm stillness, tapping, weaving, and waiting for the next drop of water to fall.
