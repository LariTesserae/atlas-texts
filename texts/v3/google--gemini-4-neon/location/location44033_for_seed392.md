There is no ground here that was not laid down by hand.

Across a wide, shallow basin—the breadth of a market valley, low-rimmed and open to the sky—the floor is an unbroken patchwork of pale lime-plaster, unglazed sun-baked brick, and thick mats of woven reed stitched together with raw hemp. Every tile still holds the sharp bevel of the wooden mold that shaped it. The ridges of wet mortar squeezed between courses are crisp as pulled wax, marked here and there by the fine parallel whorls of a thumb or the flat drag of a bone trowel. Nothing has weathered. No corner has spalled or rounded off, no cane lashing has frayed, no plaster lip has crumbled into dust. Yet the entire expanse sits bathed in a heavy, stifling, wet heat, beneath a brightness so total it strips the color out of the air.

The light has no disc and no direction. The sky above the low terrace-rim is a seamless dome of incandescent chalk-white, glowing like the inside of a fired kiln just before it cools, carrying the faint, fibrous grain of stretched linen if you squint up into it long enough. Everything below is drenched in that noon-blinding glare. Even under the wide overhanging eaves of the thatch shelters, or deep beneath the paddle-sized leaves of the wet gardens, shadows never thicken into darkness; they thin out to a luminous, milky slate, as though the clay and foliage were faintly translucent and lit from within their own thickness.

Water is everywhere, shallow and poised. The basin is terraced in low, broad steps no higher than a shin, forming wide rectangular pans, narrow runnels channeled in split bamboo, and sunken courtyards where lukewarm water lies an inch deep over herringbone brick. Condensation beads steadily on every horizontal plane—the flat tops of mudbrick balustrades, the backs of waxy leaves, the dry-looking reed bundles—turning the pale plaster gleaming and slick. Along the edges of the terraces sit waist-high troughs woven from open-slatted wicker, wide gaps showing cleanly between every strip of split willow. They are filled to the brim with clear water. The water simply refuses to pass through the lattice; it bulges out between the reeds in smooth, taut pillows, trembling slightly with surface tension, weeping a bright glassy film that never gathers enough weight to drip.

Across the basin, the architecture is sparse, leaving broad, blindingly open courts of damp tile between one structure and the next. Low open-sided pavilions of lashed pole and thick palm-thatch stand widely spaced, connected by long, raised causeways of fitted limestone blocks, each block hand-dressed with visible chisel-pocks. Between the causeways, spaced out with a patient, unhurried geometry, rise the gardens.

They are startlingly lush—dense, swollen islands of tropical green contained within notched timber cribs, woven clay rings, and sunken stone cisterns. Shield-broad taro leaves, thickets of pale-jointed cane, trailing curtains of fleshy moss braided deliberately onto knotted hemp nets, towering glossy ferns whose fronds arch ten feet out over the shallow water trays. Every plant is turgid with moisture, deep emerald and chartreuse, impossibly vigorous. Yet if you crouch beside a timber planter and part the stems, there is no soil—no dark humus, no rotting leaf-mold, no earthworms churning compost. The roots grip bundles of clean, tightly lashed straw, hollow terracotta tubes stacked like honeycomb, and hand-chipped pebbles fitted together without a speck of silt. Not a single dead leaf lies on the tiles anywhere in the valley. No frond yellows at the tip; no petal browns or detaches. The foliage holds the exact posture of high, dripping summer, swollen to the brink of bursting and going no further.

Life crowds these green islands and the bright water-pans around them, yet the whole valley lies wrapped in a thick, cottony hush.

Stilted wading birds with chalk-white legs and pale coral bills stand by the dozens in the ankle-deep basins, spaced along the mirror-smooth water like carved markers. Jewel-skinned frogs—cobalt, ochre, translucent lime—cling belly-down to the undersides of brick lintels and sit in clusters along the rims of the wicker troughs. Along every mortar coping, bronze and turquoise lizards lie pressed flat to absorb the damp heat, their flanks rising and falling in slow, silent rhythm. Huge lace-winged dragonflies rest motionless on the tips of split bamboo; porcelain-shelled freshwater crabs pick delicately along the clean algal slick of the runnels; papery white moths hang in overlapping shingles beneath the thatch eaves, thousands of them folded tight against the glare.

Despite all this abundance, almost no sound crosses the terraces. The hot, saturated air seems to swallow vibration a few paces from its source. A wading bird opens its slender beak, its throat swells with the unmistakable tension of a cry, and nothing emerges save a faint, dry click of horn. Frogs inflate their translucent vocal sacs until the skin shines like oiled bladder, hold them taut, and slowly deflate in absolute silence. Now and again, if you stand close to a water trough, you hear a single small *tock* as a bead of condensation meets the surface, or the dry, papery scrape of a crab’s claw against unglazed clay. Beyond ten strides, even those tiny sounds flatten into the warm stillness, leaving only the subtle pressure of humidity against the eardrums.

Stay still for a few minutes and the deeper strangeness of the place settles in.

Heat shimmer hangs above the sun-baked causeways, bending the outlines of distant thatch roofs into wavy ribbons—except the ripples do not rise or flutter. They are fixed in place, rigid as imperfections poured into clear glass; walk ten steps to the left and you pass out of a stationary distortion into clear air, then into another frozen wave. Shadows behave with a similar quiet disobedience. A broad clay cistern casts a crisp, pale-grey shadow that begins a handbreadth away from its base, leaving a thin bright halo of light between the object and its own darkness. Two identical broad-leaved ferns set side by side in the same open court throw their faint shadows in slightly different directions, each leaning politely away from the nearest walkway.

If you pry up a loose paving brick at the edge of a dry stair, hoping to find dirt or gravel beneath the mortar bed, you find instead a second course of freshly laid reed matting, lashed tightly with new hemp. Part the reeds and below them lies a thin gap of blinding white light—the exact sourceless glare of the sky—and beneath that, the neat plaster ceiling of another terrace turned askew, with inverted clay troughs hanging calmly above an unseen floor. There is no bedrock down there, only craft continuing past the point where support should exist.

Distances, too, do not quite honor the straight lines laid out by the builders. You can walk down a long, dead-straight causeway toward a distant bamboo trellis hung with scarlet wax-flowers, keeping your eyes on the doorway woven into its base, and find after a few dozen paces that the trellis is suddenly at your shoulder, facing the wrong way, the doorway behind you. Two low flights of four brick steps descend from opposite sides of a raised plaster platform; walk down the eastern stair and you arrive in the exact same shallow courtyard as the western one, the same motionless white heron standing three paces to your left, its reflection unbroken.

Nothing here is aging, and nothing is quite moving forward. A fat drop of water hangs elongated from the pointed tip of a taro leaf, stretched to the thinness of a thread, poised on the instant of letting go. You can watch it until the heat makes your eyes water and it will not fall; look away toward the far rim of the basin and look back, and the drop is gone, a new round bead already swelling in its place, the shallow water beneath it completely unrippled. A lizard’s foot hovers half an inch above a warm tile, mid-stride. Wait long enough and you realize the creature is not frozen—its black eye blinks cleanly, once—yet the world around it refuses to accumulate change. There is no drift of pollen on the standing water, no tide in the runnels, no shifting of the glare from east to west, no rot softening the raw clay where it meets the wet. Materials meant to dissolve in a single season of rain—mudbrick, lime-wash, green cane, knotted grass—sit in gleaming, sharp-edged permanence, as if the moment immediately after the last basket of plaster was smoothed had simply broadened sideways until it replaced the passage of time.

At the far edges of the valley, the low terraces step up once, twice, three times, terminating in a clean waist-high parapet of hand-fitted stone. Beyond the parapet there is no mountainside, no distant forest, no horizon line—only a luminous white mist of warm vapor rising gently from nowhere, meeting the bright, linen-textured dome of the sky without a seam. Standing in the center of a broad damp court, surrounded by immaculate green foliage and hundreds of bright, silent creatures, you feel less like you are standing outdoors than inside an immense, breathless vessel—hand-built down to its hidden seams, freshly washed, blazingly bright, and held forever on the clean side of beginning.
