High on the newly sheared shoulder of a dark massif, well above the bright, flat deck of the clouds, sits a basin no wider than a cathedral nave.

Three sides of it are boxed in by raw walls of fractured basalt and greenish-black glass, thrust upward so recently that the edges have not yet learned to round themselves. Every facet is sharp as a fresh knapping—conchoidal scars, stepped ledges, hairline fissures with pale mineral crust just beginning to form along their lips. The fourth side simply ends: a clean, jagged drop into bright blue air and a distant floor of sunlit cloud-tops. 

Step past the rim and the world changes temperature in the width of a hand.

Outside, the high-altitude air is thin and razor-cold. Inside the basin, the heat is immense—a heavy, radiant, almost scalding warmth that rises from the dark stone and presses evenly from every direction, as though the sky above were a clear lid over an oven. The light itself is ordinary, bright midday sun, crisp and unclouded, throwing clean shadows across the floor. There is no haze to soften it, no storm-glow, no dramatic gloom. Just honest, high-mountain daylight pouring down into an impossible furnace.

And everywhere, there is water.

It weeps continuously from the vertical seams of the fresh rock walls, sliding down the sharp black faces in thick, glistening sheets. It pools in the shallow angular depressions of the floor. It beads along the underside of overhangs and clings to the edges of fractured ledges. By every ordinary rule of altitude and heat, this water should be shrieking away into furious clouds of steam; at this height, water ought to boil well below the temperature of the stone beneath it. Instead, it lies completely still.

The silence is the first thing that unsettles the mind, even more than the heat. A drop gathers on a knife-edged overhang, swells larger than a drop should—heavy with an uncanny surface tension—detaches, and falls onto the blazing floor. There is no hiss. No sizzle. Only a muted, fleshy *thup*, as if it were oil or mercury, before it flattens into a trembling lens and rolls slowly down the slope to join a mirror-clear pool. Touch the surface and it would blister skin in an instant, yet not a single bubble breaks its skin. It is water held past its breaking point by something invisible, forced to remain liquid, dense, and quiet.

Across the basin’s wide floor, the emptiness is deliberate and spacious. Most of what you see is bare, wet, gleaming rock, divided by long, clean sightlines. Against this dark expanse, scattered at generous intervals, stand the things that make the basin possible—not buildings, not machinery with joints or vents, just a handful of small, immaculate presences that look neither placed nor grown.

Near the center, hovering two finger-widths above a shallow mirror pool, is a matte-silver ring no larger than a millstone. It turns so slowly you only notice its motion when you look away and look back. Directly beneath its shadow, the scalding water forms a smooth, permanent dimple, unrippled and glossy. Closer to the cliff’s edge, three knee-high plinths of pale, porous ceramic jut straight from the dark stone in an uneven triangle; no mortar holds them, and the rock around their bases shows no sign of cutting, as if the stone simply paused where they began. High up on the rear wall, threaded through a vertical crack, runs a seam of hair-thin violet filaments—though looking directly at them makes the eyes ache, because the rock behind them appears slightly closer than the rock beside them, and the water weeping across them slows to a viscous crawl before accelerating again below.

None of these objects hum. None of them glow. They offer no seam, no heat, no explanation. Yet the air between them feels faintly pressured, thick enough to make the inner ear gently pop as you move from one end of the terrace to the other. Sound behaves strangely here too: high frequencies seem to thin out and die within a few paces, sheared away by the same quiet regulation that refuses to let the water boil. The distant wind rushing past the mountain’s flanks is completely inaudible inside the rim. You hear only low, near things—the soft shift of wet gravel under weight, the dull drop of overheated water, the occasional dry *tick* of a rock seam adjusting to the sun.

In the spaces where the weeping water gathers and moves along the jagged runnels, life erupts in dense, localized thickets.

The vegetation does not carpet the basin; it claims it in lush, isolated islands separated by wide flats of bare, shining stone. Every thicket is crowded, layered, and furiously alive, built to thrive in near-boiling moisture under bright open sky. Broad, fleshy fronds of deep emerald and oxidized copper spread outward in overlapping rosettes, their surfaces lacquered in a slick, iridescent oil that repels the scalding droplets just enough to channel them down curved central ribs toward tangled mats of bone-white roots. Those roots grip the sharp volcanic fractures like pale fingers, secreting a faint chalky paste that slowly softens the glass-hard edges of the rock.

Above the broad leaves, slender jointed stalks rise to chest height, bearing clusters of translucent, bladder-like pods filled with clear liquid and suspended dark seeds. From the upper ledges of the enclosing walls, heavy curtains of braided, mossy ribbons hang straight down, saturated and dripping, swaying in slow, orderly ribbons on the invisible thermal currents that rise off the stone. Where a thicket happens to grow near one of the ceramic plinths or beneath the violet seam, its geometry turns odd: leaves on the near side grow thick, pale, and tightly curled, while those a pace away unfurl broad and dark, riding the invisible contour lines of the basin’s altered physics.

Nothing here is stagnant, despite the stillness. The basin runs on quiet, interlocking cycles.

Every twenty minutes or so, a mass of sun-warmed air climbing the cliff face from the cloud deck far below strikes the invisible boundary along the open rim. There is no gust of wind, no sudden draft—only a subtle darkening of the air’s clarity, a fleeting weight against the skin. Within seconds, water blooms simultaneously across every dry millimeter of stone and leaf in the basin, condensing out of nowhere until the entire cathedral-sized floor shines like freshly oiled slate. Slowly, over the next quarter hour, that sudden skin of water gathers into silent runnels, feeds the pools, swells the dripping curtains of moss, and sinks back into deep hairline cracks near the rim—only to be drawn upward through the heated rock and wept back out along the upper cliffs.

On a longer, less predictable rhythm, the basin shivers. The hovering silver ring tilts by a hair’s breadth, or the three pale plinths darken a single shade for the span of three breaths. Wherever that subtle recalibration passes—often a narrow strip only a few paces wide—the lock on the water momentarily loosens. Without a sound, a band of the scalding pool turns pearlescent as millions of microscopic bubbles bloom simultaneously through the liquid, rising in a slow, shimmering curtain like upside-down snow. Mineral flakes and pale, buoyant spores lift from the bottom, riding the brief effervescence to the surface. Certain low, cup-shaped plants along the pool margins recognize the shift immediately; their thick petals uncurl in seconds, catching the rising spores on sticky amber stigmas before the ring levels itself and the water snaps back to dead, glassy stillness.

Animals are scarce here, and those that exist share the place’s economy of motion.

On the wet black slopes where the weeping cliffs meet the floor, slow, palm-sized grazers creep across the stone. They look something like flattened limpets, with shells built of overlapping plates of translucent mica that split the midday sunlight into faint prismatic glints as they turn. Beneath the shell, a broad, dark-crimson foot clings flush to the burning basalt, rasping away the pale, heat-loving microbial films that grow along the seepage lines. They move a hand’s width in ten minutes, leaving clean dark trails behind them that vanish as soon as the next condensation pulse rolls through.

Occasionally, a pale, stiff-winged thing—half moth, half translucent seed-pod—detaches itself from the underside of an oily frond and glides silently across the empty stone between thickets. It does not beat its wings; there is no buzz, no flutter. Its rigid, glassy vanes simply catch the rising sheets of warm air, banking lazily from one green island to the next to drink hot, mineral-heavy nectar from the bladder-pods.

And once in a long while, if you stand very still near the rim and scan the bare rock between the thickets, you notice that one of the pale vertical stalks is not a plant.

It stands knee-deep in a mirror pool, motionless on four impossibly thin, jointed legs that keep its small, heat-reflective body high above the scalding surface. Its carapace is the color of bleached bone and dry ash, angled in sharp, faceted planes that echo the young rock walls behind it. Its long, narrow head tilts slightly toward the water, watching the slow mica-shelled grazers creeping along the submerged lip of a fracture. When it strikes, there is no splash—only a clean, surgical plunge of a needle-thin appendage, a faint concentric ring spreading across the unboiling surface, and then stillness again as it lifts its catch clear of the heat.

Walk back to the open edge of the basin and look out. Beyond the last sharp tooth of basalt, the clouds stretch to the horizon, dazzling white under a normal sun, cold enough to freeze breath solid in the air. Just behind your heel, a pool of hundred-degree water sits smooth as polished obsidian, reflecting the blue sky without a ripple, while a single fat drop falls from a copper-green frond with a soft, heavy *thup* into the quiet.
