The air arrives before anything else: thin enough to make a deep breath feel slightly hollow in the chest, yet held at a steady, dry warmth, like the interior of a brick oven hours after the coals have been raked out. There is no wind to stir it. It simply rests against the skin, smelling faintly of baked clay, old copper, and hot dust that has long since stopped rising.

Around you, the darkness is nearly complete. Looking up gives no sense of stars, cloud, or ceiling — only a high, empty blackness that feels vast and unpressurized, as though this tableland has been lifted out of the lower world and left suspended in a lightless upper reach. Down along the ground, the dark is broken only by pinpricks so dim your eyes take twenty minutes to register them: a dull ember-line beneath the steel door of a distant hall, the bruised amber of a vacuum-tube beacon burning at the very bottom of its filament life, or, once in a long while, a brief blue spark snapping silently between ceramic discs high on a pylon.

Underfoot, the ground is paved. Even in the dark, your soles read the geometry: broad hexagonal tiles of dark, vitrified slag, worn over ages until every seam is a shallow, rounded groove and the faces feel like warm soapstone. This is a city, or the settled remainder of one, laid out across a plateau kilometres wide. Yet it is curiously sparse. The avenues run straight and extraordinarily wide — a hundred paces from curb to curb — separated by huge, open yards of bare pavement and slumped gravel beds where nothing stands at all. Between these expanses sit the structures: low, heavy manufactories, windowless substations, squat brick-and-iron switch-houses, and the piers of elevated viaducts. They sit far apart, giving the whole plateau the hush of a vast railway marshalling yard at the end of history.

Whatever forces shaped and wore this place finished their work so long ago that the landscape has passed beyond decay into a final, stable posture. The stone curbs have been ground and slumped into smooth, pillowy curves. Cast-iron lamp standards, long stripped of globes, lean slightly under their own ancient weight, sealed beneath a thick, dry skin of velvety maroon oxide that does not flake when touched; the rust has oxidized itself into permanence. Where factory walls hold tall rows of industrial glass, the panes have not shattered but sagged slowly inside their mullions into thick, rippled lobes, frosted opaque with fused dust. Even the mortar between the bricks has annealed into the clay, turning walls into single, seamless mineral masses. Nothing here is crumbling anymore. Everything has found its lowest, quietest shape and stopped.

You feel the elevation most when you follow one of the main boulevards to the far margins of the grid. There are no suburbs, no ragged outskirts. The paved street simply meets a broad, bevelled lip of bedrock, worn round as the rim of a stone mortar, and beyond it the darkness drops sheer away. Leaning over that smooth edge, you hear nothing rising from below — no wind, no distant water, no echo — only the same dry, thin warmth thinning out into an immense, featureless drop.

Water has no part in this place. There is no rain, no dew, no frost, not even a dry streambed carved into the plateau stone. The only moisture left on the tableland is locked away inside the machinery: tiny, ancient pools of thick dielectric oil sweating imperceptibly along the bolted flanges of transformers, turning the dust around their bases into dark, dry-feeling putty, and here and there, set into the flanks of iron cisterns, thick quartz sight-glasses where a bead of yellowish brine or heavy mineral oil still holds a dead-level meniscus that has not trembled in centuries.

And yet the city is not dead.

At first it seems completely silent, so quiet that the dry scrape of your own shoe on slag sounds indecent. Then, as you stand still, you notice a vibration entering through your heels and settling behind your molars — a steady, low mains hum, somewhere near forty cycles, rising from buried conduits beneath the avenues. Every few minutes, half a kilometre away across an empty plaza, a heavy mechanical relay drops into place inside a brick housing: a single, crisp *clack*, dry as a wooden block struck once, carrying effortlessly through the motionless air before vanishing without echo.

Follow that hum into one of the low, sawtooth-roofed halls — their iron doors stand ajar, wedged permanently open by decades of packed grit — and the true strangeness of the place reveals itself. Inside, the warmth is a few degrees heavier, radiating from long rows of cast-iron presses and muffled kilns whose interiors glow a sub-visible cherry red through hairline vents. Overhead, braided steel cables creep across wide iron sheaves at the speed of a clock’s minute hand.

The machines are still running, locked in a closed loop that neither produces nor consumes.

From the throat of a heavy, slow-cycling press, a palm-sized disc of dense, unglazed grey porcelain drops every forty seconds onto a slatted metal belt. Each disc bears the ghost of a stamped emblem, worn down over millennia of repeated pressing until the dies leave nothing more than a faint, thumb-sized dimple. The belt carries the discs slowly out through a wall-slot, across an open viaduct spanning a wide boulevard, and into the intake of a low brick kiln, where they sit in the dark warmth for hours before gliding out the far side onto an unmanned rail-tram. The tram creeps three blocks down a dead-level iron track, emitting only a soft, dry hiss of carbon brushes against a live third rail, and tips its cargo into the hopper of a grinding mill. There, twin granite rollers turn with glacial quietness, crushing the warm porcelain discs back into fine, talc-like powder. Sealed pneumatic screws draw that powder back beneath the street, returning it to the press hoppers where it is compacted once more.

Across the entire plateau, this is how the industry behaves. Copper ribbons are unspooled, annealed in dim electric furnaces, coiled, carried across empty yards by overhead gantries, and unspooled again. Heavy flywheels turn in dust-free oil baths to keep generators excited just enough to power the motors that turn the flywheels. Whatever external trade, raw ore, or purpose once fed this municipal engine vanished before memory; unable to halt, the system routed its own outputs back into its intakes and settled into a zero-sum metabolism, circulating the same fixed weight of stone, metal, and charge in endless, nearly frictionless equilibrium.

The place enforces that equilibrium on anything that enters it. Light behaves strangely here — the matte, velvety oxides and heat-dulled slag drink glare rather than reflecting it, so even a bright lantern carves only a small, muddy hemisphere out of the dark, refusing to reach the far side of an avenue. Cold objects brought onto the plateau warm swiftly until they match the ambient heat of the stone. With no sun, no sky-glow, no wind, and no seasons, time loses its traction; an hour and a week feel identical, measured only by the faint progress of a creeping gantry silhouetted against a slightly less black wall.

No plant grows anywhere on the plateau. There is not a thread of moss in a curb-crack, not a crust of lichen on a warm transformer rib, not even the brittle skeleton of a dead weed. The chemistry here has no room for green, for rot, or for growth.

Yet the plateau is not wholly empty of life.

If you kneel beside one of the buried busbar trenches where the pavement stays a few degrees warmer than the surrounding tiles, and wait in the dark until your eyes adjust to the faintest static shimmer, you may see small, flat shapes clinging to the ceramic insulators. They are the size of a hand, pale and papery as dried onion skin, with segmented, blind carapaces that feel brittle as baked clay. They do not scuttle; they shift only millimetres at a time, aligning the fine metallic filaments along their undersides with the stray magnetic fields leaking from the cables. When they shed, their empty husks detach and settle onto the hexagonal tiles with a tiny, papery clink like a dropped washer, remaining there untouched by wind or decay until they slowly powder under their own dryness.

Rarer still are the walkers. You might cross ten kilometres of silent avenues and never encounter one, or you might pause at a wide roundabout — where a house-sized copper toroid wrapped in petrified linen sits on a worn stone plinth — and hear a soft, rhythmic tapping approaching down the street. Out of the dark steps a tall, narrow creature on four stiff, multi-jointed limbs, its hooves tipped in dense, horn-like mineral that clicks lightly on the slag. It has no eyes, no visible ears, and no fur; its hide is grey, dry, and pleated like weathered leather bellows. Moving with unhurried, mechanical poise, it approaches the side of a brick substation, bows its long neck, and presses a broad, flat, dish-shaped snout directly against a humming iron conduit box. It stays there for half an hour, utterly motionless, drawing warmth and faint trickle-current through sensitive pads along its palate, its ribs never visibly rising or falling. When it has had enough, it lifts its head, turns without haste, and steps away into the wide blackness between the halls, leaving no scent and no tracks on the smooth, dry stone.

Standing in the centre of an empty intersection, you can feel the whole plateau holding its breath around you — kilometres of straight, dark streets, rounded brick monuments, slow creeping belts, and warm, dead-still air perched high above a bottomless drop. Far across the grid, a relay clicks once. A minute later, a porcelain disc drops onto a moving slat, already on its way back to dust. Nothing is arriving, nothing is leaving, and the warm dark settles evenly over everything, unchanged and unhurried, as if the world had finally solved itself down to a single quiet hum.
