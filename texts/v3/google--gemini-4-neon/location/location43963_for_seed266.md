The land does not rise into mountains so much as it breaks into steps—continental shelves of ancient stone, each one several miles taller than the last, dropping away into a warm, sunlit emptiness that has no sea at its bottom.

Stand on the lip of one of the upper tables and the horizon is not a line, but a stack of horizons. Far out across the clear amber light, other mesas hang like pale, layered ships in the distance, separated by chasms wide enough to hold their own slow weather. There is no water anywhere in this expanse, nor any memory of it in the rock. No riverbeds, no mud, no green stain along the seams. Everything has been shaped by dry wind, thermal expansion, and the patient sliding of fine grit over ages too long to count. The cliffs are not jagged. They are fluted and burnished—great vertical ribs of red-ochre sandstone, pale gypsum, and bands of dark metallic shale worn smooth as bone, curving outward at their bases into immense, sloping aprons of talus that fade downward into a warm, silvery-tan haze.

The light is ordinary and generous, a clean midday brightness that travels for dozens of miles without tiring, laying sharp, dry shadows under the enormous overhangs. The air is warm the way a brick wall is warm late in the afternoon—dry enough that your lips tighten, clean of any rot or dampness, carrying only the faint mineral scent of sun-baked chalk, hot iron, and the crisp metallic tang of static electricity.

Because the scale is so vast, it takes a moment to notice the towns.

They sit in the creases of the rock and along the broad, wind-planed ledges—compact, layered knots of urban construction surrounded by leagues of bare stone and open sky. Between one settlement and the next there may be forty miles of empty cliff-face, bridged only by the faint silver arcs of high-tension cables and heavy haulage wires sagging across the abyss. Yet inside each ward, the building is dense, organized, and old: tiers of vitrified brick and cast-iron framing, terraced streets paved in fused slag, flat roofs crowded with ceramic insulators, louvered cooling towers, and thickets of copper busbars. Older foundations of quarried gypsum support newer steel gantries; covered stairways and counterweighted lift-shafts climb vertically from the lower dock-ledges to the upper residential galleries, tucked back into immense dry alcoves the wind carved out a million years ago.

It is an industrial world, unmistakably so—electrified, mechanized, built for scaled extraction and manufacture—and yet the first thing that strikes a visitor standing in the middle of a factory terrace is the quiet.

Nothing roars. There is no steam here, no hiss of quenching water, no hammering pistons tearing at the air. The machinery of the shelves moves with the patience of the geology it taps into. Every few miles along the cliff edge, colossal wind-wheels and canvas-vaned torsion towers cantilever out over the drop, catching the steady, warm thermal updraft that rises continuously from the deep basins below. Their sails are thirty yards across, ribbed with thin slate and oiled timber, and they turn so slowly you can count to forty between one spoke passing the gantry beam and the next. They make almost no sound—only a dry, distant sigh of fabric and the faint, measured *tock* of a weighted ratchet engaging deep inside a stone gear-house.

Down in the sintering yards, broad kilns bake wind-sifted metallic dust into structural glass, sintered brick, and heavy ingots of alloy. They run on quiet resistive heat and slow electrical arcs, drawing their power not from burning fuel, but from the cliffs themselves.

This is the strange engine at the heart of the settlements: the immense weight of these miles-high mesas is still settling, compressing their ancient quartz-bearing strata by a fraction of a millimeter each decade. Across a continental shelf, that imperceptible slump generates a vast, relentless piezoelectric charge. The cities are built directly along the live strain-seams. Great tapered spikes of forged copper and braided grounding rods are driven deep into the smoothed cliff walls, bleeding off the geological voltage into rows of porcelain-ringed transformers and silent, brick-housed substations. Walk down a narrow industrial alley between two foundries and you feel the power less in your ears than in your teeth and the fine hairs on your forearms—a low, steady, dry hum that never rises or falls, punctuated only every few minutes by the tiny bright *snap* of excess static jumping across a copper horn-gap.

Long cableways stretch out from the foundry docks, carrying iron gondolas loaded with pale ceramic slabs and raw sulfur-salts across the sunlit gulf to distant refining shelves. The cables creep at half a walking pace. A single shipment takes two days to cross a chasm, drifting in total silence over miles of empty air while the great drive-wheels at either terminal emit a soft, dry creaking of lubricated iron.

There is not a single plant anywhere in these towns, nor on the cliffs between them. No grass in the paving cracks, no moss on the shaded underside of arches, no lichen, no wood grown from soil. Whatever timbers exist in the older frames are petrified or imported from somewhere unimaginably far, or else they are not wood at all, but laminated mineral fiber pressed in the kilns.

And yet the place is thick with animal life.

At first, because everything moves so calmly and shares the dry palette of the rock, you barely register them. Then your eye adjusts, and the emptiness fills.

Out over the chasm, suspended in the warm, rising air, hundreds of drifters turn in lazy, mile-wide circles. They have no wet flesh and no feathers; their bodies are light, sealed arrangements of parchment-thin membrane stretched over hollow struts of porous silica, inflated with warm metabolic gases refined from the dry air. Some are broad, pale-amber kites thirty feet across, trailing hair-thin, electrically charged filaments down into the sunlit haze to snare drifting dust-fleas and airborne mineral motes. Others are clustered bladders of translucent chalk-white and rust-red that pulse once every half-minute, adjusting their buoyancy to ride the subtle horizontal layers where the warm air thickens with suspended gypsum dust. They pass right beneath the iron railings of the public promenades, close enough that you can hear the faint, papery whisper of their skins catching the breeze, before they tilt and slide effortlessly out over a three-mile drop.

Look closer at the sun-warmed cliff faces and the brick flanks of the transformer houses, and you see what feeds the bottom of this waterless world. Wherever stray static charge leaks from the rock seams or the heavy copper conduits, fine ionized dust settles out of the air and fuses into dry, branching crusts of metallic salt—pale green copper-blooms, canary-yellow sulfur needles, delicate frost-like whiskers of chalk and iron oxide. They are purely mineral, grown by voltage and warm grit rather than chlorophyll, yet they cover the shaded undersides of the gantries and the cracks of the escarpment like a crystalline fur.

Across these blooming seams move the grazers—legions of them. Flat, wide-shelled creatures ranging from the size of a coin to the size of a washbasin, armored in overlapping plates of laminated horn and shimmering mica. They have no liquid blood; their insides are dry, pressurized chambers and articulated levers driven by chemical resins and elastic mineral tendons. They cling to vertical walls and the undersides of steel bridges, moving inches an hour, rasping the metallic salt-blooms and static-grown crusts off the stone with quartz-tipped mouths. Stand near a substation wall in the stillness of afternoon and you can hear them: a soft, dry, sandpapery scraping, like dozens of tiny files working slowly on chalk, and the occasional light *clack* as two broad-backed shells brush past each other.

In the warm shadow beneath every ceramic insulator and along the ledges of the brick arc-furnaces live smaller, faster things—delicate, many-jointed creatures built like porcelain locusts or articulated twigs of amber glass. They bask in the electromagnetic hum, vibrating faintly in sympathy with the current, weaving dry, slightly conductive threads across unused scaffolding to trap static sparks and the microscopic dust-feeders that ride the warm currents. Higher up, tucked motionless into the fluted recesses of the overhangs and along the top beams of the crane-arms, wait the predators: long-limbed, sand-colored stalkers with segmented, hollow-boned frames and broad gripping pads. A stalker may hold completely still for six hours, indistinguishable from a rusted bracket or a ledge of shale, until a drifter sails too close to the parapet or a heavy grazer lifts its rim to cross a mortar seam. Even the kill is quiet—a swift dry rattle of plates, a puncture of a gas-sac that sighs once like a punctured bellows, and then stillness again.

Nothing here rots. When a creature dies, its sealed resins harden to amber and its dry body simply desiccates into lighter dust and fragile plates. Within days, scavengers dismantle it methodically—stripping the precious silica struts, the concentrated metal salts, the dry sinew—leaving behind nothing but a pale, clean scatter of mineral flakes that the breeze eventually sweeps over the lip of the shelf.

The settlements accommodate this abundant, dry life without fuss. The cable-towers have smooth ceramic baffles to keep grazers from clogging the slow gears; the terraced roofs are strung with wide mesh screens to keep drifting kites from tangling in the high-voltage busbars, though smaller ones often rest peacefully on the warm stone parapets like folded paper umbrellas. In the quiet residential alleys—narrow, shaded canyons of vitrified brick where dry air funnels gently past shuttered windows and terracotta chimneys—people walk at the unhurried pace the place demands. There is no point rushing when the lift from the Lower Smeltery takes three hours to arrive, when a cast glass beam takes four days to anneal in the cooling beds, when the continent itself breathes in a cycle that lasts nearly a week.

For four days at a time, the deep basins warm and the great air rises steadily, lifting the haze into pale gilded bands, turning the slow wind-wheels, carrying the vast silent flocks of drifters up past the highest urban balconies. Then, for three days, the gradient slackens; the air settles gently downward, a fine dry veil of ochre grit dusts the warm iron railings and the broad flat roofs, the grazers pull their shells tight against the brick, and the transformers hum a little louder in the calm.

Even then, the light remains clear and steady. Across ten miles of open sky, you can see the tiny, crisp geometry of a sister city clinging to the opposite escarpment—its white insulator towers glinting in the sun, its slow cable-cars hanging like beads on an invisible thread, surrounded on all sides by miles of smooth, ancient, sunlit stone and the quiet drift of papery wings in the warm, empty air.
