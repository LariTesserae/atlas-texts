High enough that the outer sky has thinned to a bruised, metallic indigo even at noon, a fresh needle of volcanic stone splits the wind. Nothing on this peak has had time to learn roundness. The crust pushed it up too recently and too violently, shearing basalt, dark rhyolite, and thick seams of milky quartz into vertical blades that gleam like broken bottle glass. Near the crown, where the outer gales howl hard enough to strip skin from bone, two massive slabs lean inward without quite meeting, enclosing a narrow, cathedral-sized cleft—perhaps eighty paces long, barely fifteen wide at its belly, rising two hundred feet to a crooked hairline slit of sky.

From the outside it looks like a crack in an anvil. Inside, the wind cannot reach, though you hear it continually above the roof, tearing past the sharp outer lip with the dry roar of ripping sailcloth.

The air inside the fissure is thin enough to make an outsider’s ears ring and lungs burn, yet it feels strangely heavy against the skin. Cloud-wrack brushing the exterior spire is siphoned through the overhead seam and cools against the dark, glassy stone, turning into a fine, clinging sweat. Because the cleft is shut off from the direct freeze of the outer heights—and warmed a few degrees by the sheer mass of living tissue packed along its walls—the moisture does not lock into ice. It stays just above freezing: a clammy, marrow-aching chill that hangs in the air as damp mineral vapor. Water beads along fresh cleavage planes, creeps down razor-sharp overhangs in oily threads, and gathers on the cleft floor in small, angular black pools between loose shards of talus. Nothing has smoothed those floor-shards either; they clink like dropped porcelain whenever anything shifts across them.

Light barely enters. Through the ragged seam two hundred feet overhead, a pale, cold blade of daylight falls into the upper throat of the fissure, illuminating wet rock faces and hanging pale shapes for a short stretch before breaking up against the jagged ledges. By the middle tiers, it is gone. The lower two-thirds of the cleft sit in near-total blackness—not the soft dark of a forest at night, but the hard, enclosed dark of a sealed box.

Yet the dark is never completely still. Every fraction of a second, somewhere along the sheer walls, a pinprick of cold, blue-white light winks into existence and vanishes.

Quartz struck hard against quartz flashes in the dark. And the walls are alive with things striking stone.

Before your eyes can make anything of those brief, ghostly sparks, the sound hits you—loud, dense, and strangely dry, amplified by the narrow, glass-hard walls until it presses against the eardrums. It is a continuous, layered hail of sharp *tinks*, brittle *clacks*, and rasping, metallic scrapes, underscored by a slow, wet wheezing that rolls down from the ceiling like air forced through drowned bellows. Every noise bounces cleanly off the unweathered facets, stacking on top of the last until the cleft hums with a steady, ringing clamor.

It takes a long moment to realize that the roar is not frantic. If you listen to any single source within it, the rhythm is agonizingly slow. A chime, then a wait of ten heartbeats, then a scrape. It is loud only because the cleft is saturated with bodies, stacked near-touching from the wet floor to the high overhang, every ledge and fracture occupied.

Near the top, suspended from the slick black stone by thick, elastic threads of mucus, hang hundreds of translucent, ribbed air-sacs the size of shields. They have no limbs and no eyes—only wide, fringed intake mouths and pale, veined membranes that swell with excruciating patience, taking nearly a minute to draw in the thin, damp altitude air, then collapsing slowly to expel a heavier, warmer breath. They hang in dense curtains, swaying gently against one another in the currents they create, their out-of-phase exhalations generating the deep, ragged sigh that fills the vault. Without them, the air in the lower cleft would stagnate; instead, they turn the whole fissure into a single, sluggish lung.

Below the hanging sacs, wedged onto every knife-edged shelf and clinging to the vertical seams in overlapping tiers, live the stone-tappers.

In the brief blue-white flash of a struck quartz vein, you catch only pieces of them at a time: a gaunt, pale-gray flank pressed flat against wet basalt; an elbow with an extra, backward-bending joint; four slender fingers tipped with dark horn gripping a wedged flake of black glass. They are roughly the size of crouched hounds, long-limbed and flat-ribbed so they can tuck their bodies flush against narrow vertical faces without tumbling into the dark. They have no faces in any familiar sense. Where eyes and snout should be, the head widens into a dish of pleated, leathery skin—a sensitive tympanic plate that quivers constantly, reading the shape of the cleft through the endless rebound of clicks and scrapes. Beneath the dish sits a single vertical mouth lined with blunt, grinding plates.

There are hundreds upon hundreds of them packed into this small volume. A shoulder nearly brushes a neighbor’s knee; a tail-like balancing hook curls inches above the pleated ear-dish of the climber on the ledge below. Yet there is no jostling, no sudden scuffle. Oxygen is too scarce at this height for haste. A climber takes five full seconds to lift a forearm, pauses to let its chest slowly expand, then brings its stone tool down with a single crisp, economical snap.

They build nothing. There are no walls here, no stacked stones, no woven nests or shelters—only raw, jagged geological fracture planes and the creatures pressed directly against them. Yet the entire cleft is littered with tools. Every climber carries or caches simple, handheld instruments: fist-sized knockers of milky quartz, flaked obsidian pry-bars, awls ground from the dense leg bones of their own dead, and serrated combs cut from dried ribs, all bound with crude, tight lashings of twisted tendon and tough plant fiber. Generations of slow fingers have worn smooth hollows into the sides of these stones. When a climber dies and stiffens on its ledge, its neighbors patiently pry the tools from its curled grip before nudging the body off into the darkness below.

The tools serve two purposes, and both are bound to the harsh rules of the cleft.

The first is harvest. Cemented deep inside the damp horizontal cracks are dense colonies of porcelain-shelled seam-clingers—armored, eyeless bivalve things that filter mineral dust and shed organic silt from the trickling condensation. Their shells are too hard for teeth or horn. A climber wedges a flaked obsidian blade into the hairline gap of a shell, taps the butt with a quartz knocker—*clack*, a cold blue spark—waits, and taps again until the shell parts with a wet pop, exposing the salty, fat-rich flesh inside.

The second purpose is weirder, and accounts for the cathedral’s deafening voice. Along the thickest vertical veins of quartz and glassy rhyolite, generations of climbers have scratched shallow, parallel grooves directly into the living rock. Slowly, methodically, dozens of them at a time drag notched bone combs across those grooves and strike the resonant stone ribs with their quartz hammers. The walls ring. High, piercing standing waves build inside the narrow rock chamber until the air itself feels prickly against the teeth—and high overhead, the vibration shakes loose the heavy condensation clinging to the ceiling and the skins of the hanging air-sacs. A sudden, cold patter of droplets rains down across the ledges, wetting the pleated skin of the climbers and refilling the tiny angular catch-basins where they drink. They literally ring the rock to make it weep.

Beneath them all, on the wet, shard-strewn floor, the darkness is thickest and the crowding turns horizontal. A slow-moving carpet of pale, blind, ribbon-bodied scavengers glides endlessly across the razor talus, slick as wet clay, dissolving everything that drifts down from the tiers above: broken shells, shed horn, dropped bone-splinters, drowned air-sacs, and the bodies of fallen climbers. Tiny, translucent jumping things skate across the surface tension of the black mirror-pools, leaping blindly whenever a shaken raindrop breaks the water. Even the bodies of the climbers themselves are crowded with slow, flat, leathery mites wedged behind their joints. Everywhere you might place a hand in the dark, something damp, shelled, or faintly trembling gives way beneath your palm—never darting, only oozing or recoiling with cold deliberation.

Plants barely hold on here, yet they are essential. In the upper quarter of the cleft, where the thin knife of high-altitude noon briefly brushes the sweating stone, tufts of wire-moss bristle from the cracks—stiff, charcoal-green needles that feel like rusted iron and rattle faintly when water drips across them. Lower down, in the near-total dark, pale, cartilaginous fronds jut horizontally from the wettest fractures. They have no true green in them; they are the color of wet bone, curled like dry tongues, coated in fine sticky hairs that trap chitin-dust, mineral flour knocked loose by the stone hammers, and drifting spores. Sparse as they are—just a stubby cluster every few paces—the climbers never tear them out by the root. They carefully strip single fibrous outer stalks with their flint blades, chewing them soft before twisting them into the cordage that lashes bone to stone.

The smell of the whole place is sharp and unmistakable: wet crushed flint, cold ozone blown down from the outer summit, bitter iron-sap from the pale fronds, and the dense, oily musk of thousands of cold-blooded bodies breathing in the dark.

Time moves differently inside the split needle. Outside, storms slam into the spire and vanish in an hour; clouds boil past in bright, violent rags. Inside, nothing hurries. Water gathers drop by drop across whole afternoons. A quartz hammer wears down by the thickness of a fingernail over a climber’s lifetime, passed hand to slow hand until it grows too small to grip and finally clatters down into the black pools among thousands of other discarded chips. Even the mountain is still moving, rising a hairbreadth at a time. Once every few months, deep inside the unweathered stone, pressure builds until the rock lets out a single, cannon-like crack that shakes dust from the roof.

When that happens, every hammer stops. Every notched rib freezes against its groove. For half an hour, the cathedral drops into a stunned, fragile quiet—only the ragged, wet wheezing of the hanging bellows above, the cold *plip* of condensation hitting black water, and the muffled scream of the jet-stream tearing past the distant seam of stars. Then, somewhere on a middle ledge, a pale forearm slowly rises. Quartz kisses quartz. A cold blue spark winks across a wet gray ear-dish, another answers from the opposite wall, and with unhurried, overwhelming persistence, the loud work of the cleft begins again.
