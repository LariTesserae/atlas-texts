You feel the height first in your lungs, not your eyes. The air is thin, cool, and scrubbed of every trace of damp—so dry that a breath tastes faintly of chalk and cold slate, and your lips tighten within minutes. Nothing stirs with warmth here. A steady, brisk chill sits inside the stone and draws heat cleanly out of whatever touches it, yet there is no frost, no rimed edge, no ice in the seams, because there is nothing wet enough to freeze.

Around you, the world is mostly air.

Miles above any visible ground—downward is only a pale, featureless drop into slate-grey haze—a sprawl of needle-backed ridges, detached mesas, and sheer limestone teeth hangs suspended in dim light. And across all of it, clinging to the spines and spanning the gaps between them, stretches a city.

Not a ruin in the ordinary sense, though it is unimaginably weathered. Its tiers, arcades, broad plazas, and stacked galleries are settled deeply into their forms, every sharp arris softened by millennia of dry abrasion, every flight of steps dished shallow in the center. Whole districts rise along narrow mountain ribs, five and ten stories of window-pierced stone stacked directly over vertical drops. Dry viaducts launch from one cliff-face and cross a quarter-mile of open void to meet a terraced avenue on the next spire. Between the clustered quarters lie vast expanses of nothing at all: hollow courtyards open to the sky, wide empty thoroughfares, mile-wide gulfs where you can look straight through the skeletal lattices of distant neighborhoods hanging like pale combs against the dim ash-colored horizon.

The light never seems to brighten or fade by much. It comes from everywhere at once out of a featureless, low-silver overcast—a permanent, shadowless dusk that drains color down to bone, pewter, dry ochre, and slate. Yet look at the flagstones, and you will see shadows anyway. Long, crisp, dark wedges lie across the empty plazas at angles the diffuse sky cannot possibly cast. Some belong to towers that stand three streets away; some belong to towers that are no longer there.

Walk up to any wall and run a hand over the masonry, and the first quiet impossibility reveals itself: there are no tool marks. No chisel-grooves, no saw-cuts, no mortar squeezed between courses, no iron pins, no sockets where hinges or lamps or timber beams might once have sat. There is no glass in the tall apertures, no bronze, no hearth-soot, no trace of fire or wheel or lever. Every colonnade, lintel, drainage-channel, and terraced amphitheater is cut from continuous, dry mineral—pale compacted chalk, layered salt-shale, porous pumice-stone—as if the bedrock itself had cleaved along municipal lines instead of geological ones.

And in fact, it still does. When a cool pressure-shift passes through a cliffside and a slab of shale fractures loose, it does not shatter into ragged talus. It parts cleanly into risers, sills, and chamfered slabs. On the exposed lip of a weathered terrace, you can find fresh rock-faces already indented with the shallow beginnings of a threshold, or a low bench, or a dry gutter leading cleanly toward an empty cistern. Erosion here has an architectural habit; stone wears down into corridors, rooms, and stairwells the way stone elsewhere wears down into caves and scree.

Yet for all its stone weight, the city is shockingly quiet. Sound dies quickly in the thin air, and the porous masonry swallows whatever echoes try to start. You do not hear wind, even when a cold pressure-curtain sweeps through the valley of spires and sets fine dust streaming horizontally off the parapets. What noise exists is tiny and close: the dry hiss of a single mica-flake skidding across a plaza, the faint *tick* of contracting stone as a barometric band passes, the papery rasp of your own breathing. Stand still in a six-sided square large enough to hold thousands, and the hush feels heavy enough to press against your eardrums.

Even so, the place is never at rest. Its motion is vast, continuous, and almost entirely silent.

Because there is no water, the city's drainage systems carry dry weather instead. Deep in the unseen abyss below, cool updrafts gather fine pale gypsum and powdered silt, lifting them in slow, coherent curtains up the sheer cliff-walls. The dust-tides pour *upward* through the street gutters, spill over the rims of dry cisterns, and flood down the open aqueducts like pale smoke moving along level channels. In some quarters the dust pools ankle-deep in the corners of sunken courtyards, rippling in slow concentric rings as invisible air-sheets pass overhead; then a shift in pressure lifts the whole drift cleanly off the flagstones and sends it cascading off a high balustrade back into the empty air, without a whisper.

Beneath the dust-cycles runs a deeper, stranger circulation—the slow, continuous revision of the city's own geometry.

Distance does not hold firmly at this elevation. Across the great open gaps between spires, space has weathered thin, just like the limestone. Walk down a colonnade that ends in a freestanding stone arch on the brink of a mile-deep drop, and look through the frame: you are not looking at the empty chasm beyond. You are looking straight into the dim interior of a terraced gallery on a peak two leagues east. Step through, and there is no jolt, no rush of air, no flash of light—only the sudden change in the grain of the flagstones under your boot, and the realization, when you glance back over the parapet, that the spire you left a second ago is now a pale needle across an unbridged gulf.

Not all these joins are permanent. The city rearranges its adjacencies on quiet, slow cycles, like clouds exchanging vapor. An avenue that joined the upper amphitheater to the salt-terraces yesterday may today terminate seamlessly against the flank of a blind wall, while a staircase that was sealed shut for years opens out onto an airy promenade nobody could have reached an hour before. Sometimes a revision passes through a district like a front of dry weather. There is no rumble or tremor; instead, edges go faintly double. For several minutes, a courtyard holds two different versions of its own pavement occupying the same ground—one deeply worn into hollows, one freshly fractured and sharp—overlapping like thin ghosts of stone until one settles into solidity and the other resolves into a puff of fine white grit that drifts quietly away on the draught.

Gravity, too, has worn loose along certain old seams. In the open plazas it pulls straight down, cool and trustworthy, but step through an interior doorway into a narrow stair-shaft and "down" may tilt quietly by fifteen degrees. Your inner ear accepts it; the steps feel flat and easy beneath you, even while the tall window-slit beside your shoulder shows the neighboring peaks slanting diagonally across the dim sky. Along a few high, eroded viaducts, dust falls sideways in motionless, hovering ribbons, refusing to settle on either stone or air.

Nothing green grows anywhere in this expanse. There are no roots to split the masonry, no moss in the joints, no grass pushing between flagstones, no wood, no leaves. The closest thing to flora is a brittle, papery mineral-crust that gathers on the sheltered undersides of arches and inside the dry stone cisterns. Pale ochre at its rim and bone-grey at its center, it forms thin, scalloped fans no thicker than an eggshell. It drinks nothing—there is nothing to drink—sustaining itself instead on the rising gypsum-dust and the subtle temperature drop between exposed stone and shadow. Over months, a patch of crust slowly migrates across a wall by calcifying at one edge and flaking away at the other, always creeping out of long, open sightlines as though exposure to vast empty space dried even it out. When a pressure-front passes, whole colonies shed microscopic pale scales into the air, releasing a dry, faint rustle you feel along your skin before you hear it.

Across miles of hollow masonry, animal life is rare enough that you can wander for hours and believe the city completely uninhabited. Then, far across a chasm, something moves against the pale stone.

They are tall, gaunt, narrow-chested creatures with too many joints in their long legs, sheathed in loose, folded hide the color and matte texture of weathered chalk. They move singly or in widely spaced pairs along knife-edge cornices and ruined viaducts, utterly unhurried, their weightless balance untroubled by the vertical drops on either side. They have no voices. Now and then one pauses and taps a single blunt, horn-hard toe twice against a hollow flagstone—a dry, muted knock that travels through the stone and, across the folded thresholds, surfaces half a league away. They browse the brittle mineral-crusts from the undersides of lintels, scraping them clean with flat, toothless ridges.

Because they have lived in this fractured elevation for generations, they navigate the city's broken spaces with an instinct no map could hold. Watch one approach a solitary doorway standing alone in the middle of an empty plaza; it steps through the frame and simply ceases to be on this side of the valley, reappearing instantly as a pale, moving speck on a distant gallery miles across the void, already bending its long neck to graze. They know which arches stay open through a pressure-shift and which ones close without warning; they never linger in a courtyard whose edges have begun to double.

And where they graze, something rarer still watches from the untrustworthy shadows.

It is easy to mistake one for one of the city's orphaned shadows—a dark, angular blot lying flat against a sheer tower wall or spread across the flagstones of an amphitheater. Only if you keep your eyes fixed on it do you notice that its outline breathes, very faintly, and that it slides against the grain of the dim light. Detaching itself cleanly from the masonry without a sound, it opens into a wide, diamond-shaped plane of taut, dry vellum stretched across fine, articulated ribs of bone. It never flaps; it rides the silent upward dust-tides between the towers, banking effortlessly through narrow window-slots and hanging motionless over intersections where the long-limbed grazers emerge from folded doorways. When it drops, it drops like a sheet of dark slate falling in thin air—no cry, no rush of feathers, only the sudden folding of a shadow around pale limbs, and then silence again.

Down in the corners of dished stair-risers and along the lee of low curbs live the smallest residents: dry, matte-shelled scavengers no bigger than a fingernail. Whenever an overlapping courtyard resolves and sheds its fine powder of freshly unmade stone, they emerge from hair-thin cracks to gather the grit. Working in total silence, they carry individual grains back to the sheltered angles of thresholds and stack them into tiny, neat spirals and stepped cones—minute, accidental echoes of the towers rising around them.

Stand on a high promontory at the edge of an outer district, where the terraces finally break off into nothing but open dimness, and the whole region lies bare before you. Mile upon mile of pale, layered city clinging to impossible spires; vast tracts of cool, empty air; rising veils of dry dust streaming upward through bone-dry channels; distant bridges meeting cliffs they do not actually touch; a lone dark glider spiraling slowly up a shaft of silent air between two hollow towers.

Nothing here is waiting to be reclaimed, or repaired, or finished. The stone wears into stairs, the stairs drift into new alignments, the dry tides climb the gutters, and the few quiet creatures thread their way through the gaps in distance—all of it turning slowly in the cool, dim hush, miles above an unseen floor.
