# Preparing for the High Terrace of the Still Engines

There is a particular kind of place that punishes the impulse to *do*, and rewards something else — something closer to attention, or patience, or the willingness to be a small and quiet thing in a very large and quiet machine. The High Terrace is this kind of place. Most of what passes for travel advice will not apply here. The advice that does apply is stranger.

## What This Place Is, In Practical Terms

Imagine arriving at a flat, luminous shelf the size of a city block, several hundred meters above a floor you cannot quite see, made of a poured material resembling pale concrete threaded with copper-colored rods. There are no trees. There are no houses, no signs, no path. There is rain sometimes, and condensation almost always, and a dim warm glow visible in the joints between panels after dark. There are small things moving on the walls. There is a hum you cannot quite hear. There is no one to meet you.

You will be cold, still, and overexposed in light that does not grade into shadow. Time will not behave. Things you set down may roll the wrong way. After the first day, you may stop being sure you arrived at all.

That is the place. Here is how to ready yourself.

## The Body

**Dress for stationary, damp cold — not moving cold.** The terrace has no wind, which sounds merciful until you realize it also has no relief from chill. The air just *is* cool, against you, indefinitely. Your body will lose heat through every surface it offers the stone. You need layers that trap warmth without trapping sweat, because you will sweat a little, and the sweat will not evaporate, and damp cloth against cool air is its own slow injury.

- A close-fitting base layer of wool or another fiber that stays warm when wet. Cotton is foolish here. Bring two, because one will not dry.
- A mid layer — fleece or wool — that you can add or remove without thinking.
- A wind shell, even though there is no wind. The shell is not for blocking air. It is for blocking the radiant chill of the stone and keeping your own microclimate intact. A light, hooded one is ideal.
- Wool socks, two pairs, and a second pair always drying somewhere on your body (inside the shell, against the small of your back — old trick).
- Footwear with soles you can feel through. You will want to sense the hum of the terrace through your feet, both because it's wise to know what's beneath you and because that hum, once you attune to it, becomes a kind of company. Thick rubber boots isolate you. Thin-soled leather shoes let you in.
- A wide-brimmed hat or a cap with a stiff brim. The light is wrong; it does not respect your brow.
- Gloves with the fingertips cut away, or fingerless mittens, because you will need your hands bare often — to touch the walls, to feel the warmth in them, to pry at a curling seam, to pick up a fallen beetle-thing and look at it properly.

**Do not bring perfume, cologne, or strong soap.** The fauna here are quiet and watchful, and unfamiliar scent marks you as something new and worth investigating. You will be investigated. You do not want the investigation to begin with suspicion.

**Bring water and a way to purify more.** The trough-water is real water — it is rain, after all — but it has run through channels lined with living machinery, and there is something in the flavor of it that I cannot describe as dangerous but can describe as *unsuitable*. Drink sparingly from the troughs. Carry what you need. A water filter that works on ceramics will outlast a plastic one; the environment is hard on synthetics.

**Food should be compact, dry, and quiet to eat.** You will want things that do not crunch loudly in a place where your own footsteps feel like an intrusion. Dense bread, hard cheese, dried fruit, cured meats, oil-packed fish. Chocolate is acceptable. Anything in a crinkly wrapper is not. Decant what you can into cloth bags before you arrive.

**Bring a bedroll that is also a seat, and that does not need much flat ground.** A closed-cell foam pad, thin, dense, that you can fold under you on a step. The terrace offers you surfaces to sleep on more readily than flat ground. A four-season down or synthetic bag rated well below the temperature — you will sleep cool.

## The Mind

This is the harder preparation.

**Practice being still.** I am serious. Before you travel, sit somewhere you do not particularly want to be — a basement, a stairwell, a doctor's waiting room — and do nothing for two hours. No book, no phone, no fiddling. Just sit. Listen. Notice how the air moves. Notice how the sounds come and go. The terrace rewards exactly this skill, and punishes its absence with a kind of low-grade dread that will set in by the second day if you do not have somewhere in yourself to put it.

**Lower your expectations about narrative.** You will not have an adventure in the ordinary sense. There will be no encounter, no revelation, no quest resolved. What there will be is *duration*. The terrace is the same thing for a long time, and the long time is the point. Read a novel of quiet interiority before you go — a Stoner, a *Mrs Dalloway*, the late parts of *The Rings of Saturn*. You are preparing your attention, not your itinerary.

**Tell someone where you are going.** This is real advice, not metaphor. The terrace does not announce itself as dangerous. It feels, at first, merely odd. But the wrongness accumulates, and the body of evidence about where and when you are becomes unreliable after a day or two. Have a person in your original world who knows you went there, and have a plan for returning that does not depend on your memory of having left.

## The Senses

**Bring something to soften the light.** Sunglasses are too crude; the light is not glaring, it is *flat*, and sunglasses will only make it dimmer-flat. What you want is an eyeshade for sleeping, and possibly a translucent cloth — a thin silk scarf — you can hold in front of your face when the visual overexposure becomes too much. It will become too much.

**Your hearing will do most of your work.** Bring no music. Bring headphones only as a tool to test the silence — listen to a minute of pink noise, then take them off and listen to what the place *sounds like*. Train your ear on the hum beneath the hum. The fauna, when you can sense them, will reveal themselves first as a perturbation in that hum: a small drag of sound where no drag should be, a brief absence of hiss, a flicker. Earplugs are useful for sleeping but be careful with them; you do not want to fall asleep unable to hear the slow shifting in the cavities beneath you.

**Your hands will do the rest.** You will, against all advice, want to touch everything. This is correct. The poured surface is kinder than it looks. The metal veins below it are warm and worth touching. The animals, if you move slowly, will let you touch them, and their shells are cool and faintly humming. Keep your hands clean, your nails short, and resist the urge to pry at the delaminating seams. They are not for you. They are the terrace breathing.

## The Terrors, And How To Meet Them

There are several, and you should be warned of them in advance so they do not arrive as surprises.

**The inverted terrace in the sky.** It will appear. You will look up and see another, identical terrace, hanging downward from the glare above you, complete with its own moss and its own troughs and its own small pale fauna moving across its underside. You will be tempted to climb. Do not. There is no up. Looking at it is fine; thinking about it is fine; trying to reach it is how travelers lose themselves. Bring this image with you before you arrive: a ceiling with a city on the wrong side of it. See it in advance, so when you see it in the place itself, you recognize it as a thing the place does.

**The things in the walls.** You will hear them. A slow scrabbling, far away, in the dark behind the poured skin. They are real. They are large. They do not want anything from you. If you put your ear to a riser and hear them, remove your ear, slowly, and walk away. They have been there longer than you and will be there after. The pale droppings you may find, dry and studded with metal fragments, are the only evidence you will have of them by daylight. Do not try to meet one.

**The time slippage.** An afternoon will end and you will not be able to say how long it was. A beetle will cross a riser in what feels like an hour and arrive somewhere you did not see it go. Resist the urge to keep time. The terrace is running on its own clock, and the friction between your clock and its clock is one of the unpleasant edges of the place. Decide on a rough cadence — wake, eat, walk, sit, eat, sleep — and let the *thing* tell you when to do each. It will, gently.

**The thinness of the floor.** Trajectories bend up there. Things set down roll the wrong way. Gravity is, at the upper edges, a suggestion. Stay away from the topmost tier unless you have a specific reason to be there. The lower tiers, with their buttresses and shelves, are stable enough for a careful human. If you do go up, do not throw anything off the edge to see where it lands. The answer is unfriendly.

## The Animals

**Do not feed them.** They are not starving. They are running on a slower metabolism than yours, and they have their own provisions: the moss, the soil in the planted cylinders, the metal itself, the condensation. Bringing them your food will do something to them — you will not see it, but the place will record it, and the animals you feed will become a different shape of animal over the weeks that follow.

**Do not handle them unless they invite you.** The small ones, the bone-colored ones, will sometimes come to a still hand. This is permitted. Lift nothing. The larger ones, the ones you only know by their droppings, have not invited you. Keep your distance from the deeper seams, and especially from the place where the moss ends.

**The soil pots are someone else's garden.** The stunted woody things in their cylinders of dark imported dirt are being grown by someone, or something, or for some purpose. Do not touch them. Do not steal soil. If a beetle eats at them overnight, that is the order of things; you are not part of that order.

## The Departure

**Know that you may not want to leave.** This is the one thing no preparation fully handles. The terrace is a place for the dissolution of urgency, and urgency is the glue that holds ordinary life together. By the third day you may find yourself unable to remember what you were in a hurry *for*. By the fifth day you may find the urgency itself foreign, and by the seventh you may not be sure you are still a person who has a "before." This is not damage. It is the place working as intended. But you should go in knowing that the version of you who comes back will not be exactly the version who left, and that some part of the journey home is grieving that.

Bring a token from your real life. A small object, something tactile, something with weight and meaning, that you can hold in your hand and that reminds you what century you were born in, who you were before you went to a place where time bends. Look at it once a day. Do not let the terrace have it.

And when you leave — and you should leave within ten days, fourteen at the outside — go in the morning, after a night's condensation, when the troughs are full and the smallest sounds are clearest. Walk toward the far edge of the western shelf. There is, if you pay attention, a place where the buttresses thin out and the poured surface gives way to a rougher lip; beyond that lip the geometry stops being a terrace and starts being a slope; beyond the slope, the place you came in by.

The terrace will not thank you for visiting. It will not resent you either. It will go on humming in its wires, and delaminating its seams, and migrating its beetles by centimeters, and waiting for the inverted terrace above it to do whatever inverted terraces do. You will be a small bright event in its long slow log. That is enough.

Go gently. Touch the walls when you can. Stay out of the top tier. Eat quietly. Sleep cold. Listen to the hum.

And bring back nothing.
