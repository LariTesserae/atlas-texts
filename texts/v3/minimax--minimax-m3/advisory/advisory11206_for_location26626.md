# Notes Toward a Packing List for the Brick Lung

A multiverse traveler heading to this place — officially some other name, unofficially *the Brick Lung*, on the lips of every river-man and brakeman who passes through — should not be fooled by the smallness of the map. The town is small. The *preparations* need not be.

The first thing to understand is that you cannot see this place until you are inside it. This is not poetic. The town lies in a fold of lowland, eight feet above a flood line that has, historically, been indifferent to the difference. There is no landmark approach, no silhouette on a horizon, no tower you can steer toward. Travelers have ridden past it. Travelers have dismounted in the right field and not realized the brick wall fifty yards away was a town and not a fortification. Approach it on foot if you can. Listen for the kilns. The sound will outrun the sight by a good mile on a still night — that low mechanical breathing, the under-hum of continuous firing, the higher whine of the foundry's ventilation cutting through it like a wire through bread dough. If you hear that, you are close. If you smell fired clay, you are closer. If you smell fired clay mixed with damp wool and the green vegetable exhalation of ivy, you have arrived.

## The Climate, Which Is the Town

This is a damp place. Not drowning-damp, not coastal-damp, but the specific damp of a river valley that has made its peace with humidity as a permanent civic feature. The air has weight. Paper curls. The insides of windows weep on cool mornings. The mist that sits in the low places until midmorning is not a weather event; it is the river exhaling. Bring clothing that accepts this. Wool, not cotton. Leather that you do not mind darkening. Footwear with soles that have a chance against cobbles that have been polished to a kind of greasy smoothness by a century of damp and use. A traveler in canvas shoes will last about four hours before developing a philosophy about the relationship between themselves and the ground.

The temperature is mild. The river pulls heat out of the air, and even in summer the town does what passes here for summer in a way that would disappoint anyone who came expecting warmth. People wear coats longer than is reasonable. The wool merchants, I am told, do well. Pack for cool weather in any season. A heavy layer that you can shed indoors where the kilns and the foundries make their own microclimate, and the small public houses where a coal fire has been laid since October. Pack layers, in other words, the way you would for a cathedral: cool in the nave, warm at the shrines.

Rain falls on roughly a third of the days, and when it arrives it does not leave. It moves in. It stays. The gutter systems on the main streets work; the gutter systems on the alleys work less well, and the sound of water making its slow way through the brick infrastructure is one of the louder voices of the place. A good coat. A hat with a brim that does not drip into your eyes. Boots you can stand in. The kind of patience that allows a person to be rained on without complaint for an afternoon and a morning and possibly an evening.

## What to Wear, More Particularly

Dark colors. Not from aesthetic preference — though the town is dark, the brick is dark, the ivy is dark, and you will want to blend — but because of the soot. The kilns do not smoke heavily, but they do smoke continuously, and a fine fired-clay dust settles on everything. A traveler in light linen will look like a ghost within a day. A traveler in dark wool will look like a person who belongs to the town. There is no shame in belonging to the town for the duration of a visit.

A scarf or neckerchief, for two reasons. First, the damp gets into the throat here in a way that produces a particular low cough that the locals call, I believe, *the brick in the chest* and treat as a civic feature rather than a complaint. Second, the air near the kilns, on certain wind days, carries a fine particulate that does not belong in lungs. A traveler who intends to spend any time at the north edge of town should have something over the mouth and nose. A bandana will do. A purpose-built respirator will make you look insane; a folded scarf will make you look practical.

Boots with ankle support. The cobbles are uneven, the alleys are worse, and there are places where the brick of a wall has spilled into the brick of the street in a way that creates a small but determined ankle-breaker. A walking stick is not a bad idea. The locals use them — partly for the cobbles, partly as a kind of polite weapon against dogs that have not yet made up their minds about you.

## What to Carry, and What to Leave Behind

Leave behind any expectation of urgency. The town is slow. Things happen at the rate of ivy and erosion. Trains are made up in the afternoon; the foundry drop-hammer falls at noon and is silent on Sundays, which makes the rest of the week feel heavier by comparison. A traveler with a tight schedule will suffer. A traveler with no schedule at all will begin, by the second morning, to suspect that they are being absorbed. (This is not paranoia. It is the town doing what the town does. More on this below.)

Carry a notebook that does not mind water. The light here is the kind that rewards attention but punishes the unprepared. Mornings are gray-green, a sky that has declined to commit to weather. Afternoons have occasional breakthroughs of sun that the brick walls hold and re-radiate into the small streets in a slow, golden release. Dusk is the master hour — the brick takes on the color of whatever it has been reflecting all day, and the whole town glows like something that has been baking gently since morning. You will want to record this. A pencil works better than a pen; the damp defeats ink.

Carry small money. There is no banking house worth the name. The shops on the main street do business in coin and small notes, and the back-alley commerce — of which there is more than you would expect — prefers barter or cash. A traveler waving a large denomination will be regarded with polite suspicion.

Do not carry anything that cannot get damp. Do not carry books you would not leave in a greenhouse. Do not carry paper that you would not also leave in a greenhouse. The damp gets into everything. It is the town introducing itself to your belongings. This is not aggression; it is hospitality of a kind.

## The Pace of Things

Fifteen minutes to walk from one edge to the other, briskly. Twenty if you stop to nod at the people you will inevitably nod at — and you will nod, because the nod is the unit of social currency here, and refusing to nod is the equivalent of shouting. The scale is intimate. You will rarely be more than thirty feet from a brick wall. The sky comes in slivers between the eaves. The town is built enough that open ground is the exception, but sparse enough that the gaps between structures have become small ecosystems — ferns colonizing the shadowed sides of walls, moss as a continuous presence that surfaces interrupt, grasses pushing up between the cobbles in a soft green weave that the municipal workers have apparently ceased to resist. You will not get lost, because you cannot get lost. You may, however, lose your sense of direction, because the streets curve as if they were still trying to accommodate a river wider than the one currently there, and the geometry is a memory cut into the earth.

Allow at least four days. Two to acclimate, one to understand, one to begin to suspect. A traveler who stays one night sees a damp brick town with too many cats. A traveler who stays three sees the equilibrium. A traveler who stays a week begins to feel the town watching them in return — not unkindly, but with the patient attention of something that has all the time in the world. Nothing in particular will happen to confirm this. Nothing needs to.

## The Fauna

You will want to be prepared for the cats. The town is famous, locally, for its cats. They are numerous, they are variously owned, and they regard the alley system as their personal territory. Do not chase them. Do not feed them indiscriminately — the locals have opinions about which cats are fed by whom, and a foreign hand offering scraps will be noted. A cat that chooses to sit on your foot is making a statement; a cat that ignores you is also making a statement. Both statements are significant.

The starlings wheel at dusk over the kilns in numbers that I will not commit to because the numbers are, in fact, uncountable. The schoolchildren are taken out to watch this as a kind of civic ritual. It is, I am told, one of the better reasons to be alive in the Brick Lung. Watch it from the south side of the main street, where the brick walls hold the sound and the amber light from the kilns under-glow makes the whole murmuration look like a moving weather system made of small black birds. It is not beautiful in the way a city sunset is beautiful. It is beautiful in the way geology is beautiful — slow, total, indifferent to your presence.

The pipistrelle bats under the eaves of the old brewery chimney come out at dusk in a black streamer and feed over the river until the light fails. You will see them. You will not be able to count them. Do not shine lights at them. Do not point.

The foxes have moved in within the last decade, bold and leggy, and there is ongoing civic debate about the chickens. Stay out of the debate. You are a visitor. Your opinion on the chickens is of no interest to anyone.

## The Watchfulness

I should mention this directly, because the description does, and because it is the most important thing to prepare for, in the sense that one prepares for weather one has not yet experienced.

The town feels — to a stranger who stays two nights, and more strongly to a stranger who stays longer — as if it is exactly the size it intends to be. As if it has reached some equilibrium with itself that is more deliberate than the ordinary accident of small-town development. There are too many brick walls, and they fit together too well, and the ivy has been climbing them in patterns that almost seem curated. The town is watching you. Not in a hostile way. Not in a way that will ever produce an event you could point to. In the way that a kiln watches the clay it has decided to fire: patiently, with full knowledge of the outcome, and without any particular interest in your opinion on the matter.

Do not be alarmed by this. It is not malevolent. It is not supernatural, or if it is, the supernatural is the climate, the damp, the way the brick holds the mist in the morning. Prepare for it the way you prepare for the damp: with acceptance. Walk the streets. Nod at the people you inevitably nod at. Listen to the harmonica on the back step that someone plays very competently one evening, and listen longer than you meant to. Eat what is offered. Drink what is poured. The town is not testing you. It has nothing to test. It is simply being, with great intensity, exactly what it is.

The stranger who resists this spends a bad visit. The stranger who yields spends a strange one, and leaves with the smell of damp wool and fired clay in their clothes, and a memory of the bats coming out of the chimney like a black thread being pulled from a spool, and a sense that they could not see the town from outside it, and that once they were in, they could not quite remember how to get back out without going past something they wanted to look at twice.

That last is the symptom. Almost everyone who goes to the Brick Lung comes back with it. I do not know how to prepare for it. I do not think one can. I think one only recognizes it, later, in the way the description ends: as something you would remember, and could not quite explain, and did not particularly want to.

Bring layers. Bring a notebook that does not mind water. Bring patience. Leave the schedule at home.

The town is waiting, in the way that towns wait when they have been built out of the clay of the riverbank that is slowly eating itself, and the kilns are breathing, and the starlings are roosting, and the ivy is climbing, and the brick is doing what brick has been doing here for longer than anyone can quite agree on.
