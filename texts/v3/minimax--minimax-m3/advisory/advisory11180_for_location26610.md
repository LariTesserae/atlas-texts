# Preparing for the High Extraction

The first thing to understand is that this place does not want you. That is not a metaphor. It is a working statement about what kind of terrain this is and what kind of body it expects. The plateau will give you a headache before it gives you anything else, and the headache is its handshake. If you accept it gracefully — with water, with patience, with the willingness to sit down and breathe slowly while the pressure equalizes in your skull — the place will then proceed, on its own schedule, to show you what it has. If you do not accept it, the place will make you pay in ways that escalate quietly and then suddenly. So: prepare for that first.

---

## The Body Before the Body

You cannot train for altitude in your living room, but you can train your body to be less surprised by it. Two months out, start doing cardiovascular work that pushes you into breathlessness — running, cycling, swimming intervals, anything that redlines your lungs for sustained periods. Altitude punishes the cardiopulmonary system you haven't used, so use it now. The plateau will take roughly twenty percent off your maximum output the day you arrive, and another ten the second day if you haven't acclimatized. You don't need to be an athlete. You need to be someone whose heart knows what it's doing under load.

Acclimatize if you can. If your journey allows it, spend two or three nights at an intermediate elevation first — eight thousand, nine thousand feet — before ascending to the plateau. This is not always possible. The road to the site is short, the operating window is narrow, and the mining company that controls access does not run a tourist shuttle. If you cannot stage your arrival, then arrive with medication and time. Acetazolamide (Diamox), starting the day before ascent and continuing for two days after arrival, will blunt the worst of the headache and help you sleep, which is the thing you most need to do and will find hardest to do. Dexamethasone is the second-line drug for serious altitude sickness; carry it if you're traveling with someone who can administer it. Ibuprofen for the headache, ondansetron or similar for the nausea that may follow. None of this substitutes for descending if symptoms worsen, and the plateau does not offer descent as a casual option. Helicopter evacuation exists. It is expensive. It exists.

The lungs are not the only organ the plateau will meet. The cold is also an organ, in its way, and your skin, eyes, and peripheral extremities will be its first conversation. Frostbite is unlikely at typical operating-season temperatures but not impossible, and the wind makes everything worse than the thermometer suggests. Begin preparing your skin now — get used to dry air, drink more water than you think is reasonable in the weeks before, and accept that on the plateau you will be a creature that drinks constantly and never feels quite hydrated.

## What to Wear

Forget fashion. The plateau is a layered climate in three dimensions: cold from above, cold from the ground, and wind that tries to take the cold away from your skin at exactly the rate that would kill you fastest. You need a system that handles all three without thinking.

**Base layer:** Merino wool or a synthetic equivalent. Long sleeves, long legs. No cotton. Cotton holds moisture against the skin and then becomes a slow refrigeration unit the moment the wind picks up. This is the one rule that is not negotiable.

**Mid layer:** Fleece or a light insulated jacket. Down is wonderful when dry and catastrophic when wet; the plateau's mist and snow will find the seams of any down garment that isn't protected, so if you bring down, bring a hard shell over it that you actually trust.

**Outer shell:** A proper hardshell — waterproof, windproof, with sealed seams and a hood that goes past your chin. The wind on this plateau is not a breeze. It is a moving wall. A hood that doesn't stay on in a real wind is not a hood, it's a hat you can't grip.

**Legs:** Softshell or insulated hiking pants. Bring a pair of waterproof overpants for storms. Avoid jeans entirely. Avoid anything that doesn't move.

**Feet:** This is where the plateau wins or loses. You need mountaineering or heavy backpacking boots — leather or synthetic, fully waterproof, broken in over weeks of use before you travel. Vibram or equivalent sole, ankle support, room for a thick sock. Bring gaiters. Bring two pairs of socks for every day, because a wet sock at altitude is a slow accident. Break in the boots by walking many miles in them. New boots will blister you on day one and you will not have a pharmacy.

**Hands:** Liner gloves inside insulated shells. Carry spares. The work of taking a photograph or eating lunch without gloves will cost you fingers if the wind has its say.

**Head:** A wool or fleece hat that covers the ears. A buff or neck gaiter that can be pulled up over the nose and mouth in serious cold or wind. A balaclava if you have one.

**Eyes:** Glacier glasses or wraparound sunglasses with side shields. The light is dim but the UV at altitude is not, and snow reflection doubles what your retinas think they're getting. Even on overcast days, even in summer. Cataracts are a souvenir you don't want from this trip.

## What to Carry

The plateau does not have a store. There is no town at the end of the road. The road itself is a temporary scar that exists nine weeks a year. Whatever you need, you bring, or it does not exist for you.

**Water:** A two-liter insulated bottle or hydration bladder, plus a backup. Insulated because your water will freeze overnight if you don't. Refill from springs at the base of cliffs if you can find them — the water is reputedly clear, but run it through a filter or boil it. The rats under the kitchen modules exist for a reason, and that reason is that rodents have been here longer than the machinery.

**Food:** More than you think. Altitude is an appetite suppressant, and you will lose interest in eating at exactly the moment your body needs more calories than it has ever needed. Bring foods you can eat without thinking — nuts, cheese, chocolate, dried fruit, energy bars. Bring some real food for morale. A thermos of something hot is not a luxury; it is the difference between a tolerable afternoon and a miserable one.

**Sleep:** If you are staying overnight outside the heated modules (and you likely will be at some point — the modules are for the work crews and visitors need to negotiate access), bring a sleeping bag rated to zero Fahrenheit or below, and a sleeping pad with an R-value of at least four. Cold ground steals more heat than cold air. A four-season tent if you're camping, one that can handle real wind without trying to become a kite.

**Light:** A headlamp. Extra batteries, kept inside your jacket so they don't die in the cold. The plateau is dim by day and you will forget what dark looks like until night falls and then you will wish you had the lamp.

**Communication:** A satellite phone or a personal locator beacon. Cell coverage at this elevation in this terrain is unlikely. If something goes wrong — injury, equipment failure, weather trapping you — the plateau does not know how to send help. You do, by carrying the device that asks for it.

**First aid:** A real kit. Trauma supplies if you can manage them. Ibuprofen, acetaminophen, the altitude meds, an antihistamine, blister treatment, a space blanket, a tourniquet if you know how to use one. The mine site may have medics but you may not be near them.

**Navigation:** GPS with downloaded maps. Compass as backup. The plateau is featureless to the eye in low light and the wind has been known to erase footprints within an hour.

**Documentation:** Camera, journal, drawing supplies — whatever your medium is. Bring more batteries than you think. Bring a notebook that can survive being wet. This place demands to be recorded, not because it is picturesque but because it is going to be something else within a decade and what you write down now is the only version of it that will exist in its current form.

## The Mind

Here is the part no packing list covers.

The plateau is disorienting in a way that takes most travelers three days to understand and a week to accept. The light is sideways. The shadows are the same color as the rocks. There are days when you cannot tell where the sun is, and after a while you stop trying. You use the wind instead, because the wind is constant and the wind is correct. This sounds poetic. It is not. It is a survival skill. You will feel, in the first forty-eight hours, as if some background process in your brain has been switched off — the one that tells you which way is up, which way is home, which direction the day is going. That process is calibrated for the latitudes most humans live in, and it does not work here. Be patient with yourself. Eat when you don't want to. Sleep when you can. The internal clock will eventually reattach, but it will reattach to the plateau's clock, not yours.

There is also the matter of the place's own mood, which is real and worth preparing for. The plateau is provisional in a way that most landscapes are not. The rock is young. The mine is temporary. The glacier that made the valley is retreating faster than it was retreating twenty years ago, and the geese will be somewhere else in a generation. Nothing here is asking you to stay. The plateau is a transit lounge for geology. If you are the kind of person who needs a place to feel permanent and rooted before you can rest, you will not rest here. If you are the kind of person who can sit with the temporary and not try to make it last, the plateau will be one of the most beautiful things you have ever seen. It will not wait for you. That is its gift and its warning.

The condors. They will come closer than you want. A condor at eye level, with a wingspan wider than you are tall, is a creature that makes the back of your neck prickle even when you know it is not interested in you. Stand still. Do not wave. Do not try to feed it. They are not aggressive. They are just large, and large things close to your head take some getting used to. After the third one you will not notice.

The machines. There is a constant hum. You will stop hearing it within a day and only notice when it stops, which it rarely does. When it does stop, the wind fills the gap, and then the birds do, and the silence on this plateau is a layered thing, not an empty one.

## Behavior

The mining operation is private industrial land. You do not have a right to be there. Someone has agreed to let you in, and they have agreed for a reason — research, journalism, family, contract work, scientific study — and your job is to remember that you are a guest in a working landscape. Stay out of restricted areas. Do not touch the equipment. Do not approach the working face without an escort. Do not photograph security infrastructure if asked not to. Wear your high-visibility layer when near active operations. Follow the safety briefing you will receive, even if it feels condescending. The people who run this place have buried colleagues. They are not being cautious to make a point. They are being cautious because the next rock that gives way does not know you are a visitor.

The wildlife is the host. The text is right about that. The plateau is being made by the negotiation between the animals and the mine, and you are a minor figure in that negotiation. Do not approach nests. Do not feed the foxes or the caracaras. Do not leave food out — the rats are already there and you do not want them to have more. Walk softly on the scree where the geese have scraped their nests. The goslings are vulnerable. The parents are not bluffing when they hiss.

Leave nothing. Take nothing but what you came with, including rocks. The lichen has been on that boulder for a hundred years. It does not need to come home with you as a souvenir.

## The Timing

Visit in the operating season. That is summer by the plateau's generous accounting — roughly a six-week window when the snow retreats and the road is open and the equipment can be reached by truck. Outside that window the place is functionally inaccessible except by air, and the airstrip, if there is one, is at the mercy of weather that closes without warning. Plan your visit for the heart of that window if you can. Arrive at the beginning so you acclimatize. Leave before the road closes behind you.

Plan to stay at least a week. Less than that and you will see the plateau through the eyes of a newcomer, which is dim and disorienting and not yet interesting. A week in, the disorientation recedes and the place begins to teach you its actual shape. Two weeks in, you begin to understand why the geologists stay.

## The Multiverse-Specific Note

Travelers from realities where the air is denser, the gravity is lower, or the climate is warmer will find the plateau unexpectedly heavy. The thin air is not just an inconvenience; it changes how your body moves, how your heart beats, how quickly you fatigue. If your home reality has higher oxygen content than baseline human norm, you may find the plateau less punishing. If it has lower, you may find it dangerous. Adjust your expectations accordingly, and consider a longer acclimatization staging if your baseline physiology is already oxygen-stressed.

The plateau's provisional quality may also register differently depending on the kind of reality you come from. Travelers from settled, ancient, slow-changing worlds sometimes find the place unbearably anxious — too much happening at once, too much in flux. Travelers from chaotic or fast-changing realities sometimes find it restful, for the same reason. Neither reaction is wrong. The plateau will meet you where you are.

## The Last Word Before You Go

There is one more thing, and it is not practical, and it is the most important preparation.

The plateau is a place where the temporary is the texture of everything. The machinery, the mine, the snow line, the geese, the lichen that has only had a hundred years so far — all of it is in transit. The condors will outlive the conveyor. The foxes will outlive the crusher. The tarn that fills the worked-out scar in a thousand years will outlive everything you see. You are arriving at a moment that will not recur. The particular configuration of rock and machine and bird and mist that exists this week will not exist in a year. It will not be better or worse later. It will simply be a different place.

Pay attention now. That is the only preparation that matters, and it is the one no packing list can substitute for.
