You are going to a place that is mostly *inside* something else. That is the first thing to understand. Most travelers prepare for a destination — they imagine arriving at a place, standing in it, looking around. This is not that kind of place. You will not arrive at it. You will descend into it, and the act of descent is most of what you'll know of it, because the descent will use up most of the time you have.

The writer of the description is being honest when they say an hour. Plan for one hour. Borrow nothing past that.

---

**On whether to go at all.**

Sit with this question longer than you think you need to. The place has fewer than forty people in it. They know it is closing. The bog is re-growing across the open lip of the hollow and they have begun, quietly, to argue about whether to cut it back or let it close. Your arrival is a perturbation they did not ask for. Your departure is a perturbation they will absorb.

You are not going to fix anything. You are not going to save anyone. You do not have what they need — what they need is more time, and possibly more agreement, and you cannot bring either. If you go to "see" the place, you will see what the surface sees (almost nothing) and what the inside sees (too much), and most of what you take back will be a smell and a sound that you did not expect to keep.

If you still want to go, go. The place is worth a witness. Just know what witnessing costs, and pay it ahead of time.

---

**The body.**

You will be wet from three directions at once: above, where the root-mat weeps; below, where the peat-floor is water barely held together by root; and from the air, which is saturated and never stops being saturated. Do not pack anything that cannot rot. Your good boots will not survive the trip. Your good jacket will not survive the trip. Bring leather, oiled. Multiple coats of tallow or beeswax worked into the cloth before you go — this is the technology the people use, peat-cloth of oiled moss-fibre, and it is the right answer for this place. Synthetics will hold water against your skin and pull your heat down faster. Wool will eventually mat and sour. Leather will simply become part of the wet.

Gloves. Full coverage. There is no exposed skin that does not become, within minutes, a substrate. A neck wrap that goes over the ears and ties under the chin. You cannot flinch. Flinching is what begins the scream.

A mask. Porous. You will still breathe fauna. There is no way not to. The mask is not for the air — it is for the largest things, the things that would land on your mouth. Accept that you will taste the inside of the place the whole time you are in it.

A walking stick. Not for the path — the path is trodden, bone-staked, and the people will guide you. The stick is so you can feel the ground without looking down. Looking down is bad. Looking down is where you start to count individual bodies and lose the thread of yourself.

Food: bring your own, sealed in oiled cloth. Do not eat what they offer unless you ask, first, what it is. Their food is from the bog interior. Your gut flora is not prepared. The conversation you have about what is on the plate is part of how you meet them. Take it seriously.

Water: do not bring water. Bringing water is a joke. Bring salt — to maintain electrolytes if the cold begins to hollow you out faster than you expected.

---

**The light.**

There is almost none. The hollow is lit by a bioluminescent liverwort that grows only in places which have collapsed into cold wet darkness and stayed long enough for the spores to find them. The light is a faint blue-green sheen. Enough to find a wall by. Enough to see the outline of a hand. Not enough to read a face across a room.

Do not bring a bright light. A bright light will blind you to the moss-glow and to the patterns the people move in. The people use peat-lamps — lumps of oily tallow-root burned in clay saucers — for the serious work, and the lamps throw a small warm circle that the fauna avoids, mostly. If you bring electric, dim it. If you bring chemical, accept that the place will smell like two kinds of cold.

A child born here never learns to read a landscape that is not, at its base, a moving field of small dark glints. You will, in your first minutes, try to read past the glints. You cannot. After a while you stop trying, and the place becomes legible in its own way. This is a small mercy. Do not rush it.

---

**The sound.**

This is the strangeness of the place, and it is the part that will stay with you longest.

Imagine a theatre full of people breathing. Now imagine they are all worms. A thousand small wet respirations per minute. A faint continuous seething. The sound of a wet cloth being wrung very slowly. That is the ambient hum. It does not stop for your comfort. It does not pause when you speak. Lower your voice to it. Match it.

To the hum is added the drops. Water condenses on the underside of the root-mat, gathers into long pendant drops, and falls. A single tap, every few seconds, somewhere in the hollow, moving around as the root-mat shifts above. The people read the drops. They tell the weather. They tell which direction the bog has decided to sag today. After an hour you will know what they mean. The dropping is a kind of clock, and you will find yourself listening to it the way you once listened to music.

Your own footsteps will sound strange — they will be the only sharp sounds. Walk softly. The peat gives.

If the hum stops — if everything goes still for a moment — do not move. Something is happening. A predator. A larger worm. A slug the length of a forearm moving through the thatch above you. Stay where you are. Be small. The hum will come back.

---

**The fauna.**

This is the part you cannot prepare for, only know about.

The fauna is the dominant feature of the place. By every measure except the one the humans care about, it owns more of the settlement than they do. The root-ceiling is not a ceiling — it is a living sheet of beetle and worm and slug, layered and moving. The peat-floor is not a floor — it is a kind of mud that breathes, that retreats an inch under your step and returns. The walls of the houses are patrolled. The willow rods are grazed clean of anything that is not willow.

The first slug is the one you will meet. It is the length of your forearm and as wide. It lives in the thatch of every willow house. The people step around it with the calm of long acquaintance. You will see it and you will scream. This is not a figure of speech. The instinct is absolute. There is no preparation that simulates it.

The writer of the description tells us this: once you start screaming, you do not stop. There is no way to stop, once started.

If you want to try anyway, try this before you go. Stand in a dark room. Imagine the slug. Imagine it descending from the rafter onto your shoulder. Hold the scream inside. Hold it for ten minutes. If you can do this, you might last longer than an hour. If you cannot, you are like the rest of us, and the people will be ready for you.

They have done this before. They will lay you on a peat-mat. They will scrape the worms off your skin with willow-switches. They will say, in their quiet way, *it is fine, it is fine, you are not eaten yet.* And they will mean it. The "yet" is also true. You are not being eaten. The fauna is doing what the fauna does. You are a substrate, briefly, and then you are not.

Do not try to bat the moths off your face. The moths will be dismantled, mandible by mandible, before they finish settling. This will be the most disquieting thing you watch. Let it happen. The moth is being dismantled by something that is doing what it does. You are not the moth.

Do not touch the walls. Do not touch anything you were not invited to touch. The places are patrolled. You will be felt, the way you feel a wall in the dark, and the feeling is not hostile but it is not yours.

---

**The floor.**

When you step, the floor gives. Things retreat and return. Imagine walking on a waterbed that is alive. Your feet will come up with peat and small wet bodies. Wipe them on the path. The path is wet. The path accepts.

The houses are grown, not built — willow rods, bent green and pegged into the peat-floor with bone pins, woven into ribbed walls. Living wood, still leafing in the dim warmth of the seasons, the leaves pale and small and reaching toward the moss-glow even though it does them no good. The houses must be pruned constantly or they close over their inhabitants like fists. A house in this settlement is a relationship, not a thing. The weavers speak to the walls. The walls listen, in their way.

You will not be invited inside a house for long. The houses are too small, too full of fauna, too much a relationship you are not part of. You will be invited to the threshold. That is enough.

---

**The people.**

They speak quietly, in the dark. They speak of themselves in the third person — *the dark encourages* this, they would say if you asked, and you would not be able to tell whether they were being literal or making a kind of joke that the dark also encourages.

They will not be startled by you. They will know you are coming. The arrival is signaled, long before you reach the rim, by the displacement of worms in the ceiling above the path. They will meet you at the lip of the sink and they will look at you with the small patience of people who are used to seeing surface things arrive and not last.

They will offer you a peat-lamp. This is the serious offering. A lamp means you are being admitted to the serious work of the place — counting, mapping, listening to the drops, deciding which way the bog has sagged. Accept the lamp. Hold it carefully. The lamp is the warmest thing you will touch while you are there.

They will ask where you are from. They will ask why you came. Be honest. They are not curious in the way surface people are curious — quickly, with a list of follow-up questions ready. They are curious in the way the dark is curious — slowly, laterally, with the expectation that the question will take hours to answer. If you have only an hour, answer in the way they expect. Tell them less than you know. Tell them true things in small pieces.

Do not try to help them. Do not try to teach them anything. They have a debate already, years old, about whether to let the bog close. Your arrival does not change the math. Your departure will not change the math. If you want to do something, witness. The place is closing. They know it. They are arguing about the terms of their own disappearance. Witnessing is what they cannot do for themselves.

---

**The argument.**

Listen for it, but do not enter it. The settlement is closing. The root-mat has, in places, already begun to re-grow across the open lip of the hollow, beginning to make the settlement again into the dark.

Let it close, some say. Become the inside of something larger. Stay.

Others say no — keep the mouth open, keep the sky above them, however rarely seen.

The argument is years old. The bog is winning.

You do not know enough to take a side. They have lived this their whole lives. You have lived it for an hour. If you are asked, say so plainly. The dark encourages a kind of honesty that the surface does not. The people will hear you.

---

**How to leave.**

The people know how to carry out a visitor who has started screaming. If you are still coherent, you can ask to be brought up before that happens. They will bring you up through the rim. You will emerge into the bog, into the day, into the cold wet daylight.

Lie down on the surface for as long as you can. Let your eyes readjust. Let the day be the day. After a place like this, the day will feel like a sound you forgot you could hear.

The settlement will close over you again like a slow green mouth. It was already closed before you came. It will be closed after.

---

**What stays with you.**

The sound, mostly. You will hear it when you cannot sleep for the rest of your life — the faint continuous seething of a thousand small wet respirations, the sound of a wet cloth being wrung very slowly. You will hear it in other places, in other silences, and you will not be able to find it again, and you will not be able to make it stop.

The smell will find you, too. Animal-fat smoke, cold peat, the green-rot sweetness of the root-mat. You will catch it in other people's kitchens, in other people's gardens after rain, and you will not know what you are smelling until you know.

You will remember the slug. You will remember the woman who stepped around it with the calm of long acquaintance. You will remember that she said *it is fine, it is fine, you are not eaten yet*, and meant both halves of the sentence.

You will remember that the place was small. Pocket-scale. That fewer than forty people lived in it, and were arguing, in the wet, about how it should end. That the bog was winning. That the children born there never learned to read a landscape that was not, at its base, a moving field of small dark glints.

You will not bring back anything they wanted. You do not have anything they want. The best thing you can leave behind is a quiet hour — an hour in which someone from outside came down, did not scream, listened, and went back up again.

If you can manage that, you have done the place a small service. If you cannot, they will carry you out on a peat-mat, and they will scrape the worms off your skin with willow-switches, and they will say it is fine, and they will be right.
