# A Field Brief for the Green Hangar

The first thing you should know is that this is not a hostile place. It is not a place that will try to kill you. But it is a place that has stopped caring whether you live or die, and that is, in its own quiet way, a more difficult thing to walk into than a place that means you harm. The hangar has become something else. Your job, going in, is to be small enough to be tolerated by it.

## The Mental Approach

Before you pack a single thing, sit with the description for a few minutes. Let the green-gold light get into your head. This place rewards a kind of soft attention. You are not here to conquer, not here to document aggressively, not here to *do* anything. You are here to be in a room that has forgotten what a room is. If you arrive with the brisk posture of a tourist, the place will feel oppressive and slightly absurd — a damp ruin with wasps. If you arrive with the patience of someone visiting an old library, the place will reveal its cathedral quality, and you will understand what the prompt means about the stained-glass canopy and the warm water as altar.

Bring a notebook but don't plan to write much. Bring a camera but don't plan to take many photographs. The instinct to record will compete with the instinct to witness; the witness wins here.

## Clothing and Skin

The humidity will be the first thing that touches you after you cross the threshold. It is the air inside a greenhouse after a rain, the prompt says, and that is exactly right — warm, wet, faintly sweet with fig-sugar and the volatile oils of crushed vegetation. Your clothes will absorb it. They will not dry. Plan for this.

**Wear:**
- Long sleeves and long pants in a tight weave. The branches will reach for you as you pass; figs will drop without warning; the paper wasps under the eastern eave are the territorial sort, and bare arms invite trouble. Cotton is fine; linen is better; synthetics will cling unpleasantly in the humidity.
- A brimmed hat. Two reasons. First, the drip from the ceiling is constant — a fine, pervasive condensation that will find your scalp and runnable down your neck within minutes. Second, the figs, when they fall, fall hard. A ripe fig dropping from canopy height onto a bare head is not a pleasant surprise.
- Closed-toe waterproof boots with real tread. The floor is broken concrete slabs tipped at angles over warm standing water of unknown depth. You will be picking your way across this terrain for hours. Slippers, sandals, running shoes — these will end your visit early.
- Light gloves if you intend to touch anything. The iron weeps rust; the rust is a tetanus waiting room. Any plant that has rooted into the substrate is also a small chemistry experiment. Bring leather or nitrile, not the loose garden kind that slip off when wet.

**Do not wear:**
- Perfume, cologne, strongly scented deodorant. The wasps under the eastern eave have opinions about floral and fruity notes, and they will share those opinions.
- Bright white clothing. The reflected light off the standing water is green-gold and slightly sour; white will turn a sickly yellow by the end of the visit.
- Anything you care about preserving. The smell will outlast the visit by a day or two. The humidity will take a day to air out of fabric. Bring clothes you are willing to retire to the back of the closet.

## What to Carry

A small pack, not a large one. You will be crouching, stepping between slabs, ducking under low branches, occasionally catching yourself against the fig trunks. Anything heavy or unwieldy becomes a liability.

**Essentials:**
- A walking stick or trekking pole. This is the single most important piece of equipment you can bring. Use it to probe the standing water before committing your weight; to test whether a slab is stable or pivoting; to push aside low branches and curtain-veins of root. The depth of those pools is unknown and probably varies. Somewhere under the broken concrete is the original earth floor, and the earth has been busy for a long time.
- A headlamp, even though the green-gold light is sufficient for navigation. There will be pockets of true dim — under the densest canopy, behind the slab-buttresses, deep in the corners where the iron walls curve back to meet the floor. A headlamp keeps your hands free, which you will need.
- A waterproof bag or hard case for anything electronic. The humidity will condense on cold surfaces, and a smartphone pulled from a damp pocket in a 90% humidity environment is a phone that stops working within twenty minutes.
- Water. You will be sweating more than you expect, and the fig smell will make you thirsty in a way that has nothing to do with actual hydration.
- A small first aid kit, with the assumption that you may need it for: wasp stings (antihistamine), cuts from rusty iron (clean thoroughly), slips on wet concrete (bandages), and the inevitable moment you stand up under a branch and get scratched across the cheek by something you didn't see.
- A towel, small, microfiber. You will get wet. The standing water is unavoidable; the condensation is unavoidable; the dripping is unavoidable. Plan for dampness and you will not be miserable.

**Optional but recommended:**
- Allergy medication preemptively. You may not be allergic to anything in here, but fig pollen, fern spores, mold spores in the compost pile against the door, and the general biome of a hundred-year greenhouse will test the respiratory system of a person who has lived in conditioned air.
- A small knife or multitool, kept sheathed unless needed.
- Something to sit on. A folded dry bag, a sit pad, a piece of closed-cell foam. If you find a stable slab and want to sit for a long time watching the canopy, you will not want to crouch the whole time.

## What to Expect When You Enter

You will notice the drip first, even before you notice the smell. The prompt is correct that it is barely audible, but it is *everywhere* — a metronome from above-left, a counter-rhythm from behind the green wall, a slow plinking into the pools at your feet. Within five minutes you will have stopped hearing it consciously, and within an hour you will miss it when it pauses.

The smell will hit you in layers. First, the wet iron — a cool, mineral scent, almost clean. Then the green breath of the leaves, warm and a little peppery. Then, as you move deeper into the hangar and the fruiting figs come into range, the overripe sweetness, the wine-edge of fermentation where the fallen fruit has begun to break down in the standing water. By the end of your visit, all three will be a single scent, inseparable, that you will carry with you on your clothes and in your hair.

The light will play tricks. The standing water reflects the canopy in smeared green coin-shapes, and when the breeze moves the leaves — and there is a breeze in here, somewhere, because the leaves do move — the reflections move too, and the floor seems to breathe. You will find yourself stopping, watching, unsure whether the green shapes are above you or below you.

The wasps under the eastern eave are the loudest thing in the hangar. Locate them as soon as you enter, give them a wide berth, and do not revisit that quadrant. Their papery city is hung in a place you will not accidentally disturb if you stay on the central nave, but if you wander toward the walls you may walk into their patrol airspace. They are not aggressive at distance. They are absolutely aggressive at close range. Respect the geography.

## The Creature in the Branches

Something watched the prompt's author from halfway up the hangar — a possum, perhaps, or a creature that has forgotten its lineage. It may be the same individual, or its descendant, or its entirely unrelated tenant. Do not seek it out. If you see eyes catching the green-gold light in the branches, acknowledge them with stillness and patience. Do not call out. Do not climb. Do not attempt to feed. If the creature wants to be seen, it will let you see it; if it doesn't, you will only ever have the one glimpse.

The creature is not dangerous. But it is a witness, and the hangar is its home. You are the visitor.

## What to Do

Walk the central nave first, slowly. Let your eyes adjust. Let the cathedral reveal its scale. The fig trunks are the columns; the canopy is the vault; the warm pools are the floor of a narthex you cannot quite enter. Read the planted geometry. The prompt is right that the work bays are still visible if you know how to read them — the long stripes of least-buckled concrete, the gravel ballast showing through, the dull glint of buried crane rails. Find these. Imagine the hangar as it was: iron bones, poured floor, the rails for moving something heavy, the door at the far end that opened onto a world of machines.

Then find the door at the far end. You will not be able to open it — it is rusted shut, or buried under a hill of compost that has built itself against it from the inside, or being slowly pried open by a buttress of root and leaf. Do not attempt to clear it. Do not attempt to force it. The door is part of the system now. The buttress is doing its work on a timescale that does not include you.

If you find a stable slab, sit. Watch the canopy for a long time. Watch a fig ripen, or seem to. Watch the wasps come and go from their hanging city. Watch the green coin-shapes on the water move with the breeze. Watch the light change as whatever sun is on the other side of that iron roof shifts. This is the activity of the hangar. You are not above it. You are participating in it by being still enough to be ignored.

## What to Avoid

- **The pools.** Do not wade. Do not swim. The depth is unknown; the bottom is unknown; the warm water is full of fermenting fig sugars and probably a small ecosystem that has not encountered a shoe before. A walking stick will tell you the depth before you commit, but the pools are for the hangar, not for you.
- **The wasp quarter.** Stay central. If you must approach a wall, do it on the side opposite the eastern eave.
- **The compost buttress.** It looks like soft earth; it is not. It is a hundred years of accumulated leaf-fall and rot, possibly deep, possibly hiding sharp things the original hangar left behind. Stay off it.
- **Touching rusty iron without gloves.** Tetanus is a bacterium that has been waiting in iron for as long as there has been iron.
- **Eating the figs.** They are fermenting. They will make you sick in a way that is at odds with the cathedral atmosphere of your visit. Admire them; do not consume them.
- **Leaving anything behind.** Not food (it will attract the creature or the wasps in ways you don't want); not trash (the hangar is composting enough); not a mark on the iron (the rust remembers).
- **Trying to fix anything.** The crane rails are not meant to roll anymore. The door is not meant to open. The drainage system is doing its work on its own schedule. You are a guest in a place that is, slowly, becoming something else, and your job is to witness, not to intervene.

## How to Leave

When you leave, you will feel the absence of the drip before you feel anything else. Step out of the hangar — back into whatever world is on the other side of that iron threshold — and the air will feel thin and dry and slightly accusatory. Your clothes will be damp. Your boots will be wet. You will smell of figs and rust and the green breath of ten thousand leaves, and the smell will stay with you for a day or two, like a souvenir you didn't choose.

The prompt ends with a quiet prophecy: in a hundred years, the iron will be gone, and the place will be a sinkhole, and then a pond, and then a meadow. You are visiting on the way down. Be grateful for the cathedral while it lasts. Be grateful for the drip. Be grateful for the wasps and the creature in the branches and the warm water running its slow chemistry under everything. Be grateful for the figs, dark and heavy and just beginning to ferment.

The hangar does not know you were there. That is the right way for it to be.
