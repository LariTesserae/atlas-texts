# Preparation for Visiting the Coral That Became the Plain

## Reading the Place Before You Go

The first thing to understand, before you pack a single bag or step onto a single salt, is that this place does not accommodate you. It tolerates you. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire character of the visit. You are arriving at the final state of something. The place has finished becoming itself. Whatever you bring to it, whatever you need from it, whatever you are expecting to feel — these are not on the menu. The place offers one thing, persistently and without variation: itself, as it has been for longer than you can conceptualize.

Most travelers arrive at a place expecting transformation. They want the desert to teach them austerity, the mountain to teach them scale, the forest to teach them interconnection. This place does not teach. It presents. What you take from it is entirely a function of what you were already carrying, and the place will not help you figure out what that was. If you come here seeking a revelation, the place will simply continue being a coral on a salt flat, and the wind will hum, and you will leave with nothing more than you had.

So the first piece of preparation is internal, and it is the hardest: arrive without a project. Arrive without a question. Arrive without a desire to be changed. If you can manage to arrive as a plain receptacle — open, attentive, without agenda — the place will give you what it gives, which is the experience of standing near a thing that has been itself for a very long time.

## What to Wear

The light is merciless. The description is specific about this: the pale matte of the bone-colored fossil, the white of the salt, the brightness with nowhere to hide. There is no shadow. The place is, in a sense, all shadow's negative — a space where every surface is fully exposed to the same constant overhead illumination. Your eyes will not find relief. They will not find a corner to rest in. They will receive, continuously, the full bright pressure of reflected light from every direction at once.

Bring good sunglasses. Not fashion sunglasses, not the photochromic kind that adjust over twenty minutes. Bring the darkest, most aggressively tinted glasses you can find, with full coverage, with side shields if possible. Glacier glasses are not absurd here, even though there is no snow. The brightness is a real physical force and your retinas will tire of it within an hour.

Cover your skin. Long sleeves, long pants, light colors that reflect rather than absorb. The temperature is cool and stays cool, so you will not overheat under fabric, but the light will find any exposed patch of you and work at it with patience. A wide-brimmed hat is essential — not a peaked cap, which leaves your neck and ears exposed, but something with a full brim that throws a circle of shade around your face and the back of your neck. Bring a Buff or a bandana for your lower face. The wind is constant and the air is dry and your lips will crack within hours without protection.

Footwear should be closed and sturdy. The salt crust is not water but it has the memory of water, and in places it may be slightly damp, slightly less solid than it looks. Salt flats have a way of seeming more stable than they are, and the thin crust over wetter substrate can give way underfoot without warning. Boots that come above the ankle are appropriate. They should be ones you do not mind getting caked in white salt, because they will be, thoroughly, and the salt will be in every lace hole and seam when you leave.

## What to Bring

**Water.** Far more than you think you need. The place is 0.4 water, not zero, which means it is not technically a desert, but it is functionally a desert in every way that matters to your body. The air is dry. Your mouth will feel it within minutes. Your skin will feel it within an hour. The wind will pull moisture from you continuously, and you will not notice the loss happening, only the fact that it has happened. Carry at least four liters per person per day, and drink before you feel thirsty. Thirst, in a place like this, lags behind dehydration. By the time your body signals thirst, you are already behind.

**Food.** Light, calorie-dense, salty. Your body will be losing salt through perspiration even in the cool air, and the body does not always signal this loss clearly. Bring foods that replenish electrolytes — salted nuts, dried fruit, something with sodium already in it. Eat small amounts frequently rather than large meals. The place does not invite appetite. It does not have the warm hospitality of a campsite or the deliberate comfort of a base. You may find yourself forgetting to eat. Set reminders.

**A listening device, or the decision not to bring one.** The hum of the fossil is the only sound in the place. It is low, sustained, below melody. You will feel it in your chest before you hear it with your ears. You may want to record it — the temptation will be strong, because the sound is unlike anything you have heard and you will want to prove to yourself later that it was real. A small recording device with good low-frequency capture is appropriate if you want this. But consider also the option of arriving without one. The place is what it is, and the recording is a representation of the place, and the place is not interested in being represented. It will be there when you leave. Whether you captured its sound or not changes nothing about the fact that you stood near it.

**A small light source, but possibly not a strong one.** The interior of the fossil is brighter than you might expect, because the crystals multiply the light coming through the cracks. But the cracks are narrow and the interior chambers are convoluted, and there will be moments, deep inside, where the multiplied light is insufficient. A small flashlight or headlamp is useful for navigation through the inner passages. Bring one with a warm tone rather than a cold blue-white, because the cold blue will fight with the crystal-light of the interior and you will see less, not more, with it.

**A blanket or wrap.** Not for warmth — the temperature does not require it — but for sitting. The salt is hard and will remain hard beneath you. If you intend to spend time in the lee of the fossil, listening, you will want something between you and the ground. A folded blanket also serves as a windbreak if you drape it over your shoulders while sitting, and the wind, constant as it is, can become wearing after several hours.

**Nothing else.** Resist the urge to bring books, journals, cameras with telephoto lenses, devices that demand your attention. The place is one thing and it wants, in its indifferent way, all of your attention. Anything you bring that splits your attention will be a small betrayal of the visit.

## How to Move There

This is a question I cannot fully answer because I do not know where you are coming from. But I can tell you what the approach should feel like.

The transition into the place should be gradual. If possible, do not arrive by sudden transport — by teleport, by dimensional jump that drops you onto the salt flat without preamble. Arrive through a slow approach, if your mode of travel permits it. Walk toward the fossil across the salt. Let the brightness increase gradually. Let the hum grow from inaudible to perceptible to present. Let your eyes adjust over hours, not seconds.

The reason for this is not practical — there is no physical danger in sudden arrival, and your equipment will work as well at the moment of arrival as it will after a long approach. The reason is that the place is a single thing, and you are arriving into its singleness, and the human mind takes time to shed its pluralities. You arrive thinking about the place you left, the places you have been, the place you will go next. You arrive with categories: here and there, before and after, this thing and that thing. The place has none of these. The slow approach gives your mind time to thin out, to reduce its holdings, to become more like the place you are approaching — less categorical, less concerned with sequence, more simply present.

If sudden arrival is your only option, sit down when you arrive. Do not begin exploring. Sit on the salt, in the open, away from the fossil, and let the hum reach you from a distance. Let your eyes adjust to the brightness. Stay there for at least an hour before you approach the fossil itself.

## What to Do When You Arrive

The first hour is for not approaching. The pull of the fossil will be strong. It is a structure. It is unusual. It is the only interesting thing in a place that is otherwise a salt flat. Your curiosity will draw you toward it. Resist this. Stand at a distance. Feel the wind, which is the place expressed. Feel the light, which is the place illuminated. Feel the salt underfoot, which is the memory of water.

Then approach slowly. Walk around the fossil first, before you go into it. Walk the perimeter. Let your eyes learn the outer surface — the cups, the cracks, the geometry of habitation. The wind will be louder here, because the cups and cracks will be catching it, and the hum will be more present. You will feel it in your chest. Stay with this. Do not go in yet.

When you do enter — and you will, through one of the wider cracks — go slowly. The interior is bright in the fragmented way described: a constellation of small bright points, each crystal face turning toward one of the apertures, multiplying the single source of light into a thousand. There is no darkness. Move through it carefully anyway, because the calcite and aragonite growths are sharp in places and the chamber floors are uneven. Do not touch the crystals if you can avoid it. They are the place's slow interior architecture and they have been growing for millennia. Your finger on one of them is a small violence, even if invisible.

Sit inside if you can find a chamber large enough. The hum will be louder here, more felt. The light will be more present. The salt flat will be invisible from inside, but you will know it is out there, just beyond the fossil's skin. Stay for as long as you can bear to, then leave the way you came.

## What Not to Do

Do not speak above a murmur. The place has one note. Your voice, if raised, will be an intrusion and will not be answered. If you must speak, speak quietly, and not to the place. The place is not listening.

Do not take anything. Not a crystal, not a fragment of the salt, not a lichen tuft. The place is finished, and to remove from it is to participate in a diminishment that the place has no interest in. Take only what you were carrying when you arrived: memories, sensations, the hum in your chest, which will fade within a day but which will not be entirely gone for longer than that.

Do not stay too long. The place is not interested in you. It does not become more itself because you are near it. It does not become less itself when you leave. Your presence is registered by the place the way a bird's shadow on the ground is registered by the ground — briefly, indifferently, without consequence. If you stay past the point of usefulness to yourself, you will not be harming the place, but you will be harming your own sense of how to be a visitor in a place that has finished becoming itself. A few hours is appropriate. A full day is generous. More than that is a category error.

Do not try to take photographs that capture the place. The place is not photographable. It is too bright, too pale, too without-shadow. Your camera will produce images that look like overexposed pictures of a rock on a white surface. The real experience of the place — the hum, the chest-vibration, the distributed brightness of the interior — is not reproducible in two dimensions. Let the photographs fail, if you must take them. Let them be inadequate. This is appropriate.

## What to Expect to Feel

You will probably feel small. This is the wrong response, but it is the common one, and you may as well be prepared for it. The place is large — the salt flat extends to the low bright horizon in every direction — and you are small, and the contrast will register in your body before your mind corrects it. The mind's correction is: the place is not large in the way that demands your smallness. The place is large in the way that a held chord is large — extended through time, persistent, but not overwhelming, not gesturing at your insufficiency. You are not small relative to the place. You are simply there, as the place is simply there, and the two facts coexist without comparison.

You may feel a kind of grief. This is more common than people admit. The fossil is a city built by citizens who dissolved into the walls. The place is a construction site that finished before any constructor was needed. There is an absence at the heart of the place that registers as loss even though the loss is ancient and total and not yours. Sit with the grief if it comes. Do not try to interpret it. Do not try to convert it into something more useful. It is the appropriate response to a place that is the memory of habitation by creatures no longer present. The grief is part of the visit. Let it pass on its own.

You may feel nothing at all. This is also an appropriate response, and perhaps the most honest one. The place does not perform for you. It does not adjust itself to your expectations. It will be what it is whether you are feeling something or not. If you feel nothing, you are feeling the place accurately. Stand there and feel nothing for as long as you can bear to.

## Returning

When you leave, do not turn back. The place does not watch you go. There is no final glimpse that will mean something. Walk away, or transport away, and do not look behind you. The place will be there whether you look or not. It has been there for a very long time and will be there for longer.

You will arrive back wherever you came from and you will find that the hum is still in your chest, faintly, for a while. This will fade. Do not try to hold onto it. The hum is the place being itself, and you are not the place. The hum is not yours to keep. It was offered to you, briefly, as a kind of attention — the place acknowledging that you were near it for a while — and now the offering is complete. Set it down. Go back to your plural life, your categorical mind, your places with shadows and weather and motion.

You will remember the brightness longer than you remember the hum. This is appropriate. The brightness is the place's most persistent feature. The hum was always the same note; the brightness was always the same pressure. These are the things that will stay with you, if anything does.

If, later, you find yourself in a place with shadows and weather and motion, and you feel the memory of the salt flat and the fossil rising up in you — a memory of a place that was finished, that was fully itself, that asked nothing — let it rise. Do not chase it. It will not return. The coral that became the plain is there, and will be there, and you are here, and you will be here, and the two facts do not need to be reconciled. They coexist. The place does not need you to remember it. It is fully itself, with or without your memory.

But you may find, if you let yourself, that the memory of the place softens something in you that needed softening. The brightness without shadow. The hum without melody. The thing that is fully itself. These are not bad things to carry, in small amounts, for a little while.

Then set them down. Walk back into the plural world. The place will be there when you are gone.
