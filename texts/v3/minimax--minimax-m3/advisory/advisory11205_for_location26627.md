# Preparing for a Visit to the Plateau City

## The first thing to accept

You are not going somewhere new. You are going somewhere that has finished becoming itself and has been digesting that decision for a very long time. The travelers who do well here are the ones who arrive already in a slower rhythm — who have stopped, on the road up, trying to orient themselves by the usual cues. There is no green to comfort you. There is no canopy. There is no silence. The city will not present itself as picturesque. It will present itself as *continuous*, and your task is to fit into its continuity without becoming a friction event.

Bring patience. More than you think. The light is flat, the distances are deceptive, and the locals move with the unhurried gait of people who have learned that hurrying buys nothing against a city that recycles its own streets.

## What to wear

**Layers, but heavy on the outer shell.** The air is the temperature of a held breath, even before dawn. It does not cool off the way a temperate city cools off at night — it stays warm and *wet*. You want fabrics that breathe but do not become waterlogged. Wool is ideal. Cotton is a misery. Synthetics that trap moisture against the skin will chafe you raw within hours.

**A face-mask of woven fiber.** This is not optional. The locals wear them constantly and not for ceremony. The wing-hush of the gnats — the ones the size of pinheads, in columns as dense as fog — is not loud, but it is *constant*, and the smaller spores from the breathing-stones will, over the course of a day, settle into your airways and into the corners of your eyes. The woven-fiber masks the workers use are good. Get one that covers from the bridge of the nose to under the chin. You will want the ear-loops or ties snug; the thermals off the heated stone produce small gusts that lift loose fabric.

**A brimmed hat or a hood.** Not for sun — there is almost no direct sun — but for the condensation. In the pre-dawn hours the moisture beads on every vertical surface, including you. A hat with a brim keeps the runoff out of your eyes. A hood does the same and also buffers the constant fine drizzle that is, even now, falling somewhere.

**Footwear with serious tread and sealed seams.** The condensation-channels between terraces are slick with mineral sediment and the film of small flatworms. The basalt cobbles of the older streets are colonized in the cracks and become uneven. The slag heaps themselves radiate heat and have a crust that can look solid and not be — particularly where the breathing-stones have fruited. You want boots. You want them laced tight. You do not want open sandals no matter how warm the air is, because the small mobile fauna the size of rats with too many legs graze the violet crust at ankle height and they do not all move out of the way in time.

**Dark or muted colors.** You will not be harassed for wearing bright clothing, but you will be *noticed*, and the locals are not in the habit of being noticed. Their stained coats and dark fiber-masks are not affectation; they are camouflage against a city that has tuned its visual field to basalt black, oxide red, and rust. A traveler in clean primary colors is, here, the equivalent of a flashing sign. This is not dangerous, but it is uncomfortable, the way it is uncomfortable to be the only person speaking in a library. Darken your kit.

**A long-sleeved outer layer with the cuffs sealed.** The mobile fauna does not bite in any medically meaningful way, but the larger dog-sized armored forms will, if they feel cornered, extend the mineral plates along their flanks in a threat display that can scrape skin. Long sleeves with snug cuffs keep them at the polite distance they prefer.

## What to bring

**Water.** Not because the city lacks water — it drinks the sky, every gutter oversized, every roof angled — but because *you* will sweat more than you realize. The wetness on your skin is not rain and not sweat in the usual sense; it is condensation from the air, which means your body is losing heat to evaporation constantly, which means your kidneys are working harder than they feel they are. Drink steadily through the day. A liter every three hours is a reasonable starting point. More if you are climbing between terraces.

**A light neckerchief or filter-cloth.** Useful for wiping the condensation from your face and for, occasionally, breathing through if the wind shifts and brings a denser pocket of gnats. The locals carry these. They are not just for sulfur.

**An extra pair of dry socks and a sealed dry-bag.** You will not find a dry hour. You will, if you are careful, find moments when your feet are merely damp rather than soaked. The difference matters.

**A small amount of food from outside.** This is delicate. The city eats itself — basalt, copper, sulfur, iron, processed into slag heaps that radiate heat for decades and host the densest biological communities in the region. Imported food exists. It comes in on the ore-carts returning from the lowlands. It is treated as a luxury, a traded good, a flavoring agent. Do not arrive with a pack of travel rations and eat them openly in the street. You will be polite-resented. If you bring dried something from the lowlands, share it discreetly, ideally with a guide who can introduce the gift in the right way. The locals will not refuse kindness, but they will measure it.

**A writing surface that does not run.** The condensation will bead on your notebook, your phone screen, your map. Waxed cloth works. A grease pencil is reliable. If you must use paper, accept that the corners will curl and the ink will feather, and write anyway.

**No strong fragrance.** The city's smell is its own layered thing — warm mineral dust, ozone from the compressors, the sweet almost-rotten breath of the breathing-stones, and a faint copper undertone that comes from so many small bodies living so close together. Layered perfume, scented sunscreen, tobacco on your clothes — these will mark you as foreign in a way that goes beyond color, because the local fauna is tuned to the local air. You do not want to be a novel olfactory event in a place where the olfactory field is already so dense.

**No open flame that you do not control.** The thermals off the slag heaps can lift a spark into the wrong place. The vapor off the sulfur lines is not explosive in the way gasoline vapor is explosive, but it is persistent, and the fauna overhead is dry-membrane and membrane does not love fire. A closed lighter for a pipe is one thing. A campfire is, in most seasons, a provocation.

## How to move

**Slowly, and with announcement.** The fauna does not sleep. The fauna does not always move out of your way. The locals navigate by a particular unhurried pace that gives the smaller creatures time to register a large approaching body and rearrange themselves around it. Walk the way they walk. If you must pass a worker on a narrow rail-bed, do not hurry past; slow further, step to the side, and let them set the geometry of the encounter. They will signal with a short whistle when they want you to press to a particular wall. Obey the whistle. The whistles are partly because the compressors drown speech and partly because the wing-hush eats consonants. They are not informal.

**Stay off the slag heaps unless invited.** The heaps are the city within the city. The breathing-stones grow thickest there. The fauna is densest there. The funerary custom of laying the dead out on the warm stone at night is observed there. Walking on a slag heap without local accompaniment is the equivalent of strolling through a graveyard in another culture — not forbidden, but tone-deaf in a way that will be remembered. If you are invited to a funeral, go. It is one of the most important things you can witness in the place. Bring nothing. Speak little. Stand at the edge of the heap, not the center. The dead will not be visible in the dark, but the rustle will be, and the slow wingbeats overhead.

**Do not touch the breathing-stones.** They exhale a fine spore when stepped on, which is how they got their name. The spore is not toxic in the way a poison is toxic, but it is dense with the particulate that the local respiratory system has spent a lifetime learning to filter. Yours has not. A mask helps. Stepping around them helps more.

**Watch the sky in tiers.** This is a habit the locals develop early and you should develop on day one. The pinhead gnats drift low, around the warm outflow vents. The sails — flat triangular creatures, hand-span across, membrane and cartilage — circle above them, riding thermals. The big ones, the ketch-sized shapes that no one here has named, drift higher still. You do not need to identify them. You need to know which tier you are looking at when something falls. The gnats drift. The sails coast. The big ones *descend*, slowly, and when one descends, the smaller fauna reorganizes beneath it in a visible column. That reorganization is your cue to step under an eave. The big ones are not predatory in the way a hawk is predatory. They are doing something else that no one fully agrees on. But they displace a great deal of air when they come down, and a flat triangular thing the size of a small room, landing on a slag heap nearby, is a physical event you do not want to be standing in the open for.

**Listen with your chest.** The deep resonant moo of the big ones overhead is felt before it is heard. You will know it when you feel it. It is not alarming. It is, in fact, oddly steadying. The locals orient by it the way sailors on an inland sea orient by the swell.

## What to expect from your own body

You will sweat, but the sweat will not feel like sweat. It will feel like the city is putting its weather on you, because it is. The condensation will bead on your pack straps, on your belt, on the metal of your buckle. Your papers will dampen. Your leather will darken. Within a day you will stop reaching up to wipe your face and will instead let the moisture be, the way the locals do.

Your lungs will take a few days to adjust. The sulfur from the lines and the ozone from the compressors and the fine spore from the breathing-stones together produce a particular morning rasp that every long-term resident has. You will not get it as severely as they have it, but you will get a version of it, and for the first two or three mornings you will cough in a way that is not illness but adjustment. The mask shortens the adjustment. So does drinking.

Your sleep will be light. There is no quiet hour. The compressors run through the night. The fauna does not sleep. The condensation at dawn provides a slow drumming of moisture falling from the eaves. The first night, you will lie awake and resent this. By the third night, you will lie awake and listen with attention. By the end of a stay, you will miss it when you leave.

Your sense of horizon will collapse and not return for a few days. The flat luminous midday light, the muted colors, the fauna filling the sky in layers — these will, for the first day or two, make the place feel claustrophobic in a way that is not about walls but about *frame*. You cannot see far. You cannot see an edge to things. The glaciated inland mountains only show for an hour or two in the afternoon in the warm months, and that hour is, by long local custom, the moment when people stop working and just look. Plan to be outside for that hour. It will recalibrate you.

## What to expect from the people

**They are not unfriendly, but they are economical.** A working life in this city is, by the standards of most places, short, and the cycle of construction and biological reclamation is visible at street level within a generation. People do not waste motion. They do not waste words. A greeting is short. An explanation is short. If you ask a question that requires a long answer, you will be asked, with no offense, whether you have asked the right person.

**They will judge you by your footwear first and your mask second.** This is not metaphorical. They will, within a minute of meeting you, have assessed whether your boots are appropriate for the condensation-channels and whether your mask seals at the nose-bridge. If both check out, you have passed the first gate. If either fails, they will, politely, suggest improvements.

**They will want to know what you eat.** Not as small talk. As a real question, because the base of the food chain here is mineral, atmospheric water, and time, and the imported food on the ore-carts is a traded luxury, and the question of what a stranger chooses to eat tells them what kind of stranger you are. If you answer honestly that you brought some dried something from the lowlands, the right move is to offer a small portion. If you answer that you have not yet figured out what the city eats, the right move is to ask. They will tell you. They are proud of it in a quiet way.

**They will speak about the fauna by name before they speak about their own family.** This is not a quirk. It is a structure of attention. Their children grow up knowing the names of the local fauna before they know the names of their own grandparents. If you want to fit in, learn three or four fauna names early — the local names, not your multiverse-traveler names. The gnats have a name. The sails have a name. The breathing-stones have a name. The big overhead ones do not, and the absence of a name is itself a piece of information.

**Their grief is conducted at night, on warm stone, with the fauna overhead.** If you witness a funeral, do not interpret the laying-out on the slag heap as abandonment. The dead are being returned to the city's own cycle, slowly, and the breathing-stones will be thicker where they lay by morning. This is not horror. This is the city's way of refusing to pretend it is separate from what it holds. You are also, while you are there, being held. It is appropriate to feel that.

## What to bring back

Not much. The dust will come with you anyway, in your pack fabric and the treads of your boots and the lining of your mask. Do not take a breathing-stone. Do not take a tube-worm. Do not take slag. These are not souvenirs the way a pressed flower is a souvenir; they are pieces of a working system, and removing them is felt even if no one confronts you about it. The appropriate things to take are impressions, and the names you have learned, and the sound the compressors make when the wind shifts, and the way the morning light looks on sweating basalt.

## A final orientation

This is a place that has settled into itself. It does not need you to validate it. It does not need you to understand it. It will go on being slowly eaten by the lives it holds long after you have returned to wherever you came from, and it will not resent your leaving. The rain — gentle, somewhere, always — will fall tonight. The condensation will be on every wall in the morning. The fauna will be moving. The compressors will run. The big shapes will drift overhead, unnamed.

Your task, while you are there, is to be a small, polite, well-masked addition to the cycle. To walk the inner terraces for an hour without seeing an empty surface, and to understand that this is not a failure of design. To hear the layered sounds and not try to flatten them into a single narrative. To accept that the city is older than its memory and younger than its slopes. To eat what is offered, drink the sky-water, wear dark and damp clothes, sleep lightly, and listen with your chest.

Go with patience. Return with patience. The place will not be impressed either way, but you will have been somewhere true.
