# A Brief for the Traveler Headed to the Stack at the Edge of the New Sea

Written for someone who has not been. There will not be another copy.

## First, the honest framing

You are going to a place that is in the middle of becoming. Not ruins. Not a destination in the sense your travel instincts understand it. The column has not finished arriving. The sea has not finished taking. Whoever was here before you is not coming back, and they did not leave for you. They left, or they were left, and what they left is small and a question. Bring that knowledge with you. It will make the climb easier, or it will make it harder. Either way it is the truth of the place and the truth will not negotiate.

The ordinary human part of you will want a goal. There is no goal. There is a pocket, and there is a sound, and there is a plant that may or may not put out a fourth leaf while you are there. If you go looking for a payoff you will be miserable and you will leave too soon, and you will tell people it was just a wet rock, and you will be technically correct and completely wrong.

## Getting there

The column is in open water with no land on the horizon. Whatever route you take from your home world, you will end the land portion of it some distance from the stack — probably by boat, because the currents at the base are not friendly to a swimmer and the rock is too sheer at waterline for a casual landing.

**Boat.** A small open boat is fine. A kayak is questionable in the colliding currents. A larger vessel will keep you dry but will not be able to come alongside the base, because the surge throws water upward by ten or fifteen meters on bad days and the swell has no rhythm you can time. Plan to disembark some distance out and paddle or be ferried the last stretch in something low. The seabirds and the occasional gull that lives in this part of the world do not give warnings about currents. Pay attention to the water yourself. Where the two currents meet there is a visible line of chop, a standing seam of darker water. Do not cross it. Go around. It costs you ten minutes and saves you a swim.

**Tide thinking is wrong here.** The water is not a tide. It is the residue of a drowned world, behaving on a longer and less predictable clock. Do not trust your coastal instincts. There is no high tide that you can wait out, no low tide that exposes a ladder or a stair. The pocket is sometimes submerged for an hour in the afternoon. You cannot know in advance. You must choose, before you climb, whether you are willing to be on the column when the water comes up.

**The bird, if you see it.** The grouse-shaped thing that comes in from the southeast. It arrives when the weather is least bad. If you see it on approach, the morning is probably safe. If you don't, you should think twice.

## The climb

There is no path. Whatever ladder or carved steps used to lead to the pocket is under the sea. The column is fifty to seventy meters of soft, apricot limestone-marl, banded, recently emerged, still being unmade by the wind.

This means three things.

First, the rock is not trustworthy. It is soft enough that a confident handhold can crumble. It is banded, so it has planes of weakness running horizontally, and ledges that look solid can be no thicker than your palm. Test everything. The original description says the stone is "almost soft in places." That is not a metaphor. You will come away with orange dust under your fingernails and a fine, flour-like grit in your cuffs. This is normal.

Second, because the column is emerging, it is shedding. Every few minutes a piece comes loose and falls. The little *tock* you hear below the roar is not optional — it is a fact about your climb. If you are below a piece that wants to come loose, you will learn something about gravity and pain. Do not climb directly below another climber. The rock does not aim at you, but it does not avoid you either.

Third, because the column is being unmade as you climb it, the route up is slightly different each time you take it. Do not trust a guidebook written by someone who came before. Trust your hands.

**Hands and knees, mostly.** This is not a free-climb of the kind a sports climber would enjoy. It is an awkward, slow, smeary ascent on a wet, exfoliating surface. Bring approach shoes with sticky rubber and a fine edge, not boots. Bring gloves. The rock is kind to leather and unkind to skin — the orange dust gets into small cuts and stings.

**Tether.** A short length of rope is a comfort, not because the climb is technically hard but because the noise and the wet and the exposure make your hands shake even when your technique is fine. Tie off to nothing. Tie off to yourself. It helps.

**Do not climb in the afternoon if you can avoid it.** The water surges in the afternoon. The spray gets worse, the rock gets more dangerous, and if the sea takes the pocket it will take the upper third of the column too, in a sense — visibility collapses, the rock face weeps harder, and the wind picks up a cross-grain that wants to peel you off. Mornings are calmer. Mornings are when the column exhales.

## What to wear

The place is wet in three directions at once. Nothing you bring will stay dry. Plan for that.

**Outer layer.** A proper waterproof shell with a hood. Not a fashion jacket. You want taped seams and a high collar. The spray is constant and finds every gap. The hood needs to cinch tight around the face — the horizontal rain is small but it stings, and it carries salt that will chap your lips within an hour.

**Mid layer.** Fleece or wool. Cotton is a mistake you only make once. You will be wet from the outside and you need a fabric that stays warm when wet. Wool is better than fleece because the salt gets into everything and wool forgives it longer.

**Legs.** Trousers you can move in. The climb requires high steps and awkward straddles. Shorts are tempting because of the heat of effort, but the spray will numb your knees within twenty minutes. Long trousers, synthetic, quick-drying.

**Hands.** Gloves for the climb. A second pair dry inside the shell for the pocket. When you arrive at the top your hands will be cold and wet and you will want to handle the slate and the arranged stones, and you will not want to do it with numb fingers.

**Feet.** Approach shoes. Wool socks. A dry pair of wool socks in a sealed bag inside your pack, for the descent, because wet feet on a wet climb down are how people break ankles.

**Eyes.** Sunglasses, surprisingly. The pewter sky is bright in the way that overcast water is bright — a flat, directional glare off the cloud-base. You will squint without them. Polarized, ideally. If you wear prescription glasses, bring a strap.

**Ears.** This is the one most travelers do not think about. The roar of the two currents meeting at the base is louder than you expect, and the pocket funnels it and adds harmonics. After an hour in the pocket you will have a slight, dull ringing in your ears that lasts the rest of the day. If you have any sensitivity to noise, bring foam earplugs for the time you spend sitting in the pocket. Take them out periodically to listen. The roar is part of the place. You do not want to miss it. But you also do not want to leave with damaged hearing.

## What to bring

**The minimum.** Water, in a sealed bottle. Something to eat that does not require two dry hands and a flat surface. A small first-aid kit with waterproof tape and something for cuts, because the rock will give you some. A headlamp, even if you intend to be off the column by dusk, because the cloud can drop a premature dark. A lighter or waterproof matches, even though the fire-pit is cold.

**One small object.** Not to leave. To hold. A smooth thing that fits in a closed fist. You will understand why later.

**A notebook, if you are that kind of person.** Not to write a lot. To write a few words when the place tells you what the words are. If you are not that kind of person, leave the notebook at home. You will know.

**Nothing electronic.** The wet will find it. The place does not invite screens anyway.

## What not to bring

**Do not bring a drone.** The wind will take it. Also, the bird does not need to be filmed.

**Do not bring food to share with the bird.** It is not a pet. It is a fact about the column.

**Do not bring instruments.** There is no silence here to play over, and the roar is already a kind of music that does not want accompaniment.

**Do not bring more than you can carry up one-handed.** You will be climbing a wet, soft, exfoliating column. You cannot have a heavy pack. Whatever you bring must be in a small bag you can swing out of the way without losing balance.

## The pocket, once you arrive

You will arrive tired, wet, and slightly triumphant. The pocket is small. You will be surprised by how small — you read "arms spread and almost touch both walls" and your body did not believe it until you got there. The floor is a rough shelf. There is a place to sit if you do not mind being wet. There is a place to stand if you want to look out at the sea.

**Take off your pack before you turn around to look at the pocket itself.** You want to see it with empty hands. The first time you see the arranged stones, the standing slate, the cold fire-pit, you want to see them without the weight of your world on your back.

**Spend the first ten minutes just looking.** At the stones. At the notch. At the lichen that looks like the inside of a lung. At the cress, which has three leaves and may have a fourth coming. The pocket is arranged, but not by anyone who is here now. It was arranged by use, or by storm, or by something that wanted a question to be carried forward. Look at it before you decide what you think.

**The decision about the fire-pit.** This is yours. The pit is cold and has been cold for a long time. You can lay a fire in it. You can light it. You can sit by it. You can also not. Lighting it is a kind of statement and not lighting it is a kind of statement and the pocket will receive both. There is no correct answer. There is only the answer that is true to why you came.

If you do light it: use driftwood or something you have carried up, not anything from the arranged stones or the standing slate. The smoke will bloom against the back wall the way the old smoke bloomed, and you will see, for a moment, the thumbprint in dust that is no longer there. This is the moment to write in your notebook, if you brought one.

If you do not light it: sit with the cold pit. It has its own kind of warmth, the way old campfires do, even when they are out.

**The decision about the slate.** Do not move it. Do not chip at the notch. Do not take a rubbing, even a soft pencil one. The notch is a question. If you answer it by altering it, the pocket will become a different pocket, and the difference is the difference between a place and a souvenir. Stand near it. Look at the angle. Try to picture a person kneeling. Try to picture the column flexing in a storm a hundred years ago. You will not be able to tell which. That is the point. Leave it.

**The decision about the stones.** They are arranged by the storm. They are not yours to rearrange. You may, if you are very gentle, pick up the fist-sized one on top of the two others and feel the smooth facet that has been worried by something over many years. You may put it back exactly as you found it. The pocket will not know, but you will know.

**The scallop.** Do not touch it. It is the wrong shape for here. It should not exist in the pocket. Someone brought it up out of the deep water, and the fact that they did is the most human thing in the place, and it does not need your fingerprint on it.

## The sound

You cannot prepare for the sound. You can only be told about it, and then you will stand in the pocket and it will be different from anything you were told.

The roar is not coming from below. It is coming from everywhere. The two currents collide at the base and throw themselves upward, and the rock takes that sound and re-emits it, and the pocket catches it and turns it. There is a low fundamental that you feel in your sternum before you hear it. There is a mid-range that is the actual noise of water on water. There is a high, almost-not-there whistle from the wind passing the notch in the slate, which you only hear when the roar briefly hesitates, which it never quite does.

You will, within twenty minutes, stop trying to hear your thoughts. This is not a loss. It is the place giving you the thing it has, which is a different kind of thinking, slower, less verbal, more like the lichening thought of the back wall. Let it happen.

If the roar becomes unbearable, which it may: cup your hands over your ears. You will hear your own blood. This is also part of the place.

## The wet

You will be wet. You will continue to be wet. There is no dry here.

**Salt.** The spray is salt. Your lips will chap. Your hands will prickle. Bring something for your lips and use it before you need it, not after.

**The weeping of the column.** Up at pocket height the stone sweats a slow mineral water, orange-crusted where the weeping has been going longest. If you sit with your back against the back wall you will get a slow, faintly orange stain on your jacket. This is fine. It is not damaging. It is, in a way, the place touching you back.

**The horizontal rain.** It moves in sheets. When it comes through the pocket, everything pools. The arranged stones darken to the color of a wet mouse. The fire-pit fills with an inch of water within minutes. The cress, which is doing the only thing available to it, which is to be a plant, gets beaten flat for a while and then stands up again. Watch it. It is the most resilient thing in the place and it is not trying very hard.

## The bird

If it comes, it comes. Usually from the southeast, in the calmer weather, usually mid-morning. It stands on the slate for ten or twenty minutes and looks out at the sea with one eye and then the other and does not make a sound.

**Do not approach it.** It is not tame. It is not a sign. It is a fact about the column, like the crab below the waterline. If it lands on the slate while you are standing near the slate, stay still. It will look at you and decide you are not interesting. This is fine. You are not interesting to the place either, in the way that matters. You are a temporary fact about the pocket, like the bird, like the rain.

## How long to stay

The pocket is not a place to stay long. This is not because of danger, exactly, though the afternoon surge is real. It is because the pocket gives what it has in the first hour or two, and after that it begins to give you yourself, and most people are not prepared for that.

**Two hours is right.** Three, if the weather is mild and you are not in a hurry to be anywhere else. A full day is too long. You will start to rearrange things in your head. You will start to compose explanations. The place will let you, but the explanations will not be true. The place is in the middle of becoming, and explanations are for things that have finished.

**Watch the waterline.** In the morning it moves down the column by a foot or two — the column exhaling, the sea remembering itself. If you are going to be there through the surge, stay high in the pocket, near the back wall. If the spray starts to leap the lip of the pocket, get onto the rough shelf at the back, the higher one. You will be wetter. You will be safe.

## The descent

Take the dry socks out of the sealed bag and put them on before you start down. Your feet will thank you for the rest of the day.

**The climb down is harder than the climb up.** Your fingers will be colder. The rock will be slightly wetter. The afternoon may be coming on, which means the surge is coming on, which means the spray is going to get worse as you descend. Plan to be at the base before the worst of the afternoon surge, or plan to wait at the top until the surge passes. Waiting at the top is fine. The pocket will keep.

**Tell someone, before you go, what time you expect to be back on the boat.** If you are not back by then, someone should come look. Not because the place is deadly. Because the column is soft and the sea is undecided and you are one person and ordinary.

## The coming back

You will come back changed in a small, hard-to-describe way. Not converted. Not enlightened. Just adjusted, slightly, to the idea that the world can be in the middle of becoming, and that the middle can be a place, and that places can carry forward a question they do not need to answer.

If you lit the fire, the inside of your nostrils will smell of it for a day. If you did not, the inside of your nostrils will smell of salt and orange stone. Either way, you will find, in the days after, that you listen to running water differently. You will catch yourself trying to hear the argument in it, the long slow ongoing one, the one that does not have words but is about something. You will not always succeed. You will sometimes try.

The cress, when you were there, may have put out its fourth leaf, or may not have. You will never know which. That is part of the agreement with the place. You were there for a small piece of its becoming and it was, briefly, a small piece of yours, and the two pieces did not need to line up to mean something.

Take care on the boat. Mind the currents at the base. Go around the seam, not across it.

Come back if you want. The pocket will be different and the same. The stones will have been moved, or not moved, by a storm. The slate will still be standing. The notch will still be a question. The lichen, which is the slowest thing in the pocket, will have grown by an amount you cannot see.

The roar will be exactly the same.
