# Preparing for the Singing Stack

The first thing to understand about this place is that it will not tell you what it is. You will stand on it for hours, maybe, and still not know. So the preparation begins in the head, not the suitcase.

## What you have to give up before you go

Give up the idea of a path. There isn't one, and pretending there is will make the first hour miserable. You will look for the horizon, for the seam where this place meets another place, for any indication that you came from somewhere. There is no such indication. The water is in every direction and the water is below you and the water is somehow at the level of your own throat when you breathe. Stop looking for edges. Stop looking for origins. You rose. That's all you get.

Give up the idea that sound is information. Here, sound is weather. It's continuous, all-directional, sourceless. The roar of breaking water against young rock is so complete that your own voice, even shouted, will feel like a whisper in a hurricane. Within twenty minutes you will stop trying to speak. Within an hour you will stop forming words in your head. This is not a problem to solve. It is the place, the way a forest is made of trees. You will adjust. Your inner monologue will become gestural, pictographic, slow. Let it.

Give up trust in your eyes. The light is filtered through so much atmosphere that it arrives without direction. Shadows fall upward. Bright patches gather under overhangs. The water changes color — bruise, pewter, dull copper — without reason. You will see things that aren't there and fail to see things that are. The carved marks halfway up the basalt? They are real. The orange-rust growths in the cracks? Real. The small archipelago of younger stones around you, some of them throwing shadows upward onto the underside of waves as though lit from below? Also real. The geometry doesn't matter. The persistence does. Trust what stays. Don't trust what's bright.

## What to bring on your body

**Nothing you can't afford to lose to salt water.**

The entire basalt is wet. Not rain-wet, not spray-wet — the wetness of something that has been submerged for a long time and only just emerged. Every surface weeps a thin mineral tear at its joints. Your shoes will be saturated within ten minutes. Your clothes within twenty. Anything paper or cloth or absorbent will become a small miserable creature you have to carry around. Bring synthetics. Bring things that shed water. Accept that you will be wet and that the wetness is warm, the warmth of a body that has forgotten how to cool itself, and that this is closer to being held than to being rained on.

You will not need warmth. You will not need shade. You will not need food for the duration of a normal visit, because the visit will be shorter than you think, and because the place has no interest in whether you eat.

You will need your hands free. The stairs — if stairs is even the right word — are part of the stone, possibly cut, possibly natural, you cannot tell, and the same step is sometimes two steps and sometimes no step at all. You will need to grip. You will need to test each surface with your palm before committing weight. The basalt is sharp-edged. It is young. It is still deciding what shape it wants to be, and it has not yet decided to be kind to hands.

If you must carry a container, make it sealed. Make it of something that does not corrode. The air itself tastes of salt and warm mineral and the breath of something very large and very old exhaling through water.

## What to bring in your mind

**A long, slow rhythm.** Not a task list, not a research agenda, not a checklist of marks to find and catalog. The place will frustrate all of those. The water doesn't recede in any predictable way — there is no tide here, only motion — so the lower carved pits appear and disappear by rules you cannot deduce. You will see three of them. You will suspect there are dozens more, underwater, still legible somehow. You will not see the dozens. Don't go looking for them. Go up.

**Permission to not understand.** The faint marks older than the basalt should be impossible, and they are, and they aren't, and you will start to suspect that everything you are taking as evidence of human intention is actually evidence of geology's tendency toward pattern. This suspicion is correct. This suspicion is also incomplete. Both of these are true at the same time. Sit with that. The place does not require you to understand it. The place does not require you to do anything. The place is not interested in you.

**A willingness to feel the basalt attend.** This will happen. Between waves, in the gaps where the roar drops by some small fraction, the stone will check its own inventory. It will count its own columns. It will briefly consider whether it is a place at all and find the question difficult. You will feel this. It is not your imagination. It is also not something geology is supposed to do. Let it happen. Don't try to make it happen again. Don't try to record it. It will come again on its own, briefly, and it will leave on its own.

## How to move

Slowly.

Climb. The stairs spiral up the basalt — or orbit it, it's not always clear which — and the pillars narrow and sharpen as you ascend, becoming more vertical, more aggressive in their geometry. Higher up, the wind will be worse. The spray will be thicker. The wave-compressed channels between this basalt and its neighbors will be squeezing water into a vertical fist that hits the rock like a punch, and this happens often, and you do not want to be at eye-height with one of those impacts.

Stay near the protected lee of the higher steps when you can. The orange-rust, blood-rust, shell-inside growths in the hollows are not edible and not useful and not interesting to you, and that is fine; they are interesting to themselves, and they cling to the stone with the determination of the only living thing on this rock, and you should not disturb them. They are not plants. They might be lichen. They might be coral. They are tougher than you are.

Do not try to swim. There is no animal life visible, audible, or sensible. There is also no reason to believe the water is safe. The whirlpools open and close without leaving a trace. Waves rise from places where there is no slope to generate them. The depth goes down much further than the basalt goes up. The basalt is a needle in a floor of warm ink. You are not a needle. You are a person. Stay on the needle.

## How long to stay

Shorter than you want.

The place will not warn you when it is time. It will not give you a sign. It is not interested in you. It is not interested in anything. It will simply continue being a place for as long as you continue standing on it, and not one moment longer. You will not see yourself leave. You will not feel a transition. One moment you will be standing there, warm and wet and loud and small, and the next moment you will be somewhere else, and you will not be able to remember the moment of going.

So don't wait until you have to leave. Leave when the leaving still feels like a choice.

## What you will bring back

The sound. You will bring back the sound. It will live in the bones of your feet for weeks, the folded-in weeping of the stone below the threshold of the roar. You will hear it in your sleep. You will hear it in your sleep for a long time.

The light, unreliable. You will find yourself, back in ordinary places, looking at shadows and not trusting them. You will look twice at bright patches under overhangs. You will feel briefly, absurdly, that the sky has been filtered through too many layers, and that somewhere above it, the source of the light is not a single source.

The warmth. Not the air temperature. The other warmth. The warmth of being held. This will not fade as quickly as you want it to.

And a small, persistent question, lodged somewhere in you like a basalt joint, that you will never quite be able to formulate: whether the place was a place, and whether you were there, and whether there is a difference.

There isn't. That is also the answer.
