# Preparing for the Sunken Room

There is no preparation that will make the Sunken Room ordinary. What you can do is decide what kind of visitor you will be inside it — alert or unaware, hardened or open, hurried or slow — because the place will treat you differently depending on which one you bring down the fig root. A little forethought is the difference between a place that unsettles you for a week afterward and a place that you leave as a guest leaves a friend's house, with the dim green smell still on your clothes and the click-click-click of the small mouths somewhere behind your ears.

## Before You Go

**Mind first, then body, then the bag.** The Sunken Room is not a hostile place but it is an *engaged* one, the way a beehive or an anthill is engaged. It is busy. It is watching in the slow, distributed way that a system watches itself. If you arrive tense, suspicious, or in a hurry, you will make the place feel adversarial, and it isn't. If you arrive curious, you will be permitted more.

Spend a few days beforehand being patient in small ways. Sit still for twenty minutes somewhere you don't usually sit. Listen to a building breathe at night. Watch condensation form on a cold glass. The room rewards a person who has practiced noticing; it punishes almost no one, but it confuses the inattentive badly.

If you have any history of claustrophobia, do not pretend you don't. The room is not a cave in the cinematic sense — there is a clear vertical shaft and the sky is always somewhere above — but the *air* is close, the light is low, and the heat bears on you like a hand. If you have ever felt the walls move inward during a panic, take a companion who knows the signs and who will not, themselves, panic.

## The Bag

Pack light. You will be carrying everything down a twisted fig root in a fold of clay wall, and you will be carrying everything back up the same way, and the sweat will not dry on the handles. A small shoulder bag, ideally one with a single strap you can swing to your back when you need your hands.

**Light.** Bring one, but be ready to put it away. The room has its own dim bioluminescence — moss, lichen, the white threads of fungus — and your eyes will adapt within ten minutes if you let them. A bright flashlight ruins more than it reveals: it flattens the shadows the room is built from, and the shadows are where most of the living happens. Carry a small headlamp on low, ideally with a red mode. Use it to look at the chevron on the basin's lip. Use it to read what you can read on the carved stone at the fig's base. Otherwise let the room's own glow be enough. You will see more than you expect.

**Water to drink.** A liter, sealed. The basin's water is not for you. It is old, it is the room's water, it is shared with the salamanders and the fig and the insects and whatever lives in the channels below. Drink from your bottle only.

**Something to sit on.** A folded square of cloth, a square of foam, even a flattened pad of dried leaves from above. The floor is not a floor in any hygienic sense. It is compost. It is alive. Things have been eaten and reincorporated into it. Sit on your cloth and you will not think about it; sit on the floor and you will not stop thinking about it.

**Nothing that smells.** No food in open packaging. No strong cologne. No sweet sunscreen. The fauna are serious and efficient, and a moth in the light-shaft is taken in the time it takes you to blink. You are larger than a moth but the principle is the same. Eat before you go down. Eat after you come up. Do not bring the smell of eating into the room.

**A thin layer you don't mind ruining.** Long sleeves, long pants — not for modesty but because every surface in the room is alive and damp, and the moss will mark you, the lichen will mark you, the residue from the bark will mark you, and the sweat that does not dry will mix with all of it and you will come back out looking faintly green and smelling of fig rot for two days. Wear something you can wash hard. Or wear something you are prepared to never fully reclaim. I knew a traveler who kept a dedicated set of clothes for visits to damp places. He called them his *room clothes*. He never wore them anywhere else.

**Grip on your feet.** This is not optional. The fig-root staircase is a kindness, but it is a kindness made of knots and knuckles polished by generations of hands, and when your hands are slick with the air of the room they will be less kind than they look. Shoes with soft rubber soles, broken in, that you trust on wet stone. No new boots. No flip-flops. No bare feet, tempting as bare feet will be when the heat climbs.

## The Descent

Stand at the rim for a full minute before you go down. The desert above you is bright and dry and the light will be in your eyes. Let them adjust to the contrast. The drop is not vertical; the wall folds inward in places, and the fig root traces a path through the folds, but a fall from even a third of the way up would not be a small thing. Go down facing the wall, three points of contact at all times. The root is strong but it is not symmetrical, and a grip that holds at noon may not hold at five o'clock when the air inside has shifted.

There is a smell change about halfway down. Above: dry clay, dust, the faint green of the scrub. Below: the heavy vegetal breath of the fig, sweet-rotting, slightly fecal, faintly fermented. Your stomach will decide what it thinks about this. Listen to it. If it recoils, do not push past it; the smell only intensifies at the bottom, and what your body is telling you is that this is a place where digestion is *active*. You are walking into someone else's stomach, in a sense. Or a mouth. The metaphors the room invites are not gentle, and they are not wrong.

The last ten feet are the worst. The wall is black where the water has stained it, and your hands come away dark. The condensation begins to fall on you in single drops before you reach the floor. Each drop is the room's only large sound. Try to step softly when you arrive; the floor, which is to say the soil, will take your weight, but the small things on it will notice.

## Inside the Room

**Move slowly.** Not for reverence, although reverence is not misplaced, but because the lizards on the walls are doing serious work and your shadow arriving fast will scatter them. When they scatter they drop into the cracks and do not return for hours, and then the room feels emptier than it is. Move slowly and they will continue to hunt while you watch, and you will see what the room looks like when it is being itself.

**Do not touch the basin from inside.** The lip is worn into a curve by something other than weather, and the wear is smooth and deliberate in a way that suggests a hand — many hands, many years, the same gesture repeated. You may kneel at it. You may look down into the water. You may put the backs of your fingers against the rim if you want to feel the temperature of the stone. The water itself is not for you. The fig's pale tendrils lie on the surface like the fingers of a drowned hand. They are not decorative. Do not pull on them.

**Do not peel the bark of the fig.** When you push past it to reach the basin, you will see the bark lifting at the edges in places, and underneath, the small white shapes that look like hands. They are not hands. They are the colony of something — a fungus, an insect's nursery, a coelenterate of some kind — and they are part of the system. Peel them and you will find out what they are, and what you will find out will be worse than not knowing. The room is built from not knowing. Most of what works in it works because it has not been inspected.

**The chevron is for looking at, not for touching.** It is on one of the dressed blocks of the basin. It is a chevron, or an eye, or the letter of a script that has no descendants. If you can read it, do not say what it says out loud. If you cannot read it, do not make up a reading. Sit with it. Sit with it for a long time. The room will tell you, eventually, in the small ways rooms tell things — a drop timed differently, a lizard that pauses and watches you, the light moving one notch further along the wall — whether you have understood.

**The heat will not break.** Do not wait for it to break. It is the heat of a greenhouse and the heat of a compost heap and the heat of an old body, and it is the room's working temperature, and it will be your working temperature for as long as you are in it. Drink your water slowly. Let your sweat do what sweat does. Sit on your cloth and lean against the wall if the wall will hold you, and the wall will hold you because the wall is warm and the warmth is honest.

**The sounds will increase.** This is the strangest part of the visit, and the part that most travelers report on afterward. When you first stop walking, you hear the drip of the condensation, and a slow papery rustle from the fig, and the chirr that is not a cricket. After a few minutes, you hear beneath all of this the continuous, wet, clicking — many small sounds, too many to count. It is the sound of the room feeding itself. It does not stop when you notice it. It does not stop when you listen for it. It is the room's resting metabolism. Some travelers find it soothing. Some find it a preparation for something. Sit with it the way you sit with the chevron. It will tell you what it is, or it won't, and either way you will leave with it still going in your head for a day or two afterward, the way a long note on a bell keeps sounding after the bell has been struck.

## What to Do With What You See

You will want, afterward, to describe the place. This is fine. Most travelers do. But try to write it down the same day, while the smells are still on your hands, and do not edit for clarity. Clarity is what the place is *not*. The place is a bowl of dense, slow, dim, wet, hot life that has been running on the same water for longer than anyone can say, and any description that makes it sound tidy has lost the thing it was trying to keep.

If you find a small stone, or a fragment of carved masonry, or a piece of pale fig root — and you will be tempted; the carved stone at the fig's base is half-consumed and would not be missed — do not take it. The room is not a ruin. The room is a system that is *running*. Things are not abandoned here. They are in use. Removing a piece of it is not theft in the way you usually mean theft; it is closer to pulling a working part out of a watch. You will not see the watch stop. But you will not be sure it hasn't.

## Coming Back Up

Wait for the light to move before you climb. The single shaft of light that comes through the ragged opening in the ceiling falls in a pale column across the room, and it is the most legible time-of-day indicator you will have. When the column has moved well off the basin — say, when it is up on the wall — the air above will be cooler, and your hands will grip the fig root better, and the climb will be easier. Climbing in the full heat of the room is possible but miserable, and the misery will follow you out into the desert and you will not enjoy the contrast as much as you expected.

Climb facing the wall. The root is the same on the way up as on the way down. Take your time. The room will not close behind you. It has been here longer than the fig, and the fig has been here longer than whatever is living under its bark, and it will be here when you are gone. You are a single slow breath in its long schedule.

When you reach the rim, stop. Do not turn around to look down at it immediately; let the desert light do its work on your eyes first. When you do look down, you will see the small green bowl in the cracked clay, and from above it will look like the kind of place you might walk past without noticing. That is the room's gift to you — the agreement it makes with the surface, the way it lets itself be forgotten so that the things inside can be busy without being watched.

The lizards will still be on the lip when you come up. They have never been hunted. They will watch your shadow arrive and your shadow leave with the same unhurried patience. Do not bother them. They were there before you.

Wash your hands before you eat. Drink more water than you think you need; the heat of the room pulls moisture out of you for hours afterward, and you will not feel thirsty because the room replaces thirst with a kind of low, satisfied torpor that is not actually hydration. Eat something salty. Sit in the dry air for a while. Let the smells come out of your clothes naturally if they will, or wash the clothes if they won't.

You will think about the room for several days. This is normal. You may dream of the drip. You may find yourself listening for the clicking in places where there is no clicking. You may try to describe it to someone and find that what you have is not sentences but sensations — a smell, a temperature, a particular shade of dim green. This is also normal. The room is not a story. It is a place that is still happening. The best preparation is to accept that you will leave more changed by it than you will change it, and that this is, in the end, the correct order of things between a visitor and a place that has been holding water since before the script that carved the chevron had any descendants at all.
