# Preparing for the Induction Chamber

## Before you arrive

You should understand, before you step through whatever gate or threshold deposits you here, that this place is not waiting for you. This is the first and most important thing to accept. Most places you might visit in the multiverse have at least the courtesy to acknowledge visitors — a climate that adjusts, a gravity that compensates, a light that notices you. This place does not. It is in the middle of something. It has been in the middle of something for longer than you have been alive, and will be in the middle of it long after you are gone. Your visit, when it ends, will leave no mark. The plants will not remember you. The walls will not adjust.

This is not hostile. It is simply indifferent in a way that human places rarely are. You are a mote passing through a lung.

You should prepare for that indifference the way you would prepare for a long sea voyage or a stay at a remote outpost: not by trying to make the place care about you, but by making yourself self-sufficient in advance.

---

## The body, and what to do with it

The heat is the first thing you will notice, and you should prepare for it specifically. It is not the heat of a hot day or a sauna. It is the heat of a place that has been running for a long time — the heat of compost, of a kiln in slow use, of a machine that has not been turned off. It presses. It has weight. Your body will read it as oppressive at first, but it is not dangerous; it is the temperature the place requires for what it is doing. Plan to be uncomfortable but not in peril.

Drink water before you go, and bring water with you, more than you think you will need. The air is dense and feels saturated, but it is not water-saturated — it is saturated with something else, something metallic and faintly sweet, like the smell of a coin held in a closed fist for a long time, or the smell of blood not quite cleaned from a surface. Your throat will want moisture. Trust that want.

Eat lightly before you go. The air will sit heavier on a full stomach than an empty one. Do not arrive hungry; you may be there a long time. Do not arrive full; the density of the air is incompatible with fullness.

Wear loose, light clothing that breathes, but cover your skin. The metallic quality of the air may irritate exposed surfaces over time — your lips, your eyes, the soft skin of your inner arms. Long sleeves of thin fabric. A scarf you can pull over your mouth if the taste becomes too much. Nothing tight at the waist or chest; you will want to breathe shallowly or deeply as the moment requires, and you don't want fabric arguing with you about it.

Wear the most secure footwear you own. The floor here cannot be trusted. It will give under your weight by inconsistent amounts, not because it is weak but because it has not committed to being a floor at a particular depth. You will feel, occasionally, that you have stepped down a little further than you meant to. This is not falling. This is the floor deciding, briefly, where it is. Stable boots with good ankle support will keep you oriented when the geometry wavers. Do not wear sandals. Do not wear anything with slick soles; you want friction with the stone.

If you wear glasses or contacts, consider this: the light here comes from everywhere and from no place, and your eyes may struggle to find a focal point. Bring a familiar lens prescription if you have one. Bring sunglasses, though the light is not harsh — the relief from the omnidirectional glow can be real when it becomes too much.

---

## The senses, and how to use them

Your eyes will not be your most reliable instrument here. The light is even and sourceless, and the place is unfinished, and the geometry is not committed. Shadows will fall from things that have no source. Distances will disagree with each other depending on which eye you close. Plants you saw a moment ago will be standing, on second look, in two places. Do not trust your first reading of anything. Give yourself a moment, and then another, and let the place settle on a version of itself before you act.

Your ears may be more reliable. The hum is real. The slow ticks of cooling are real. The faint whisper from inside the flanges is real, and worth listening to, though you will not be able to make sense of what it is saying. If you must orient yourself, do it by sound. The hum lives in your sternum more than in your ears; you can feel which direction it is densest from, and that direction is roughly toward whatever the place is making.

Your skin will be your best map. The temperature has a gradient. The floor is warmest. The upper reaches of the plants are coolest. The circulation is upward through the plants and downward through the air. If you lose track of where you are, stop and feel. The temperature will tell you where down is.

Your nose will tell you what the air is made of. Trust the metallic sweetness but do not investigate it. It is a byproduct. It is not for you.

---

## The mind, and what to leave behind

You should arrive with as few expectations as possible. This is hard, because you have probably read about this place or heard about it from someone who has been, and you have built up some picture of what it will be. Let that picture go. The place is not what you have been told. The place is what it is, and what it is, is in the middle of becoming.

You should not bring the need to understand. Whatever is being made here does not have a name in your language, and probably does not have a name in any language. If you find yourself reaching for a word to contain it — *machine*, *organism*, *ritual*, *thought* — let the word pass through you without grasping. The place is patient with this. It will let you misname it indefinitely. But you will be less tired if you don't try.

You should not bring a sense of urgency. Nothing here is urgent. The plants have been growing for what may be centuries. They will continue growing after you leave. If you are afraid of wasting your time, you will feel every minute. If you accept that your time is not the kind of time being used here, you will feel none.

You should bring, instead, the willingness to be a witness without being a participant. You are standing in the smallest room in the world watching something be made. You cannot help make it. You cannot stop it. You can only see it. This is a particular kind of attention, and it requires a particular kind of discipline: the discipline of staying present to something that is not addressing you.

---

## What to bring

Bring water.

Bring, if you have one, a small object of home — something handled, something ordinary, something that has no purpose except to remind you that you came from somewhere finished. A stone from a beach. A key that doesn't open anything anymore. A photograph. Not because the place will notice it, but because you may, at some point, need to remember what a place looks like when its geometry has committed to itself.

Bring something to mark your starting position. A piece of cloth, a small stone, a thread. The distances here will not stay reliable. If you want to know whether you have moved, you need a fixed point that is not part of this place.

Bring, if you can, a way to record — a notebook, a recorder, a camera. Know in advance that whatever you record will not capture what you experienced. The place resists being captured. The recordings will be of shadows that fall wrong, of distances that have already shifted, of a hum that has already changed pitch. They will be documents of failure, which is itself a kind of documentation. Keep them anyway. They will tell you later what you were not able to see in the moment.

Bring patience, and bring more patience than you think you have.

Do not bring weapons. There is nothing here that can be threatened, and nothing that can threaten you. The plants cannot hurt you. The walls, which are not walls, cannot hurt you. The worst the place can do is confuse you, and it will do that without meaning to.

Do not bring anything you cannot afford to leave behind. If you drop something, you may not be able to find it again — not because it has been hidden, but because the floor has decided it is somewhere else now. Do not bring things of irreplaceable value, except to yourself, in which case, bring yourself carefully.

---

## What will happen

You will arrive in the heat. You will see the plants. You will not, for a moment, understand them. Then you will understand them too well, and wish you hadn't. You will walk through the arranged clusters and feel, with growing unease, that the arrangement is not an accident. You will hear the hum. You will feel the breath of the place.

At some point, you will notice that the geometry has stopped matching itself. A shadow without a source. A plant in two places. A floor that gives differently each time you step. This will feel like a mistake at first, then like a feature, then like a revelation — *the place is unfinished, and I am inside the unfinishing*. This is the part of the visit that changes people. Not the heat, not the plants, but the moment when you realize that the room has not decided what it is yet, and you are in it while it decides.

At some point, you will want to leave. This will not be because the place has become hostile. It will be because you have been a witness to a process that does not include you, for longer than a person can sustain, and the loneliness of that witness is its own kind of exhaustion. The room does not need you to leave. But you will need to leave. This is fine. The room will continue making itself long after you are gone.

---

## How to leave

The door will not appear. There is no door. The walls are not walls. But the place is responsive in a slow, gentle, indifferent way. If you turn, and walk, in a direction that feels like *out* — not toward any particular landmark, but toward the absence of more — you will eventually find yourself at the threshold you arrived through. It may not be where you remember it being. It will be where it is.

Do not look back once you have committed to leaving. The plants will have grown a little in the time you were watching. They will continue growing. The hum will continue. The breath will continue. None of it is about you.

When you are out, and back in a place whose geometry has committed to itself, you will find that you are slightly different. Not in a way you can name. You will have been somewhere that was making itself, and you will have stayed long enough to see the making, and a place like that cannot be entirely left behind. Carry it carefully. It is not heavy. But it is not nothing.

Drink water when you return. Eat something. Sleep if you can. The taste of warm metal may stay with you for a day or two. That is normal. It will pass.
