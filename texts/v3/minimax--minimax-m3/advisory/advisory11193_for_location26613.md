# Preparing for the Col

There is a particular kind of place that asks very little of you, and that is what this is, and that is why it is dangerous. Not dangerous in the way a crevasse is dangerous, or a cornice. Dangerous in the way a question is dangerous when the questioner has been waiting a long time and is patient enough to wait longer. You do not prepare for this place the way you prepare for a climb. You prepare for it the way you prepare for a long silence with someone you do not know. You make sure you can be quiet. You make sure you can leave.

## How to Get There

The description tells you almost nothing about the country below the col, and that is honest, because the col itself is the place. The faint warm air and the sourceless green scent are there to tell you that down there is a different world, and that you came up through it to arrive here. The route is irrelevant in detail; what matters is the *mode* of approach.

Do not arrive by vehicle as close as possible and then walk the last hour. That is the approach of a person who has not yet committed. The right approach is one that slows you down before you get there. Walk the last ridge on foot, ideally having slept the night below, so that the first time you see the col is from a distance of a few hundred meters, and the second time is across its edge, and the third time — the third time is when you are standing in it and the cairns are casting short black shadows and the basin water is only just beginning to move.

If you cannot manage that, at least walk the last kilometer with your mouth closed and your hands out of your pockets. The place is interested in whether you arrived with your full attention or whether you were still trailing your last hour behind you.

## What to Wear

This is high and cold, but the cold is not the enemy. The cold is just a fact, like the color of the stone.

Bring more layers than you think you need, and bring them in pieces, not in one thick shell. At this altitude the sun is strong enough to make you overheat when you are working — climbing the cairn, say, or crouching to look at the basin — and cold enough to find every gap in your clothing the moment you stop. The old rule is the right one: long underwear, a mid layer that breathes, a wind shell that actually blocks wind, a puffy you can stuff into a pocket and pull out when the cloud-shadow lifts and the temperature drops ten degrees in thirty seconds. Gloves with the fingers free, or gloves you can take off without taking the whole kit off, because you are going to want your fingers.

Boots are the most important thing you own. They need to be stiff-soled, because the close-packed stone will bruise you through a soft sole in an hour. They need to have tread that bites into the gritty dust between the blocks when the dust is wet, because wet grit is the only slick surface in the place and you will cross it. They need to be broken in. Do not bring new boots to a place like this. Bring boots that have already argued with you and made up.

A hat with a brim. Sunglasses that are actually sunglasses — not fashion sunglasses, the kind that are dark but do not block UV. The light here is the kind that gives you snow blindness in a clear sky, and the col will not be there to hold your hand while your eyes recover. The stone is dark enough that you may forget the danger. The sky is not.

Bring something to cover your face in the afternoon wind. Not a balaclava, something simpler. The wind that comes through the channels in the stones is not a cutting wind, not usually, but it is a wind that will chap your lips until they crack, and that will dry the corners of your eyes, and you will not notice until you are already miserable.

## What to Carry

Less than you think.

A water bottle, full when you arrive. You can refill it from the basin if you trust yourself to read the water, and you can, the water is clean — the col is too high and too clean to be polluted, and the only thing in the basin is meltwater off stone — but the basin freezes at night, and the meltwater is a thread. Do not count on it.

Food that you can eat with cold hands and that does not need to be cooked. You will not want to light a stove here. A stove feels right in a tent, wrong on a col. Eat cold food. Eat it slowly.

A knife. The stone is interesting, and you will want to turn pieces of it over, and a knife is the right tool for that, and also, you will be glad to have a knife in your hand when the wind picks up and the slabs are doing their thing in the late light. This is not a superstition. It is just that human beings do better in a place like this with a tool in their hand.

A light source. Not because the place is dangerous after dark — it is not, it is just dark, the cleanest dark you have ever been in, and worth seeing — but because you will want to see the stars from here, and the dark is deeper than you are ready for, and you will be glad of the option.

A camera, if you must. But know what you are doing. The place photographs badly. The dark stone eats light and the bright stone gives it back and the dynamic range is wider than your sensor, and you will spend two hours trying to make a photograph that does the col justice and you will fail, and the failing will use up the part of you that should have been looking at the col directly. Bring a camera if you are a photographer. Leave it behind if you are a tourist.

Do not bring a notebook, or if you do, do not plan to write in it. This is a place to be in, not a place to record. Whatever happens to you here will be muddled if you try to pin it to paper. Let it stay muddled. The cairns were not inscribed. Take the hint.

Do not bring a radio. The silence here is part of the place, and a radio is an argument against silence. There is no cell signal and there should not be.

## What to Do When You Arrive

The first thing to do is stop at the edge of the col, where the stone begins, and stand for a while. Do not step onto the close-packed stone until you have stood at its edge for a while. The col will be there. It has been there longer than you. It is not going anywhere.

When you step onto the stone, walk to the slab. Not to the cairns, not to the wall, to the slab. The slab is the entrance. The cairns are the old people of the place and they do not need to meet you first. The slab has been worked and the working is the part that is meant for visitors, even though no visitors were ever meant. Read it with your hands if you are allowed to, or with your eyes if you are not. The polish is the message, and the message is short. It is some version of: *we were here, we touched this, you are not the first and you are not the last, and the touching matters*.

After the slab, go to the basin. Look at the water. It will be black-bottomed if it is before late afternoon, and it will be moving, or it will be ice, or it will be just unfrozen. The water in the basin is the only thing in the col that is not stone, and you will find yourself staring at it the way you stare at a fire. That is the right response. Stay with it.

After the basin, the cairns, in order of size. Put your hand on the largest one. The largest one is the heaviest. It will feel heavier than it should, as the description says. That is not a trick of expectation. Stone at altitude is cold in a way that makes you feel the weight of it through your glove. Take your glove off first. You will want to feel the cold of it.

The smaller cairns can be looked at, not touched. They are not yours to add to, by the way. If you have brought a stone in your pocket to add to a cairn, this is the moment to put it back. The cairns are not for you. They are for the people who made them, who you do not know, who will never know you, and who are not you. The cairns are also for the next person, but that person is not you either.

The wall last. Sit inside the L if the wind is from the west. The L is the only shelter in the col, and the wind will find you if you are not in it, and you will be colder than you mean to be. The hollow in the corner is not a hearth. Do not build a fire in it. The blackening on the surrounding stones is older than you and it is the right amount of blackening.

## How Long to Stay

Longer than an hour. Less than a day. There is a sweet spot, and it is the length of a winter afternoon, with the light doing its thing, and the basin water turning its submarine green, and the slabs catching the sun along their edges. Two hours, maybe three. Long enough for the place to stop feeling new. Long enough for the silence to stop feeling like silence and start feeling like weather. Not long enough to start feeling entitled to be there.

When you feel yourself beginning to settle in, leave. The place will tolerate you, but it will not welcome you, and the difference matters. A place like this lets you be a guest for a window, and the window is not very wide. The cairn-builders knew this. They did not live here. They came and went.

## What Not to Do

Do not speak above a whisper. Not because the col is sacred — the word does not apply — but because the sound of a human voice at full volume in a place like this is a kind of violence, and you will be embarrassed by the echo, which will not echo, which will just be absorbed into the close-packed stone and never come back.

Do not carve your initials anywhere. This is obvious. The place has no alphabet and is not asking for one. The polished face of the slab is the only place where working is allowed, and the working that is allowed is the kind that takes years and is done by hand. The kind you would do with a knife is a different kind and is wrong.

Do not take a piece of the stone home. The stone does not belong to you and it will not travel well and it will sit on your shelf and look at you and you will not be able to explain to it why you took it.

Do not try to interpret the place. The cairns, the wall, the slab, the basin — they are not a code. They are not a message. They are the remains of people who were here and who did the things they did and who did not write down why, and the not-writing-down is the most important thing about them, and you should respect it by not making up reasons for them.

## What to Bring Back

Nothing. Or almost nothing.

The cold in your fingertips, if you were not wearing gloves when you touched the largest cairn. That will fade in an hour.

The smell of the greenness from the country below, which you did not quite catch, and which you will keep reaching for, and which you will not quite catch, all the way down.

The thin bright line of light thrown by the slab across the stone floor an hour before sundown. You will think about this at odd moments for the rest of your life, and you will not be able to describe it to anyone, and that is the right outcome.

The knowledge, which the col will give you if you are patient, that you arrived a long time after whoever built the cairns, and a long time before whoever comes next, and that the col itself, in its close dark patience, will be here for all of it.

That is the thing to bring back. That is the only thing the col has to give. Do not ask for more.
