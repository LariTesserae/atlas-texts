# Notes Toward Preparation for the Still Cathedral

The first thing to understand about this place is that it is not a place you *visit* in the usual sense. You enter it, you endure it, and you leave. The chamber does not change to accommodate you. It does not notice you. It is not waiting for you. Your visit will register on it the way a single dust mote registers on a balanced scale — as a disturbance so minor it falls within the margin of error, and the system will correct for it the moment you are gone, and by the time the next traveler arrives there will be no evidence you were ever there at all. Go in peace with this. It is the kindest thing the place offers.

## What the Body Will Be Asked to Do

The air is dry. Not "dry for a desert" — dry in the way a kiln is dry, the way a polymerized plastic is dry. Your mucous membranes will dry within minutes. This is not a metaphor and not a slow process. Drink water before you go in, and know that no amount of water you carry will be enough, because the dryness is not a lack of moisture in the air — it is a property of the air itself. The air does not want your water. It will take it from you anyway. Plan for chapped lips, cracked sinuses, the gritty feeling of eyes that cannot produce enough tear film. Saline nasal spray, carried sealed, is not comfort but tool. A damp cloth held over the mouth does almost nothing. Do not rely on it.

The warmth is the warmth of a closed system at equilibrium, and the equilibrium temperature is *exactly* at the edge of comfortable. You will not sweat — there is not enough moisture in you to sweat. You will simply feel warm, persistently, without the relief of evaporation. Wear as little as the place will allow and what you wear should be loose, breathable, light-colored not for fashion but because dark cloth against that sodium-light glow will make you feel hotter than you are. Cotton is right. Synthetics will trap the warmth against your skin and you will not be able to shed it.

The sound is the part you cannot train for, and you should not pretend otherwise. The hum is not a sound *in* the chamber. The hum is the chamber. It has no silence around it — there is no moment when it stops so your ear can adjust. The pressure on the chest is real; it is not psychological; it is acoustic and it will, if you stay long enough, slow your breathing and raise your pulse and make your thinking feel like it is moving through something thicker than air. Carry ear protection not for the volume but for the *texture* — the deepest industrial earmuffs you can find, the kind worn on airport tarmacs. They will not silence the place. They will give you a buffer between the hum and your nervous system, and you will need that buffer more than you think. Soft foam in-ear plugs under over-ear muffs. Both. A second pair in your pocket because you may lose the first pair in the machines and the second pair may save your visit.

The light will fool you. You will keep expecting your eyes to adjust. They will not. Bring a headlamp. Bring two. Bring batteries you have never opened, because whatever battery life you estimate, double it, then double it again. The reddish dim eats batteries faster than darkness does, possibly because the warmth of the place is also the warmth of any device you bring into it. (This is a hypothesis. The travelers who returned with battery data did not always return with anything else, and so the data is thin.) The headlamp is not for seeing *better*. It is for seeing *at all* in the spaces between the engines, where the chamber's own glow does not reach, and where you will have to go, because the engines are layered three and four deep and what you want will sometimes be behind them.

## What the Mind Will Be Asked to Do

The stillness will get to you before the loud does.

This is the part I want to write carefully, because it is the part I most want you to believe.

The chamber does not change. You will, at first, find this interesting. You will look at the engines and think about what they are doing, and then about what they are *for*, and then you will realize the second question has no answer, and you will find this profound. You will notice the creatures and wonder at their abundance and their stillness, and you will think about closed systems and ecological equilibria, and this will be interesting too. You will find a brass plaque with characters you cannot read, and you will run your fingertips over them, and you will wonder who laid the brick in the bricked door, and you will wonder what was on the other side, and you will think this is a mystery and you are a solver of mysteries.

After some time — and the time is different for everyone, but it is not long, it is never long — the interesting will leave.

What replaces it is harder to describe. It is not boredom exactly. Boredom is what you feel when nothing is happening and you want something to happen. In the Still Cathedral, something *is* happening, continuously, loudly, in every direction. The engines are running. The creatures are moving. The fungi are growing at whatever rate they grow. The chamber is full of activity. The activity simply never *goes* anywhere. There is no arc. There is no arrival. There is no departure. There is motion without destination, sound without source-meaning, warmth without source, light without origin, life without telos.

If you stay, you will begin to feel this not as an observation but as a *pressure*. It is the same pressure as the sound, but it presses on a different part of you. The sound presses on the chest. The stillness presses on the part of you that expects the world to keep changing. That part is older than you. It is the part that scans for threat, for opportunity, for the next thing. It is the part that keeps you awake at night. In the Still Cathedral, there is no next thing. There is only the current thing, repeating, forever.

The travelers I have spoken with who stayed longest describe a kind of *softening*. Not peace. Not relief. A loosening of the grip the mind keeps on its own continuity. They describe, sometimes, a moment — usually around the third or fourth hour, sometimes the second — when they stop waiting for something to happen, and then they stop noticing that they have stopped waiting, and then they are simply *in* the chamber, the way the creatures are in the chamber, the way the moss is on the underside of the engine. They are not happy. They are not unhappy. They are *present* in a way that is, for most people, only available in deep meditation or near death.

This is not dangerous. It is also not nothing. Bring a way to mark time. Bring a friend, if you can, and a hard rule about who is responsible for whom. Bring, most of all, a reason to leave. Not a deadline, not a schedule — a *reason*. A person you have to return to. A piece of work unfinished. A small animal that needs feeding. Anything that is *outside* the chamber, anything that pulls you back into the world of arcs and arrivals and departures. You will need this. The chamber will not give it to you.

## Practical Notes on the Engines and the Creatures

Do not touch the engines with bare hands. The metal is hot in the way metal is hot when it has been hot forever — not scalding, but *too warm*. Your hand will not burn, but it will feel afterward as if it has been held too long, and you will carry that feeling for hours. Gloves are useful but not always practical, because you will want your fingers free to feel the plaque, to feel the trowel marks on the bricked door, to touch the creatures if you choose to. Touch them if you choose to. They are warm. They are soft. They do not react to being touched, or they react so minimally that you cannot tell. They are not dangerous. Nothing here is dangerous in the way predators or poisons are dangerous. The danger here is older than that.

The creatures do not attack. They do not flee. They part around you the way water parts around a stone, and they close again behind you, and the chamber does not notice. If you sit still for a long time — and you may want to, the warmth invites it, the hum invites it — the creatures will climb on you. They will use your legs and arms as terrain. They are light. They are curious in a way that is not curiosity, in a way that is only the movement of small bodies across available surfaces. They will not harm you. They will not harm your clothes, though some of them secrete a faintly mineral-smelling fluid that dries to a pale residue. This is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is simply what they do.

Do not try to fix the engines. I know you will want to. Every visitor wants to. The engines are *doing something* — they are clearly doing something — and the human instinct is to identify the something and intervene. There is no intervention. The engines are not broken. They are running exactly as they have always run. Whatever you see as wrong, whatever you see as missing, whatever you see as wasteful or pointless or unfinished — that is the engine. That is the state. If you took a wrench to one of the housings you would not repair a machine. You would break a closed system, and the chamber would rebalance, and you would not be there to see how, and when the next traveler arrived the engines would still be running, and you would not be able to tell that anything had changed.

## On the Marks

The bricked door, the half-stair, the column that stops at shoulder height, the plaque with the worn characters — these are the most human thing in the chamber, and they are the thing most visitors remember longest. They are the residue of intention. Someone, at some point, was *here*, in this loud warm dim place, and they wanted to do something — go somewhere, label something, close something off — and they did it, or they started to do it, and then they stopped. They left, or they died, or they were absorbed into the system the way the creatures absorb each other. The marks remain.

You will want to read the plaque. You will not be able to. The heat has worn the characters smooth, and the only way to read them would be to feel them with your fingertips, and even then you will not read them so much as *sense* them — the bumps and valleys of a script you may or may not recognize. This is the right way to read it. Do not bring a rubbing. Do not bring a camera with a flash. The flash, if it works, will do nothing the sodium light does not already do, and it will startle the creatures near you, and they will scatter, and the scattering will disturb the moss on the underside of the nearest engine, and the moss will not recover in any time you will be there to see, and you will have made a mark of your own on the chamber that does not belong to you.

Sit with the plaque. Feel it. Move on.

## On Leaving

Leave before you are ready to leave. This is the single hardest rule of the Still Cathedral and the one most often broken. The chamber does not announce when you have stayed too long. It does not get louder, or warmer, or dryer, or stranger. It gets *easier*. The hum becomes your pulse. The warmth becomes your temperature. The creatures become furniture. The stillness becomes comfort. And then you have stayed too long, and you do not know it, because the chamber has succeeded at being the only thing.

Set a time. Bring a watch that ticks, not a digital one — the ticking will anchor you, will remind you that time outside the chamber continues, will give you a small mechanical heartbeat that is not the chamber's. Set an alarm. Set two. Trust the alarm more than you trust yourself. When the alarm sounds, leave within five minutes. Do not finish what you are doing. Do not take one more look. Do not say goodbye to the engines or the creatures or the plaque. They were here before you and they will be here after and they will not notice your going any more than they noticed your coming.

When you leave, the air outside will feel wet. It will feel cold even if it is warm. It will feel *moving*, in a way you had forgotten air could move. Your ears will ring for hours. Your eyes will sting. You will feel, for the first hour or so, that you are seeing too much — too many colors, too many edges, too many shadows. This passes.

You will also feel, for a longer time than you expect, the absence of the hum. Not as silence — there is silence outside the chamber too. As *lack*. As a pressure on the chest that is not there and that you half-expect to be there. This also passes. It takes longer.

You will want to go back. Most visitors do. The Still Cathedral is not seductive in the way beautiful places are seductive. It is seductive in the way a held breath is seductive — in the way anything that has filled you completely and then withdrawn makes you want to fill yourself again. Do not go back soon. Go back when you have forgotten the exact pitch of the hum. Go back when you have stopped reaching for the creature-warmth on cold nights. Go back when you can remember the chamber as a place you visited rather than a place you once were.

The chamber will be exactly as you left it. The engines will be at the same phase. The creatures will be in the same density in the same places. The moss will be on the same undersides. The plaque will still be unreadable. The bricked door will still be bricked. The chamber does not keep you. It does not forget you. It does not know the difference.
