# Preparation for the Basin of Enormous Plants

The first thing to understand is that nothing you have packed for a mountain, a desert, a tundra, or a swamp will be sufficient on its own. This place is all of those things, in moods, and none of them, all the time. Pack for a tundra that sweats, that hums, that has a sense of humor about direction.

## The cold is not a temperature

The cold here is described, in every account that has ever filtered back, as something you can feel in your teeth and in the back of your skull. It sits on the neck. It is gray. It is almost a sound. Do not trust your thermometer. Trust the small bones of your face. When the cold is a pressure behind the eyes, that is real. When it is a taste of metal at the back of the throat, that is real. When the air at your knees is colder than the air at your chest and you can feel the line between them across your ribs, that is also real, and you should not be alarmed by it, only respectful.

What to bring against it:

- A neck gaiter or balaclava that you will not take off. The neck is where the cold sits. Cover the throat, the ears, the jaw. Wool is good. Silk against the skin is better. Two layers.
- Layers that address vertical bands of temperature, not just overall ambient cold. Your legs and torso may be experiencing different weathers. Loose trousers over thermal underlayers. A vest or short jacket over a longer shell, so you can modulate the midriff line where the air changes character.
- A small piece of soft leather or heavy felt to press against the sternum if the cold becomes a pressure. A physical counter-stimulus. It helps. Not metaphorically.
- Ear protection that does not seal you off — musician's earplugs, ideally. The valley is loud the way an old house is loud, and after three days you will start hearing the chord when there is no sound at all. You want to be able to lower the volume without losing situational awareness. The groans of the plants may be warnings. You need to hear them.
- A soft cloth or scarf to breathe through, loosely. The mist is not air anymore, not by the time it has been breathed by the plants and the rocks. It carries iron. It carries copper. It carries ozone. It will taste of metal within an hour. The cloth is not a filter, exactly. It is a way of reminding your lungs to be cautious.

## The wetness is a country

You are going to be wet the entire time. Plan for wet. Plan for wet, then plan for wetter.

- Footwear: serious mountaineering boots, fully waterproof, resoleable, with a stiff rand and aggressive tread. The slate is near-flat but it sweats a thin film of dark water that has no business being as slick as it is. The boots should break in before you arrive, but not be broken down.
- Gaiters. Tall ones. The slate also weeps around the ridges, and the mist condenses on every surface at every height. Your lower legs will live in a fine constant rain.
- A shell that is not just waterproof but actually waterproof after being touched by an oily branch. Bring a small tin of reproofer. Plants in this valley exude a sap and a fine dark powder. The powder smells of ozone and it gets into everything. Your gear will not behave the way gear behaves elsewhere. Reproof on day two. Reproof on day four.
- Everything you value goes into two waterproof bags nested one inside the other. Notes, lighters, batteries, backup clothing, food, recording equipment. Double bag. The single bag fails. The double bag survives.
- Do not drink the floor water. Do not collect the luminous drops that fall from the leaves at certain hours. The valley is a factory and the products of the factory are not for you. Bring all your water from outside, for the full duration, plus a margin of three days in case your exit is delayed.

## The light is not a friend

The light is dim. The light is also, at certain hours, too much of itself. The plants hold the day and then release it. The release is slow, heavy, and luminous in a way that gets behind the eyes. Do not stare into it. Do not try to photograph it with your phone at full brightness; the screen will be a small sun to your dark-adjusted eyes, and you will be half-blind for a minute in a valley where half-blind is a serious condition.

- One reliable primary light, head-mounted, with a red-light mode. The red preserves your night adaptation and does not attract whatever the flyers are.
- Two backups, hand-held, one of them chemical (a glow stick is not embarrassing; it is dependable).
- A small piece of opaque cloth to cover one eye if the luminous hours catch you out in the open. You will still see. You will not be fully dazzled.
- No flares. No strobes. The flyers come for sudden sounds, and possibly for sudden lights. The mist will diffuse a flare into a useless gray wall. You will not be rescued by it, and you may be marked by it.

## The plants are not what they seem

This is the most important paragraph in this document, and you should read it twice.

The plants are tall, dark, fibrous, cold to the touch. They are alive. They are not only alive. They are not buildings but they are architecture. They are not machines but they are engines. They are not religious but they are, in some sense, devotional. They are the only structure in the valley and they were not built. You must resist, the entire time you are in the basin, the temptation to lean against one, to shelter beneath one, to climb one, to enter one.

- The trunks are cold. Do not touch them with bare skin for warmth. The cold will go into your hand and not come out.
- The dark powder at the lower bark is not food. Do not handle it without gloves. The ozone smell is a warning. The powder is something the plant has finished with, which means it is something the plant has processed, which means it is not for you.
- The sap, where it weeps from the bark, will stain anything it touches and will not wash out. It will also, in some reports, leave a mark on skin that takes weeks to fade. Do not test this.
- Some of the plants are taller on the inside than on the outside. If you find one whose interior is warm and contains a room, you have found a place that is older than the rest of the valley. Do not enter. The room was not made for you. The warmth is not offered. Standing at the threshold is acceptable. Crossing it is a decision you cannot undo.
- Do not shelter under a plant in a storm. The storms here are mostly sound, and the plants intensify the sound. Stand in the open, in the middle of the slate floor, if a sound-storm comes through. The plants groan in sympathy with the floor and you do not want to be near a groaning thing when the ground is also groaning.

## The ground is moving

This is the part that is hardest to live with and the part that is most important to plan around.

The slate floor is in motion. Slow waves pass through it. Sections rise. Sections sink. The floor briefly becomes translucent and you can see, beneath it, something that may be roots or pipes or veins or a darkness that is also a machinery. Then it is stone again.

- Do not stand still in one place for longer than an hour. Move. Walk. Drift. The floor is more forgiving of motion than of stillness, and you do not want to be standing on a section when it becomes translucent.
- If the slate under your feet begins to glow faintly, or to feel thin, or to make a sound like a struck bell, walk. Do not run. Running creates vibration. Walk steadily away from the affected area.
- If the floor begins to remember being a sky, and you begin to remember being a bird, lie down on your side, close your eyes, press your forehead into the slate, and wait. It passes. It is not you. The valley is dreaming. You are in the dream by accident. Hold a small stone in your hand. The weight of it is yours. The weight of it is a fact.
- Use the ridges as your reference. The ridges are not stable either, but they are more stable than the floor. Stay within sight of the ridges at all times. Do not go into the deep center of the basin unless you are prepared to navigate back by the slow stars the flyers make, which is a navigation reserved for people who have been here before. You have not been here before. Stay near the edges.

## The creatures

There are many of them. Most of them are not your problem. Some of them are.

- The small dark ones in the cracks are doing small dark business. Leave them. They are not interested in you. They are interested in the seams of the slate. Do not step on them, but you will not see them in time, so do not be too careful; this is the valley, and the valley is full of life that has been here longer than the plants.
- The large pale ones in the open are browsers, mostly, and will not attack a thing as small and as upright as you unless you approach. Do not approach. If one is browsing on a tree near where you want to walk, walk a different way. The basin is wide. There is no path, but there is always another way.
- The flyers are not birds. They move on something other than lift. They will not land while you can see them. Do not throw anything at them. Do not shout at them. If one descends toward you, crouch, lower your face, become less like a thing that notices flyers. This is not a proven method. It is the only method anyone has reported.
- The worms in the slate are longer than memory. They hum when you stand over them. They are not a threat. They are, however, an indication that the slate there is thinner than you would like. Move on.
- The thing that is so large and so patient that it can only be inferred: you will not see it. If you think you see it, you are probably seeing a ridge. If you are sure you are seeing it, sit down, lower your head, and wait for the certainty to pass. The certainty passes. The thing does not.

## Sound and direction

Sound arrives here from distances that have not yet happened, or from directions that are not on a compass. You will hear footsteps behind you. There are no footsteps. You will hear your name called, in a voice you almost recognize, from a direction that is north and also down. The voice is not calling you. The valley is rearranging sound the way it rearranges light. Do not answer. Do not turn toward it. If you must, speak aloud yourself, in your own voice, naming the day and the year and your own name. The sound of your own voice, properly yours, is a small anchor. Use it sparingly.

- A small bell on a cord, worn at the waist, is reported to help. The small constant sound of a thing you are carrying is harder for the valley to mimic than a thing you are hearing in the air. The bell also lets the small dark creatures know where you are, and they will move out of your path.
- A whistle is not recommended. The flyers.

## Time, food, and the body

The valley has a rhythm that is not the day. The mist breathes. The plants hum. The temperature shifts in vertical bands. You will begin, within a day or two, to breathe in time with the mist, to walk in time with the hum. This is the most dangerous thing that will happen to you in the basin, and it will not feel dangerous. It will feel like settling in. It is not settling in. It is the valley taking your rhythm. You must keep your own.

- A mechanical watch, wind-up, on a strap, worn over the sleeve so you can see it without thinking. Check it. Every time you check it, say the time aloud, and the date. This is the cheapest, most reliable piece of equipment you will carry, and the most important.
- Three meals a day, on your schedule, not the valley's. Bring food that requires some preparation, that involves a small fire if you can manage one (you can, with care, on the slate — the slate is wet but it is not flammable, and a small spirit stove is acceptable if you set it on a flat stone). Food that takes effort to make is food that reminds you that you are a person with a schedule. Food that is eaten from a wrapper is food that lets the valley's time become your time.
- Sleep. You must sleep. Sleep in shifts if you must. Sleep during the dim hours, not during the luminous hours, because the luminous hours are not night and your body needs night. The plants will not be warmer at night; the floor will not be more stable. But you will be more tired in the morning if you have not slept, and tired is dangerous here.

## What to bring home

Nothing physical. Bring notes, recordings, the small stone you held in your hand during the floor's dreaming. Bring the metal taste in the back of your throat, which will fade in a week. Bring, if you can carry it, the memory of the slow chord of the plants, which will fade in a month. Bring, if you can hold it, the knowledge that you were in a place that did not need to be understood to continue, and which continued while you were in it, and which will continue after.

Do not bring samples. The valley is a factory and the products of the factory are not for transit. The dark powder will not behave the way it behaved in the basin. The sap will not stay sap. The luminous drops will become, in any container you can carry, a small cold thing that hums.

## Departure

Plan the way out before you go in. Mark your entry point with a cairn of three stones, with a small cut in a stable ridge rock, with something that is yours that you can find again. The valley will move the floor, but the ridges are more stable, and a cairn on a ridge is a cairn that may still be there.

When you leave, walk steadily. Do not look back at the plants for longer than a breath. Do not listen for the chord for longer than a count of three. Step out the way you stepped in. Speak your name, the date, the time, the place you are going. The valley does not need to understand you to let you go. It has been letting things go for longer than most things.

If, on the walk out, you find that you do not want to leave, that the basin feels more like home than the place you came from, that the hum is already inside you and you would like to stay and listen until it makes sense — that is the valley. That is its last, quietest, kindest strangeness. It does not want to trap you. It is so old that it has forgotten how to want. It is simply being itself, and you have begun, briefly, to be part of it.

Sit down. Eat something from a wrapper, even though I told you not to. Drink water from your own bottle, not from the slate. Look at your watch. Say the date.

Stand up. Walk out. The basin will still be there, in roughly the same shape, in the morning. It has been there since before shape was finished. It will be there for a while yet. It does not need you, and that is the safest thing about it.
