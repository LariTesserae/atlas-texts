# A Traveler's Preparation: Notes for the Town Above the Underview

There is no way to prepare for this place. Let me say that first, because honesty is the only courtesy that survives the descent. What follows is less a packing list and more a way of holding yourself before you go.

## The Body

The altitude will not behave as altitude has behaved to you elsewhere. The 4,200 meters is a number, and the number is one of the truest things the place offers, which is to say: not very. You will feel it in your teeth first. Then in the hinge of your jaw. Then in the way your pulse sits in your wrists, slightly off-rhythm, as if the blood is being asked to negotiate with the air on its way past. Drink more water than you think is reasonable, and then drink more. The water here tastes faintly of metal and of nothing; this is correct, and your body will adjust to it within two or three days. Do not fight the taste. The taste is the place saying hello.

Bring layers, but not for warmth. The temperature is not the issue. The dimness is, and the dampness, and the way the air seems to thicken and thin without warning. A wool base layer you don't mind getting wet. A heavier mid-layer that can be shed. Something waterproof that you can pull up over your mouth when the smelter vapor rolls through. The vapor is not smoke, but your lungs will not initially know the difference. After three days they will, and you will find yourself breathing it in like the locals do, almost with affection.

Boots. Good ones, with soles you trust, because the steps between terraces are slick with moss that is older than the moss you have known, and the paving stones shift their patterns over the years, slowly, the way a glacier shifts, which is to say: the step you took yesterday may not be the step you take tomorrow. Walk the way you would walk on something alive. That is not a metaphor. The postmistress will nod at you if you walk this way. The nod is meaningful.

A small amount of strong, dark food you can rely on — chocolate, dried fruit, something with salt. The bread here takes a little longer to rise, which means everything you bake or buy will be slightly underdone by your home standards. This is not a flaw. It is just time being itself. Eat at the pace the town eats. Do not hurry your meals. The hurry will not save you time. Time here is not a thing that responds to hurrying.

Sleep will be strange. The dimness does not really become dark, so the body never quite gets the signal it expects. Bring a sleep mask. Use it for the first week. By the second week you will not need it, and you will find that you can sleep in a kind of soft gray that you previously could not have imagined was sleep. Some travelers report that their dreams change here. Some report they stop dreaming. Both are correct.

## The Mind

The most important thing to bring is a willingness to be wrong about your surroundings. Not in a dramatic way. In a quiet, continuous, almost affectionate way. The map will be a map, and the map will be wrong, and it will not be lying. The buildings will be stacked, and the stacking will not be quite what you think stacking means. The stone will be the color of a bruise after it has stopped hurting, and the bruise will not have a name, and the name will be one of those words that doesn't sit right in your mouth.

Do not try to make the place cohere into something you already understand. The town is not metaphor. It is not dream. It is not hallucination, though the line between the two will sometimes feel thin, especially after dark, especially in the upper shafts. It is a place. It is a real place, with real people in it, who will offer you tea and ask you about the lowlands and not press you if you cannot answer. They have hosted travelers before. They have hosted travelers who could not stop trying to explain the place. They are patient with this. The patience is not infinite.

Bring something to write with. Not a journal exactly, though a journal is fine. Something to take notes on. The place gives you things — small observations, half-phrases, the way the light bends around the slag-yard lanterns, the way the chimneys breathe. If you do not write them down they will not stay. They will be real while you are there, and they will become difficult to remember after you leave, the way a dream becomes difficult to remember. The locals do not have this problem because they are not leaving. The ledger the postmistress keeps is, I think, partly a way of keeping the place real. You may want one of your own.

The hum. I should mention the hum. The mountain makes a sound that is felt more than heard, and the sound will, after a day or so, begin to feel like your own pulse. Some travelers find this comforting. Some find it unbearable. Some find it both, and the both-ness is the most accurate response. If you stand in one of the older shafts, in the right place, you can feel it in your teeth. This is not dangerous. It is just the mountain. The miners will tell you, if you ask, that the mountain talks to them. Not in words. More in the way a clock talks to a room. You will understand what they mean after the second or third day. You will understand it has always been true.

## The Heart

The people are kind. This is not a small thing. In a place where the census is done by a clerk who is also, by most accounts, the same person as the mayor and the postmistress and the woman who runs the smelter and the man who keeps the goats and the goat, kindness is not a luxury. It is the structural material of the place, the way stone is the structural material of the buildings. Bring small gifts if you like. Cheese is appreciated. The thing in the upper mine workings also likes cheese, but you will not be meeting it on this visit, probably, and you should not seek it out. The miners leave offerings; this is their custom, not a tourist protocol. If you are given something — a flower, a cup of water, a piece of bread that did not quite rise — accept it. The acceptance is part of the exchange. Refusal is not a thing that is done here. It is not rude, exactly. It is just not legible.

The children. The children play in the vapor from the chimneys, and they have named the things in the shafts, and they are not afraid. This is not because the things are not dangerous. It may be because the things are not dangerous. It may be because the children are made of different material here, the way the lichen is, the way the stone is. Do not try to warn them. They are not in need of warning. They will look at you with eyes that are slightly too old for their faces, as children in mountain places often do, and they will ask you questions you cannot answer, and that is fine. That is part of how the place works.

The goats. The goats are mostly normal, except for their eyes, which glow in the dim light, and they will follow you on the terraces, and they will look at you with an expression that is hard to read but is not, I think, unfriendly. They are part of the town in a way that animals in lowland towns are not part of those towns. They are witnesses. The dogs are longer in the body than dogs should be, and they move with a kind of liquid grace, and you will not be able to tell if you are afraid of them or in love with them. The both-ness, again. The most accurate response.

## The Going

The rail down the mountain loops through the cloud for forty miles. Take the rail when you leave, not because the ascent was hard but because the descent is the place's last gesture to you, and it deserves to be received. The car will rock. The cloud will thin. The dimness will, by degrees, become something more like weather. You will, toward the end, see a strip of sky that is the wrong color, or the right color, or a color you have not seen in a while, and you will feel something in your chest that is either grief or relief or the long quiet space where those two things stop being different.

When you get to the lowlands, you will find that you have been gone longer than you thought. Or shorter. Or both. This is reported by every traveler. There is no way to verify it from the lowlands, so the report stands as the report stands.

Write things down while you are there. Accept what is offered. Do not try to explain the place to the people who live in it. Walk on the steps as if they are alive. Drink the water. Hold the ore up to the dim light and see, faintly, the other side of it than the side you are looking at. Sit with the postmistress if she will let you, and let her show you the ledger, which has been going for eleven years and has become something more than a ledger. Do not ask her what it is. She will tell you if she wants to. The telling, when it comes, will not be the whole story. The whole story is the place. The place is what it is, and it has been for longer than it hasn't been.

Bring a willingness to be changed in a small, specific way that you will not be able to name for a long time afterward, and may not be able to name ever. This is the real visa. Everything else is just luggage.
