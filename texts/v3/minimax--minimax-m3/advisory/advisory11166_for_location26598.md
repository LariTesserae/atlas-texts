# Preparation for the High Plateau

You are going to a place that has finished. I need to say that clearly, first, because most of what you know how to prepare for assumes a place that is still in the middle of something — still happening, still changing, still answerable to weather, hunger, distance, time. This plateau is not in the middle of anything. It has reached the end of a process so old that the process itself has been forgotten, and what remains is the residue of completion. Everything I am about to tell you is shaped by that fact.

## The first thing to understand

This place is not hostile. That is the trap. It is not dangerous in any way your body will recognize — there is no weather, no predators in the usual sense, no disease, no violence, no sudden fall. The temperature is even and cool, the air is still, the light is diffuse and kind. The plateau wants you to be comfortable. The plateau wants you to settle. What it wants, in fact, is for you to join it — to become part of its long, quiet equilibrium, the way the dust hangs and the lichen has stopped spreading and the cairns stand where they were placed ten thousand years ago and will stand there ten thousand years from now, or what passes for years here, because time passes strangely and you should not trust it.

So when I say "prepare," understand that I am not preparing you against the place. I am preparing you to remain yourself inside it.

## The body

**Water.** This is the single most important thing. There is no water on the plateau. There has not been water there for so long that even the memory of rain is gone from the stone. You are bringing every drop you will need for the entire visit, plus a reserve you will not think is reasonable but that you will be grateful for on the second day, when you realize you do not know how long you have been there and your thirst has become a fact you cannot negotiate with. Bring it in multiple containers, because if you lose one — and you may, given the broken geometry of the place, which I will get to — you do not want to lose all of it. I would suggest a primary vessel worn close to the body and a secondary sealed in your pack. Drink small amounts often. Do not ration based on the length of stay you planned. Ration based on the length of stay you might actually experience, which may be longer than you think. The diffuse light and the absence of exertion will deceive you into feeling less thirsty than you are. Trust the water, not your throat.

**Food.** Nothing here is food. I will say this clearly: do not attempt to eat the creatures, the lichen, or the mineral crusts in the shallow basins. The creatures are ambiguously alive and ambiguously mineral and will not behave as food should — I cannot promise you they are poison, but I can promise you they are not food, and that distinction matters. Bring rations that are calorie-dense and that you have eaten before. Hard bread, dried meat, pressed fat, nuts, whatever your body knows how to digest. The thin air and the cool temperature will increase your caloric needs — not dramatically, because you will not be exerting yourself, but noticeably. Eat even when you are not hungry. Hunger here is unreliable.

**Warmth.** The air is cool. It is also thin. You are likely to be at a real altitude, or something analogous to one, and your lungs will notice. Bring layers — more than you think you need. The temperature does not shift, which is a mercy, but it does not shift *upward*. You will not warm yourself by moving. Wool or equivalent. A hat. Something for the hands, because the still air will feel colder than it is when you stop moving. A sleeping layer if you intend to rest — and you must intend to rest, because the place will encourage wakefulness in a way that feels peaceful but is actually just the absence of any reason to sleep.

**The lungs.** Acclimate before you go, if you can. If you cannot, go slowly for the first hour. The air is thin and cool and absolutely still, and your body will register it as high altitude even if altitude is not the right word for what is happening. Breath shallow at first. Let your blood adjust. Do not run. There is nowhere to run to.

**The eyes.** There is no sun. There is no shadow in the usual sense — light comes from everywhere above, uniform and pale, and in some places shadows do not fall at all, or fall slightly to the left, or fall upward. Bring eye protection against the diffuse luminance if your eyes are sensitive — a light tint, not darkness. You will want to be able to see. But you will not want to be dazzled, because the broken geometry means your eyes are already doing too much work.

## The equipment

**Do not bring a map.** This sounds counterintuitive. Bring the means to navigate, but do not bring a map. The geometry of the plateau is broken. Two observers standing apart will not agree on the shape of the same outcrop, its size, or the number of stones in it. The horizon sits at the wrong distance for the world — closer than it should be, as if the plateau is the inside of a bowl rather than the outside of a sphere. A walk that should take an hour takes two, or half, or does not finish. If you draw a map, you will be drawing a map of a place that is not consistent with itself. Trust the map less than you trust your confusion.

What you bring instead:

- **A line.** A length of cord, strong, marked at regular intervals. A hundred meters is a good start. Unreel it as you walk. Do not rely on it for absolute distance, because even the cord may behave strangely here — the broken geometry may affect measurement — but it is more reliable than your eyes. If you must return to where you started, follow the cord back. Bring a second, smaller line for short-range marking.
- **A marker that does not depend on the stone.** A piece of chalk, a stub of soft pencil, something to mark the stone itself with a sign that is yours. The stone accepts marks. The cairns and walls suggest this. Mark your path discreetly — small signs, only you will recognize them — because the dust closes behind you and you will leave no footprints. But the marks you make on the stone will stay. They will not decay. Be sparing with them.
- **A weight and a cord.** A small plumb bob. If shadows lie, if light arrives without traveling, if distances are uncertain, gravity may be one of the few things that still behaves correctly here. Use the plumb to confirm vertical. Use the cord to triangulate yourself against outcrops you have already marked. If even the plumb lies — and I cannot promise it will not — then you are in worse trouble than equipment can solve, and you should sit very still and wait for the equilibrium to release you, if it will.
- **A light source.** Not because it is dark — the diffuse luminance is adequate — but because your own light, a small lamp you control, may behave more reliably than the ambient light. If the ambient light is broken, a local source may not be. Bring oil, not batteries, because you do not know how long you will be here and oil keeps. A small bronze lamp, a length of wick, a flask of oil sealed against the thin dry air.
- **A container for the dust.** Not to take it — to test it. If you sweep a handful of the hanging dust into a sealed jar and bring it home, you will be able to show someone what you saw. The dust hangs. It does not fall. You should understand this in your hand before you trust your eyes to it.
- **Writing materials.** A notebook, a hard pencil. You will want to record. You will want to remember. The place is dense with meaning, and the things you notice at the start of your visit will not be the things you notice at the end, because you will have become a different observer. Write early. Write often. Write even when you do not understand what you are writing.
- **A knife.** A real one. Not for the creatures — they cannot be harmed, or should not be — but for the stone, for the lichen, for prying up a slab to see what is beneath, for cutting your own markers, for the ordinary business of being a person in a place. The tools at the shelter were a stone axe head, a bone needle, and a bronze knife with a wooden handle that has gone to powder. Bring a knife with a handle that will not powder. Keep it on your body, not in your pack.

**What not to bring:**

- A compass. The magnetic field here is unreliable or absent, or possibly lies. Trust your feet, not a needle.
- A camera, unless you are certain it will work. The broken geometry may affect what is recorded. You may take a photograph of an outcrop and find, in the developed image, a different number of stones than the ones you counted. Bring the camera if you must, but trust your notebook more.
- A clock with a mechanical heart. Bring a watch, by all means, but do not trust it absolutely. Time passes strangely here. The watch may run correctly. Your sense of duration almost certainly will not.
- Expectations. Leave them at the threshold.

## The senses

I need to prepare you for what your senses will do, because they will not behave.

**Sight.** The light is even and comes from above without a source. There is no sun to orient you. Colors are reduced — bone, ash, dried milk, the faintest green-grey where the lichen has reached its limit and stopped. Your eyes will want to find contrast and will fail. Depth perception is unreliable. The horizon sits at the wrong distance. Outcrops appear closer than they are, or further, or shift between the two as you watch. The creatures, when you look at them directly, dissolve back into the mass — they are too numerous, too layered, too close together to be seen as individuals. If you focus on one, it loses definition. This is not a trick. It is the density of the place. Let your eyes soften. Look slightly to the side of what you want to see. Use peripheral vision. The creatures are easier to perceive as texture than as objects, and that is the correct way to perceive them.

**Sound.** Almost none. If you stand very still for a long time — and long is uncertain, here — you may hear a faint hiss, a faint creak, a faint rhythmic whisper. It is at the edge of perception. You will have to want to hear it. Do not strain. Strain produces nothing here. The sound, when it comes, is the sound of many tiny things moving at once, or the stone settling, or the hum of a place that has become something between alive and not. It does not change. If you listen for danger in it, you will not find danger. If you listen for company, you will find something that is not quite company. Listen the way you would listen to a held breath.

**Touch.** The stone is smooth in some places — wind-polished to a near-reflective surface — and broken in others, where ancient joints have given way. The dust is everywhere, and it hangs rather than falling, so you will not feel it settle on your skin the way dust settles elsewhere. You may feel it brush past you, slowly, in pale layers. The creatures do not touch you. They settle around you at a distance, if you sit, that feels respectful. They will not approach your skin. They will not climb you. They will not enter your clothes. This is one of the few rules that holds. Respect the distance they keep.

**Smell.** Almost nothing. Cool, dry, mineral. The air is too thin and too still to carry scent. Your own body will be the strongest smell in your vicinity. Be aware of this.

**Proprioception.** This is the one that will betray you. Your sense of where your body is in space, of how far you have walked, of which direction is forward — these will lie, gently and consistently, because the geometry is broken. You will feel that you have walked for an hour and it will have been two hours, or twenty minutes, or you will not be able to tell. You will turn to retrace your steps and find that the outcrop you were walking toward is now behind you, or beside you, or has changed shape. Trust the cord. Trust the marks. Do not trust the feeling in your legs.

## The mind

This is the preparation that matters most, and it is the one you cannot purchase or pack.

The plateau is in equilibrium. The creatures are in equilibrium. The lichen has reached its limit and stopped. The dust hangs. The cairns stand. The shelter is partly collapsed and will remain partly collapsed for as long as you can imagine, and for as long after that. Nothing is in the process of becoming. Everything is in the state of having become. If you sit down, the creatures settle around you at a respectful distance, and you become part of the equilibrium, and the place accepts you as it accepts itself, and nothing changes.

This is the seduction. It is not malicious. It is not even intentional. It is simply what the place is. The plateau has reached a state so complete that it has become absorbent. It does not attack you; it incorporates you. The way it incorporates you is by making you feel that there is nothing to do, nothing to fight, nothing to decide. The place is already decided. The question is whether you will be decided with it.

The previous inhabitants — if they were inhabitants, if the word fits — left a shelter, low stone walls, a partial roof of flat slabs. They had tools: a stone axe head, a bone needle, a bronze knife with a wooden handle that has gone to powder. The tools are the right size for hands. The hands are gone, or have become part of the place in some other way. I do not know what that other way is. I do not think you want to find out by participating in it.

So: **do not stop.** Not in the frantic sense. Move with intention. Walk with a goal, even a small one. Mark a stone, walk to the next outcrop, mark it, walk to the next. Maintain a project. Maintain a question. Carry your notebook and write in it often. The act of writing is an act of change, and change is the thing the plateau cannot metabolize. As long as you are changing — recording, observing, deciding, turning, walking — you are not part of the equilibrium. You are visiting it.

When you sit, sit briefly. When you rest, rest with the cord in your hand and the next step in your mind. Do not let the respectful distance the creatures keep around you become a comfort. It is not comfort. It is the plateau including you in its long pause.

## The conduct

**Walk softly.** Not because the creatures are fragile — I do not think they are fragile — but because the dust will close behind you anyway, and walking softly is the only way to hear the faint hiss at the edge of perception. If you walk heavily, you will not hear it.

**Do not touch the creatures.** They shift slightly when you approach. This is not fear. It is equilibrium rebalancing. If you touch one, you may become part of the rebalancing in a way that does not reverse. I cannot promise this. I cannot disprove it. Leave them where they hang.

**Do not move the stones of the cairns or the walls.** They are placed as they are placed. The plateau has reached its final form. The cairns mark something — the slow, deliberate hand of whoever placed them is the closest thing this place has to intention — and disturbing them is not disrespectful because the place does not respect or disrespect; it simply is. But disturbing them will not help you, and may include you in a way you did not intend.

**Visit the shelter.** It is in one place, low stone walls and a partial roof, tools scattered around it. Go. Look. Pick up the bronze knife if you can find it — the wooden handle will powder at your touch, and that powder is the only decay this place permits. See what was made here. See the size of the hands. Understand what the equilibrium did to them. Then leave.

**Do not try to map it.** I said this above. I will say it again. The geometry is broken. Distances lie. Outcrops change shape between observers and between observations. A map drawn here will be a map of one moment of one walk from one position, and it will not transfer. Use the cord. Use the marks. Do not waste time trying to make the place legible in the way that places are usually legible.

**Listen.** The sound at the edge of perception is real. It is faint, and you have to want to hear it. Stand still for a long time. Let your breath slow. The hiss, the creak, the rhythmic whisper — it is the place being what it is, and being what it is is the only activity the place permits. Listen to it the way you would listen to a sleeping animal. Do not wake it.

**Do not stay long.** I cannot tell you how long is long, because time passes strangely here, and your sense of duration will lie. Bring enough water for the stay you planned, and then the reserve, and then plan to leave before you would have used the reserve. The plateau is patient. It has been patient for longer than mountains. It can outlast you. Do not test whether it can include you. Leave while you are still yourself.

## Returning

The way back may not be the way you came. The geometry is broken. Two observers do not agree. A walk that should take an hour takes two or half. If you have marked your path — and you must have marked your path — follow your marks back. If your marks are gone, follow the cord. If the cord is gone, find the shelter, because the shelter is the only fixed structure and is more likely than anything else to be where it was. From the shelter, orient yourself by the cairns and the low walls, even if their shapes seem to shift. Walk outward in straight lines from fixed points. Be prepared for the walk to take longer than it should, or shorter. Be prepared for the horizon to be in the wrong place.

When you leave the plateau — when you step off it, or when the multiverse transition takes you elsewhere — you will not leave footprints, because you did not leave footprints while you were on it. The dust closed behind you as you walked. The plateau will not remember your passage in the way places usually remember. But you will remember it. The marks on your notebook will remember it. The water you drank and did not finish will remember it. The faint hiss will sit at the edge of your hearing for days afterward, and you will have to want to hear it to know it is there.

## The single most important thing

Maintain the intention to leave. This is not a place you stay. This is a place you visit. The plateau will accept you if you let it. The plateau has accepted everything that has come to it, including the hands that made the bone needle and the bronze knife, and the hands are gone. You are going to walk across a place that has finished, and you are going to walk back off it while you are still walking, still changing, still in the process of becoming. Bring everything you need. Bring the cord and the chalk and the lamp and the water and the knife and the notebook. Bring the will to keep moving.

The plateau will not harm you. It will simply wait, the way it has waited, until you stop. Do not stop.

Go carefully.
