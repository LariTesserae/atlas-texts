# Preparing for the Stone Confluence at the Tindal Pass

There is a particular kind of place that gets into the bones of a person, and this is one of them. Reading the description, what comes through clearly is that this is not a destination you visit lightly — it is a place that requires a different kind of preparation than a beach holiday or a city break. The traveler who arrives here unprepared will be miserable for the first three days and may not understand what they are seeing for the rest of their stay. The traveler who arrives prepared will have one of the more memorable experiences available to a multiverse hopper.

Let me work through this systematically, starting with what the place actually is, then what the body needs, then what the mind and the spirit need.

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single thing, you need to understand the basic facts of the location. This is a high-altitude settlement — somewhere in the alpine zone, probably 2,500 to 3,500 meters equivalent based on the description of the air being thin enough to be an effort with each breath. It is permanently wet. It is built on a geological fault line. The light is extreme — dazzling for two hours around sunrise and sunset, dark as a cellar in between. The wind is violent and unpredictable. The place is inhabited by roughly two thousand people who are competent, reserved, and who do not owe you anything.

The last point matters. This is not a tourist economy. The town exists because of the springs and the wool trade. You are coming as a guest of a place that has been receiving occasional outsiders for centuries and has developed a clear etiquette for doing so. The etiquette is: small at first, then watched, then, if you are lucky, slowly admitted.

## The Body: Physical Preparation

**Acclimatization is the first and most important thing.** The description says every breath is "a small effort." That is not a poetic exaggeration — it is the literal experience of thin air. If you are coming from a low-altitude universe, or even just a sea-level one, you should plan at least two to three days of staged ascent before reaching the pass. Spend time at intermediate elevations. Walk slowly. Drink more water than you think reasonable. Eat light, salty food. Avoid alcohol entirely for the first 48 hours. The townspeople are adapted; you are not, and the thinness of the air will punish you if you try to pretend otherwise.

The most common mistake visitors make is treating the scree approach as a quick hike. Three miles of unstable, ringing stone at altitude is not a casual walk. It is a slow, deliberate, breath-by-breath negotiation with the mountain. Allow half a day minimum. Start at first light. Wear boots with stiff soles and good ankle support — the stones shift underfoot and the locals apparently choose specific stones to step on. You won't know which ones, so plan to step on them all and trust your ankles.

**Clothing is critical, and the locals' solution is the right one: layered wool.** Avoid cotton — it will stay wet forever and will slowly drain your body heat. Avoid synthetics that don't breathe — you'll stew in your own moisture inside an hour. Wool, wool, wool. The herders' heavy wet wool is not a fashion statement; it is engineering. Wear it.

You need layers for at least four temperature zones: the cold dry air of the pass (cold but manageable), the wet interior of the town (cold and damp, the worst combination), the sunlit open squares (suddenly warm when the sun hits), and the evening when the temperature drops again as the sun goes behind the western ridge. A good wool base layer, a wool mid-layer, a weatherproof but breathable outer shell, and a felted wool hat.

**Boots need to be waterproof but not rubber.** Rubber boots will keep water out but will not breathe, and your feet will be soaked in sweat within two hours. Leather boots, well-oiled, with wool socks, are the right answer. Accept that your boots will darken with moisture and stay that way for the duration of your visit. Bring a second pair to rotate if you can; otherwise, stuff your boots with dry wool at night.

**Gloves.** Two pairs. One thin wool pair for dexterity, one heavier pair for the wind. The wind on the high scree is serious, and you will lose heat from your hands faster than anywhere else.

**A wide-brimmed or peaked hat.** The light is described as having "opinions." Sun protection at altitude is no joke, and the sun here is reflected off wet stone from every direction, including up. Bring something that shades your eyes and the back of your neck.

**Eye protection.** Real sunglasses, not fashion ones. The glare off wet stone and moving water in thin air can cause snow blindness equivalent even when there is no snow on the ground. The description mentions mica in the stone flaring — that is the kind of light that damages eyes over hours.

**Lip balm and a good skin salve.** The air is wet but also thin and mineral-laden. It will chap your lips and crack the skin of your hands within a day. Bring something waxy, not watery. The herders presumably have their own solutions; ask, but don't be surprised if the answer is animal fat.

## The Mind: Psychological Preparation

This is, I think, the more important preparation, and the one most travelers will neglect.

**You need to be prepared to be quiet.** The town is quiet in a way that is not an absence of sound but a kind of acoustic pressure. The water sounds cancel rather than add. After a day you stop hearing them. Then you start hearing the other things — the bell, the creak of the wool-press, the wind in the rock, the low piping from the moss. You need to be the kind of person who can sit still and listen without filling the silence with their own noise. If you are someone who talks to fill dead air, you will find this place uncomfortable in ways you won't be able to articulate.

**You need to be prepared to be watched.** The townspeople are not unfriendly, but they are not yet sure of you. The description is very specific about the timeline: first day, cold observation; second day, a bowl of something hot and bitter; third day, questions about where you sleep. If you want a different timeline — if you want to befriend people faster — you will need to bring a specific skill or trade that they value. Wool-dyeing knowledge, perhaps, or stonemasonry, or something related to the geology of the spring line. Or you simply accept the timeline. I would recommend the latter. Trying to rush the trust of people who live on a fault line is unwise.

**You need to be prepared for the breathing of the mountain.** This is the thing that is not spoken of, and I want to be very clear about it because I think it is the part of the visit most likely to disturb unprepared travelers. The mountain breathes. The cracks in the street open. The water brightens. The Coldwell hisses higher. The Hollowspring pulses when it wasn't going to. Things in the built environment shift — doors that opened one way now open the other, steps change depth.

This is not a metaphor. This is reported as observed phenomenon.

If you are the kind of person who experiences shifts in physical reality as threatening, you should not come to this place. If you are the kind of person who can sit through a minute of low-grade geological distress and emerge on the other side of it curious rather than panicked, you will be fine. The herders have lived with it for centuries. They stop. They watch. They exhale. Then they continue.

The bracket above the tanner's door is the part I keep returning to. A wave with something in it. Something that looks, if you look long enough, like an eye. The text tells us that no one looks long. I would suggest respecting that.

**You need to be prepared for the light.** Not just the physical glare, but the way the light structures the day. Morning is for outside, where the sun has dried the stones. Midday is for inside, where the covered ways and interior courts give relief from the dazzle. Evening is again for outside, and the description of the town "crying light" at the end of the day is one of the most haunting lines in the whole text. You should plan to be outside at both ends of the day and inside during the middle. This is not a suggestion; it is the rhythm of the place, and to live against the rhythm is to be constantly uncomfortable.

## The Spirit: How to Behave When You Get There

The herders are described as "not numerous, not rich, very competent." They know every spring, every leak, every slow bulge in every wall. They have an agreement with the white grazers that no one has ever written down. They have a game with the children at the Hollowspring that involves being thrown bodily into the market square and laughing.

You are walking into a place where the social fabric is woven very tightly, and where the relationship between the inhabitants and the place itself is intimate and long-standing. Your job, as a visitor, is to be small. Small, observant, slow to speak, slow to ask, willing to be wrong about which stones to walk on, willing to wait three days for a conversation.

**Do not photograph without asking.** I don't know whether cameras exist in your travel kit or in this world, but if they do: ask. The herders do not smile at strangers. They are not yet sure of you. Pointing a device at them is not a good way to become sure-of-you-ed.

**Do not touch the carvings on the brackets** unless explicitly invited. They are not yours. They are older than the language currently spoken here.

**Do not approach the Coldwell basin alone.** Children are not allowed there alone and they come anyway, which suggests the danger is real. The water is not freezing but not warm; you stop being sure you have feet. I would interpret that as a slow-onset numbness and possibly mild hypothermia if you stay too long. Stay on the edge. Stay with someone. Do not climb on the wet stones around it.

**Do not try to stand in the dome of the Hollowspring.** The children do this as a game and are thrown into the market square, which sounds fun until you realize that being thrown by a column of rising water is a real physical event. Watch from the edge. If you are invited to participate by a child, you are probably being tested.

**Do not hunt the white grazers.** They have an agreement. The agreement is unwritten. The agreement has held. Do not be the person who breaks it.

**Do not kill a splitwing.** They are not afraid of people because people do not threaten them. The silence of the town when one flies over is a kind of respect, or a kind of recognition, and you should participate in it.

**If the mountain breathes while you are there: stop. Watch. Exhale. Continue.**

## What to Bring, Specifically

A short list of practical items:

- Wool base layers, two sets minimum so you can rotate
- Wool socks, at least four pairs
- Leather boots, broken in, well-oiled
- A wide-brimmed hat or peaked cap
- Real sunglasses
- Wool hat and gloves, two pairs each
- A weatherproof but breathable outer shell, both jacket and trousers
- A heavy wool sweater or two
- A scarf or neck gaiter that can be pulled up over the face in the wind
- A small daypack for the scree approach
- Water bottles, two, with a liter capacity each — you will drink more than you expect at altitude
- High-energy food for the approach: dried fruit, nuts, hard cheese, dark chocolate, flatbread
- A basic first aid kit: blister treatment, painkillers, anti-diarrheal, water purification tablets
- A good headlamp, with spare batteries — the dark interior courts are dark
- A notebook and pencil, not a phone, if you want to take notes
- A small gift, if you have one to give: salt is mentioned as a trade good and is probably appreciated; good steel for tools would be valuable; dyes or dyeing knowledge would be a graceful gift given the importance of the wool trade

## What to Expect, Emotionally

The first day will be overwhelming. The altitude, the wet, the cold, the dazzle of the light, the strangeness of the architecture, the language you cannot understand, the food you are not yet used to — all of it will press in on you. You will probably want to leave by evening.

The second day will be better. The altitude will have begun to acclimate. The rhythm of the day will start to make sense. Someone will offer you a bowl of something hot and bitter. Drink it. It is probably a kind of tea brewed from local herbs and is doing you more good than you realize.

The third day is when the place starts to become real to you. You will be asked which side of the fault you sleep on at home. The question is not about geography; it is a way of locating you in the world. Answer honestly.

Somewhere between the third and the fifth day, depending on how long you stay, you will have the experience of sitting on the edge of the Coldwell basin for an hour, or watching the Hollowspring pulse in the market square, or hearing the cracked bell struck once at an hour you cannot name, and you will understand why the herders stay. The town is, in its way, complete. It has its springs, its springs have their names, its names are kept the way one keeps a tooth — out of the mouth, in the hand, not thrown away. The people are small inside it, and willing to be small. The mountain is settling into itself, very slowly, very quietly, very deliberately, and the town is settling with it.

You will not be the same when you leave.

## A Final Note

The text ends with a sequence: the spring line is in the crack, the crack is in the mountain, the mountain is in the weather, the weather is in the light, and the light is in your eyes all the way home. Read that sequence carefully. It is not a metaphor. It is a description of what will happen to you on the way down the scree, with the town behind you and the wind in the rock and the cracked bell in your ears. The place will be in your eyes. For days, maybe weeks, you will see the wet walls and the long horizontal light and the way the water catches the sun. You will hear the water sounds that cancel rather than add.

This is not a bad thing. It is the place's gift to you, given in the way the place gives gifts: without comment, without ceremony, without your consent, and in such a way that you cannot quite put it down.

Bring a good scarf. Bring wool. Bring patience. Bring a willingness to be small.

The rest will follow.
