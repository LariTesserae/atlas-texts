# Preparing for the Drowned Yard

## Understanding Where You Are Going

Before anything else, you have to accept that this is not a wreck, not a reef, and not a place where you can take your mask off. The Drowned Yard is a working industrial facility the size of a city district, sitting at depth. The "yard" is real, with walls the size of blocks arranged in a grid that extends until the water itself becomes too thick to see through. The surface exists — it is far above you, so far that you cannot see it, only the daylight it sends. The whole volume between you and that surface is filled with engineered water moving through pipes and channels, past concrete and steel, around the working points marked by pale yellow lamps.

This is someone's workplace. People are doing jobs here. There is a shift structure. The machines have not stopped since they were turned on. You are visiting a place that is, in a very real sense, still humming from its own operation.

Treat that fact as the central fact, and the rest of your preparation will follow.

---

## The Hardest Part Is Not the Water

Most visitors will arrive worried about drowning. That's the right worry, but it is not the dominant one.

The dominant thing about the Yard is the *scale and the sensory envelope*. The walls are large. The distances are large. The water is the medium; you move through it the way a bird moves through air around the columns of a ruin. Once your eyes adjust, you will notice that most of what is in front of you is *nothing*. Open water. Open water in every direction, broken up by enormous flat surfaces that go up into the bright zone and down into the dimness. Your brain will not handle this well at first. It wants to put you in a small, bounded space. You will instead be a mote in a hall.

On top of that, the noise. The Yard is loud in a way that does not stop. There is a deep pump-throb you feel in your ribcage. There is a higher turbine whine from the electrical plant, which your ears will localize without your permission. There is the constant hiss and gurgle of water past edges and through grates. Sometimes there is the clatter of a loose piece of hardware rattling in the current somewhere out of sight. You will hear your own breathing in the regulator, and you will see your own bubbles climbing away from you in silver ropes. None of this stops. After a few hours, you stop noticing it. Before that, it will work on you.

Then there is the current. The Yard is pumped. The water has direction and force. If you stop kicking, you will move. The worker you are traveling with will drift; so will you. This is not dangerous in itself if you know where you are, but it is unsettling, because you will feel the world pushing on you in a way the surface world does not.

## Physical Preparation

You should not arrive unfit. This is not a casual snorkel. You will be carrying your own weight in water plus the weight of your suit, working against or with a current, at a depth where the surface is a memory.

You should be a competent, calm swimmer before you go. You do not need to be an athlete. You need to be able to relax underwater, to control your breathing when it wants to panic, and to move deliberately when the water wants to move you.

Ears equalize on descent. Sinuses equalize. If you have any congestion, any cold, any recent diving injury, any reason your middle ear might not clear — wait. The Yard will be there next week.

Hydrate well in the days before. Avoid alcohol. Avoid heavy meals the morning of descent. The suit will press on your abdomen and you will be breathing dense gas at depth; both of those work against a full stomach.

Learn to breathe slowly and from the diaphragm before you go. The regulator will tell you when you are panting, because it will start to work against you. Slow, long, deliberate breaths. A panicked snorkeler at the surface is annoying. A panicked snorkeler in the Yard, with walls the size of buildings around them and a current pushing them somewhere, is a problem for the people who have to come get you.

## Equipment You Will Be Issued

The Yard is not a place to bring your own gear unless you are a certified saturation diver with your own kit. You will be fitted out by people who work here every day. Expect the following, and accept it without negotiation:

- A helmet or full-face mask with communication. You want comms. The Yard is loud and you cannot shout through a regulator. Your guide will be on the other end of a wire, and you will be grateful for it.
- A dry suit or hot-water suit for thermal protection. The water is sixty degrees, which sounds mild until you remember you will be in it for hours and that heat leaves a body underwater about twenty-five times faster than in air. You will be cold in places the suit does not cover well — backs of the hands, the bridge of the nose, the small of the back if the suit gaps. Plan for that.
- A proper harness with a working-line attachment. You tie into the Yard. The current is real and you do not free-float through an industrial site. This is not a reef; this is a plant. You are a visitor in a plant.
- A bail-out cylinder, redundant gas, and a knife or line-cutter. You will be told why.
- Lights. Your own, on a Goodman handle or helmet mount. The lamp-cones of the Yard fixtures only reach a few dozen meters. Beyond them, you rely on daylight, and daylight fades with depth and with distance from the surface.

Do not bring jewelry. Do not bring loose straps. Do not bring anything that can catch on a pipe bracket or a cable run. The Yard is full of things to snag on, and a snag in a current at depth is the kind of problem that ends with someone else's hands.

## Mental and Sensory Preparation

The Yard does something to time. There is no horizon. There is no sky. There is no direct sun. Your reference frame is the walls, the lamp-cones, the plumes of warm discharge that shimmer the light, and the fish moving past you in their schools. Without a watch and a plan, you will lose track of how long you have been down. Decide with your guide how long the visit is, and stick to it. Decompression obligations do not negotiate.

Practice in advance being bored. The Yard is not visually overwhelming. It is vast and repetitive. Most of what you see, after the first ten minutes, is more of what you have already seen — wall, water, wall, water, a pipe run, a school of fish turning in unison, a plume of warm water bending the light. If you arrive expecting constant revelation, you will exhaust yourself. If you arrive expecting long quiet stretches punctuated by occasional things, you will do better.

Get used to the idea that you will not see the whole place. You cannot. The grid goes to the haze. You will see a few walls, a few channels, maybe one of the larger working intersections. Accept that. Do not try to "explore."

## What Can Hurt You Here

Be honest about this.

**The current.** If you let go of the working line in the wrong channel, you will be taken somewhere you did not intend to go. Stay tied in. Move with the structure, not against the water.

**The machinery.** Pipes and cables are not decoration. Water moves through them at pressure. Some of the warm plumes you see are process discharge — heat, sometimes chemistry. Don't swim into a plume to see what it is. Don't put your face near a grate. Your guide will tell you which ones are safe to approach and which are not. Believe them.

**The growth.** Sparse as it is, do not touch it. It is the first chapter of biology on a substrate that has only just been put down. The people who run the Yard care about its colonization. The fish care about it. Touching it is the equivalent of picking a flower in someone's garden and also, possibly, getting cut on whatever it is anchored to.

**The large things at the edge of sight.** You may see one. The worker you are with has seen two. They are not aggressive. They have the unhurried pace of something that knows it has no enemies here. Stay near your guide. Stay near the lights. Do not chase. They have made the structure their home; you are the visitor.

**Your own air consumption.** You will breathe harder than you think you are breathing. Excitement, cold, the effort of finning in a current — all of it raises consumption. Watch your gauge. Watch your guide's gauge. Turn the dive when the gas says turn the dive, not when your curiosity says turn the dive.

**The ascent.** Do not, under any circumstances, decide you have had enough and bolt for the surface. The Yard is at depth. You will have a stop, possibly several. Your guide will manage the ascent. Your job is to breathe and to keep your rate of ascent slower than you think you need to. If something goes wrong underwater, the answer is to stop, breathe, signal your guide, and follow instructions. The answer is never to go up fast.

## Etiquette

You are in someone's workplace and, increasingly, someone's home — the fish, the growth, the slow dark shapes. A few rules:

- Move slowly. Fast movement stirs sediment that takes a long time to settle out of a pumped water column and obscures things for everyone downstream.
- Do not block a working point. The lamp-cones mark places where someone is meant to be doing something. If a worker is at one, swim around it. Wait if you have to.
- Do not hold tools or touch valves. You are not qualified. If you see something interesting, point at it. Your guide will decide whether you can approach.
- Keep your bubbles off other people. A face full of another diver's exhaust is miserable, especially at depth where the gas feels thicker.
- Do not chase the fish. They will come around you anyway. They part around you and re-form behind you, like a stream around a stone. Watch them do that; it is one of the better things to watch down here.

## What to Actually Do With Your Visit

Once you are down and your eyes have adjusted, you will find that the Yard rewards a particular kind of attention. It is not a place for adrenaline. It is a place for noticing.

Notice the way the daylight falls in columns that shift with the surface chop far above you. Notice the seams between the concrete panels, the ghost of the formwork boards in the casting. Notice, where a piece of facing has broken away, the steel reinforcement inside — bright at the cut, rust-orange, fresh-looking, as if the wall had been built yesterday. It may have been.

Notice the fish. They are abundant and they keep their distance without fleeing. Watch a school turn past a distant wall. Watch one part around you. Notice that the larger, darker shapes do not turn at all.

Notice the plumes. The slight shimmer where machine-heated water meets the cool ambient is its own weather inside the larger weather of the Yard. The fish follow the plumes. Something is being processed somewhere. The Yard is not a monument; it is a working thing.

Notice the sound changing as you move between walls. The pump-throb stays with you, but the higher whine of the electrical plant shifts in your head as you change direction. You can localize the plant by ear alone, the way you can localize a highway by the way its noise sits in one side of your skull.

Notice, if you are very lucky, a lamp-cone in the distance that is brighter than the others, or one that has gone out. Notice that the lamps mark a kind of address system. You are at the intersection of grid lines you cannot see, in a volume of water, and the only proof that you are somewhere specific is which pale yellow cone is nearest you.

If you can do this — if you can be still inside the suit, and quiet in your breathing, and present to the hum and the hiss and the green-blue light — the Yard will give you something. Not excitement. Not spectacle. Something rarer. The feeling of being inside a working thing that does not need you to be impressed by it.

## Getting Out

When the time comes, your guide will bring you to the ascent line. You will stop, breathe, and begin to come up. The walls will fall away beneath you. The current will release you. The light will change — the green-blue daylight brightening toward something almost white as you approach the surface you have not seen in hours.

You will break into air. The noise will change instantly and dramatically. The pump-throb will cut off. The hiss will vanish. Your own breathing will sound different — louder, rougher, full of the moisture of your exhalation.

The first breath of surface air will taste like nothing you have ever breathed. It will taste like a room. Like the inside of a building. Like the absence of water.

That is when you will know the Yard took you somewhere, even if you only spent an afternoon inside it.

Take off your gear slowly. Drink water. Eat something. Sit still for a while. Do not talk about it yet, if you do not want to.

The Yard will be there. It is working. It is always working. The pumps have not stopped. The fish are still turning past the walls. The slow dark shapes are still moving at the edge of sight.

You were a visitor. You were in it for a shift. That is enough.
