# Preparing for the Bason Floor

## Reading the Place First

Before thinking about what to pack, I want to sit with what this place actually *is*, because it changes how I think about everything else.

This is not a hostile environment in the way a desert or a storm is hostile. Nothing here is trying to harm me. Nothing is even aware of me. The cold is not a wind cutting me — it is a state the rock has been in for a billion years, and it will outlast the conversation I had about whether to come. The turbulence is not weather — it is the slow respiration of a basin that has been breathing longer than mammals. The silence is not empty — it is the consequence of moving so much mass so slowly that sound itself gives up.

This is a place that will simply *not register me*. I have to make peace with that before I pack a single bag, because my gear — my preparation — has to compensate for a place that will not compensate for me.

The two things that define it, for me, are:
1. The cold is structural, not meteorological. It is in the rock. It will outlast my heat source unless my heat source is very serious.
2. The silence and the gray are not absences. They are presences. I will be living inside an absence that has its own weight.

## The Body

**Core temperature is the entire problem.** Everything else is secondary. The water is not freezing — it is well above 0°C, probably 2–4°C — but it is *thermally hungry*. It wants my warmth with the patience of something that has been wanting warmth for a billion years. Wet-cold at this temperature kills in hours, not minutes, which is worse, because it feels almost bearable at first.

I would prepare for cold-water diving at the serious end: a drysuit with thick undergarments, but I would not trust a standard diving drysuit for an extended visit because the insulation is designed for hours, not days. For a longer stay I would want an actively heated dive suit — the kind used for deep commercial work — with redundant heating elements and a primary battery that can be recharged. I would carry two complete heating systems. I would bring a third battery that I never used unless the first two failed. The rule on the Bason Floor is: if you have one of something critical, you have none.

I would train in the suit beforehand. Not in a pool. In cold water. I would want my body to know the suit, to know the slow creep of chill on the inside of my forearms when the suit works less well than I hoped. I would want to have felt the particular type of cold that is below notice at first and then everywhere at once, so that when it arrives I don't mistake its arrival for an emergency.

**Pressure is real but probably not the dominant issue.** A basin floor implies depth, but the description does not suggest the absolute abyss. I would plan for 200–600 meters of seawater equivalent, which means a mixed-gas supply, decompression planning, and the awareness that any technical mistake is permanent. I would plan my bottom time and deco so conservatively that my future self, lying on the shell-pavement with all the time in the world, does not regret my past self's optimism.

**Breathing** — I would bring a rebreather, not open-circuit. A rebreather because the silence here matters. I do not want to be the loudest thing in this basin. An open-circuit scuba system would broadcast every exhaled bubble up through the water column, and I do not want to leave that signature here. The rebreather also gives me the oxygen efficiency to stay long enough to understand what I am looking at instead of grabbing and leaving.

I would bring a backup rebreather. I would bring a bail-out open-circuit with enough gas for a controlled ascent even from maximum depth. I would test every regulator and every loop on the surface until the act of breathing through them was as unconscious as breathing through my own throat.

**The silt is the second body problem.** The basin description makes clear that the floor is a thick suspension of fine carbonate — "a thickness you could almost drink." Any thrashing, any uncontrolled movement, will lift a plume that will reduce my visibility to zero and stay reduced for an hour. My preparation has to assume that I will be functionally blind for long stretches.

This means: I move slowly, I touch the floor as little as possible, I do not use my hands as fists or paddles. I bring a simple rope-and-compass navigation system so I can find my way back to a marked entry point without sight. I practice moving by feel on the floor beforehand, in training, on similar terrain.

## The Senses

**Light.** I cannot count on my eyes. I would bring lights, but I would not bring *much* light, because bright artificial light in a place this dark will flatten what little depth-perception the gray affords and will attract the few mobile creatures in the same way a bonfire attracts moths — disorienting them, probably attracting the long slow shadow at the edge of perception, and certainly destroying the slow visual adjustment that the gray itself is trying to give me.

I would bring one low-power, red-shifted primary light, for navigation and for examining the tools up close. I would bring a small UV light to look at the shells — bioluminescence and phosphorescence are more common in deep-sea fauna than we sometimes credit, and a UV lamp will cause certain shell-bound organisms to fluoresce in ways that reveal patterns not visible in white light. I would use the UV sparingly and never shine it directly into the silt, only at specific objects.

I would spend most of my visit in the gray, letting my eyes adjust, looking for the pale thing darting between the humps and the slow shape that does not move like a shadow.

**Sound.** I would not bring a sonar. Sonar in a basin like this would tell me the shape of the humps and the density of the floor, which I do not actually need to know — I would learn it more truthfully by touch — and the active pings would be the loudest thing the basin has heard in a very long time. I would bring a hydrophone, passive, to *listen* to the place. The low sub-audible moan of current passing the humps may, given enough time and the right equipment, become something I can record and analyze later. The faint ticking of shells cracking would, if I recorded enough of them, give me a real clock — the only clock, as the description says. I would want that recording when I came back up.

**Touch.** This is the sense I am actually here for. I would bring my hands. I would bring the ability to move them slowly along the polished flanks of the humps and across the shell-pavement without disturbing it. The chert hand-axe, the wedge of volcanic glass, the threaded bone needle — these are the things I am here for, and they have survived because everything around them moved slowly and stopped when it had to. I would treat them the same way. I would pick up the chert axe once, feel the flake-scars, feel the edge that held something once, and set it back in the exact orientation I found it. I would not pocket anything. The basin is not a museum and I am not a curator, but a place this old has earned the respect of being left as it was.

I would bring a small notebook and a graphite pencil — graphite will write in this humidity, ink will not, and any electronic recording will be subject to the cold. I would write down what I touched and where I left it, so that the next visitor can find the things I found.

## The Mind

This is the part I cannot buy in a store.

The basin is the kind of place that does not punish a hurried mind. It does something worse. It lets a hurried mind have its hurry, and then the hurry turns out, three hours in, to have been completely empty. The pale thing darted and was gone. The slow shadow is still where it was. The chert axe is still sharp. The shell I was sure I would pick up on the way back is now under an inch of silt that I cannot disturb without ruining the visibility for the rest of my visit.

I would prepare for stillness the way I prepare for cold. I would practice sitting in a featureless room for an hour without checking the time. I would practice *wanting nothing* in a dark room. The skill of receiving the basin without reaching for it is the only skill I cannot fake when I get there.

I would not bring company. A second diver would double the silt-stirring, double the bubble-noise, double the heat-loss, and halve the silence. I would come alone, with a surface team above, and the surface team would know — explicitly, in writing, with a documented chain of command — that they are not to send anyone down after me except on my explicit recall signal. The basin does not want a rescue mission. A rescue mission in a place like this is just another way of adding to the silt.

## What I Bring, What I Leave

What I bring:
- A serious, redundant, dry-insulated, heated diving system with mixed gas and full deco plan.
- A rebreather and a backup rebreather and a bail-out.
- One low-power red primary light, one UV examination light, one hydrophone.
- A graphite notebook and a spare pencil.
- A simple rope and compass for moving by feel.
- Enough patience for a longer stay than I think I want.

What I leave:
- The chert axe. The bone needle. The volcanic glass wedge. In their places.
- The shell-pavement, mostly as I found it. I might shift my weight onto a few shells near where I kneel; that is the only disturbance I will permit myself, and I will note it.
- The pale thing and its single black eye. The slow shadow at the edge of perception.
- My idea that I will understand this place. I will leave that above, on the surface, where it belongs.

What I take with me, when I come back up:
- The sound of a shell cracking, recorded.
- The exact position, in a small sketch, of the hand-axe.
- A new understanding of the word *preserved*, which on the Bason Floor turns out to mean something quite different from *kept*.

I am not a fit person for this place. Few people are. But if I were to go, I would go small, quiet, patient, well-lit only in the places the gray cannot go, and willing to leave the chert in the silt for the next visitor, who will also not be the one who placed it.

The basin will be here. It will not have noticed me. That is the part I am preparing for.
