# Preparing for the Plateau

There is no point pretending this is a trip you can take lightly. The plateau is not hostile in the way that mountains are hostile, or oceans, or deserts. It is hostile in the way a thing that has never heard of you is hostile: with complete indifference, and the patience to outlast your entire visit by a factor of geological time. Your preparation has to account for that. Most of what you bring will be for your own comfort and survival, because the plateau is not going to help.

## The Air

This is the first thing. The air is thin in the way that the word "thin" was invented to describe. It is not just that there is less of it; it is that there is so little of it that it has stopped behaving like a gas and started behaving like something else — a slow liquid, the description says, and that is the kind of poetic exaggeration that turns out to be accurate. Your lungs will not be able to extract enough oxygen from it to keep you upright. You will be functional for perhaps ninety seconds before you notice the world graying out at the edges, and after that you will have perhaps another two minutes before you go down.

**Bring an oxygen supply.** A small cylinder, a regulator, a mask — the standard mountaineering kit for altitudes well above Everest, except that even Everest has more air than this. You should assume you will need supplemental oxygen for the entire visit. The mask should seal well against your face because the air is cold and dry and you will lose moisture fast. If you can rig a full pressure garment, do — the description is ambiguous about whether you can stand on the platforms without your blood boiling, and the safe assumption is that you cannot. If you cannot rig a full pressure garment, at minimum bring a mask and accept that you may swell in places, and that small cuts may bleed more freely than you expect.

Do not remove your mask to speak, to vomit, to wipe your face, to look at something with both eyes, to take a photograph, because you will have a strong urge to do all of these things and none of them are worth dying for.

## The Cold

The cold here does not bite. It does not whistle around your collar or find the gap between your glove and your sleeve. It simply *sits*. It is a cold that has been cold for a long time and is not negotiating. On the platforms, in the lower strata, near the running meltwater, you will be at or just below freezing most of the time. In the upper constructions, in the open air, in the parts where the wind moves (and there is wind, slow and patient and from nowhere), you will be substantially colder than that.

**Bring serious insulation.** A heavy parka, insulated trousers, sealed boots, gloves, a hat, layers you can add or shed. The cold will not chill you the way a mountain wind chills you — it will simply stay cold against you until your body heat runs out. Wool or modern synthetic equivalents. Do not rely on cotton. Bring a spare set of everything in a sealed bag because your first set will be wet.

**Bring waterproofing.** This is not because of rain. It is because of the condensation. Every surface here weeps. The rock weeps long pale seams down its face; the structures run with thin films of water; the undersides of bridges drip in curtains. Anything you put on a surface will be wet within minutes. Anything you take out of a bag in this air will pick up a fine film of condensation almost immediately. Sealed bags. Dry bags. Zip locks for anything that cannot get wet — electronics, food, spare clothing.

## The Light

The light is not light. It is the absence of darker. It is a grey pearl-shimmer that rests on surfaces like soot. After an hour your eyes will adjust, and you will be able to see quite a lot — more than you expect. But until they adjust, you will be functionally blind.

**Bring a low-intensity light source.** Red or amber is best because it preserves your dark adaptation. A bright white light will reveal the terrain but will also flatten the strange depth of the grey-pearl dark and will reset your eyes. If you have to use white light, do it briefly, and accept that you will need another twenty minutes for your eyes to readjust. Do not use a search-sweeping motion; the fauna are adapted to this darkness and bright moving light may attract them in numbers you do not want.

**Bring a marker for your path.** The walkways branch like capillaries. You will get lost. Bring something you can leave behind — reflective tape, chemical light sticks, anything — and use it to mark your turns. You will think you can remember. You cannot. The walkways all look the same after an hour. The capillary branching is fractal in a way that makes every junction feel like the one you just came from.

## The Fauna

You will hear them before you understand you are hearing them. They are in the walls. They are under the platforms. They are the size of a fist, mostly, or the size of a thumb, mostly. They are dark and pale. They are so numerous that the buildings seem to breathe.

The first thing to know is that they are not afraid of you. They have never heard of you. You are a new shape in their world and they will treat you as an interesting new shape. They will come to investigate. They may crawl on you. They will not, on the evidence of the description, attempt to harm you — but they will be everywhere, and there will be moments when their sheer abundance is distressing even though they are individually small.

**Bring ear protection.** Not for the loudness exactly, though they are loud. For the *texture* of the noise. The description is precise about it: a constant low industrial hiss, like a generator room heard from far off, broken by clicks and scrapes and the occasional trill that rises and is answered by seventeen others. This is not a sound the human ear is built to comfortably parse. It is not loud enough to damage hearing. It is loud enough to slowly wear you down. Ear plugs or muffs. Take them out periodically to listen for the larger things at night, which are different.

**Do not, under any circumstances, attempt to make the fauna leave.** They will not leave. If you disturb them they will not panic — they will simply become *more attentive*. Brush one off your arm and another will replace it. Disturb a wall of them and you will have set a colony in motion, and the colony will not return to its prior arrangement in your lifetime. They are not aggressive. They are simply present in numbers that human beings are not equipped to comfortably cohabit with.

**Do not eat the fauna, do not drink the water, do not eat anything that grows from the rock.** You do not know what biochemistry runs here. You cannot know. Assume everything is incompatible with you in ways that range from mildly nauseating to fatal.

## The Walkways

The walkways are not paved. They are the rock beneath, polished to a soft gleam by the passage of fauna and the slow trickle of meltwater and the long slow shuffling of whatever larger things also move through here at night.

**Bring soft-soled boots with serious grip.** The polished rock will be wet — it will always be wet — and it will be slippery. Soft soles so you can feel the texture through them, hard rubber or modern equivalents. Avoid anything with smooth leather soles. Avoid anything with metal studs that might catch on the unknown seam-edges of the platforms.

**Move slowly.** This is not a courtesy to the place; it is a courtesy to yourself. The walkways curve and branch and rejoin, and the platforms are stacked at heights that may not have railings, may not have any indication of edge. Moving slowly lets you read the ground. Moving slowly also lets the fauna read you — they will become accustomed to your presence faster if you are not alarming.

## The Unknown Construction

The construction is not a thing you can read. The surfaces are not stone and not metal and not any polymer you have a name for. Some of the structures hum. If you press your hand to a seam, you can feel something running through it — a faint warmth that is not warmth, a vibration that is not vibration.

**Do not touch the construction more than you have to.** You do not know what it is. You do not know what it does to the skin, or to the oxygen in your mask, or to the electronics in your pockets. You do not know whether the warmth-vibration is information, or current, or something else. The description suggests it does you no immediate harm. The description does not promise you any of this.

**Do not lean against the humming structures.** They are louder than the others, and the fauna is thicker on them, and the condensation on them runs in patterns that almost resolve into writing. None of these things are reasons to approach them. They are reasons to keep your distance.

**Do not put anything into a seam.** If you feel inclined to test the seams with a finger, a knife, a sample container — don't. The seams are not seams. They are something else. The technology that made them is older than anyone you know and operates on principles no one you know can read.

## The Sky

The sky has specks in it. Slow things, drifting across the upper air, too high to be fauna, too low to be stars. They make no sound. They are very cold.

**Do not go up to investigate them.** Stay on the platforms. Stay in the lower strata. Stay where the construction is thickest and the sky is least visible. The specks are not described in enough detail for you to know what they are, and they are described as very cold, which means if one of them lands on you, or falls on you, or comes down to where you are, the encounter will not be neutral.

## The Larger Fauna at Night

There are larger things that move through here at night. The description does not name them. The description only says you can hear them — a slow shuffling — and that there are fewer of them than the smaller fauna.

**Plan to leave before dark.** The description implies this itself: *you will not stay long.* If you cannot leave before dark, find a place where the construction is thickest and the walkways are narrowest, and wait. Do not sleep here. Do not lie down here. Stay awake, keep your mask on, keep your ears uncovered so you can listen, and wait for the day to come back. The larger fauna are described only by their sound, and this is exactly the right amount of description to ensure you do not want to meet them.

## The Time

The place is being made. You are watching it be made. The meltwater is patient. The cold is patient. The construction will go on being added to in the places where the rock is still raw, slowly, for as long as the dark lasts.

**Plan for a short visit.** Hours, not days. Bring enough oxygen and enough supplies for twice as long as you intend to stay, because something will delay you. But intend a short visit. The place is not for you. You are not the audience it was made for. The fauna will be here after you are gone. The meltwater will be here. The construction will still be patient in the dark, humming in places, silent in others, being added to in the places where the rock is still raw. The cold will keep its seat. You will leave. The place will go on making itself.

Bring what you need. Take nothing with you that the place might miss. Move slowly. Listen. Adjust your eyes. And when it is time to leave, leave. The plateau has been here longer than you, and will be here longer, and the only thing it asks of you is that you understand this.
