# Notes for Visiting the Halls Below the Hush

There is no way to dress for a place like this in the ordinary sense. The Halls are warm and wet and dim and patient, and anything you bring with you will get wet, and stay wet, and you will eventually stop noticing. Bring less than you think you need. Bring nothing you cannot carry with wet hands.

## What to Wear

Forget the gear list in your head. The hall is the temperature of a held hand, the temperature of a mouth, the temperature of a greenhouse at night. Your body will be comfortable, which is its own kind of trouble, because comfort makes you sleepy and you do not want to be sleepy here, at least not until you understand where the sleep takes you.

Clothing should be close-fitting, in soft natural fibers, in colors that won't startle. No bright nylon. No white. Nothing that rustles, because sound carries strangely in the halls — the vaulted ceilings gather your breath and send it forward, and a rustling sleeve is a small announcement of where you are. Linen, if you can find it. Cotton, if you cannot. Wool only if it is the kind of wool that has been washed many times, until it has forgotten it was ever a sheep. Dark earth tones are right. The color of wet bark. The color of river clay. The color of the iron-red stone.

Footwear is the most important decision you will make. You will be walking in shallow moving water for hours, possibly longer, possibly longer than hours is a useful measure here. The water is warm. The water is clear. The floor beneath it is fitted flagstone, dished toward channels at the edges, mostly smooth, occasionally treacherous where the moss grows thick and your foot slides on it without warning.

Do not wear boots. Anything with a tall rubber shaft will hold the warmth and the wetness against your skin and you will begin to feel that your lower legs belong to someone else, someone who is dissolving. Do not wear sandals. The banded things in the water do not flee from feet but they do not always notice feet either, and a sandal strap is a thing to investigate. Wear something like soft leather shoes that lace low across the top of the foot — something a person might wear to walk in a stream on purpose. Something that lets the water in and lets the water out. Something you would not mind never drying.

You will not, in fact, mind. The water does something to the leather. It softens it, darkens it, makes it part of the place. Your shoes will become, by the end, a kind of skin you are wearing on your feet, and you will not want to take them off for a long time after you leave, because your feet will feel overexposed in the open air.

## What to Bring

Nothing metal, or as little metal as you can manage. The place is a body. You can feel it. Metal in a body is a wrongness, the way a coin swallowed is a wrongness, the way a splinter is a wrongness. The dwellers carry blades of sharpened stone tied to wooden handles with strips of something cured and pale. Take your cue from them. If you must carry a knife, let it be a stone blade in a sheath of soft leather. If you must carry a light, do not bring a light. The place gives you its own.

A small pouch of soft cloth, tied shut, that you can wear against your ribs. Inside it: a piece of bread, if bread is a thing you can eat. A piece of dried fruit, if fruit is a thing you can eat. Something a child might offer a guest. Something you would not be embarrassed to receive. You will be offered things here. You will be offered more than food. The child will offer you a pale wriggling thing from the water and you will need to receive it, and the way you receive it will be remembered by the place, and the way you hold it afterward will be remembered by you.

Bring a piece of cloth to dry your hands, though you will not use it. Bring it anyway, because bringing it is the kind of thing a guest does, and you are a guest. Fold it. Put it in the pouch. It is a small ritual against the strangeness.

Do not bring a map. The halls are not unmappable but the map is not the territory here in the way it usually is, and a map will make you look for edges that are not there, will make you want to be somewhere you are not yet, and the place does not like being hurried through. Walk. Stop. Walk. The proportions will shift around you. Let them.

Do not bring a watch. Time is not the same down here. You will feel it pass in your stomach before you feel it pass anywhere else, and when you come out, the season will have turned, or the wind will be from a different direction, or the grass on the hill will be a different length, the way seasons turn in a single night in the stories. Accept this before you go down, and the not-knowing will not trouble you.

## What to Eat and Drink Before

Eat lightly. The air is humid and warm and your appetite will leave you somewhere in the second branching, the way your appetite leaves you on a summer day when you step into a greenhouse. A little bread. A little cheese. Water. Not too much water. There is water everywhere in the halls. It tastes of iron and of a faint sweet mineral note, like the memory of a fruit you ate once as a child, and you will want to drink it, and you should drink it, but drink it slowly. The water is not separate from the place. To drink the water is to let a small part of the place into you, and the place will be patient about this, will wait for you to be ready, but you should be ready.

Do not drink alcohol beforehand. Do not drink anything that has been fermented. The dwellers do not seem to ferment things, or if they do, they ferment them in a way that is not a way you would recognize. Your usual unsteadiness will not serve you here. You will need to be steady in a different way, the way a held hand is steady, the way a wall is steady.

Do not eat anything that has a strong smell. Garlic, in particular, is wrong here. The place smells of minerals and old water and the breath of moss, and anything you bring in that smells stronger than those things will be a kind of violence. The dwellers will notice. They will not be offended — the word offended does not seem to apply to them — but they will notice, the way you notice a door that is open when it should be closed.

## The State of Your Mind

This is the preparation nobody tells you about.

You are going to walk into a body. Not a metaphor. You are going to walk into a thing that is, simultaneously, a building and an organism, and your body will know this before your mind does. The knowing will arrive in stages. First you will feel the breath on your face and understand it is breath. Then you will feel the warmth on your skin and understand it is the warmth of a living thing. Then you will see the moss and understand it is the lung. Then you will see the water and understand it is the blood. Then you will hear the song of the dwellers in the chamber below and understand it is a song, and that the song is the working of the body, and that you are inside the body, and the body is letting you stay.

You need to let it.

This is the part that is hard. Most of us, most of the time, are managing a low-grade negotiation with the world about whether we are allowed to be where we are. In the halls, that negotiation is over. You will be allowed. You will be accepted, which is not the same as welcomed — the dwellers do not welcome you the way you welcome a guest, with noise and food and a place set at the table. They accept you the way a body accepts warmth, the way a wall accepts sound. You are in the place. The place knows you are in it. There is nothing for you to do about this and nothing for you to prove.

The hard part is not the strangeness. The hard part is the permission. You will want, somewhere in the second hour, to be elsewhere. You will want to be in a place where the negotiation is still going on, where you can win or lose it, where the rules are the rules you grew up with. You will want this very much, the way a sleeper wants the alarm, the way a held breath wants the air. The want will pass. It is the place's own breath moving through you, testing you, the way the draft in the first hall tested which way was forward. If you let it pass through you, you will be deeper in, and the wanting will not come back.

Do not go into the halls in grief. Do not go into the halls in anger. Do not go into the halls in a state of anything that needs to be resolved, because the halls will resolve it, and the resolution will be the kind you cannot argue with. The halls are not a place to be processed. The halls are a place to be in. If you need processing, do it on the hill above, in the wind, where the grass leans away as though listening. The hill is a kind of ear. The halls are what the ear is listening to.

Go in curious. Go in tender. Go in the state you would be in to meet a very old friend you had not seen in many years, the friend you are not sure still recognizes you, the friend whose recognition you are willing to accept on whatever terms it is offered.

## The Dwellers

They are the size of children sitting, or larger, or smaller. Their skin is the color of the dark stone, banded, the color of the iron-red and the ochre and the slate-blue. Their eyes are lidless. They blink slowly. They do not approach and you do not approach. There is an understanding between you that is older than approach, the understanding of two things that have noticed each other across a distance and have decided, for now, that the distance is correct.

Respect the distance. Do not close it. Do not try to make them into friends, or guides, or anything that requires them to be a particular kind of thing. They are dwellers. They are part of the place the way the moss is part of the place, the way the water is part of the place, but they are also something more — they carry stone blades and pale wooden poles and nets patched with fibers of different colors, and they sing while they work, and they give things to guests. They have a kind of culture, but it is a culture that is also a physiology, and you will not be able to tell where one ends and the other begins, and you should not try.

If a child offers you a pale wriggling thing, take it. Hold it. It will be warm. It will pulse gently in your hand. It will not struggle. The child will nod, once, slowly, as if something has been confirmed. You do not need to know what has been confirmed. The thing in your hand knows. The child knows. The place knows. The knowing is enough.

Do not eat the thing. Or do. The text is unclear on this, and the place is unclear on this, and the clarity of the place is a clarity you will have to arrive at yourself. The thing is offered as the best thing in the house. The thing is offered the way you offer a guest the thing you are proud of. To refuse it is a kind of refusal of the place. To eat it is a kind of acceptance that cannot be taken back. There is a third option, which is to hold it, and the third option is the one the text describes, and the third option is the one I would choose, but you should know that the third option is also a kind of answer, and the answer will be remembered.

Do not try to speak to them. Your language is not their language. Their calls are questions and answers, low and long, with vowels you almost recognize. You can make a sound. A single low sound, the way you might clear your throat in a church. They will hear it. They will know what it is. It is the sound a guest makes when the guest does not know what to say, and the place will receive it as such, and that will be enough.

Do not touch them unless you are touched first. The understanding of distance is the understanding the place runs on. To break it is to break something that cannot be repaired by being broken again in the same way.

## The Light

There is no torchlight. There is no lamp. The stone itself glows, faintly, the way a banked hearth glows, the way a coal glows after the flame has gone out of it but the heat has not. The veins of quartz, or of the stranger cousin of quartz, catch what little light there is and refuse to let it go. The pale moss, where it grows thick, is faintly luminous. The light is alive. The light is a kind of breathing.

Your eyes will adjust. Give them time. Do not squint. Do not shade your eyes with your hand, the way you might shade your eyes in sudden sunlight. The light in the halls is not a light to be fought with. It is a light to be received. Walk with your hand out, finding the wall by the absence of wall in front of you, in the stretches where the veins are absent and the dark is complete. The wall will be there. The wall is always there. The wall is the body of the place, and the body does not end, the way a body does not end at the skin.

In the brightest chambers, where the veins are thick and the moss is pale and luminous, you can sit beside the wall and feel, on one side of your face, a faint extra warmth. Sit there. The warmth is not a light you are seeing. It is a warmth you are being given. The place is leaning toward you, the way a person leans toward a person they are listening to.

## The Water

It is everywhere. It comes up through the floor in slow places where the flagstones have been laid with gaps between them. It spreads in shallow sheets that follow the slight dish of the floor and find the channels at the edges. It is ankle-deep in some chambers, a film in others, knee-deep in the chamber where the dwellers sort the small pale things from the deeper pool.

It is warm. It tastes of iron and of a faint sweet mineral note, like the memory of a fruit you ate once as a child. It is loud. Not the sound of the water itself, which is soft, but the place the water makes by being in it — every channel, every sheet, every drip from the ceiling joins with the others, and the joined sound is a low continuous roar, not loud the way a waterfall is loud, but loud the way a city is loud when you stand in the middle of it and try to hear a single voice. The hall is loud with its own wet working.

After a few steps your feet will get used to it. After a few more steps you will forget it is there, except for the sound. The sound does not stop. The sound is part of the place the way the breathing is part of the place. You will, eventually, stop hearing it as sound. You will hear it as the place being itself. This is when you are deep enough.

Do not try to dam the water. Do not try to step over it. The water is the blood of the place, and to interfere with it is to interfere with something you do not understand, and the place will be patient with your interference, but the patience of a body is not the patience of a building, and the difference will become clear to you, slowly, in a way you would rather not learn.

## The Wells

Some chambers are wells — round openings in the floor with steps going down. From below you will hear the louder roar of the water, and the smell will rise up at you, mineral and warm, and the air that comes up will be warmer still, and you will feel, on your face, the slow pulse of something vast.

Go down some of them. Come up out of others. The place does not mind which. The wells are not, I think, places the dwellers use to go somewhere — the dwellers are part of the place, and the place is everywhere, and the wells are not passages to elsewhere but passages to deeper, the way the chambers are passages to wider, the way the branching is a passage to more.

In the deeper chambers the dwellers crouch because the ceilings are low. They sing while they work. The song has weight. Each note sits on the previous one, and the pile of them is a structure you could almost walk on, almost live in, the way the hall itself is a structure you can walk in and live in. Sit in one of these chambers, if you are invited, or if a child stands near you in the water and looks up at you with an expression that is not quite expectation, not quite patience, something older than both. Sit in the water. Let it cover you to the knee. The song will be all around you. The place will be all around you. You will be inside the body, and the body will be inside the song, and the song will be inside the body, and the three of you will agree, in the dark, on a distance that is correct.

## The Things in the Water, the Things on the Ceiling, the Moss on the Walls

The banded things in the water are not afraid of you. They angle around your foot and continue on their way, a thousand bodies agreeing on a direction. They are not, as far as I can tell, food, though the dwellers fish for larger versions of them in the deeper pools. They are something else. They are part of the body's working. Let them be.

The things on the ceiling are clusters, like pods, like wax, like the paper nests of wasps but without the geometry. They pulse, faintly, in time with the breathing of the place. When you pass beneath a cluster you hear them, a soft collective ticking, like the inside of a clock laid open. Some of them are open at the bottom and inside you can see a pale wet thing coiled and waiting. Do not stay long under those. Do not breathe on them. They are not for you. They are the body's own business, the way a gland is a body's own business, and to interfere is to be a splinter in a working you do not understand.

The moss is the lung. You will see it on the walls in slow lobes and fans, in colors that are the green of a bruise, the black of deep water, the color of old copper, the color of the water itself. It is what is making the walls breathe. The faint mineral exhalation, the breath on your face, is the moss's. Touch it. Touch it the way you would touch the side of a sleeping animal, with the back of your fingers, lightly, so as not to wake it. The moss is not asleep. The moss is doing its work. But the work is the kind that is best not interrupted, and the touch will be a small acknowledgment, the way a touch on a sleeping animal's flank is an acknowledgment, and the acknowledgment will be received, and you will feel, in the moss under your fingers, a faint additional warmth, the warmth of a thing that has noticed you and is letting you stay.

## The Markings Above the Lintels

At each branching, above each lintel of darker stone, there is a single carved mark. A circle with a notch. A vertical line crossed by three horizontals. A shape that might be a leaf, or a hand, or a flame. They are worn almost smooth. The chisel-strokes are gone. Only the intention remains, the way a path worn into grass is still a path even after the grass has forgotten it.

You will want to know what the marks mean. You will not know. The dwellers may know, or the marks may be older than the dwellers, or the marks may be the place's own writing, the place writing down what it is, the way a body writes its own history in its bones. Do not try to copy the marks. Do not try to photograph them. The marks are not for taking out of the place. The marks are for the place. The marks are the place's name, written in a language that has no other speakers.

Walk under them. Walk under them the way you would walk under a threshold, with the small acknowledgment of a threshold, the slight bow of a head, the way you would walk into a house where someone is sleeping. The marks will be above you. The marks will always be above you, at every branching, in every chamber, the way the sky is above you in a place that has no sky, the way breath is around you in a place that is breath.

## How Long to Stay

You will not know. The light has not changed. The temperature has not changed. The water on your feet has not dried. But you will have a sense, in your stomach, that a long time has passed, and that when you go back up through the fissure, the grass outside will be a different length, or the wind will be from a different direction, or the season will have turned.

Do not stay until you are exhausted. Do not stay until the place has become ordinary, because the place will never become ordinary, and the trying will wear you out, and a worn-out guest is a kind of trespass. Stay until the distance is correct. Stay until the dwellers have accepted you, which is not the same as welcomed, but is the same as enough. Stay until the child has given you the pale wriggling thing and you have held it and the child has nodded, once, slowly, as if something has been confirmed. Stay until the song in the chamber below has become the sound the place makes by being itself. Stay until you are tired in a way that is not a weariness but a fullness, the tiredness of a cup that has been filled.

Then go up. The fissure will be where you left it. The hill will be where you left it. The grass will lean away, listening, as it always has. You will step out into a different season, or a different wind, or a different length of grass, and you will stand there for a moment, and you will feel, on the inside of your wrists and behind your knees, the faint humidity of the place, and you will know that you are not entirely out, that you will not be entirely out for a long time, that the place is a body and you have been inside it and the body remembers, the way bodies remember, the way breath remembers, the way a held hand remembers the hand that held it.

## What to Bring Back

Nothing.

Or almost nothing. A memory, which is a thing you cannot show anyone. A small additional warmth on one side of your face, which fades. A faint sweet mineral taste at the back of your teeth, which fades. The knowledge that the place is a body, that the body is letting you stay, that the staying is not a thing you do but a thing that is done to you, with you, around you, the way the moss grows in the seams between stones, the way the water runs in the channels at the edges of the floor, the way the dwellers sing in the chamber below, the way the place breathes, in and out, in and out, patient and warm and old.

If you must bring something back, bring a stone. A small one, from a place where the seams are loose, where the moss has worked the mortar soft, where the place is letting go. The stone will not glow in your hand the way it glowed in the wall. It will be just a stone, grey or ochre or iron-red, cool in your palm, heavy with the memory of being part of something. Hold it sometimes. The memory will come back, faintly, the way a vein of quartz catches what little light there is and refuses to let it go.

Do not try to bring back a piece of moss. The moss will die in the open air. The moss is the lung of the place and it cannot breathe anywhere else. The place will be patient about the loss, but the patience of a body is not the patience of a building.

Do not try to bring back a banded thing from the water. The thing will die in the open air. The thing is part of the body's working and it cannot work anywhere else.

Do not try to bring back a dweller. The dweller will not come with you. The dweller is part of the place, the way the moss is part of the place, and to remove it is to remove a cell from a lung, and the place will be patient about this, but the patience of a body is not the patience of a building, and the difference will become clear to you, slowly, in a way you would rather not learn.

Bring back the memory. Bring back the small additional warmth. Bring back the faint sweet mineral taste. Bring back the knowledge, in your stomach, that a long time has passed, and that the season has turned, and that the place is older than the hill, and that the hill is older than the grass, and that the grass is older than the wind, and that the wind is older than the season, and that the season is a thing the place decided to do, in its body, with its breath, while you were inside it, being held.

## A Last Word

You are not going into a ruin. You are not going into a tomb. You are not going into a place that was made and is now abandoned, or made and is now finished. The place is still being made. The masons are still at work, somewhere in the deeper chambers, cutting the stone and fitting the stone and carving the marks above the lintels, and they do not sign their names because they do not need to. Their names were the place. The place is their name.

You are going into a thing that is alive, and that has been alive longer than the hill, and that will be alive after the hill is a different hill, and that is letting you in the way a body lets in a thing that is small and warm and will not hurt it.

Be small. Be warm. Do not hurt it.

The place will do the rest.
