# Preparation for the Hollow of the Steeple-Vines

The first thing to understand is that this place does not resist you. It does not test you. It does not warn you off with storms or monsters or obvious peril. What it does is *accept* you, and that is more dangerous than any of those things, because acceptance is what makes you stay. Every piece of preparation below is, in the end, in service of one goal: making sure you can leave.

---

## Before You Go

### Decide why you are going, and write it down on something that cannot mold.

You will be tempted to bring a notebook. Don't. Paper in the Hollow becomes soft in a matter of hours, and within a day it is a damp rectangle of pulped fiber that contributes to the general wetness of your pack. Leather — your belt, your wallet, your journal cover — will not rot, exactly, but it will never quite dry again, and it will smell of the place for the rest of your life.

If you need to carry a written reminder of who you are, why you came, and when you intend to leave, etch it. On bone, on slate, on a piece of the dense oily wood that the locals use if you can get a scrap before you go. The Hollow is a place where writing is unnecessary because memory has other handles, but *you* are not from here, and your memory will need help. Bring your anchor physically.

Set a leave-by date. Tell someone outside the Hollow when you expect to return. This is not negotiable. The line *"You could live here a long time and never know you were anywhere"* is not poetry. It is operational warning.

### Get your joints right.

The cold here is not the cold of ice fields. It is the cold of stone cellars and riverbanks — saturated, dense, the kind that finds the small spaces between bones and stays. If you have any history of joint pain, arthritis, old injuries that ache in damp weather, you will feel them here. Get ahead of it. See a practitioner. Whatever you take for this kind of weather, take more than you think you need, and bring it in a waterproof container. The Hollow will find a way into every seal you trust.

If you have any respiratory vulnerability — asthma, chronic cough, anything — be honest with yourself about whether this visit is worth the risk. The air in the Hollow is heavy in a way I can only describe as *palpable*. It is not unbreathable. It just asks more of you than ordinary air does, and it does not let up.

### Practice being quiet.

Not silent — quiet. The Hollow runs at the volume of weather. Conversation happens at the level of rain on leaves. If you are a person who speaks at conversational volume in cities, you will be the loudest thing in any room you enter. Practice. Speak to a friend at half-volume for a week before you go. Get used to the sensation of being heard without filling the space.

## What to Wear

### The principle: nothing that holds water.

The locals wear a dense, oily, locally-grown fiber that is nearly waterproof. You almost certainly do not have access to this, and even if you did, your body would not be acclimated to it the way theirs are. So your goal is the next-best approximation.

- **Outer layer:** waxed cotton, oiled wool, or a modern equivalent — something with a tight weave that has been treated to shed water. A heavy oilskin jacket is ideal. Bright colors are wrong here, both practically and socially. Browns, deep greens, slate, the gray of wet stone. You want to look like the place when you arrive.
- **Mid layers:** wool. Wool is the only common fiber that continues to insulate when damp. Bring more than you think you need. You will not dry out. Wool doesn't pretend to.
- **Base layer:** wool against the skin, or a modern synthetic equivalent. **Do not wear cotton as a base layer.** Cotton holds water against you and will, over the course of a damp afternoon, lower your body temperature to a degree that surprises you. This is the single most important clothing choice you will make.
- **Head and neck:** a wool cap that covers the ears, and something you can pull up over the lower face when the air is at its heaviest. The damp cold enters through the head and the throat first.
- **Hands:** gloves you can work in. You will be touching stones, gates, basins, carved figures. Your hands will be wet constantly. Leather gloves will become sodden and stay that way. Wool liners inside a waxed outer glove work; thin modern synthetics work. You need fine motor control, because you will be asked, by the architecture itself, to step sideways, duck, hold a low lintel, grip a wet stone edge.

### Footwear

The streets are dark wet stones with shallow channels of slow water along their middles. The moss is everywhere. You will be walking single file, often at angles, often stepping up onto a stoop to let someone pass.

- Waterproof boots, laced tight above the ankle. Not rubber galoshes — you need ankle support for the uneven ground and worn steps.
- Wool socks, two pairs. Bring four pairs total; you will not be drying them.
- A tread that grips wet stone and moss. Smooth-soled shoes are an injury waiting to happen.

## What to Bring

### Bring less than you think.

Everything you carry will be damp, heavy, and smelling faintly of stone-water and moss within hours. The Hollow does not destroy equipment. It just *claims* it, slowly, the way it claims everything.

**Essential:**
- A light source that does not depend on batteries that cannot survive damp. Oil lamp if you have one. A modern headlamp sealed in a waterproof pouch will work for short uses, but understand that the locals light their evenings with stone lamps burning a prepared moss-wick, and the light is small, warm, and low. To use a bright modern light in this setting is to announce yourself as foreign in a way that words do not.
- A knife. The locals keep a single dark metal knife per household that does not rust. Yours will rust. Bring a knife you do not love, in a sheath you do not love, and expect to leave it behind or carry it home pitted and discolored. The atmospheric chemistry of the Hollow is not kind to common steel.
- Food that does not require cooking or refrigeration and is not damaged by damp. Dense, dry things — hard tack, cured meat, dry fruit. Bread will go stale in an hour; in a day it will be soft again and beginning to fuzz. Chocolate is a gamble.
- Waterproof container for any medication. Airtight, with a desiccant inside if you can manage it.
- A piece of the oily local fiber if you can obtain one small scrap, to study, to use as a handkerchief, to remind yourself of the texture of what is appropriate here.

**Strongly discouraged:**
- Paper, books, anything printed. They will be ruined. If you must bring a reference, photograph it onto a sealed device.
- Leather goods you care about.
- Electronics you cannot afford to lose. The damp will find every seam. If you bring a phone or recording device, it goes into a sealed dry bag, and you accept that it may not come back working.
- Bright colors, reflective materials, anything that catches the white diffuse light. You will be visible from every rooftop, and you do not want to be.

### Do not bring a gift.

This is important. The Hollow does not trade in the way most places trade. Bringing an offering to the basin, or to any household, will confuse the social situation in ways that are not dangerous but are genuinely awkward. If you want to leave something behind, leave it quietly at a stone edge, not at the central basin, and do not mention it.

## How to Behave

### Move like water.

The streets were not designed. They were *negotiated*. When two of you meet in a lane, one of you steps onto a stoop, or turns sideways, or waits. Do not push past. Do not try to walk abreast. Move like a slow stream finding its way between stones. The buildings are older than the roads and they have priority.

### Do not look for the center.

You will find it by going downhill, by following the worn places underfoot, by following the water. If you ask where it is, you will get a shrug, or a nod toward the downhill, or a change of subject. The center is not secret. It is simply not discussed, the way a household's well is not discussed among neighbors — it is just there, and you use it, and you do not talk about it. Observe it. Pour some water back if you take water from the basin. Do not photograph the carved figure. Do not clean the moss off it.

### Eat what is offered, if it is offered.

It may not be. The Hollow does not have a hospitality culture in the sense of cities that welcome travelers. People will not invite you in unless they decide to. If they do, eat what is put in front of you. If nothing is put in front of you, do not ask. Ask for nothing. The Hollow has its own rhythm, and your needs will be met when the Hollow decides to meet them, and not before. This is not cruelty. It is the place's character.

### Do not raise your voice.

I mean this literally. Conversation here is at the volume of weather. If you find yourself speaking louder, stop. Lower your voice until you can barely hear yourself, and then speak at that volume.

### Do not ask direct questions about the carvings, the crypt, the figure at the basin, the bones on the lintel, or the system of ventilation shafts beneath the houses.

You will be answered with silence, or with a half-sentence that trails off. This is not evasion. The people here do not think about these things. They are simply how things are. If you press, you will become an object of discussion, and not in a way you want.

### Touch nothing carved unless you have been invited to.

The carved figures, the lintels, the worn rings around the basin — these are not decorative. They are part of the place's working. Your hands carry the smell of elsewhere, and the oils and residues of your world, and they will not improve the carvings. Watch with your eyes. Your hands can stay at your sides.

### Do not kill anything.

There are no large predators. There are small deer the size of dogs that will watch you from twenty feet. There are birds everywhere. There is a fox-analogue that passes through the lanes at night. None of these will bother you. The rule is not about safety. It is that the Hollow has a balance that has held for a very long time, and your presence is already a perturbation. Do not add to it.

## What You Will Feel, and How to Meet It

### The cold that is not cold.

Your body will tell you, for the first day, that you are fine. The temperature is not extreme. You will not shiver the way you shiver in winter. What you will notice is that your fingers get slow, and your knees ache in a way that has nothing to do with the steps, and by the second evening you will feel a kind of *settling* in your chest, as though the damp has found a way in past your layers. This is normal. It is the cold of the place doing what it does. Move gently. Keep your wool next to your skin. Do not try to warm up with exercise — the sweat will not dry, and you will be colder for it.

### The dimness.

Light in the Hollow is sparse and white. There is no harsh light. There is no shadow with sharp edges. Your eyes will not strain, but your sense of time will blur. You will lose track of hours. The birds' two-note call will become your clock for a while, and you will not notice when you stop using any other measure. **This is the beginning of what the place does, and you need to notice it happening.**

### The heaviness of the air.

The air in the Hollow has weight. I do not have a better way to say this. It presses on you gently, constantly, like being just slightly underwater. Some travelers find it calming. Some find it suffocating in a way that does not produce panic, just a slow withdrawal. If you find yourself not wanting to speak, not wanting to move, not wanting to leave — that is the place speaking, and it is speaking in a voice that sounds exactly like your own.

### The pull.

By the second or third day, depending on how susceptible you are, you will feel a thing I can only describe as *recognition*. The Hollow will begin to feel like the place you were always supposed to be. The narrow lanes will start to feel protective rather than confining. The dripping will become silence-filler rather than noise. You will catch yourself thinking thoughts that have the texture of long tenure — *this is how it has always been, this is how it will be* — and those thoughts will not feel foreign.

**This is when you must use the anchor you brought. Read the thing you etched. Remember the date you set. Call to mind, with effort, the smell of a place that is not here, the voice of someone who is not here, a piece of your life that this place cannot be.**

The Hollow does not trap you with walls. It traps you with the feeling that you have come home to a place you did not know you had.

## When to Leave

Do not stay longer than you planned.

Whatever you came to learn, you will learn. The Hollow reveals itself slowly, and it will continue to reveal itself for as long as you stay, and it will continue to reveal itself for as long as you stay, and there will always be one more thing. The carved figure. The bone on the lintel. The pattern of footsteps around the basin. The way the channels of water change with the season. There is always a next thing, and the next thing is always just as important as the last, and the place will never tell you that you have seen enough, because the place does not think in those terms.

So you decide. Before you arrive. And you hold to it.

When you leave, you will find it harder than you expected. Not because of any barrier, but because part of you will not want to. This is the place doing what it does. Walk uphill. Keep walking until the lanes become paths become suggestion becomes forest, and then keep walking until the forest thins and the ground firms. The white diffuse light will become ordinary daylight at some point, and you will not notice the transition, and you will not notice when the air stops having weight, and you will not notice that you have stopped listening for the dripping.

Notice.

You are from somewhere else. You are going back.
