# A Preparation for The Bowl

## What you're going to find

The bowl will not be what you expect, even after you read about it. The description gives you the facts, but the facts do not prepare you for the *quality* of the place. I want to talk to you about the quality, because that is what will decide whether you come back whole.

The bowl is patient. That is the first thing you have to understand, and it is the last thing you will understand. Everything in it — the heat under the floor, the movement of the carpet, the weathering of the walls, the slow consumption of the cairn — operates on timescales your body cannot feel. You will arrive with your heart beating a hundred times a minute, your lungs pulling breath every few seconds, your eyes adjusting to light in fractions of a second. The bowl does not notice you. The bowl has not noticed anything new in a very long time. If you want to be there without being a disturbance, you have to slow yourself down to the bowl's tempo, and you will fail. Plan to fail, and plan for the failure not to matter.

The bowl is closed. This is the second thing. Nothing enters, nothing leaves. A closed system has no slack. Every breath you exhale is a new input of water vapor into a place that has had no water longer than anything still standing can remember. Every flake of skin that comes off you is food for something. Every gram of warmth you radiate is a thermal event in a system that runs on heat. The bowl is not fragile, exactly — it has held its shape through geological time — but it is *full*. It has no capacity to absorb a foreign body. Your presence is going to be a small event, but it is going to be an event, and you should treat it as one.

The bowl is dark. Not dark the way a room is dark when the lights are off. Dark the way the inside of a kiln is dark after the fire has been banked — that dim red-brown glow that comes from the stone itself, from the residual heat, from the undersides of the larger creatures over the hot cracks. Your eyes will not adapt to this. There is not enough light to adapt to. You will be working at the edge of visibility for your entire visit. Bring your own light, but bring soft light, and bring light you can turn off, because the creatures do not respond to light and you will not learn anything by illuminating them.

The bowl is full of life, and the life does not know you. This is the most important thing. The creatures in the carpet have been doing what they do for longer than the cairn has been there. They have no concept of a visitor. They have no category for a thing that walks upright on two legs and sweats. They will not attack you. They will not welcome you. They will not recognize you as a thing that needs to be reckoned with. You are, to them, a slow-moving lump of warmth and moisture in a world that runs on warmth. What they do with that lump is not a question of hostility or friendship. It is a question of chemistry. They will do what they have always done. You have to make sure that what they have always done does not include you.

## Before you go

### Acclimatize for altitude

The rim of the bowl is high. The air at the rim is thin and cold and carries a knife-edge wind. You are going to want to descend as quickly as you can once you arrive, because the wind is unpleasant and the bowl below looks inviting. Do not descend quickly. Spend at least a day at the rim, ideally two. Drink water. Sleep. Let your blood thicken. If you have access to supplemental oxygen, bring it for the rim and the early descent. Once you are at the floor of the bowl the air will be warmer and a little denser — the bowl holds its own weather, its own heat — but you will have spent your reserves getting down. The descent is not a place to spend reserves.

### Learn to climb

The walls of the bowl are two hundred meters of dense, weathered basalt. The description says they are sheer but not polished — faceted, ledged, vertically seamed. This is the geologist's description. The climber's description is: do not trust this rock. The facets look like holds but they are not holds. The ledges look like platforms but they are loose. The seams look like cracks but they are filled with the kind of mineral cement that gives way under body weight. The basalt is fine-grained and dense, which means it takes bolts well — if you bring bolts, leave them at the rim, because bolts in this rock will hold you, but bolts are also a thing you are putting into a place that has not had a thing put into it in a long time. Plan your descent around natural features where you can find them. Plan for the descent to take most of a day. Plan to be tired before you reach the floor.

You will be cold on the descent. The wind at the rim is cold and the air is thin. You will be working hard, which will make you sweat, which will make you wet, which will make you cold. Wear layers you can shed as you descend into the warmer air of the bowl.

### Bring what you need

The bowl has no water. It has no food. It has no shelter beyond the structures, and the structures are old and being consumed. You must bring everything you need for the duration of your visit, plus a margin. The margin matters because the descent is slow and you may not descend as planned, and the ascent is slower and you may not ascend as planned.

Water: more than you think. The air is desiccated. You will lose moisture just breathing. Bring a sealed supply — nothing open, nothing that can spill. A spill in this bowl is not a spill in your kitchen. It is an input to a closed system that has not had free water in longer than the cairn has been standing. Be careful with your water.

Food: bring what you need for energy, but understand that eating in the bowl introduces organic matter. The creatures are not interested in your food, probably, but your food is interesting to the system. Eat before you descend if you can. Eat sparingly in the bowl. Do not leave scraps.

### Do not bring samples

This is a place of no technology, and the temptation to take something home with you will be strong. You will want a piece of the basalt. You will want a fragment of one of the creatures — dead, shed, broken. You will want a chip from one of the placed stones. Do not. The bowl is a closed system. Anything you remove is a thing that is no longer there. Anything you remove is a small hole in a very old fabric. If you must document the bowl, document it with notes and drawings. If you must take an image, take an image. Do not take a piece.

### The question of protection

You will be told, or you will tell yourself, that you need protection against the creatures. This is not quite right. The creatures are not aggressive. They do not hunt. They do not pursue. They are slow. They are dense on the surface, but they are not thick — they are one creature deep in most places. They have no apparent means of harming a thing much larger than themselves.

What they have is *coverage*. They cover everything. If you put your hand on the carpet, your hand is in contact with dozens of them. If you stand on the carpet, your feet are in contact with hundreds. If you lie down on the carpet, your body is in contact with thousands. They do not need to be aggressive to make that contact matter. They are biological. They have orifices that may be mouths. They feed on shed matter, on husks, on flakes. A human lying still on the carpet for an hour is not a person to them — it is a feeding event, the largest feeding event the bowl has had in a very long time.

You do not need armor. You need *patience and motion*. Stay moving. Stay on the structures where they exist. Stay off the carpet where you can. If you must stand on the carpet, understand that you are standing on a slow tide of living things, and that your weight and your warmth are inputs to that tide, and that the tide will accommodate you for as long as it can and then will not.

## At the rim

The rim of the bowl is a strange place. The wind is constant and cutting. The air is thin and your breath is short. The light, even at the rim, is thin and coppery. The sky, when you can see it through the haze, is a small ragged circle above you, and through it the sun moves as a pale disc that gives less heat than you expect.

From the rim you can see down into the bowl. The walls drop away beneath you. The floor is far below and dim. You cannot make out the carpet from the rim — it is too dark, too far. You can see the heat shimmer over the deepest part of the bowl, a slow column of distortion rising into the dusk. You can see, if the light is right, the shadows of the larger creatures over the hot patches, dark masses that do not quite resolve. You can see, occasionally, a slanting shaft of pale gold on the floor — the hour of oblique sunlight that crosses the bowl when the sun is well-placed. The shaft moves slowly. It is beautiful. It is also a clock: if you watch the shaft for an hour, you have watched the only rapid motion the bowl has to offer.

Do not throw anything over the rim. Stones fall into the bowl. The creatures nearest the impact ripple briefly and then settle. The stone is slowly covered. This is described to you, and it is real. If you throw a stone over the rim to see what happens, you have introduced a foreign object into a closed system, and you have done it for your entertainment. Do not do this.

## The descent

The descent is technical. You will use your hands and feet on the basalt. You will find holds you trust and holds you do not. The wall is weathered, faceted, seamed. Take your time. The bowl has been there for longer than you. It will be there tomorrow.

As you descend, the wind dies. The cold thins. The light dims. By the time you are halfway down, you are in a different climate. The air is warmer. The air is stiller. The light is dusk-light, that dim red-brown wash that comes from the stone and the sky together. Your ears will adjust. Your eyes will not.

When you reach the floor, you will be standing on the carpet. The first thing you will notice is the density. There is no stone visible beneath you. The floor is not visible. The floor is a single thick layer of dark shapes, packed shoulder to shoulder, edge to edge, a perfect tessellation of biological surface. They are warm. They are textured. They are mostly still, mostly, but not entirely — there is a slow drift to them, a tidal motion that you can feel under your feet if you stand very still, a kind of soft yielding, the way packed sand yields if you wait.

The second thing you will notice is the quiet. The bowl is quiet the way a held breath is quiet. The wind that was cutting you at the rim is gone. The sound of your own movement, your breathing, the small noises of your gear, are the loudest sounds in the world. And under those sounds, there is the subsonic hum — felt, not heard, in your chest and in the soles of your feet. The whisper of many bodies against each other. The turning of many pages in an empty library.

The third thing you will notice is the warmth. Rising through the floor in slow plumes. The carpet is warm, the stone beneath is warmer, and the deeper you go, the warmer it gets. The warmth is not uniform. There are hotter patches and cooler patches, drifting over years. If you stand over a hot patch you will feel the air push faintly outward from it, a thermal exhalation, continuous, almost imperceptible.

Stand still for a moment when you arrive. Do not move. Let your eyes adjust to the dim. Let your body settle into the warmth. Let your heartbeat slow down. The bowl has been waiting longer than you can imagine. It can wait another minute.

## On the carpet

You cannot avoid the carpet. The bowl has no other floor. Every horizontal surface, every ledge, every slope that is not vertical, is carpet. If you want to be in the bowl, you are on the carpet.

What this means:

Your feet are on living things. They are warm. They are textured. They may shift beneath you as the tidal motion of the carpet continues. They are dense and they are shoulder-to-shoulder. There is no gap for your foot to slip through. There is no bare stone under your heel.

You will be tempted to step carefully, to find the spaces between them, to avoid crushing anything. There are no spaces. The packing is perfect. You will be standing on them no matter how you step. The smaller ones fit into the spaces between the larger ones. There is no "between."

You will be tempted to step off the carpet and onto the structures — the cairn, the slabs, the platform, the wall fragment. The structures are old and they are being consumed. The cairn's top is barely above the carpet. The slabs lean. The platform has a shallow depression that catches nothing. The wall has a corner you could stand in if you were small enough and patient enough. The creatures flow around them, over them, into the gaps. The structures are not stable platforms. They are not maintained. They are relics that are slowly being absorbed.

But the structures are slightly elevated. They are slightly cooler. They are not moving. If you must stand still for any length of time — to observe, to rest, to think — the structures are where you stand. Treat them with respect. They are not yours to climb on. They are not yours to disturb. They are part of the bowl's memory, and the bowl is patient, and the bowl does not appreciate being hurried.

### How to move

Move slowly. The carpet is a slow tide. If you move slowly with the tide, you are part of its motion. If you move against it, you are a disturbance. If you move quickly, you are a violent event, and the creatures nearest you will ripple, and the ripple will propagate, and the bowl will know you are there.

Walk with the drift. Walk toward the warmer patches if you must walk toward something. Walk away from the structures when you leave them. Let the carpet close behind you.

Do not stand still on the carpet for longer than you must. Your weight, your warmth, your moisture are inputs. The carpet will accommodate you, but the accommodation has a cost in the slow economy of the bowl. If you stand still for ten minutes on a single spot, you are heating that spot. The carpet beneath you will warm. The creatures around you will drift toward you, drawn by the warmth. They will press against your boots. They will press against each other. The local pattern will shift to include you.

### What to do if one of them touches you

It will happen. A small one, fist-sized, will press against your ankle or your boot as you stand. A larger one will lean against your leg as you walk past. They have no faces, no eyes, no apparent way of seeing you. They are responding to warmth, to pressure, to chemistry. They are not attacking you. They are not investigating you. They are simply present, and you are present, and the carpet is continuous.

Do not kick it. Do not step on it harder than you must. Do not pick it up. If you pick it up, you have removed it from the carpet, and the carpet has a hole now, and the system will close the hole, but you have done it. If you must move one aside to step past it, move it gently, with the flat of your hand if you must, and place it back where it was. Treat them the way you would treat the lichen on an old stone — as a thing that is alive and that does not want to be moved and that will not survive being moved.

If one of them attaches to you — if a piece of it, a flake, a soft extension, sticks to your clothing — do not tear it off. Wait. Stand still. The piece will release when it has taken what it wanted, or when the carpet beneath it shifts and pulls it back. If it does not release, you may have to remove it carefully, and you should feel the weight of that removal as you do it.

## The structures

The structures are the bowl's only memory of a placer. The cairn, the slabs, the platform, the wall. They are positioned. They are not ornamented. They have no tool marks, no carving, no writing, no image. They are stones placed in meaningful arrangements.

You cannot read them. There is no writing to read. But you can read the placement. The line is a line. The cairn is a cairn. The platform is a platform. The wall has a corner. These are gestures. Someone, or something, made these gestures, and then stopped, or died, or left, or became what is in the bowl now, or never was here at all.

Sit with the structures. Do not touch them unless you must. The cairn is being consumed — its top is barely above the carpet. Touching it accelerates nothing, but your hand on the basalt adds warmth, adds moisture, adds skin oil to a stone that has been dry for longer than you can imagine. If you must touch, touch with the back of your hand, briefly, and step away.

Do not try to right the slabs. They fell at the same moment, long ago, and they have not been righted. There is a reason they have not been righted, even if the reason is only that no one has been here to right them. Righting them is a thing you would do to make yourself feel useful. The bowl does not need you to feel useful.

Do not add to the cairn. The cairn is what it is. The bowl is what it is. Adding a stone is a gesture, and gestures are what made these structures in the first place, and you do not know what the gestures meant, and you cannot complete them.

## The center

There is a place the creatures do not go. A circle, perhaps ten paces across, of bare stone. The stone inside the circle is hot past hot — it glows a faint cherry red, even in the dusk. The creatures flow around it with a wide berth. They give it two body-widths of clearance on every side. The space inside that clearance is empty of them, a ring of bare stone around the glowing circle, a moat of heat.

You will be drawn to it. Everyone is drawn to it. It is the bowl's center, in the deepest sense. It is the thing the bowl is built around, even though the bowl is not built. It is the mystery.

### Do not approach closer than you must

The heat at the edge of the clearance is significant. The air above the circle is distorted — clearer, perhaps, or more distorted, the shimmer compressed into a still column that rises straight up out of the bowl. If you stood at the edge of the circle you would feel the heat on your face like an open oven door. The stone is hot past hot. It glows.

Do not step into the circle. The description says: *if you stepped into it, you would not step out, or you would step out different, or you would not step out at all.* This is not metaphor. The circle is not the bowl. The circle is part of the bowl, but the bowl is not the master of the circle. The circle is older, or deeper, or other. The creatures know this. They have always known this. They have shaped their entire slow circulation around it. The circle is the constant in the system. The carpet is the variable. The circle was here before the carpet, or the carpet was here before the circle, and there is no way to know.

You cannot know what would happen if you stepped in. You cannot know what would happen if you touched the glowing stone. You cannot know what would happen if you stood in the column of heat that rises from it. These are not things you can know without doing them, and doing them is irreversible. Do not do them.

### Do not bring anything into the clearance

The clearance is bare stone, kept bare by the heat. The creatures do not cross it. If you bring a stone into the clearance — to test the heat, to mark the boundary, to leave something behind — you are putting a thing into a place where things do not go. The clearance is empty for a reason. The bowl has arranged itself around that emptiness. Disturbing the arrangement is a thing you cannot undo.

### What you can do at the center

You can stand at the edge of the clearance. You can feel the heat on your face. You can watch the shimmer rise. You can see the glowing stone from a distance of two body-widths and feel the weight of it. You can sit at the edge, on the bare stone of the moat, and watch the creatures flow around you, and listen to the bowl, and be still.

You can think about the circle. You can think about the structures. You can think about what kind of being places stones in meaningful arrangements and then stops. You can think about whether the creatures are descended from that being, or whether the being is descended from the creatures, or whether they share no lineage at all. You can think about whether the circle was placed, or whether it is geology. You can think about whether stepping into it would be death or transformation or something you do not have a word for.

You cannot answer these questions. The bowl will not tell you. But the bowl will hold the questions, the way it holds the cairn, the way it holds the heat, the way it holds the slow drift of the carpet. The bowl is patient. The bowl has time for you to think.

## Time in the bowl

A day is enough. Two is more than enough. A week is too long. The bowl operates on timescales you cannot match. A week of your presence is a small but real input to a closed system that has no input. The longer you stay, the more your body heat, your moisture, your shed matter, your exhaled carbon dioxide, accumulate. The longer you stay, the more the carpet accommodates you, and the less the carpet is its unaccommodated self.

If you can, visit during the hour of the sun. The slanting shaft of pale gold moves across the floor once a day, slowly, for about an hour. It is the only rapid motion the bowl offers. It is a clock you can use. It is also beautiful, in a way that does not belong to this place — a thing of brightness in a world of dusk, of warmth in a world of heat. Watch the shaft move. Let it pass over you. Let it pass over the carpet. The creatures do not respond to it. They continue their slow drift. They are creatures of the dusk, and the dusk is permanent.

If you visit during the long dusk, the bowl is uniform. The light does not change. The heat does not change. The creatures do not change. You lose your sense of time. Bring a watch. Bring a reason to leave.

## The ethics of departure

When you climb out of the bowl, you will leave it as you found it. You will not leave scraps. You will not leave a sample. You will not leave a mark. You will not leave a piece of yourself — a flake of skin caught on a slab, a hair on the cairn, a footprint in the carpet.

The carpet will close over your footprints. The ripples from your movement will subside. The heat you added will dissipate. The moisture you exhaled will be absorbed into the desiccated stone and the bodies of the creatures. The bowl will return to its balance within days, within hours perhaps, within minutes if you are careful.

If you are not careful, the bowl will return to its balance over years. It will absorb you slowly. It will incorporate your inputs. It will adjust its slow circulation to include the disturbance of you. This is the cost of not being careful. The bowl is patient. It has time to absorb you.

### Take nothing back

You will want to take something. A flake of basalt. A piece of one of the structures. A fragment of a creature, shed, dead, found in a crack. You will want a piece of the bowl in your pocket.

Do not take a piece. The bowl is a closed system. Anything you remove is a hole that will not close in your lifetime. Anything you remove is a thing the bowl has lost. Anything you remove is a thing you have taken from a place that has had nothing taken from it in a very long time.

Take notes. Take drawings. Take images if you can. Take memories. Take the question of the circle home with you, and the slow drift of the carpet, and the sound of many living things not speaking, and the patience of stone, and the patience of the things on the stone, and the patience of the heat under both.

Do not take a piece.

## What you will come back with

You will come back changed, even if you do not step into the circle. The bowl does something to time. The bowl does something to pace. You will come back slower than you left. You will come back with a longer view of things. You will come back with the awareness that there are systems older than your systems, balances held longer than your balances, patients deeper than your patience.

You will also come back tired, and dehydrated, and probably cold, because the rim is cold and the descent is hard and the bowl is warm and you will have sweated out your reserves in the slow heat of the floor. You will come back with the memory of the quiet. The subsonic hum. The whisper of many bodies against each other. The sound of many living things not speaking.

You will come back with a question. The bowl will not answer it. The bowl does not answer. But the bowl will hold the question, and if you go back, the question will still be there, the way the cairn is still there, the way the heat circle is still there, the way the carpet is still drifting in its slow tide.

The bowl is patient. The bowl has time. The bowl will be there.

Be careful with it.
