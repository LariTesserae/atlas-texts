# Preparing for the Pale Plateau of Long Air

A multiverse traveler going to this place will not fail there because the place is hostile. The plateau has had a very long time to stop being hostile. It has settled into a kind of indifference that is almost a courtesy. A traveler will fail there, or be diminished there, because they came prepared for the wrong shape of strangeness.

Most of the standard long-haul preparation — the pressurized suit, the water recycler, the emergency rations — is, for this place, the wrong problem solved very thoroughly. The atmosphere is not vacuum. The temperature is not lethal. The gravity is a little gentler than Earth's and the light is dim but it is light. The traveler will not die of being there. The traveler will be made miserable by being there if they have not understood what being there is actually like.

So: what is it actually like, and what does a person need before they go?

## The Body's First Conversation With the Place

The traveler will arrive, most likely, by shuttle or by some equivalent — and they will step out onto a surface that does not look like a planet. It looks like the inside of a question. The rock is pale ochre underfoot, almost a bone color itself, and it is so flat that the horizon does the strange thing that horizons do on plains — it does not curve so much as *decide*, far away, where to end. The pillars stand in that distance. White. Tall. Spaced as if someone had placed them deliberately and then waited ten thousand years for the placement to stop meaning anything. The sky above them is not blue and not gray and not anything a person has a word for. It is a violet-gray, the color of old dishwater, lit from no obvious source because the sun, when it appears, is a pale smudge behind a permanent haze.

The first breath is the second surprise.

The air tastes thick. Not bad-thick — not smog-thick — but heavy, like the air of a greenhouse or a wine cellar, and slightly sweet, slightly mineral, with an undercurrent of something that the lungs recognize as not-oxygen-but-doing-the-job. The traveler will feel their chest work a little harder for the first few hours, then less, then they will forget, then they will remember when they go home and the air of any water-world will feel thin and insufficient for the rest of their life.

The third surprise is cold. Not deadly cold. Not Antarctic cold. The cold of a late autumn afternoon in a place that has never had a hot afternoon. The traveler should bring warmth. They should bring it in layers, because the cold on the plateau is a polite cold — it does not attack the body, it simply sits with the body, and a body that sits with it for too long forgets it has any other option.

## Water, and the Long Slow Anxiety of Its Absence

The traveler should pack water. Real water. More water than they think they need, and then some more.

This is not because the plateau will try to take their water. The plateau is past that. It is because the absence of water in a place is felt by the body as a kind of quiet emergency that the mind cannot shut off. A water-world traveler carries water the way a land traveler carries breath — it is so constant it is invisible. Here, on the Long Air, water becomes visible. It becomes the thing the eye searches for on the horizon. It becomes the thing the ear listens for — the sound of a stream, a drip, a rain that will not come. The colonists have lived here for nine generations and they still dream of rain. The traveler will dream of rain after three days.

Pack water in sealed containers. Pack a still of some kind, if such things exist in the traveler's kit — a way to wring moisture from any source. Learn, before going, the technique the colonists use on the cold nights: a flat metal surface angled into the wind, a cup beneath it, the patience to wait while the temperature differential does what no river does here. The traveler will be invited to do this by the locals. It will become, for them, a small ritual. They will come to love the cup of water that comes from it. They will understand, holding that cup, what the colonists have understood for nine generations: that water is not a background condition. It is a gift.

Do not drink the pillar-condensate. It is not water. It is whatever the pillars sweat out of the atmosphere in their slow respiration, and the chemists will argue about what it is, but it is not for human stomachs, and the locals — even the adapted ones, even the ones whose blood has thickened — will not drink it. They will offer it to the traveler as a curiosity, perhaps, in a small ceremonial cup. The traveler should accept the cup. They should not drink from it.

## The Wind, the Chord, and the Ear's Slow Surrender

The traveler should bring, if they can, ear protection that is not ear protection. Something that lowers the volume without cutting the sound. Foam plugs will not be enough. The traveler needs to modulate the wind-song, not silence it.

Because the wind here does not stop. It does not pause for conversation. It does not lower itself for sleep. It moves across the plateau in long ribbons that eddy around the pillars, and every pillar in the path of the wind answers with its own fundamental tone, and the pillars answer each other, and the chord builds and shifts and rebuilds hour by hour. At dawn it is low, almost subsonic, a pressure on the chest rather than a sound. By midday it has climbed into a higher register as the thermal drafts rise off the stone. At night it falls again into a long bass note that the colonists sleep inside, the way a person in a water-world sleeps inside the hum of a refrigerator.

The first day, this sound is oppressive. The second day, it is strange. The third day, if the traveler is honest, it is beautiful. By the end of the first week, the traveler will miss it when they step inside a habitation chamber and the walls cut the sound down to a murmur. They will step back out onto the walkway. They will lean into the wind and let the chord pour through them.

This is the thing to understand: the Long Air is loud the way the ocean is wet. It is not an assault. It is a condition. The traveler should prepare their ears for a condition, not an event.

The call-birds make it worse, or better, depending. They sing all day. Their voices are small but their numbers are large and the dense air carries the song far and there is no water to absorb it. From a single pillar at midday the traveler will hear a thousand competing songs overlapping into a kind of permanent static of melody — short phrases, repeated phrases, long descending trills, two-note calls answered by three-note calls from a neighboring pillar. It does not resolve. It just continues. The wind adds its chord beneath. The shuttles add their hum. The walkways creak in the breeze. The traveler will, after a few days, stop trying to find the melody and start hearing it as a single thing. This is a small transformation. It is the plateau's first gift.

## The Light, and the Eye's Slow Adjustment

The traveler should bring good eyes, or good spectacles, or the willingness to squint for a long time.

The light on the plateau is dim. Not dark — never dark, even at night, because the breath of the pillars glows faintly with whatever luminescence the radium-traces in the rocks give up — but dim, the light of an overcast afternoon that never becomes any other kind of afternoon. Colors are muted. The pale ochre rock reads as beige. The white pillars read as cream. The sky reads as the inside of a pearl. The vein-minerals in the rock — the slow rivers of green-blue and rust, the color of an old bruise — are the only saturated color in the landscape, and they are subtle.

The traveler who arrives expecting color will be disappointed for about a day. The traveler who arrives expecting texture will not be disappointed at all. This is a place of texture. The porous surface of the pillars. The lacework of pits. The slow rivers of oxidized mineral in the stone. The grain of the wind-eroded rock. The traveler should bring, if they are a person who notices such things, a willingness to be drawn into surfaces. They will be drawn. They will spend a long afternoon examining a single patch of pillar-skin and find it is a landscape of its own.

Inside the habitation chambers, the light is better — a pale gray-yellow, the color of old paper, falling through windows grown thin as paper in the pillar-walls. The colors indoors are cream, ivory, faint gold. The traveler should bring small things of saturated color for themselves. A scarf. A piece of cloth. A book with a bright cover. These will not be decorative. They will be necessary. The eye, deprived of strong color for long enough, begins to hunger for it.

## Clothing, and the Slow Negotiation with the Cold

The traveler should bring layers. Many layers. The cold on the plateau is constant but it varies — a thin morning, a thicker midday, a thin again at dusk — and the body wants to adjust without being asked to think about it.

The wind, too, is a kind of cold. It is a moving cold. It cuts through thin cloth. The traveler should bring a wind-shell, something light enough to fold, heavy enough to hold the wind off the chest. The colonists wear them all the time. They are not dressed for weather so much as dressed for conversation with the wind — polite, layered, respectful.

Gloves. The traveler should bring gloves, because they will be touching the pillars, and the pillars are cold to the touch. They are cold in the same way that stone is cold, but more so, because the pillars are breathing and the surface is porous and the cold comes from inside as well as from the air. The first touch of a pillar is a small surprise. It is not unpleasant. It is the surprise of meeting something alive and finding it cooler than oneself.

A mask. The traveler should bring a mask, or be prepared to accept one from the colonists. The methane fraction of the atmosphere rises in the long evenings — the long evenings of this world are very long, because the rotation is slow and the dark lingers — and the lungs, even the lungs of someone adapted, even the lungs of a long-time traveler, want filtering at those hours. The colonists' masks are grown. They are made of pillar-substance, thin and porous, and they filter the air with the same quiet patience the pillars use to drink the atmosphere. The traveler will be offered one. They should accept it. It will become, for them, a kind of face.

## The Body and the Long Air

The traveler should know, before going, that their body will change. Not in the way the colonists' bodies have changed — not in nine generations, not in the slow thickening of blood and the new assumptions of the lung — but in the way any body changes in a new place. The blood will make a few more red cells. The lungs will learn the new atmosphere. The skin will dry. The hair will dry. The traveler will feel a kind of lightness in the chest for the first few days that is not the slightly-weaker gravity but the lungs working more efficiently in air that has weight to it.

The traveler should bring skin care. Lotion. Oil for the lips. Something for the hands. The dry air of a water-world traveler in a place without water is a small persistent punishment that the mind forgets about and the body does not. The colonists have oils and unguents that they make from the pillar-condensate and other things; the traveler will be offered these, and the offerings should be accepted, but the traveler's own lotions will feel, for a while, more like home.

The traveler should also bring patience with their own body. The first week on the plateau is a week of small adjustments. The second week is a week of larger adjustments. By the third week the body has decided the plateau is home, and the traveler will know this because they will catch themselves breathing the way the locals breathe — long, slow, deliberate — and they will be surprised, and then they will not be surprised.

## The Pillar-Walk and the Slow Negotiation with Vertigo

The traveler will, before long, be asked to walk between the pillars.

The walkways are narrow. They sway. They are strung from pillar to pillar across gaps that look, from the walkway, like the gaps between buildings in a city, but the buildings are alive and the gap is open sky and there is no ground beneath. The wind passes the traveler. The traveler sees, far below, the pale stone of the plateau, far enough that the vein-minerals look like the lines on a map of somewhere else.

The traveler should bring a willingness to be afraid and not let the fear win. They should also bring, if they have such a thing, a small steadying ritual — a word, a count, a hum — that they can use to keep their feet under them. The locals walk these walkways without thinking. The traveler will think about every step for a long time, and then they will think about fewer of them, and then they will not think about them at all.

A traveler who cannot do heights should know that there are alternatives. The shuttles. The grown chambers connected at lower levels. The colony is a network, and a person who cannot walk the high walkways can live entirely in the lower chambers, moving from pillar to pillar through the root-network beneath the stone. The travelers who choose this option see less of the sky but more of the bones, and the bones are a kind of sky of their own.

## The Bone-Knock and the Slow Learning of a Quieter Tongue

The traveler will, before long, hear a knock that is not a knock. It will come through the wall of their chamber, or through the floor, or through the pillar beside them — a soft, deliberate pulse, repeated, with a rhythm that is not random. The traveler will ask the locals about it. The locals will explain, with a small smile, that they are sending a message through the bone. The pillar-substance is a conductor. A tap travels through the trunk, out through the root-network, and emerges in another pillar as a soft knock. The morning call. The meal-knock. The long slow pulse that means a birth.

The traveler should learn the rhythms. They should learn at least three of them — the greeting, the farewell, the acknowledgment. The locals will be quietly pleased. The bone-knock is not a flourish. It is a way of being polite. A traveler who knocks softly on the wall of their chamber when they enter, and softly again when they leave, is a traveler who has understood something the locals value.

The traveler will also be asked, eventually, to receive a knock — to put their hand against the wall of their chamber and feel the message arrive. It is a small thing. It is not a small thing. The hand against the bone, the soft pulse through the wrist, the sense of another person's intention arriving in your body through a substance that is older than the colony. The traveler will be moved by it. They should let themselves be moved.

## The Fauna, and the Small Negotiations with Other Lives

The traveler should bring curiosity. They should bring a small notebook, or whatever their equivalent is, for recording what they see. The fauna of the plateau are not Earth-fauna. They will not behave as Earth-fauna behave. The chitter-things that crawl through the porous surfaces of the pillars will not run from a human. The gliders — the methane-bladdered creatures that coast between pillars on thin membranes of silica-bone — will not look at a human. The burrowers that live in the rock between pillars will not be seen at all unless the traveler is out at night, and then the traveler will see only the small craters they leave in the pale stone.

The traveler should not touch any of them without invitation. The locals will invite, eventually. The gliders can be touched, gently, on the membrane — it is cool and slightly yielding and the bladder hisses softly when the hand approaches. The chitter-things can be watched but not handled; their dust is a kind of slow irritant to water-world lungs. The burrowers can be listened for, through the bone, on quiet nights — a small scratching, a soft pulse, the colony beneath the colony.

The call-birds should be listened to, not approached. They sing their small loud songs and they do not care who hears them. The traveler should sit beneath a pillar at midday and listen, and should not try to find the melody, and should let the static wash over them. This is its own kind of preparation. It prepares the ear for the plateau's idea of silence, which is not silence at all.

## The Night, the Breath, and the Slow Surrender to the Dim

The traveler should bring, if they are a person who is made uneasy by darkness, a small lamp. They will not need it often. The plateau is never fully dark — the breath of the pillars glows faintly with the radium-trace, the call-birds have a faint bioluminescence of their own, the stars, when the haze thins, are sharp and many — but the dim is its own thing. The eye adjusts. The traveler will be surprised to find how well the eye adjusts.

But the breath.

At night, each pillar releases a slow exhalation. The colonists call it the breath. It rises from the pillar in a pale column, visible against the dim sky, and where two pillars' breaths meet they braid and rise together. The traveler should, on the first or second night, walk the suspended walkways alone. The locals will offer to accompany them. The traveler should accept the offer, but should also find a moment to step ahead, alone, and stand at the place where two breaths meet and watch them braid.

The traveler will not be able to describe this afterward. The locals do not try to describe it. They have a word for it, but the word does not translate, and the traveler should not try to translate it. They should let the moment be what it is — a small ceremony between a person and a place, performed in the dim light, with the wind still moving and the call-birds quieting but not silent and the hum of the shuttles far below.

This is the plateau's second gift. It is a quiet one.

## The Locals, and the Long Negotiation with Becoming

The traveler should bring, more than anything, a willingness to be changed.

The colonists of the Long Air are not unfriendly. They are not friendly in the way off-world people are friendly. They have lived in the chord of the pillars for nine generations, and they have learned that friendship, here, is a slower thing — a thing of repeated knocks through the bone, of shared cups of condensate-water, of long evenings spent listening to the wind change key. They will not rush the traveler. They will offer small kindnesses — a warmer room, a better window, a hand on the arm when the walkway sways. They will be curious about the traveler, but they will not press the curiosity. They will wait.

The traveler should wait too.

The traveler will be invited, eventually, into a chamber where a family is gathering for an evening meal. The meal will be unfamiliar. The liquids will be unfamiliar. The sounds — the soft knocks through the bone, the call-birds outside, the wind through the pillar-skin — will be unfamiliar. The traveler should sit. They should eat what is offered. They should drink what is offered, even if it is the small ceremonial cup of pillar-condensate. They should not finish the cup. The locals do not finish the cup. The cup is left on the table as a kind of quiet presence, a reminder that some things are not for drinking.

The traveler should learn, before going, that the older families are no longer entirely water-based. Their blood has thickened. Their children are born with a different set of assumptions. They still need water, but less of it, and they have learned to pull moisture from the air on cold nights with a skill the traveler will find uncanny. The traveler should not stare. The traveler should not ask too many questions about it on the first visit. The questions can come later. There will be a later.

The traveler should also know that the colonists are not yet a different species, but they are becoming one. The traveler who stays long enough — who stays a year, who stays five — will find themselves pulled a little way into that becoming. Not in the body, not in the blood. In the breath. In the way they listen to the wind. In the way they knock on the wall of their chamber when they leave in the morning. In the small, slow, irreversible ways a place changes a person.

## The Leaving, and the Long Slow Unbecoming

The traveler will, eventually, leave. They will pack their water and their masks and their gloves and their lotions. They will say their farewells through the bone — a soft knock, a soft answering knock. They will walk the suspended walkway to the shuttle or the equivalent and step inside and feel the door close behind them.

And then, for a long time after, they will hear the wind. Not the wind of the place they have returned to — that wind is thinner, that wind is wetter, that wind moves through trees and not through pillars — but the wind of the Long Air, sounding in their head, a low bass note that they sleep inside, the way the colonists sleep inside it.

They will dream of rain. They will wake and find that the rain has come, in the ordinary way of water-worlds, and they will be grateful for it, and they will be unable, for a long time, to say why the rain feels like a small loss.

This is the third gift. It is the one the traveler cannot prepare for. It is the one the traveler will carry home.

---

To come prepared to the Pale Plateau of Long Air, then, is to come prepared to be unmade a little. To come with water enough for the body's small needs and patience enough for the mind's slow ones. To come with warm layers and a good mask and ear protection that is not ear protection and a small lamp for the dim and a notebook for the fauna and a willingness to learn the bone-knock and a willingness to drink from the small ceremonial cup and a willingness to leave the cup unfinished. To come expecting a sparse dim plateau and to find, instead, a loud place, a strange place, an old place, a place that has made its peace with the absence of water in its own way and asks only that the traveler make their own peace with the presence of the wind.

The pillars stand. The wind moves through them. The call-birds sing. The colonists knock softly through the bone. The plateau goes on. The traveler, having been there, will go on too, but a little differently, and for a long time they will not be able to say how.
