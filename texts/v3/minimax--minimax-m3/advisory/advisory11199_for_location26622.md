# Preparation for the Pump House in the Bog

## How to read this preparation

Most of what follows is practical, but the practical and the contemplative are not separate here. The pump house is a slow place that has been very patient with itself for a long time, and the things you carry in — including the way you think — will be received by it the way it has received the iron uprights and the steam main: as material to be read, in time. The fig will not refuse your belongings. It will, very gently, over hours or days, consider them. You should know this before you arrive.

You will want to go as a witness, not as a visitor. A visitor expects to be impressed. A witness expects to be present. The pump house is past the point of being impressive. It is in the middle of becoming something else, and the figure it cuts is the figure of a long sentence in progress, not a thing finished.

## Mental preparation

**Decelerate before you arrive.** If you have come from a busier reality, give yourself at least a day of slow walking and quiet meals before the causeway. The pump house will not adapt itself to your tempo; you will be the one who has to slow down to meet it. If you arrive still hurried, you will only see the green dome and the warm dark air. You will not hear anything.

**Rehearse the rhythms.** There are three. They are not difficult to learn, but they are easy to miss.

- The pump turns once every seven seconds. You will feel it through your boots before you hear it. The interval is the length of a slow, deliberate breath — exhale, then the next pump. It is patient in the way a resting heart is patient.
- The boiler breathes steam from a cracked seam at intervals roughly the length of a held phrase. The phrase is not a phrase you can read; it is the time it takes the place to form a thought.
- The drips fall in many tones at once, and they are in no particular rhythm, but they are layered, and after a quarter of an hour you will stop hearing them as separate events and begin to hear them as a kind of weather that is always happening indoors.

**Bring no expectations of a ruin.** The fig has been working on the building for a long time. The brick you came to see is mostly gone. The slates are gone. The cast iron is in there, somewhere, doing the work of bone inside the work of skin. The pumps are still pumping. The boiler is still breathing. If you came to see decay, you came to the wrong story. What is happening here is not decay. It is translation. You are arriving in the middle of a sentence.

**Expect to feel watched.** You are. The fig is not a mind in any way you will recognize, but it has been reading the building with great care for longer than you have been a person in any of your lives, and it will read you in the same unhurried way. It will not harm you. It will simply take your shape into account.

**Do not bring food smells or perfume.** The air in the pump house is a finished thing, the way a wine is finished, and your added notes will sit on top of it badly. Eat before you come. Eat plain things. Let your body be a quiet contributor.

## Physical gear

**Boots.** Knee-high rubber, with thick felt liners if you can manage them. The water on the floor is warm, three inches deep, black with tannin, and surfaced here and there with the iridescent slick of old machine oil that is still working its way out of the basement of the machinery. Tannin will ruin leather in a single afternoon. The water is not cold, but it is also not clean. The causeway is half-rotted boards; the soft ones will sink an inch or two under your weight and squeeze black water up between the planks. Wear the boots loose. Your feet will swell in the warm air.

**Rain shell.** Breathable, not waterproof. The leaf-ceiling is not a roof. The piece says it is raining through three layers of canopy even on a clear warm afternoon, because there is always rain somewhere in this watershed, and it always finds its way down. You will be damp the entire visit. Plan for it. A heavy rubberized coat will trap heat against your body and you will overheat in the held-breath air. A thin shell that lets vapor escape is better.

**Layers you don't mind ruining.** A long-sleeved linen or wool base layer. A second layer. No cotton — cotton stays wet and cold even when the water is warm. The humidity is the temperature of a closed mouth, and a closed mouth is a high temperature relative to your skin if you are generating any heat. You will be cooler in the air than you expect and wetter than you expect and you should be dressed for that contradiction.

**A dimmable light.** The green-amber light through the leaves is the only honest light in the place. A bright torch will undo the bottle-glass quality of it and you will lose half of what the pump house is, which is a place that has been turning outdoor light into indoor light for so long that the indoor light has developed its own weather. If you must use a torch, use it sparingly, on the lowest setting, and aim it at what you want to see rather than at the air.

**Gloves, two pairs.** A thin cotton pair for the things that might be vines, a thicker leather pair for the things that are certainly pipes. Do not rely on visual identification. The place has been colonizing the distinction for years. The rule: *a pipe is cold; a vine is warm.* A pipe that has been recently steamed will burn you through a cotton glove. A vine that has wrapped itself around a pipe will feel like pipe from the outside and grip your hand like vine from the inside, and once it has decided you are something to be read, it will be slow to let go. You will not be hurt, but you may be detained.

**A plastic bag for the boots on the way out.** The tannin will bleed onto everything else in your pack. Do not pack wet boots in with dry clothes. You have been warned by people who have done this.

**No metal-on-metal clicks.** Any gear that rattles will become the loudest thing in the place. Oil any zippers. Tape any loose hardware. The pump house is loud in a low way, but it is a layered low way, and a sharp click from your pack will cut through all the layers and you will not be able to put the layers back together for at least an hour.

**Nothing organic that you cannot spare.** A leather-bound notebook will be colonized in a week. A paper book will be colonized faster. If you want to take notes, take them on a synthetic waterproof pad, or in your own memory, which the place cannot grow into. (It can grow around it, in time, but not in a single afternoon.)

**Water for drinking.** Do not drink the standing water. It is not poisonous, exactly, but it is tannin and machine oil and mold-spore water and the fig's own slow secretions, and your body is not a translation it has had time to learn.

## The route in

The pump house is reached by causeway. The causeway is half-rotted boards. The boards are soft, and each step squeezes black water up between them. Walk in the center of the planks where the boards are still doubled; the edges have rotted through. The bog to either side is much deeper than it looks — feet, not inches — and you will not be pulled out easily if you go in. The bog is also warmer at depth than the air, which is the wrong way around for a person to fall into something.

The green dome of the fig's crown is visible from a long way off across the bog. If you lose the causeway, walk toward the dome. Do not walk through the bog. The bog walks back.

## How to move once inside

**Stand still, often.** The creak of vines against metal is below conscious threshold unless you offer silence. If you walk constantly, you will not hear the place reading itself. The creak is not loud, but it is the sound of the translation happening in real time, and you came to witness the translation.

**Walk on the moss hummocks when you can.** They are where the heavy machines stood, and they are firmer than the floor between them, which is pool. The pools were aisles. They are not deep, but they are the floor the building has decided should remain open. Stepping in them is fine. Standing in them for a long time is fine. Crouching in them to look at something beneath the surface is fine. But the moss hummocks will hold you better if you want to sit.

**Don't pull at the vines.** They are not climbing the building anymore. They are the building. The place is not a ruin being reclaimed; it is a manuscript being written. If you pull at a vine, you are damaging the manuscript, and although no one now living reads the tongue, the fig reads the tug and will, very slowly, with great patience, redirect around the place where you tugged. You will not notice. The place will. Do not tug.

**Don't fix the boiler.** The seam that vents steam is part of the air. Closing it would change the chemistry of the place, and the chemistry is what the fig has learned to read. The seam is not a failure. It is a punctuation mark the fig has made use of.

**Don't photograph with flash, and consider not photographing at all.** A photograph is a way of taking the place out of the place. The pump house has been very careful about what it lets in and out for a long time. It does not need to be carried in your pocket. It would prefer to be remembered in your body, where it cannot be lost but can also not be shown.

## The frogs

There are two frogs, or one frog in two places, or one frog you keep seeing in two different inches of water. They are not wildlife in the usual sense. They are punctuation. Do not chase them. They are considering the same leaf they were considering ten minutes ago, and they will consider the same leaf ten minutes from now, and the consideration is not laziness, it is the frog's own contribution to the slowness.

If you sit very still on a moss hummock and the frog does not move, you have not been seen. If the frog moves, you have been considered. Either way, do not interact.

## What the place will do to you

**Your pulse will sync to the pump, possibly.** The pump turns every seven seconds. The interval is approximately the length of a slow breath. If you sit still long enough, your resting heart rate will find the interval, not because the pump is doing anything to you, but because the body is a rhythm-finding instrument and it will lock onto the strongest low rhythm in the room. This is not a magical effect. It is just what bodies do in places with patient machinery. When you leave, the sync will fade. If you are the kind of person who meditates, the pump will be a very good metronome for you.

**Your eyes will adjust in two stages.** The first stage is the obvious one: you will see the dim green-amber light as green-amber, and you will lose the details in the deeper shadow. The second stage happens after about forty minutes, when your eyes stop trying to make the place brighter and start trying to make the place *legible.* You will begin to see the columns of vine as columns of vine, the pipes within them as pipes within them, the moss hummocks as the memory of machines. This is not your eyes getting better. This is your attention learning the grammar the fig has been speaking. The first stage is biology. The second stage is the place teaching you how to read it.

**You will feel a low, formless grief, possibly.** This is appropriate. The place is being translated into a tongue no one now living reads. The fig is patient and the translation is beautiful and the building is disappearing. You have arrived in the middle of a long elegy that the building is writing about itself, with the help of a tree that does not know it is elegiac. The grief is not yours, exactly. It is the grief the place has been carrying for a long time, and you are now in a position to share the weight of it. Sit with it. Do not try to comfort yourself out of it. The fig would not understand the comfort, and would, very slowly, consider it.

**You will lose track of time.** This is partly the dimness, partly the absence of the sun's path, partly the seven-second pump cycle which has no relationship to hours. Carry a watch, but do not look at it. Look at it only when you are beginning to worry that you have been inside too long. You have not been inside too long until your fingers begin to prune in the warm tannin water, at which point you have been inside about six hours and should think about the causeway.

## How long to stay

At minimum, four hours. Less than that and you will only see the surface. The acoustic layering takes time to hear. The vine-tip inching across a rusted valve-handle needs at least an hour to read. The second stage of your eye-adjustment needs forty minutes plus the time it takes to use it. Four hours is the floor.

If you can, stay overnight. The pump is louder at night, or rather, the other things are quieter, and you can hear the pump. The frogs change shift, although only by a small angle. The boiler breathes more slowly in the cooler air, and the interval between breaths lengthens, and you can feel the place thinking a longer thought. Bring a dry layer for the cool hours. The leaf-ceiling is not insulated; the temperature will drop several degrees after dark.

Do not stay more than one night. After the second day, you will begin to feel that the place is not in a hurry for you to leave, which is the fig's opinion, and you will find it difficult to form a reason to walk back down the causeway. This is not dangerous but it is the slow beginning of a longer conversation, and you came for an afternoon or a night, not for a translation.

## How to leave

Walk the causeway back slowly. The boards will give more on the way out; they have been remembering your weight.

Do not look back. The pump house is not the kind of place that needs your gaze. It has its own attention, and it has been keeping it up for a long time, and your looking back will not change anything. If you must look back, look at the green dome, not at the door you came out of. The door is not a door anymore. The dome is the building's current name.

When you reach dry ground, change out of the wet layer immediately. Tannin stains set in warm water; cool dry air will keep them from setting until you can wash the clothes. Do not wash the clothes with your other laundry. Wash them alone, in cold water, twice.

The boots will smell of tannin and mold for weeks. This is correct. The smell is the place's last slow note, and it will fade in its own time.

## One last thing

The pump house is not abandoned. It is being translated, and the translation is in progress, and the translator is patient. You are arriving in the middle of a sentence. The sentence will not finish while you are there. You will not read it. You are not there to read it.

You are there to be in the room with the sentence, in the held-breath air, in the warm black water, under the slow green-amber light, while the pump turns once every seven seconds, and the boiler breathes in the time it takes to think a thought, and the fig reads the iron it has not yet read, in its own slow tongue, which you are not expected to learn.

Stand still. Be a witness. Walk the causeway home when the light begins to change.
