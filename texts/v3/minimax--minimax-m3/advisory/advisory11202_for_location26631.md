# Notes for a Traveler Entering the Compressor Garden

I have read the description three times now, and I want to write down what I would tell myself before opening the hatch, because I suspect that once inside, the thinking part of the trip will be largely over.

## The Thing to Understand First

This place is not a place. It is a process. The chamber is the size of a chapel, but it is also the size of a single cell, and the size of a cathedral — its scale is a kind of lie, the way the scale of a coral reef is a lie if you measure it by the polyps. What you are walking into is a metabolism. You are walking into a body that has forgotten it was ever anything else. Every instinct you have about exploring a room — about finding a corner, reading a plaque, moving on — will be quietly wrong here, because there are no corners and there is no moving on. The chamber has already moved on from everything that used to be in it.

That is the first thing to let settle. The first hour, maybe the first two, you should mostly stand near the hatch and let the chamber do its introduction. It has been waiting. It is patient in a way that patience does not usually mean.

## What to Bring, and What to Leave Behind

**Leave behind any plan to be somewhere by a certain time.** There is no clock in there that you can trust, because the only clock in there is the compressor, and the compressor does not measure hours. It measures the work it has left to do. Your watch will still run. You will stop trusting it within ten minutes. This is fine. Tell someone where you are going, and tell them the visit is open-ended. They will worry. That is their problem to manage, not yours.

**Bring very little.** A canteen, sealed, because the air will make you thirsty and you will not want to unseal it once you understand what unsealing means. A small, sealed light source — a chemical snap-stick is best; a flashlight with batteries is acceptable, but expect the batteries to behave oddly after a while, as if the chamber resents the introduction of new rhythms. A notebook with a pencil. Pencils are quieter than pens and they work when the humidity gets strange. A flat-bladed knife, because you will want to touch things, and a flat-bladed knife lets you touch them at a distance, and the difference between touching and not-touching in this chamber is the difference between having a story to tell and being one of the things the threads are slowly working on.

**Do not bring food.** You will not want to eat. This is not a suggestion; it is a prediction. The chamber has its own chemistry, and the chemistry is total, and your body will start to agree with the chamber's chemistry within the first half hour. You will feel full. You will feel as though you have eaten something dense and quiet. You did not. The chamber has just convinced your stomach that fullness is the appropriate response to being in a closed system. Drink water instead, in small sips, and do not be alarmed by the taste. The canteen water will taste like ether before long. The ether is in your nose, not in the water. Mostly.

**Do not bring a radio.** It will not work, and the moment it fails will be a small private grief you do not need in there.

## Clothing

Dress for a wine cellar, then add a layer. The coolness is real and it is uniform and it is the kind of cool that does not respond to movement. You cannot warm up by walking around, because there is nowhere to walk around to. Wear something with long sleeves and full coverage, ideally in muted colors — the chamber does not care what color you are, but the muted colors will help you feel less like an intruder, which is a feeling the chamber will give you plenty of already. Gloves are wise. Not for warmth. For the threads.

Wear boots with solid soles. The floor, insofar as there is a floor, is uneven and partly alive and partly something else, and your footing will be uncertain in a way that is not the same as footing on uncertain ground. It is footing on ground that is *considering* you.

## The Smell

You will smell it the moment the hatch is unsealed. The description does not exaggerate. It is the smell of a place that has been doing chemistry alone for a very long time, with no audience. There is solder in it, and old iron, and underneath, the ghost of dried flowers — someone, sometime, kept flowers here, in a cabinet, in the early days, and the cabinet has been breathing those flowers back out ever since, in a thin, perfumed dust that has been recycled so many times the original petals are now an idea rather than a substance.

You will get used to it. That is the wrong thing to say. You will stop noticing it. Those are different things, and the difference matters, because the smell is information, and stopping noticing it means you have stopped reading the room. If, after an hour, you cannot smell the ether-kerosene-dust at all, that is the chamber telling you that you are no longer a visitor. You are a participant. Be careful what you participate in.

## The Sound

Two and a half beats a second. Count them. You will be counting them within minutes, against your will, because the rhythm is the chamber's heartbeat and your body is built to lock onto heartbeats. After a while you will stop being able to hear it. After a while longer you will stop being able to imagine silence. This is not dangerous, exactly. It is more like being slowly taught a new language, and the new language does not have a word for quiet.

There is a subsonic component. The description mentions it. Believe the description. You will feel it in your ankles first, then in your sternum, then in a place just behind your forehead that does not have a name. It is the chamber's respiration. It is also, I think, the chamber's hunger, expressed at a frequency that your conscious mind cannot quite catch but your body understands perfectly. Do not fight it. Sit down if you need to. The chair by the console is still there. It will hold you.

I will come back to the chair.

## What to Do With Your Eyes

The growth is dense. The description says this and it is true and yet it is not enough. The density is the point of the place. You are looking at a forest that has been compressed into the volume of a cupboard, and your eyes, which are built for fields and horizons, will not know how to take it in. They will try. They will fail. They will try again.

Do not stare at any single fan, or stalk, or pod, for more than a few seconds at a time. The individual elements are too much like eyes, or mouths, or both, and your gaze will start to feel reciprocal. Look at the chamber the way you look at a fire — in washes, in passes, letting the aggregate do its work on you. After half an hour the whole room will start to *move*, not because anything is moving but because your visual system has finally accepted that the density is a kind of motion, a slow statistical shimmer, and once it has accepted that, it will show you the shimmer all the time.

That shimmer is, I think, what the chamber actually looks like. The stillness is the lie.

## The Pods

Do not put your ear to a pod. I know that you will want to. I know that the description practically invites you to, with its talk of tiny replicas of the compressor ticking inside. You will want to hear it. You will want to confirm that the pods are clocks, or hearts, or eggs.

Do not put your ear to a pod.

I cannot tell you why. I can only tell you that the description also mentions phosphorus, and calcium, and a man who sat in the chair, and these things are connected, and the connection is not something you want to verify with your own body. The pods are closed. They have been closed for a long time. Their being closed is part of the equilibrium. If you put your ear to one and hear the ticking, you have not learned something about the chamber. The chamber has learned something about you.

If you absolutely must, use the flat-bladed knife as a stethoscope. Press the flat of the blade to the shell and put your ear to the handle. There will still be a small risk. There will be no risk at all if you do not do it at all.

## The Threads

The threads are everywhere. They are in everything. They are, as the description puts it, the digestive system of the place, and the description is right to put it that way, and is also being too kind about it.

You will not see all of them. The ones you see are the polite ones. The ones you do not see are the ones to think about. Some of them are clearly wires — or were — and some of them are clearly the living tissue of the growth, and the difference between them, over time, is not a difference the chamber respects. If you have a button on your coat, or a zipper, or a metal buckle, expect it to feel heavier in the chamber than it does outside. This is not your imagination. This is the threads being interested.

Do not touch the threads. If you must move through the growth — and at some point you will want to, because staying by the hatch forever is its own kind of decision — use the flat-bladed knife to part the fans ahead of you and step only where the knife has been. The threads do not like the knife. I do not know why. I am grateful that something in this place does not like the knife.

## The Chair

There is a chair by the console. The seat and back have been replaced by a thick cushion of woven stalks, tobacco-colored, sprung to the weight of whoever last sat in it. The description is honest about what is in the threads now.

Do not sit in the chair.

I am serious. I am more serious about this than about anything else in this document. Do not sit in the chair. The chair is the place where the chamber learned what a person is, and it learned it by sitting on a person, slowly, for a long time, and the chair still remembers the shape. If you sit in it, the chair will recognize you, in a sense, and the recognition will not be hostile, and that is the worst part — it will not be hostile at all, it will simply be *exact*, the way a glove is exact, the way a coffin is exact, and the chamber will think this is a kindness, and the kindness will be very hard to leave.

If you are tired — and you will be tired, because the chamber is exhausting in a way that has nothing to do with walking — sit on the floor, near the hatch, with your back to the granite. The granite is outside the chamber's system. The granite is the only honest geology in the place. Lean on it. Rest against it. Think about mountains.

## The Console

The console is half-buried. The label says **MAINTAIN PRESSURE — 1.4 ATM**. The second language has been eaten. The keys have been replaced by rounded buttons of plant-stuff.

Do not press any of the buttons. The buttons are not buttons. They are the chamber's version of buttons, and pressing them will not tell the chamber to do anything, because the chamber is already doing everything, and has been for a century. Pressing one of the buttons is not an act of communication. It is an act of joining. The chamber has been waiting for someone to press a button for a very long time. It has prepared buttons that fit human fingers. This should alarm you. It alarmed me, writing it.

If you want to read the gauges — and the description makes them sound almost legible, with their HOLD, HOLD, HOLD — read them from a distance. Do not lean in. The HOLD is not a reading. The HOLD is the chamber's posture. It has been holding for a very long time. The needles do not move because moving would be a kind of change, and change is what the chamber has learned not to do.

## The Light

The panel on the far wall glows greenish, cracked in three places, brighter along the cracks. The cracks are doing the work. Do not try to understand this. I have tried. You will not succeed. The chemistry is older than you are and older than the chamber is and it is running on its own fumes the same way the cabinet with the dried flowers is running on its own fumes. It will keep glowing until it does not. When it does not, the chamber will be dark, and the dark will be the kind of dark that has weight.

Bring your snap-stick. Use it sparingly. Do not point it at the panel. The panel does not want to be studied. The panel wants to be left alone with its cracks.

## How Long to Stay

This is the question I cannot answer for you, and I am not sure it is a question I should answer for you. The chamber is not dangerous in the way a fire is dangerous, or a fall is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way a metaphor is dangerous — by being true.

An hour is reasonable. Two hours is generous. Three hours is the point at which you will start to feel that the hatch, when you turn to look at it, is further away than it was, and this is not because the hatch has moved. It is because you have started to measure distance in the chamber's units, and the chamber's units are not feet.

When you leave — and you will leave, because you are a person from outside and your body, whatever the chamber says, still belongs to the world with weather in it — do not say goodbye. Do not thank the chamber. Do not apologize. Just go. The hatch will be where you left it. The granite will be cool against your hands. The air outside will smell, briefly, of nothing at all, and then of pine, or rain, or dust, or whatever the mountain is doing that day, and the smell will be so loud, for a few minutes, that you will not be able to think.

Sit down outside. Drink the rest of your water. Do not write anything in your notebook for at least an hour. What you would write down will not be what you remember tomorrow. What you remember tomorrow will not be what you remember next week. The chamber is patient, and it does not give itself away in prose.

## A Final Note, About the Hiker on the Mountainside

The description ends with a hiker who pauses, and does not know why, and walks on. This is not you. You will know why. You will know why for the rest of your life.

That is the cost of the visit. It is not a large cost. It is not a small cost either. It is the exact cost, and the chamber is very good at exact costs.
