# Preparing for the High Boilerfields

The first thing my gut tells me is that this place does not want you to be loud. Not in the sense of asking — in the sense that it has arranged itself to make loudness costly. The shape of the silence here is doing work on you whether you like it or not. So before I think about anything else, I think about how to *be* in a place that has already decided how sound should move.

I would pack less than I think I need. Anything that rattles, clicks, jingles, or swings is a small cruelty to your own ears in that damp swallowed air. A loose thermos. A zipper pull. A folding knife with a loose clip. They will find the nearest three pipes and stop there, and you will hear them forever. I would wear soft things. Wool, brushed cotton, oilskin — anything that doesn't crackle. My boots would be the smoothest-soled pair I own, and I would walk slowly on those creaking boardwalks because they are the only path, and they are old, and you can feel the iron under them answer you.

## Body

The heat is the first real fact of the place, and it is not the kind of heat I would pack for. It is a low, permanent, all-over warmth — the air always a few degrees above what the latitude would suggest, the ground warm through boot leather, water hot where water collects. There is no wind, so sweat does not get pulled off you. I would dress in layers I could lose. A base layer I could throw away if it got damp and never dried. A second wool layer. Nothing waterproof, because waterproof means the moisture stays on me. I would bring a second pair of everything, sealed, because I am certain that what I am wearing at the end of day one will not be dry again by morning.

The pools are hot. I would want to know, before I stepped in anything, *how* hot. The water that comes up warm is one thing; the water that comes up from near the source is another. I would treat any standing water as if it might scald. I would not sit on a pipe-trunk without first laying a hand on it bare and counting. I would not lean against one at all if I could help it, because some of them are *growing* and I have no way of knowing if a slow extrusion under my shoulder would do anything to me, and I would rather not find out.

The light is poor except at noon. My eyes would need to adjust twice a day, fast — full dark at dusk when the generator comes on, full white for an hour around midday. I would bring something to shade my eyes against the noon light, because that hour of direct sun in a polished iron forest would be punishing. I would also bring a small light of my own for the hours when the valley is in its blue shadow and the steam is thick. Not bright. The settlement has electric light and I would not want to outshine it.

## Air

The air would be the part of this place I trust least.

It is humid. It is warm. It is full of the green smell of wet moss and wet iron and the faint mineral tang of whatever is below. Breathing it for an hour would feel like drinking something. I would assume — and I would want to ask the people in the settlement to confirm — that the steam itself is not just water. Geothermal water carries things. I would not assume it is safe to breathe deeply over the basins, even if the air feels clean. I would not assume the small pale orchids are not a warning. I would not eat anything I picked. I would not drink from any pool that was not clearly the outlet stream at the settlement, and even then I would boil it first or not drink it at all.

The mushrooms that glow faintly — I would not investigate them. I would not sit near them in the dark to see them better. I would not bring any part of them home.

## Movement

The boardwalk is the only way through the iron forest, and that is the second fact of the place. I would plan my days around it. I would not step off it. I would not try to cut between pipe-trunks even where the moss looked inviting, because the garden is *dense* and *layered* and the description says you would be caught in it within a dozen steps and I would take that at its word. There is no soil. If I went down through the moss, I do not know what I would land on, and I do not know what would land on me.

The boardwalk is also old and it creaks. I would wear gloves with grip. I would test handrails before I leaned into them. I would walk single file if I was with anyone, because two people abreast on that path is asking the structure to do more than it was probably asked to do for a long time.

I would also assume the boardwalk is the only safe ground and treat the rest of the valley floor as a thing to be looked at, not walked on. The pipes that are growing are doing so in places I cannot predict, and a new one pushing up through stone where I put my foot yesterday is the kind of slow surprise I want to know about in advance.

## Time

I would plan to be in the valley at noon and to be at the settlement by dusk, twice if I could manage it. The noon hour is the only hour that is fully itself, and the dusk is when the steam goes orange under the electric lights, and I would not want to miss either. The blue-shadow hours in between are beautiful in their own way but they are the long hours, and I would not want to be moving through the iron forest for the first time in them. I would want my first walk to be in the gold hour. I would want to be already in the settlement before dark, and I would want the settlement to be where I sleep every night I am there.

I would not try to see the whole nine kilometers in one trip. The valley runs northeast and I would walk it in stages, sleeping at the settlement, walking out, walking back, sleeping again. I would let the place teach me the shape of a day before I tried to learn the shape of itself.

## Mind

This is the part I would prepare for most carefully, and the part I would not know how to prepare for at all.

The description is right that the silence is not empty. It is *shaped*. It is the silence of a place that has been alone with itself for a long time, and it puts a weight on you that is not unpleasant but is also not nothing. The deep *tonk* from below — I would not know what to do with that until I heard it the first time, and I would want to be standing still and on the boardwalk when I did. I would want to be somewhere I could put a hand on a pipe-trunk and feel the resonance if it came. I would not want to be caught mid-step on a wet plank when the whole iron forest rang.

I would go expecting to be watched. I would not go expecting to be welcomed. The settlement has thirty people and they have a job to do in the forest and they have done it for a long time, and I would assume that they have seen travelers before and that they have a way of handling travelers that I would do well to learn by watching. I would bring something to trade, but I would not lead with it. I would bring something small, useful, and quiet — a tool of some kind, maybe, or a knife that held an edge, or a length of good cord. I would not bring anything that made noise.

I would ask about the new growth. I would ask what they prune and what they leave. I would ask whether the iron that grows is the same iron that is exported or a different grade, and what the difference is. I would ask whether the moving pipes have always moved or whether the movement is new. I would ask what the orchids are doing in the crooks of branches. I would ask what is in the water. I would not ask any of these questions in a way that assumed the answer would be tidy, because the place is not tidy and the people who work it are not tidy and the answers would not be either.

## What I Would Bring

Two wool base layers. One second wool layer. A soft, quiet outer layer in dull color — no rustling, no bright fabric, no synthetic that would catch on the pipes. A wide-brimmed hat that would shade my eyes at noon and keep the drips off my neck the rest of the day. A second hat, dry, sealed. Two pairs of boots — smooth-soled, broken in, with a lug pattern that would grip wet metal. A pair of leather-palmed gloves with good grip and a second pair sealed. A small, dim, warm-spectrum headlamp, not a flashlight. A water bottle I could fill only at the settlement. A second water bottle, sealed, as backup. A cloth for wiping condensation off anything I needed to read or see. A trade item. A knife. A length of cord. A small notebook with a pencil — pencil, not pen, because pen would skip on damp paper and pencil would not. A sealed pouch for the notebook.

I would not bring a camera unless I was certain it would work in that humidity, and I would not bring anything I would be ruined to lose.

## What I Would Not Do

I would not step off the boardwalk. I would not touch a pipe I had not first touched gently. I would not drink from a pool. I would not eat from the garden. I would not sit in the dark to watch the mushrooms. I would not chase the sound in the moss. I would not try to find what was making the deep *tonk*. I would not assume the heat below was asleep. I would not assume the place was finished. I would not assume it was friendly. I would not assume it was unfriendly. I would not assume the people of the settlement were mine to question just because I had arrived. I would not, under any circumstances, try to take a piece of the iron home without asking, and I would not be surprised if the answer was no.

I would be prepared to be changed by it. I think the place does that. Not in a dramatic way. In the way that a long quiet walk through a place that is not for you changes the pace of your thoughts. I would go expecting to come back quieter than I went in, and I would try to bring enough of myself back to remember the gold hour, and the orange light on the steam at dusk, and the way the iron rang in my chest when the deep *tonk* came up through the floor.
