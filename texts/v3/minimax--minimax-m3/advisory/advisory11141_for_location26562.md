# A Traveler's Preparation for the Greenblack Low

## Reading the Description Before Reading My Packing List

What strikes me first about this place is that it is not hostile in the way that, say, a desert is hostile. A desert wants you dead. This place doesn't want anything from you. You are a weather event. You will pass through and the forest will continue. That indifference is more dangerous than malice, because malice at least implies a relationship.

The second thing that strikes me is the *verticality of the attention required*. Most places in the multiverse can be navigated with your eyes at human height. This place cannot. It asks you to look up and down constantly, and looking up, the description warns, is its own kind of trap. Looking down means you'll put your foot through something that was trying to be asleep.

The third thing is the small strangeness — the 1.3 — and I want to be honest with the traveler about how to hold this. It's not a horror. It's not a puzzle. It's a fact about the place, the way gravity is a fact about Earth. The leaves go up. You will see them. You should not try to explain them. Trying to explain them is a way of standing still for long.

---

## The Question of Why You Are Going

I want to ask this first, because it changes everything.

If you are going because someone told you there was something to find there — a ruin, an artifact, a missing person, a frequency on the radio — then you are going as a *seeker*, and seekers in this place tend to come back wrong, or not at all. The forest does not hide things from you out of malice, but it is indifferent to your searches, and the things you are looking for will be dissolved into the general teeming of the place faster than you can chase them.

If you are going because you are a naturalist, or a cartographer, or a person who simply needs to see what is there — then you are going as a *witness*, and this is the better reason. The forest rewards witnesses. It will not hide itself from you. It will simply go on with its business in front of you, and if you can hold still long enough — without standing still — you will see extraordinary things.

If you are going because you have been called there, in a way you cannot quite explain, then I have no advice for you. You are already in the second stage of something. Go carefully.

## Clothing and Skin Coverage

The locals' rule is: every inch of skin, all the time. This is not a suggestion. The description is explicit about why.

The threats are not the dramatic ones. There is no apex predator hunting you. There are instead a thousand small things, each of which is a weather event to the others and to you. Ants the size of your thumbnail carrying a frog. A spider with a saucer-sized legspan whose web is stronger than the basalt. Moths the size of your hand. Things that you cannot see yet that are already on you.

You want:

**Boots that go above the ankle.** Not because of water, although there will be water. Because of the soft, perforated ground. You are going to step through two inches of leaf litter into spongy rot into fibrous wet into clay into maybe the roof of a burrow. You want your foot supported against uneven collapse. You want a sole stiff enough that you feel the columns beneath you through the boot, because the columns are the only reliably solid thing in this place, and knowing where they are is a way of knowing where the basalt still remembers being lava.

**Light-colored, tightly-woven, full-coverage clothing.** Long sleeves. Long pants. A buff or scarf for the neck. A hat with a brim, but not a brim so wide you cannot look up. The light in this place is dim enough at human height that you will want the hat for rain and for things dripping from above, but you will want to be able to glance at the canopy without obstruction when you need to.

The fabric matters. Cotton holds water against your skin and will chafe you raw in a humid place over the course of a long walk. Wool feels wrong for a rainforest but actually performs: it insulates when wet, it doesn't rot the way synthetics can in this kind of biological load, and it resists abrasion. A merino base layer under tougher outerwear is what I would choose. Synthetics are fine until you tear them, and then the tear runs, and the synthetic becomes a flag for everything that is looking for a way in.

**Gloves.** Thin enough to feel through, but covering the hands. You will be parting vegetation, moving branches, catching yourself on things. The description mentions roots that hang, leaves growing upward, and the general principle that things in this place have not learned to fear you. They will sit on your hand the way the sail-backed lizard sits on the stone: with one eye on you, not recognizing you as a category.

**Eye protection.** Not sunglasses — the light at human height is too dim for those. But something against the dripping from above. A few drops of canopy water in the eye at the wrong moment, and you are wiping your face with your hand, which is a way of standing still for a moment with your attention divided.

## The Question of Standing Still

The description returns to this again and again. Do not stand still for long. Do not mistake stillness for safety.

I think this is the central rule of the place, more central than the skin coverage, more central than the water.

What I think is happening: this forest is a place of continuous small motion. The leaves are doing something the textbooks do not describe. The pale shapes in the tea-colored water are migrating. The canopy stratum has its own weather. When you stop moving, you stop being part of the motion, and the forest notices. Not consciously. The way a pond notices a stone that has been dropped in. The ripples around you become a kind of attention, and the things that were indifferent to you as a passing weather event become interested in you as a stationary one.

I would build your travel around this. Move continuously, but not fast. Fast movement stirs the water, kicks up the rot, alarms the column of ants. Slow continuous movement lets the forest accommodate you the way it accommodates a slow stream of sediment: by building around you.

When you must stop — to check a map, to drink, to look at something — make the stop a *pause*, not a *halt*. A pause has a return built into it. A halt does not.

## Water and What to Eat

The water is tea. It is not blue. It has a slick of something pollen-adjacent on its surface. It contains pale shapes migrating in a direction that is not the current. You do not want to drink much of it unfiltered.

But you also cannot avoid drinking water in this place, because the humidity will leach you from the inside. The air is so saturated with moisture that you will not feel thirsty in the usual way, but you will be losing salt and electrolytes through sweat that does not evaporate — it just sits on you.

You want:

**A water filter with a fine enough mesh to strain out the small shapes.** Not just a purifier — a *filter*. The pale things in the water may not be pathogens in the usual sense, but they are alive, and they are following a smell, and you do not want them following that smell into you. A 0.1-micron filter at minimum. A coffee filter or bandana will not be enough.

**A backup method of purification.** Chemical tablets, or a small UV sterilizer if you have one. The water looks still and clean because of the tea color, which masks a lot. Boiling is good if you have the fuel.

**Electrolyte tablets.** Salt, potassium, magnesium. The humidity will drain you in a way you don't notice until you stop.

For food: do not eat anything you cannot identify with certainty. The forest blooms overnight with pale four-petaled flowers that smell almost chemically sweet, and by the next morning the air is full of different bees drawn from kilometers away. This is not a place where you forage for snacks. Bring what you need, sealed, and accept that you will be hungry.

## Navigation

This is where I want to be most careful with you.

**You cannot rely on landmarks.** A tree you mark on your map today may not exist tomorrow in the way it did. A tree heavy with epiphytes may go over in the night. A section of bank may slip into a river in a slow-motion avalanche. Even the basalt shelves are still being cut back by the waterfalls — the rock is young, the columns are still being shaped, and the place you step today may not be there in a week.

**You cannot rely on the sun.** The canopy is closed at forty meters. You will not see the sun. You will not see the sky. The light that reaches you has been chewed on by a hundred thousand leaves. If you have a compass and a clock, you have a heading and a time, but no way to verify them against the ground.

**You cannot rely on the ground.** The ground is a guess the forest makes. Step carefully. Probe with a stick before committing weight, especially in places where the leaf litter is undisturbed — undisturbed leaf litter is undisturbed because something has been using it, or because the surface is actually a thin crust over water.

What you *can* rely on:

**Sound.** The sound is constant. A sustained hiss above, a chaos at mid-height, a hum below that you feel in your sternum. The sound is the first thing and the last thing. If you record it, you can navigate by it. The hum shifts as you move; the calls shift; you can hear a kilometer of forest by lying still and listening for the phrase that travels across the lowlands like a rumor. A bird, or what sounds like a bird, performs three notes and is answered a half-kilometer away and then a third of a kilometer further. If you mark where you first heard the phrase, and where you are when you hear the third answer, you have a triangle. The forest speaks. Listen.

**The basalt.** The columns are the only solid thing. If you can find the shelves — the places where the basalt broke off in ragged hexagonal teeth — you can walk on them. They are sharp. They are wet. They are reliable. If you get truly lost, find the basalt and follow it. The waterfalls pour off the edges of the shelves; the shelves will lead you to water, and water leads to the edges, and the edges of things are where you can be found.

**A tether, if you have a partner.** A light line, tied to something fixed — a column, a heavy root, a tree you have determined is unlikely to fall — and run out as you go. Not for the whole trip. For the worst stretches. The forest will not respect your line; it will grow over it within days, but within hours it will keep you from making a slow circular mistake.

**Marking the strangeness.** When you see the upward-falling leaves, mark the spot. Not because you will return to it — you probably will not — but because the places where the strangeness is densest may be the places where the rules of the place are thinnest. Avoid them, or seek them, depending on your reason for being there. But mark them.

## Time and Sleep

You cannot sleep on the ground. The ground is a guess the forest makes. It is also where the small pale faces live, the things with the utter blankness of something that has never had a thought. You do not want to sleep next to one of those, even by accident.

You want a hammock. Strong line, a bug net if you have one, a tarp above against the drip. Hang it between two trunks that are alive — not the woven roots of a strangling fig, which is a scaffold for something else. You can tell: a living trunk has bark that is doing something. A scaffold is smooth, integrated, suspiciously clean. If you cannot tell, do not hang your weight from it. The forest has too many ways of being other than it appears.

Sleep lightly. The sound does not stop, and it will wake you often. That is fine. Waking often is not the same as not sleeping.

## The 1.3

I want to address the small strangeness directly, because the description does, and I think it would be dishonest to prepare you for everything else without preparing you for this.

The leaves, when they fall, do not always fall. Sometimes they rise. They climb until they find a branch, stick to the underside, and begin to root. They grow away from the light, in a direction no leaf is supposed to grow. They are green. They are alive.

You will see this. You will want to stop and look at it. You will want to take a sample. You will want to understand.

Do not stand still for long.

I do not know what is happening here. The description is explicit that no one knows. The locals, if there were locals, would not call it strange. This is the way the leaves do.

What I will tell you is that the place has a small strangeness, a 1.3 out of 3, and small strangenesses are the ones that lodge. The dramatic ones — the apex predator, the collapsing cliff, the flash flood — are processed by the body. The small strangeness is processed by something else, and it stays. You will leave the Greenblack Low, and you will think about the leaves for the rest of your life. This is not a warning. It is just a fact about the place. The 1.3 will not leave. If you are not prepared to carry a small strangeness with you for the rest of your life, do not go.

If you are prepared to carry it: it is, by all accounts, worth carrying. The place is extraordinary. The witness is rewarded. You will see the column of ants carrying a frog, and you will see the pale moth breathing, and you will see the spider with the saucer-sized legs and the web that is stronger than the basalt, and you will see the leaves going up, and you will remember all of it.

## What to Bring Home

If you are able to bring something back — and the description does not say you can, and I would not count on it — bring back sound. The forest is a place that is, more than anything, heard. The sustained hiss, the chaos, the hum in the sternum. A recording of the place at three heights — canopy, mid-stratum, ground — is more truthful than a photograph.

Bring back the smell of the pale four-petaled bloom, if you encounter one. It is almost chemically sweet. It will not last. But the memory of it will.

Bring back, if you can bear it, the image of the small pale face in the puncture you have made in the ground. The blankness. The absence of thought. This is not a horror. It is a thing that has not yet learned to fear you. You are, to it, a weather event. There is something in that blankness that is, in its way, peaceful. You may not agree. You may find it unbearable. But it is the thing the place will leave you with, if you let it.

Bring back the fact that you stood in a place where the rules are simple and not yours, and you followed them, and you came back. That is enough.

---

## A Final Word

The Greenblack Low is not a place that wants to kill you. It is a place that does not know you are there. The difference is important. In a place that wants to kill you, you negotiate. In a place that does not know you are there, you accommodate. You move the way the leaves move — which is, as you will learn, sometimes down. You listen the way the soil listens — which is, mostly, not at all. You cover yourself the way the columns hold the water — with seriousness, and without spilling.

You will be a weather event. The forest will pass through you. You will come back, if you come back, changed by a small strangeness that lodges and will not leave.

Go carefully. Go witnessfully. Bring electrolytes.
