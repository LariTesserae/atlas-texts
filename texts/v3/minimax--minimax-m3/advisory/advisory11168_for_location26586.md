# Preparing for the White Basin of Hethran

There is no gentle way to arrive at Hethran. The traveler should understand this first and make peace with it, because every other piece of advice flows from the same fact: this place does not soften itself for visitors, and the people who live there have built their entire culture around the principle that the basin is endured, not enjoyed. You do not come here to be comfortable. You come here to be present in something that is older and stranger and louder than you are.

The first thing the body registers is the sound, and the first thing the mind does is try to refuse it. Resist that refusal. The basin does not negotiate, and the attempt to tune it out is the attempt that will exhaust you fastest.

## The Defile and the Approach

You will not find the defile by wandering. The basin is hidden, and the entrance is a single narrow cut between crumbling escarpments of shale and ochre tuff through which a person must turn sideways to pass. The walk in will be quiet — that is the last quiet you will have. Use it. Stand in the defile and listen to the absence of the singing before you round the last bend. Notice what your own breathing sounds like when there is nothing underneath it. You will want to remember this.

The wind begins before the forest begins. You will feel it on your skin and in your teeth before you hear the chime. By the time you see the first stalks — pale, faceted, catching the sun — the sound will already be inside you.

## The Light

The basin sits low. The air is so dry and so free of diffused particulate that the sun arrives almost in slabs, and the white floor gives it back, and the result is a doubled brightness that the eye was never built to tolerate. Bring eye protection that is not optional — a wrap-style shade, opaque and broad, not sunglasses, because sunglasses admit the prismatic color from the crystalline lattice and that color, sustained for hours, will leave afterimages that persist for days after you leave. The townspeople narrow their eyes by reflex; the reflex takes a lifetime to set. Do not rely on yours.

The light is most punishing between midmorning and midafternoon, when the sun is high enough to rake across the basin without the long shadows of the walls. If you can choose when to be out in the open, choose morning or late afternoon. At noon the white floor and the blazing lattice together make the open basin almost uninhabitable for an unacclimated visitor.

The strange mercy is the color. After the first hour, the moving prismatic smears thrown by the crystalline forest become the only thing the eye can rest in, because the eye cannot rest in white. Learn to look at the color rather than at the white. The townspeople tell time by it. So can you, after a few days — north wind at this hour, south wind at that, the slow rotation of the spectrum across the salt crust. Watch it. It is not decoration. It is the closest thing the basin has to a clock.

## The Sound

This is the central fact of Hethran and the one most travelers underestimate.

The basin is a sounding box. The crystalline forest is tuned to the wind. The wind is constant, because the pressure differentials across the rim never resolve. The result is a sustained, layered, high-pitched music — chime, shrill, almost unbearable — that does not stop. It is not loud in the way a noise is loud. It is loud in the way a frequency is loud. It lives in the upper register of the ear, and it lives there for as long as you are in the basin.

Things that help:

- **Soft ear protection that does not seal you off.** Foam plugs will muffle the chime but also muffle conversation, footsteps, and the warning sounds of loose crust. The townspeople do not seal their ears; they dampen the worst of it with padded headwraps, and they live inside thick-walled rooms whenever they can. Bring a headwrap of soft, dense cloth that covers the ears but does not block them.
- **Something to live inside.** The first hours will be the worst. The body will try to reject the sound as foreign and the rejection will produce fatigue, headache, a kind of internal grinding. The fatigue passes, partly, after two or three days, but it never fully passes. Plan to be indoors for the middle hours of every day.
- **An acceptance of humming.** The townspeople hum to themselves, low, under their breath, when they are out in it. The humming is not musical so much as it is a way of giving the inner ear something of its own to hold onto, so that the basin's frequency does not become the only thing the skull contains. Bring a low, simple tune you do not mind repeating.

The sound also has a peculiar feature that the traveler should know in advance: it carries, and it returns. Conversations had indoors can be heard outdoors, distorted. Strikes made deep in the forest arrive at the walls hours later, repeated. In late afternoon, when the wind shifts, the town sometimes fills with the echo of something that was struck somewhere far away, sometime far ago. You will hear things that are not happening now. Do not chase them. They are not for you.

## The Air

The air is dry beyond any dryness you have likely experienced. The lips crack within hours. The skin of the hands and face tightens and flakes by the second day. The moisture you bring with you is the moisture you will have, because the basin gives nothing back.

Bring:

- A sealed supply of drinking water sufficient for your whole stay. The basin has none to spare for you.
- A lip balm that is waxy rather than oily, because oil evaporates here faster than it can protect.
- A face covering that is dense cloth, not gauze, and that wraps under the chin and over the bridge of the nose. The townspeople wear these always. They are not for the cold. They are for the mineral dust.

The mineral dust is the other thing in the air. The crust is constantly being ground by the wind, and the air carries a fine, sharp particulate that abrades cloth, abrades skin, and gets into the lungs. After a week the breath will feel scraped. Cloth that goes into the basin should be considered disposable. Do not bring textiles you are attached to, and do not bring anything with moving parts that cannot be sealed, because the dust will work its way into them.

## Clothing and Body

The people of Hethran dress in pale cloth wound tight against the abrasive air. Pale is correct. Dark cloth absorbs the sun and becomes unbearable within minutes; pale cloth reflects it back. The cloth should be:

- Pale, dense, and tightly woven. Linen will not last a day. Cotton will survive but will shed fibers into the dust. A fine, tight weave is what you want.
- Wound rather than tailored. Loose fabric flaps and the flapping itself makes a small sound that adds to the din. Wrapped cloth lies still.
- Complete. Throat, wrists, the backs of the hands, the ankles, the neck. The dust finds every uncovered inch and uses it.

Footwear is a particular problem. The salt crust is sharp and the gypsum pans ring like pottery when struck, which means every step you take in the open basin is a small percussive event that the lattice receives and amplifies. The townspeople wear thick-soled, soft-footed wrappings rather than hard shoes. Bring something of this kind if you intend to walk in the forest or on the open flats. If you walk in hard shoes, you will be adding your own music to the basin's, and the basin will return it to you amplified and delayed.

The traveler should expect to be perpetually narrowed-eyed, perpetually half-deafened, perpetually abraded, perpetually thirsty. Plan for this. Do not try to fight the environment on its own terms. The townspeople have not won against the basin; they have only learned how to live inside it without being destroyed.

## The Crystalline Forest

The forest is not a forest in any sense a traveler will recognize. The stalks are translucent, faceted, brittle, and they grow out of the salt crust without roots, or with roots that go only into the upper crust and drink dust. They ring when struck. They chime in the wind. They throw prismatic color in moving smears across the white floor. They are slow — a finger-length in a season — and the oldest of them are as thick as a thigh, partly buried in the drifts of their own fallen spicules.

Do not strike them. The traveler will want to, because they look made to be struck, and because striking one is a temptation almost physical in its pull. A struck stalk sounds beautifully for a moment and then the sound carries across the basin and is held by the walls and returned to the town hours later, distorted, prolonged, and at a frequency that has been known, in some accounts, to crack the salt plaster of older buildings. The townspeople will know if you do this. They will not say so immediately. They will know.

Walking through the forest is possible but should be done with care. The spicules at the base of the larger stalks are sharp enough to cut wrapped cloth and the skin beneath it. The drifts of fallen fiber are unstable and conceal the actual depth of the crust. The traveler who goes into the forest should go slowly, with a sound-sense of where the ground is solid, and should not expect the lattice to behave as a forest behaves. It does not block wind. It does not give shade in any usable sense. It does not provide cover. It sings.

## The Town

The town is built against the leeward wall, where the escarpment is highest and casts the longest partial shadow through the day. The buildings are packed tight, low, thick-walled, made of salt block and cut crust slabs and mud reinforced with the mineral fiber of the very plants that surround them. They are layered — older structures embedded in newer ones, the evidence of past attempts to live here visible in the floors and walls of the present ones.

The streets are alleys hardly wider than two people walking abreast. The wind, even between the buildings, is a thing you lean against. Walking in the town is a constant negotiation with the pressure that pours through the defile and has nowhere else to go.

Inside the houses it is bearable. There is a low hum, an undertone, and the walls and ceilings are shaped to damp it. The traveler should plan to spend the middle hours of every day indoors. The townspeople will offer this. They have thick-walled rooms for visitors, and they understand that an unacclimated person cannot endure what they themselves have spent generations learning to live with.

Conversation in the open is impossible. The townspeople mouth at each other across distances and use gestures and wait. The traveler who tries to speak aloud in the alleys will find that the words are taken up by the basin and not returned in any usable form. Learn a few gestures before you arrive. A beckoning of the hand downward, palm toward the body, means come inside. A flat hand held out and slowly turned means wait. A hand laid across the chest means I am listening.

Inside, speech is possible. Speak softly. The walls absorb the worst of it but they do not absorb all of it, and what passes through them comes back changed.

## Time and the Color

The basin has no water-clock and no shadow-clock in the ordinary sense. The people tell time by the color thrown across the floor by the crystalline lattice. North wind at this hour, south wind at that. The spectrum migrates through the basin on a rhythm that takes a day to learn and a season to read fluently. The traveler who stays long enough will begin to feel time as a thing carried in the eye rather than heard in a chime. This is not unpleasant. It is one of the things the basin gives back, and it is, perhaps, the reason the people have stayed.

## The Buried Town

Underneath the present town there are older towns. Fragments of walls, foundations, the evidence of past attempts to live here. The traveler will not be shown these by default. They are not secret, exactly, but they are not advertised. Some of the older houses have floors that have thinned over generations and now show, through the salt plaster, the geometry of rooms that were not built by the present inhabitants. The traveler should look down sometimes, and should not ask directly about what is beneath. If the townspeople choose to show you, they will. If they do not, the asking will be understood as the kind of question that does not belong to a visitor.

## Health and Endurance

The traveler should expect:

- **Skin and lip damage** within the first two days. Treat early. Cracked skin is harder to heal here than it is elsewhere, because there is no moisture in the air to support the healing.
- **Headache and fatigue** from the sound, especially in the first three days. Rest. Stay indoors. Sleep in the thick-walled rooms. Do not push through.
- **Respiratory irritation** from the mineral dust, particularly by the end of the first week. A damp cloth over the face helps in the houses, less so outside.
- **Visual afterimages** from the prismatic color, particularly if you have used sunglasses. They fade over a week after leaving.
- **A particular kind of disorientation** that does not have a name in most languages. The body is being asked to be present in a place where every sense is overstimulated, and the mind, after a few days, begins to treat the overstimulation as normal. This is not actually normalization. It is the early stage of a kind of surrender. The traveler should know this is happening, and should plan to leave before the surrender becomes the condition rather than the visit.

## Leaving

The basin does not give up its visitors gently. The defile is narrow and must be passed sideways, and the sound of the basin follows you out of it for some distance. It will take a day, perhaps two, before the chime fully fades from the inner ear. The afterimages of color will take longer. The taste of the mineral dust will stay on the tongue for a week.

The traveler who has been in Hethran for more than a few days will, on returning to a watered world, find that the world is too quiet. This is normal. It passes.

## A Final Note

The basin is in its own state. It has been in its own state for a long time. The erosion is settled. The place will not change quickly. The people have learned the rules, and the rules are not negotiable, and the visitor who tries to negotiate will be met with patience and then, if the negotiation continues, with a quiet turning away.

Come quietly. Bring less than you think you need. Wear pale cloth wrapped tight. Drink your water and do not ask for more. Learn the gestures. Stay indoors at noon. Watch the color move across the floor. Hum, if you need to. Do not strike the stalks. Do not chase the late-afternoon echoes. Accept that you will be tired in a way that sleep does not entirely repair.

And on the last day, before you go back into the defile, stand for a moment where the town ends and the white floor begins, and let the sound be in you, and let the light be in you, and notice that you have, without quite deciding to, begun to tell time by the color.

That is when the basin has you. Not before.
