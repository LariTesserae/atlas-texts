# Preparing for The Gullet

The first thing to understand is that this is not a destination. It is a *process* that happens to have a location, and your body will need to be ready for that fact before your mind catches up. You will not arrive at The Gullet and feel that you have arrived anywhere. You will descend into it and feel that you have entered something that is still becoming itself, and that you are visiting a construction site, and that the construction site is also a small ecosystem, and that the ecosystem is also a kind of slow prayer to a city that does not yet exist. Hold all three of those in your head at once, and you will have the right frame. Pick only one, and the place will bewilder you.

---

## Before you go

**Heat acclimatization matters more than anything else.** Do not arrive fresh from a temperate climate expecting to handle this. The heat is not dramatic. It will not seize you the way a desert would. It will simply *sit* in your chest, slowing your lungs a fraction, and you will not notice it slowing until you try to take a deep breath and find that you already have. Spend a week beforehand in humid heat if you can — a sauna habit, hot baths, a humid climate, even just leaving the bathroom door closed while running hot showers. You are training your lungs to accept thicker air and your skin to accept moisture that never leaves it.

**Get your shots.** Tetanus especially. The water has iron in it and has been moving through basalt and old concrete for years; you do not want an open wound meeting it. Hepatitis A is worth the conversation with your doctor. Anything respiratory — make sure your lungs are in good shape. This is not the place to discover you have mild asthma.

**Eat lightly the day before and the morning of.** The heat and humidity suppress appetite, and the smells of the place (wet concrete, machine oil, mineral rock, and the thing no one wants to investigate too closely) will not help. You want to be neither hungry nor full. A banana and some salt is about right.

**Do not plan to drink alcohol the night before.** You will lose more water than you think, and the air down there will not give it back.

---

## What to wear

The single most important article is a **good pair of waterproof boots with aggressive tread**. The floor is the most confused surface in the place — patches of older concrete, newer concrete, temporary steel plating, puddles, channels of water — and your footing will change every few steps. The boots should come up over the ankle. Leather work boots are traditional down there. Sneakers will be soaked within ten minutes and will not dry for the rest of your visit.

**Long pants, light and quick-drying.** Long because of the dripping from the ceiling and the things that hang from cable trays. Light because of the heat. Quick-drying because nothing here is going to dry you. Avoid cotton — it will stay wet and chafe. A lightweight synthetic or merino is right.

**A long-sleeved shirt, same considerations.** You will be tempted to go in short sleeves. Don't. The drip is steady and unpredictable, and the heat *isn't* the kind of heat that rewards bare skin. You want a layer between you and the condensation, the cockroaches, and whatever that long sinuous thing is that moves along the cable trays. A button-up or a zip-front works because you can open it when the machinery is off and close it when it starts.

**A real hard hat.** Not a bicycle helmet. The ceiling is eight meters up but the cable trays and low-hanging pipes come down further in places, and the men will not let you past the access ladder without one. If you don't have one, you will be issued an old one with a sweatband that has seen decades of use. Wear it anyway.

**Gloves.** Light work gloves, leather or nitrile-dipped. You will be reaching for cables, pipes, handrails, rock surfaces. Every one of them is wet and many of them have things living on them. Gloves also save you from the surface temperature of the basalt, which can be uncomfortable even through cloth.

**A headlamp.** Even though there are sodium lamps, half of them flicker or hold their sullen orange without quite illuminating. A headlamp lets you read your own footing, look down a cable before you grab it, see what is on the bench before you set your hat on it. Bring a small handheld as backup; the men lose headlamps to the water channels regularly.

**Eye protection.** Clear safety glasses. Concrete dust when the borer runs, condensation drips at unpredictable angles, the occasional chunk of scale from the ceiling.

---

## What to bring

**Two liters of clean drinking water, minimum.** The water in The Gullet is warm, iron-rich, and *not for you*. It has been migrating up from somewhere below for years and has things growing in it. You may see one of those pale things surface and consider the lights with flat, disinterested eyes. Do not be tempted. Bring your own.

**A few salt tablets or electrolyte mix.** You will sweat more than you realize because the air is so humid you won't feel it evaporate. Replace what you lose quietly.

**A respirator or at least a dust mask.** The air is heavy with moisture and the borer produces fine basalt particulate when it runs. The men work here for years and develop the coughs of men who work here for years. You are visiting. Take the mask.

**Earplugs.** The fans are constant and the borer, when it runs, is a sustained grinding moan that you feel in your molars. The men wear heavy earmuffs. You, as a visitor who will not acclimate, will want earplugs at minimum.

**A small pack, not a big one.** You will be moving through a confined space and you do not want to be the person who keeps catching their bag on pipework. A small waist pack or a vest with pockets is right.

**No phone, or a phone in a sealed bag.** The humidity will find it. The condensation will find it. The drips will find it. If you must bring one, accept that it may not survive the day, and back up what matters before you descend.

**A small gift if you want to make friends.** Coffee. Pastries. Something to share at break time. The workers are patient, slightly amused men who have been waiting on machinery and funding for longer than some of them have been alive, and a small kindness lands well down there.

---

## What to expect from your body

The first ten minutes will feel almost pleasant. The heat is thorough rather than aggressive, and the dim light is forgiving to eyes that have been staring at screens. You will think, *this isn't so bad*.

Around the half-hour mark you will notice that your shirt is damp and has been damp the whole time. You will notice that you have been breathing more slowly without deciding to. The heat will have settled into your chest the way a warm coat settles onto your shoulders. You will stop thinking about it and start being inside it.

After two hours, your feet will be wet inside your boots no matter what boots you brought. Your lower back will be tired from the uneven footing. Your eyes will be working harder than usual because the light is so *propositional* — you will find yourself leaning forward to confirm shapes, and then finding that the shapes were what you thought they were.

The heat accumulates. Do not stay for more than three or four hours on a first visit. The men's lungs work slower down there and they've had years to adjust. Yours have not. Come up before you feel tired. Come up the moment you feel tired.

---

## What to expect from your mind

The dimness is the trick. It is not the dimness of a cave that wants to scare you. It is the dimness of a place that has not yet decided what kind of place it is, and the dimness is part of that indecision. You may find your imagination doing more work than your eyes. The far end of the chamber, where the tunnel bore continues into raw basalt, is a darkness that has weight to it. You will keep looking at it. It will keep not being anything in particular. That is fine. That is the place.

The dripping will become a language. Each drop has a different *tok* depending on what it lands on — steel, rock, water, the top of your hard hat. After an hour you will be hearing individual drops again instead of a general sound. After two hours you will be unable to hear them at all because they have become the floor of perception.

The fauna will move from being a thing you react to to being a thing you navigate around. The first cockroach you see, you will see. The tenth you see, you will step around. The thousandth you see, you will step around without seeing. This happens faster than you expect. The men have stopped being surprised by the fauna in the same way they have stopped being surprised by the heat — the place has simply absorbed them, and it will absorb you too, for as long as you are down there.

---

## How to behave

**Move slowly.** Not because of danger but because of the place. The workers move at the pace the rock allows, and they have been doing it for thirty-one years, and they will know within thirty seconds if you are the kind of person who respects that pace. Walk at their walk. Don't ask them to hurry.

**Do not touch anything without looking first.** This is not a place where you reach behind a pipe and grab whatever is there. The cable you are about to grab may have a brown smear on it that is alive. The bench you are about to set your hard hat on may have a palimpsest of cockroaches on it that have been working out a traffic pattern around it for years. The standing water in the tie-rod holes is full of pale grubs. Look first. Touch second.

**Do not put your hand in any hole in the raw stone wall.** The long sinuous shapes live there. They are not hostile. They are simply *theirs* and you should respect that.

**Do not stare up at the bats for more than a few seconds.** They are at the edge of the light in the highest reaches of the ceiling. They are there, and they have been there, and the old foreman says they were there before construction began, though no one believes him. A glance is respectful. A sustained look is a disturbance.

**Do not ask when the line will be finished.** You will feel the urge. You will not be the first person to ask it. The men will answer you in the patient, slightly amused tone of men who have heard the projected completion date revised downward many times. You do not need to ask. The place is telling you already: *it will be done when it is done, and right now it is not done, and that is the entire dynamic.*

**Speak at half your usual volume.** The chamber is small and the ceiling is low and the walls of poured concrete carry sound. The men speak in measured tones. Match them.

**If a man offers you his thermos, take it.** It will be tea, probably, and it will be hot, and you will not want hot liquid in this heat, and then you will want it very badly. Drink it and hand it back with a word of thanks.

---

## When to leave

You will feel, around the third hour, that the place has accepted you. The heat will feel normal. The dripping will be inaudible. The cockroaches will be part of the floor. You will think you could stay longer. This is the place telling you that you have been there long enough, and that to stay longer is to start *belonging* there, and that belonging to The Gullet is not a thing you want to do as a visitor. It is a thing that takes years, and the men who do it grow into it the way the celery grows into its drip — they are the only things in the chamber that are obviously thriving, and the trade is the rest of your lungs.

Come up while you still feel like a guest. Come up while the sodium lamps still feel like a place you've visited and not a place you live. Drink a full liter of cold, clean water at the surface before you do anything else. Sit somewhere dry. Let your boots drain.

If you go back — and you may want to, because the place has a way of pulling at people — bring the same gear, bring the same patience, and bring less of yourself than you brought the first time. The Gullet is not a place to be filled. It is a place to be visited lightly, and to be thanked for the visit, and to be left alone to become itself.
