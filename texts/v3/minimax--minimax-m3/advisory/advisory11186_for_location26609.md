# How to Prepare for the Continuous City

So you're going. The rail you arrive on is, in practice, the only rail you leave on, and you should figure out its schedule on day one. Not because the city stops you from leaving — it doesn't, not actively — but because it absorbs you. The wet gets in. The sound gets in. The constant motion gets in. The fifth material — the one nobody can name — gets in, and after a while you forget you ever felt separate from it.

Preparation is mostly about boundaries. About maintaining the things that make you you against a place that doesn't really care whether you remain you.

## The Sound Is the First Thing That Breaks People

If you got briefing materials, they probably described the ambient noise level. They were wrong. The sound here isn't loud the way a factory is loud. It's continuous, full-spectrum, and it includes frequencies that aren't supposed to be audible. There are sub-frequencies you feel in your sternum, in your pelvis, in the soles of your feet. There are high pitches that make your back teeth ache and your vision swim at the edges. There are percussive elements and machine tones and voices that aren't voices and tones coming from the structures themselves. The sound has no off-switch. The sound has no quiet hour. The sound has been continuous for so long that silence, in this city, is a kind of rumor.

Get hearing protection before you go. Not the foam plugs from the pharmacy — those will save your hearing, but they'll also make you deaf to the rhythms of the obsidian insects, which carry information, and to the murmurs of the three-eyed pigeons, which are more important than they look.

Get musician's earplugs. The kind with a filter that cuts volume but preserves frequency response. Wear them from the moment you step off the rail. The sound here doesn't ramp up; it just is, all the time. Your ears won't adapt — your brain will just stop noticing the damage.

For sleeping, you need full foam plugs. Accept the silence. The silence is a refuge. Bring a sleep mask too — the city doesn't have a true dark hour and your eyes need to believe it does.

For the worst moments — construction crews working near you, something massive being moved through a narrow space — bring industrial earmuffs. Foam plugs and earmuffs together, when you can stand it.

The sub-frequencies you feel in your chest — there's no protection for those. They will sit in your body like an unburied thought. It's uncomfortable. It's not dangerous, but it's uncomfortable, and you should know it's coming so you don't think something is wrong with you.

## The Wet Is the Second Thing

The air is warm and the air is full of water that hasn't decided what form it wants. It will be on your skin within minutes of arriving. It will be in your clothes within the hour. It will be in everything you brought within the day. Nothing dries here. The locals' clothes are designed for this climate; yours are not.

Synthetics only. Base layers that wick. Nothing cotton — cotton stays wet and grows things you don't want growing. Nothing wool — it felts itself into a heavy sad mat. Nothing leather — it will mold and never recover. Taped seams on everything. The wet finds the seams.

Bring two of everything important, because nothing dries here.

For your feet: waterproof boots with serious tread. Vibram or your universe's equivalent. The floors have a thin film of moisture on them — the description calls it trembling, ringing when struck — so there's a layer of water on every surface. You will slip on everything. Get boots that grip when wet. Bring multiple pairs of synthetic socks. Change them twice a day. Foot powder — the antifungal kind hikers use. Your feet will macerate in hours if you don't take care of them.

For your skin: anti-chafe balm for every place skin meets skin or fabric. A light barrier cream. The orange-black mineral crust that creeps along the older foundations — don't touch it. It looks like rust, it's not rust, and I wouldn't find out on my skin. The lichens that look like moss and aren't moss — same advice.

For your bags: dry bags. Roll-top closures. Your backpack, your daypack — put them inside dry bags inside other dry bags. The wet gets into zippers. The wet gets into everything.

For documents: pencil, not pen. The wet will make ink run. Pencil doesn't care. A paper map, because GPS satellites here may or may not work depending on the day, and the anomalous physics makes compasses drift. Bring a real compass. Update your position constantly. Mark the rail station. Mark your lodgings. Mark every landmark you trust, because in this city the landmarks move.

## Sleep Is the Third Thing

The city doesn't sleep. The crowds move through the narrow channels in rivers that never slow, never thin, never stop. Your body will insist it's the middle of the night. The crowds will still be moving. The construction crews will still be working. The sound will still be on.
