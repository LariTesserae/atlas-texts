# Preparation for a recently-drowned mountain range

## What the place actually is, in case you're deciding whether to go

Before you pack anything, understand what you're agreeing to. The description is precise about it: this is a young landscape, either one forming now or one that became this recently and catastrophically. The water is somewhere between ten and maybe fifty meters deep over most of it — deep enough that a free diver can reach the troughs and short of it, deep enough that a snorkeler sees only the upper canopy. The water is bath-warm. The rocks are knife-sharp. The place is essentially silent. There are no large animals. The plants are dense, confident, and not quite the ones you think they are.

That combination — warm shallow water, sharp rock, sparse fauna, dense flora — is not dangerous in the way a coral reef or a kelp forest is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way a workshop full of brand-new tools is dangerous: nothing is trying to hurt you, but nothing has been worn down yet, and the edges are still on.

## Body and gear

**Swim capability is non-negotiable.** You will spend most of your time either in the water or with wet feet on emerged basalt. A snorkel and mask at minimum. If you intend to leave the upper ten meters and follow a trough down to wherever it bottoms out, you want a small recreational diving setup — not a full technical rig, just enough for twenty minutes at twenty meters, with a cutting tool clipped to the harness. The troughs are described as narrow enough that you could almost touch both sides at once. That is good news for visibility and bad news for the orientation of anyone who gets turned around.

**Protect the skin you have.** The ridges were mountains a geological moment ago. They are not weathered. Anywhere you brace a hand, knee, or fin against them, you are putting weight on a chisel edge. Bring booties or light wetsuit shoes with hard soles; the emerged peaks will cut bare feet through to the tendon. A thin neoprene suit — long-sleeved, full-length — is the right answer. It guards against the rock, against sunburn from light coming through the surface, and against the small abrasions that become the large infections a day later. In warm water you'll overheat in a thick suit; in nothing you'll overheat less and bleed more.

**Bring a mask that lets you see green.** Standard dive masks will be fine; the water is clear where the silt hasn't been kicked up, and the light that reaches the rock is described as soft green-gilt. Polarized lenses help with glare off the surface if you spend time looking up.

**A small cutting tool, accessible without looking.** Real kelp forests snag divers; this is a denser analog of that. Whatever the local stipes are made of, they trail down in curtains and may layer onto each other. A diver's knife or a pair of shears clipped to the chest strap.

**A float and a line.** A small inflatable and a length of thin line. Drop the float at the spot you entered the water from. In an underwater mountain landscape with parallel ridges all running in roughly the same direction, every trough looks like the trough you came in on. The float is your only anchor to a specific place.

**Do not rely on compass, GPS, or radio.** A different universe may have a different magnetic field, no satellites, or a radio environment that absorbs the signal. The description says sound doesn't carry. Plan to navigate by landmarks and by the float line, not by instruments.

## Mindset and pace

This place rewards slowness. The water moves in cycles that take hours. The plants move at a pace you have to lean in to see. The single small fish mentioned in the description holds position all morning without waiting for anything. If you arrive with the expectation of an activity-rich dive — a reef, a wreck, a wall — you will come back impatient and disappointed, and you will have stirred up the silt, which is the one thing the place does not easily recover from.

Resist the urge to touch what isn't necessary. The silt at the trough bottoms lifts in clouds and resettles in patterns the description calls "faintly deliberate." That is a polite way of saying the bottom is being rewritten. Whatever you kick up, you are part of the rewriting for the next few hours.

The plants are confident, not tentative. They are not testing the water; they are the water's tenants. Treat them as established, even if you are told they aren't. Whatever they are, they have decided this place is theirs. That isn't a hazard, exactly, but it is the right frame.

## Hazards, ordered by likelihood

1. **Cuts from the rock.** By far the most common injury. Treat any cut from this stone as needing thorough cleaning; the rock is sharp because nothing has polished it, and a knife-cut from a freshly folded surface is exactly the kind of wound that gets infected in warm water.
2. **Entanglement in the canopy.** Less likely than in a true kelp forest but possible. A calm, slow swimmer pulling free from a single stipe by hand usually gets clear in a second; panic and thrashing make it worse.
3. **Disorientation.** Parallel ridges, uniform water, no wind, no sound. The brain will, after an hour or so, lose track of which trough is which. The float line exists for exactly this reason. Reorient yourself to it regularly.
4. **Heat strain.** Warm water plus exertion plus a wetsuit is a real combination. Drink water — the dehydration is non-obvious because you don't feel thirsty in the usual way. Take breaks on the emerged rock.
5. **Sun.** The light through the water and onto the emerged peaks is described as softened, but it is still light, and it is still on your skin, and you may stay out longer than you planned because the air is warm and the breeze is absent.
6. **Unknowns in the flora.** The shrubs on the emerged peaks flower in colors "that don't quite match the spectrum any local pollinator would prefer." That is the description telling you the biochemistry may be off. Don't eat them. Don't rub them into your eyes. If you must handle them, use the back of a glove and wash your hands before touching your face.
7. **The trough.** A long, narrow channel of disturbed silt, possibly deeper than expected, possibly with a slow current that runs in one direction for hours. If you go down, leave a tag line; if you don't, stay shallow.

## What to bring and what to leave

Bring: minimal dive kit, a knife, a float and line, water to drink, a small first-aid kit with attention to wound cleaning, a dry bag for whatever you keep out of the water, a notepad if you want to remember anything afterwards.

Leave: jewelry, anything you can't afford to lose, anything that relies on the assumption that water is wet in the way you expect, expectations of seeing large animals, and any plan that depends on making phone contact with someone who isn't in this dimension.

## The one thing the description is actually telling you

It says: *this is a young place trying to look old, or an old place that has been recently and suddenly drowned, and the two are not mutually exclusive.*

That is not a riddle. It is the only orientation you will get. The place is unfinished. The fauna hasn't filled in. The silt hasn't settled. The rocks haven't softened. The plants are acting as if they've always been here, but they haven't. That mismatch — between the apparent age of the biology and the actual age of the geology — is the strangeness of the place, and it is the part that will stay with you longer than the green-gilt light.

Go slow. Stay shallow until you know where the float is. Bring a cutting tool. Don't kick the silt. You will be fine.
