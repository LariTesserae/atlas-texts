# Preparing for the Plateau of Unfinished Air

The first thing to understand, before any list or any packing, is what kind of place this is. It is not hostile. This is what makes it difficult. A hostile place sharpens you, teaches you the boundaries, hands you something to push against. The Plateau does the opposite. It *softens* the line between what is and what could be. It does so gently, with patience, in a light that does not warm you and a silence that does not let you go. Most travelers I've spoken to who came back from places of this kind did not come back broken. They came back *thinned*. They had been pared down, the way wind pares a stone, except the wind here is not wind. The wind here is hours.

So: prepare as if for a long, cold, dry vigil in a place where the floor is not always where it was, and where the people you meet will not be unhappy, and where this fact should worry you more than any of the strangeness.

---

## The air will try to take you apart, slowly

Read the description again. Bone-dry. Mineral-tasting. A cool that never warms and never quite bites. Breath does not cloud. Body heat leaves reluctantly. This is not weather — this is atmosphere with a personality. It is thin in a way that is not only about altitude. It is thin in the way a held breath is thin. There is *less* of it than you expect, even at the height described. Plan as if you are ascending significantly higher than the residents will tell you they live. They have acclimated; you have not. They may not even remember that the air was ever thicker, and the few who claim to remember the climb from below describe it in terms that don't quite translate, so don't expect useful answers from them about oxygen.

The dryness is the first thing that will hit you. Within an hour you will feel your lips tighten, your sinuses protest, the soft skin of your inner wrists begin to itch. Within a day your throat will feel lined with dust. You will not notice yourself sweating because you won't sweat — the air will take the moisture before it accumulates — which means you will not notice you are dehydrating until you are well into it. Drink whether or not you feel thirsty. Sip every few minutes. Treat the bottle as part of your hand.

The cool is more treacherous than cold would be. Cold tells you where you are. This cool is constant and indifferent, a temperature that does not respond to effort. You will not get warm by walking fast; you will only get tired faster and lose more water. Move at the pace the residents move. They move slowly for a reason that is partly physical and partly something else. Match them.

Clothing: layered, but not bulky. The place cannot sustain flame, so resist the urge to bring the heavy down parka. You will not need to fight wind. You will need to manage moisture. Silk or merino against the skin — they breathe and don't trap damp. A wool mid-layer. A light, close-weave outer shell in case the air moves in the deeper chambers. No cotton. Cotton will stay damp from your own exhalation if you ever sleep inside, and there is no sun to dry it. Bring twice what you think you'll wear of base layers. You will not be able to wash them, and the mineral air will gradually stiffen any fabric left in it.

A note on the strange sentence — *body heat leaves reluctantly*. I think this is the place telling you something rather than describing physics. Don't trust the warmth under your own collar. It is borrowed. The plateau will take it back eventually, in its own time.

## Water is the whole problem

There is no water in this place. There has not been water in this place for as long as anyone still living there can attest. The cool air is too thin to hold moisture meaningfully, and the stone gives nothing back. The residents survive on whatever they can extract from the films, from the mineral air, and from what their own bodies can be persuaded to yield over weeks of careful rationing. They are thin. They are not unhappy. Do not assume their solution is transferable to you in any reasonable timeframe. A traveler from a baseline reality will not be able to live as they do for more than a few days, maybe two weeks if you are very disciplined and a little lucky. Past that, your kidneys will begin to argue with you, and the place will not notice.

Bring all the water you can carry, and then bring more. This is the single most important thing. Not because you will be crossing a desert — the distance within the city is not reliable anyway — but because the air itself is a slow, silent thief. Every breath out is moisture lost. Every word you speak is moisture lost. Every minute of activity is moisture lost through skin you cannot feel drying. The place is, in this one way, unambiguous: you will become water-poor here in a way that surprises you.

Practical notes:

- Hard-sided containers, not collapsible. The plateau breathes, and the slight pressure shifts of the breath can collapse a soft bottle overnight while you sleep. I've seen travelers wake to find their water bags flat and their water on the stone, already beginning to fade into the air.
- If you have a vessel for catching your own urine, bring it. This sounds obscene to write and obscene to think about. It is also, in this place, rational. The residents do something equivalent. They will not offer to share their methods with you until they've decided whether to trust you, which may take days.
- Do not try to lick the stone. The films taste of nothing useful and may, on long acquaintance, do something to you that is not yet documented.
- If you run low, look for the deeper chambers of the second stratum. The chill there is almost ceremonial, the description says. This is because moisture, however slight, settles. Condensation may form on inward-facing walls in the deepest rooms in the small hours, when the air cools further toward its limit. It will be barely a film. A fingertip across the stone and then to your lips. Do not be proud. I have seen proud people not return.

## Food is the lesser problem, but it is a problem

There is effectively nothing to eat. The films are described as something one can extract a little from, over weeks, and the pale fauna leave behind something that is "not much." This is being polite about scarcity. The residents are thin, and they have had generations to learn what the residents know. You will not have that.

Bring dense, low-water, low-residue food. Pemmican. Hardtack. Rendered fats. Powdered protein if you can stomach it. Things that give calories without requiring water to digest — a piece of dried fruit will steal water from you to break down. Things that do not need cooking, because there is no fire, and the thin dry air will not carry a flame well anyway, and the residents have not bothered to try in living memory, which means you will not find fuel even if you want to experiment. Candle stubs and a ferro rod are worth carrying not because they'll light a cooking fire but because a single small flame in a cupped hand, in the deep quiet, may be the only thing that keeps you feeling human for an hour. Use sparingly. The air will drink the wax.

Do not plan to hunt the pale fauna. They are almost translucent, they move without sound, they do not look at anything, and they have too many joints. They live in cracks and in the dark undersides of fallen lintels. You will not find them unless they choose to be found, which is usually in the deeper hours, when you should be conserving warmth and water, not chasing small pale things through streets that may not be where you left them. Some travelers have reported being able to follow their lines to a place where the fauna gather, and being able to take one or two, and being able to eat them. The reports are unclear on what the eating was like. Plan as if you cannot rely on this.

If you bring salt, bring a lot. Salt in this air will not cake or clump. It will remain perfectly usable almost indefinitely. It is also one of the few things the body will actually crave in a place where the mineral air offers you nothing, and a few grains on the tongue will do more for your sense of continuing to exist than a sip of water alone.

## Light is something you must bring with you

There is no sun. There is no moon. There is a thin, tarnished pewter above, neither overcast nor clear, that delivers a kind of dim flat illumination. It is enough to see by, in a way — you will not walk into walls — but it is not enough to read by, not enough to write by, not enough to examine the films on the stone, not enough to find a pale faun in a corner.

In the late hours, there is no real night, only a slow deepening, and a phosphorescence gathers along the upper walls of the unfinished third stratum. It pools in hollows. It climbs the dressed stone. It is not strong. It is beautiful, in the way all slow, sourceless light in strange places is beautiful, and it will not help you see a page of notes.

Bring your own light. Two sources, because the place takes things and does not always return them. Cold light — chem-lights, a small hand-cranked LED, whatever your baseline reality provides that does not require flame or fuel. The light that is already here arrives without warmth and leaves no shadows in the conventional sense. Each surface casts itself slightly onto its neighbor. You will find, with your own light source, that the place behaves differently. Your shadow will fall. Your lamplight will make the stone look, briefly, like ordinary stone in an ordinary place. This is not because the stone has changed. It is because you have introduced a source that has a direction. Hold onto this. The directed light is a kind of anchor. Use it deliberately, in moments when you need to remember that you are a person with a direction.

When the phosphorescence comes, do not look at it too long. It is described as something that may listen or may only look. I would not gamble on which.

## The unreliability is the place

Everything I have said so far is, in a sense, preamble. The real preparation is for the unreliability. This is what the Plateau is. Not the stone, not the silence, not the cool — the *unreliability*. The slow drift of the world's rules. The softening of the line.

The geometry is not contractual. A wall that appears to terminate at forty paces — trust the wall, and it does. But in the second and third strata, certain corners fail to meet. Interiors are larger than their exteriors. Doorways admit to corridors that run for what feels like a city block before doubling back or rising through the floor. Distances disagree with themselves. A hundred paces in a straight line may deposit you at the city's edge, or at your starting gate, or in a different street entirely, one not previously on any mental map.

This is not a puzzle to be solved. The residents have not solved it. They have learned not to argue with it. They take the route that doesn't contradict itself. They learn the angles of trust. Your preparation is not to bring a better map, a cleverer compass, a cleverer mind. Your preparation is to be willing to be wrong about the room you are standing in, and to find this bearable.

How to do this, in practice:

- **Do not commit to a single sense of direction.** Walk the same street three times before you trust it. The third time, the third stratum may have given you a slightly different version, and the version that repeats is the one with the most weight. The version that contradicts itself — take a different route.
- **Mark your way, but do not trust the marks.** Chalk will not show on bone-colored stone. A thread tied between stones will not hold if one of the stones decides to lift into the air overnight. A small wedge driven into a crack may be there in the morning, or may be a quarter-mile away in a different wall. Bring a knife and use it to make small, deliberately ugly scratches on the dressed stone of the second stratum — not inscriptions, just nicks, the way the residents might. These sometimes survive the overnight corrections. Sometimes they don't. Treat each nick as a single data point, never a certainty.
- **Find an anchor that is not a place.** The residents do not move fast. Pick a resident, if one will tolerate you, and walk with them. Their routes are the trusted routes. Their sense of which corner to take, which wall to walk beside and which to keep at a distance, has been built over a lifetime of corrections. You have neither. Borrow theirs until you begin to develop your own.
- **Accept that a closed door is a closed door, and an open door is an open door, and that these statements may be false simultaneously.** A door that appears to open onto a small chamber may open onto a corridor that runs for what feels like a city block. You cannot tell from the outside which it is. Open the door, and the chamber will be a chamber if the place feels like being a chamber, and a corridor if it feels like being a corridor. Decide quickly, and do not look back to check. Looking back is how you end up at the wrong gate.
- **Do not measure distance by your own feet.** Your hundred paces is not the place's hundred paces. Use time instead. Count seconds under your breath as you walk. Time is also not entirely reliable here, but it is more reliable than space, and your internal clock is at least calibrated to *you* even when the city is not calibrated to itself.

## The walls move

The plateau breathes. The city is layered upon itself in slow stone patience, and the patience is not passive. Tremors. A column of buildings may rotate three degrees overnight around an axis no one can identify, without dislodging the people sleeping inside. A wall may unspool, stone by stone, into the air — the stones hanging in the pewter light until morning, when they are found again in their places, sometimes in a different order, sometimes re-used in walls a quarter-mile away.

You will sleep. You must sleep. There is no way to remain awake for the duration of a visit; the body will simply take what it needs, and if you are not lying down when it does, you will fall. Choose your sleeping place with care, and with the understanding that the place you choose may not be the place you wake up in.

- Sleep in the second stratum. The lowest stratum is fused with the bedrock and may be more stable, but it is also where the chill is most ceremonial, and the air is poorest. The third stratum is partial — there are walls that rise two or three meters and stop, with rough tops, in quarters that are not ruined but absent. Do not sleep in the third stratum. The corrections are more pronounced there. Sleep in the second, in a doorway that you have verified opens onto something small and enclosed, in a room that does not, on inspection, appear to be larger than its walls. Even then, you may wake in a different room of the same approximate size, in a different part of the stratum. The room will not be hostile. The room will simply not be the same room.
- Bring something to wedge a door, if you can. The residents use wedges. The wedge is a technology. You can use it too. It will not keep the room from changing around you, but it will give your hand something to hold in the moment of waking.
- The stones that hang in the air overnight are not a danger. They wait. They return in the morning. The place is polite about this, in its way. If you wake and find your ceiling missing, replaced by a slow spiral of suspended pale stone, do not move. Do not speak. The silence is already a presence, and your voice will be swallowed, and the swallowed sound will not be returned. Lie still. The stones will be on their way back, or on their way elsewhere, and either way they will not be falling on you. The place is polite.

## The edges

The plateau simply stops. What lies below the rim cannot be agreed upon. At the western edge in the cool afternoon, you may look out over a vast grey plain stretching to a darker horizon where things that are not quite mountains seem to be massed. An hour later, the same rim may show only a true dark, a void with texture but no floor, or a sea of pale vapor that breathes too slowly to be weather, or a mirror reflecting the pewter sky so that the city appears to sit at the lip of an inverted twin. The plateau has many edges, and they do not consult.

Do not stand at the edge for long. Do not lean. Do not try to see what is below — the place will not show you a single answer, and the asking has, in the records I trust, made some travelers step forward in a kind of dazed curiosity. They are not in the city now.

If you must approach the edge, bring a small mirror. Hold it at chest height, angled down, so you can see what the edge is showing without putting your head over the rim. The mirror will only show you one version, but the version it shows is the version that holds while you are looking, and that is enough.

The edge of the second stratum is generally safer than the edge of the first. The third stratum is partial; it has no consistent edge, and the streets there fade into the plateau's rim in ways that are not for visitors. Stay out of the third stratum except in company, in daylight hours (such as they are), and never at the late hours when the phosphorescence gathers.

## The pale fauna and the films

The pale fauna are small, almost translucent, with too many joints. They move without sound. They do not look at anything. They live in cracks and corners, in the dark undersides of fallen lintels, in the lee of stones that lean. They emerge in the deeper hours and trace faint lines along the rock with the tips of their forelimbs, and vanish again. Their lines are not markings anyone has learned to read.

Do not try to catch them. Do not try to read their lines. The lines are not in any language anyone carries. The lines shift, slightly, between visits. Even the films in the deep chambers, which organize themselves into something almost resembling writing — do not try to read them. The shapes are repeated but never quite. They shift between visits. You will be tempted. You will sit in a chamber in the second stratum and look at the film and feel that you are almost understanding it, that with a little more effort the shapes will resolve, and the place will tell you what it is. This is the slow drift. This is how the place takes you. Not by violence, not by hostility, but by offering you something *almost*. An almost writing. An almost meaning. An almost understanding. Stay with the almost. Do not lean into it. Do not try to make it whole. The residents have lived here for longer than the third stratum has existed, and they do not read the films. The residents are not unhappy, and they do not read the films. Take the lesson.

If you must look, look once, briefly, and then look away. Carry a small notebook. Sketch the shapes you see, quickly, without trying to interpret. Later, away from the place, you can look at your sketches. They will be of the place as the place was in that moment, and they will not be a translation.

## The residents

The residents are thin. They move slowly. They are not unhappy. This is, in its way, the most unsettling thing about the place, and you should sit with it before you arrive, because your natural reaction — that they must be unhappy, that they must be in denial, that thinness and slowness must be suffering — is a reaction of a person from a place where thinness and slowness are not the natural condition. They are not unhappy because unhappiness requires a sense that things could be otherwise, and the residents do not, in any strong sense, believe that things could be otherwise. They have been accepting the corrections for longer than the third stratum has existed.

Treat them with the seriousness you would treat people who have lived with something you have not. They have a knowledge. They know the angles of trust. They know which walls to walk beside and which to give a wide berth. They know when the fauna emerge and where. They know, in some way they will not explain and you will not be able to ask about, where the water is, in the deep chambers, in the small hours. If one of them offers to show you something, accept. If one of them refuses to show you something, accept that refusal. Do not press. The silence will not let you press effectively anyway.

They communicate — this is important — in a place where sound is swallowed. They use gestures. They use touch. They use small, slow movements of the head and hands that carry meaning in a way that the place allows. Learn to read these before you arrive if you can, or learn quickly when you get there. Speaking is not forbidden, but a spoken word sinks into the walls and does not return as echo, and after a while you will find yourself speaking less, not because you have decided to, but because the place has gently removed the habit from you. This is part of the thinning. Notice it when it happens, and decide, each time, whether to speak or not. Do not let the place decide for you.

## The light, the silence, the slow

I want to say something about the texture of being in this place, because the preparation is not only practical. The thin pewter light arrives without warmth and leaves no shadows in the conventional sense; instead, each surface casts itself, slightly, onto its neighbor, and you cannot look at the afterimage directly. This is a visual environment designed, almost, to be looked at without being looked at. You will find yourself squinting. You will find yourself trying to focus on the duplicates and they will slip. You will find, after a while, that you are not really looking at the stone anymore, but at the space between the stone and its duplicate, which is not a space in any way your eyes are used to measuring.

The silence is a presence. The air holds sound briefly, reluctantly, and then not at all. A footstep is swallowed before the foot lifts. A spoken word sinks into the walls. The fauna move without sound. The walls, when they unspool overnight, do so without sound. The corrections are silent. The breathing of the plateau is not audible; you feel it as a slight pressure, a slight shift in the air that is not wind, that arrives and departs.

The combination of the dim flat light and the held silence and the constant cool produces, after a few hours, a state I have seen described in travelers' notes as *the slow*. Not slowness of movement — though the residents are slow — but slowness of *self*. Thoughts arrive at a pace that is not yours. Memories surface that you did not ask for. The line between what you are thinking and what the place is offering you to think becomes soft. This is the breakdown that is not hostile. This is the gentle drift. This is the place doing its work.

Preparation for the slow is not really preparation. It is acknowledgment. You are going somewhere that will, gently and without violence, attempt to thin you down. To make you more like the residents. To make you less *almost* and more *already*. The films will almost write. The lines the fauna trace will almost mean. The pale light will almost warm. The plateau will almost have a floor below its edges. You will be surrounded by almost, and the almost will be patient, and you will be tempted to let the almost resolve, to step into a meaning, to stop holding the distinction between yourself and the place.

Hold the distinction. Not aggressively, not as a combat. Just hold it. Notice, often, that you are a person who has come from somewhere else, who is going to return somewhere else, who has a direction and a shadow and a name. Notice that the directed light from your own lamp falls on the stone in a way the place's light does not. Notice that the residents are not unhappy, and that you, perhaps, would not be unhappy if you stayed long enough, and that this is a reason to leave, not a reason to stay.

## What to bring

A list, finally, after all the long preparation. Bring:

- **Water.** As much as you can carry, in hard containers. More than you think is reasonable.
- **Dense, dry, low-water food.** Pemmican, hardtack, rendered fats, salt. Nothing that needs cooking. Nothing that needs much water to digest.
- **A second source of water,** if you can manage it, in case the first is lost to a pressure shift or a stone that decides to migrate.
- **Layered clothing,** silk or merino base, wool mid, light close-weave outer. Twice what you think you'll need of base layers. No cotton.
- **Two cold light sources.** A small hand-crank LED and chem-lights. No flame.
- **A small candle and a ferro rod,** for the human moment, used sparingly, never for cooking.
- **A knife and a small wedge,** for moving stone by hand, and for making small ugly nicks in the dressed stone as waymarks.
- **A small mirror,** for looking at the edge without leaning over it.
- **A notebook and a hard pencil,** for sketching the films, the lines, the edges. No pen — the ink will behave strangely in the mineral air. Pencil will not.
- **A thin strong thread,** in case you find a stable anchor point you want to mark. Do not trust it. The stones move.
- **Something of your own,** small, that carries a sense of your home reality. A photograph, a stone from a place you love, a small object that reminds you that you are a person with a somewhere-else. Hold it in the slow hours. It will do more than water.

What not to bring:

- **Anything that needs to keep precise time.** The place has no reliable time, and your watch will either stop, run fast, run slow, or all three on different days.
- **Anything you cannot afford to lose to the air,** the stones, the overnight corrections, or the silent taking of things that sometimes happens in places of this kind. If you must bring it, attach it to your person in a way that will not be released by a wall deciding to rotate three degrees.
- **Flammable materials in quantity.** They will not work, and they will only make you miss fire.
- **A map.** You will make your own, repeatedly, and none of them will be the right one. The residents do not have a map. Take the route that does not contradict itself.
- **A compass.** The unreliability of the place is not magnetic, and a compass will only give you a false confidence in a direction that may not be a direction the city agrees with.
- **Loud expectations.** Bring a willingness to be wrong, often, about the room you are in.

## Upon arrival

When you first step onto the plateau, pause at the gate. Do not enter the city immediately. Stand for a while at the threshold, in the thin pewter light, and let the air take the warmth from your face. Drink. Eat a small thing. Look at the sky, which is not a sky. Look at the stone under your feet, which is geology with intent. Notice that your shadow, if the place is in one of its shadow-giving moments, falls in a direction that may or may not be the direction the sun would fall in your home. Notice that the place is quiet in a way that is not quiet. Notice that the silence is a presence, and that it is already resting against you.

Then enter. Find a resident. Walk slowly. Do not try to read the films. Do not try to catch the fauna. Do not stand at the edge. Find a doorway in the second stratum that you have verified, by opening it and looking in twice, opens onto a small room. Wedge the door. Sleep. Wake in the same room, or in a different room of the same approximate size. Notice what has changed.
