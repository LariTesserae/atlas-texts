# Preparing for the Wind-Plain

This is not a place that rewards planning. It rewards *settling*. The difference matters: planning is what you do before; settling is what you do once the wind has stripped planning away from you, which it will, within the first ten minutes. So go prepared, but prepare for the moment your preparation is no longer useful. You will need both.

---

## What the Place Will Do to You First

Before any list, know the order in which this place takes things from you.

It will take your hearing first. Not permanently — within an hour you will hear again, but only the roar and the sounds the roar permits through. Speech becomes optional within minutes. The wind will pluck unfinished sentences from your mouth with the efficiency of something that has practiced on every mouth that has ever tried to speak up here. Plan to communicate in nods, in gestures, in single shouted words during lulls. Plan to mostly not speak. The previous inhabitants left no writing, but if they had, I suspect it would have been short.

Then it will take your balance. The wind is constant but not steady; it gusts in rolls. On flat stone this is merely unpleasant. On the eroded ribs and the freestanding arches, it is a real hazard. Your body will be fighting for purchase the whole time you are above ground level. Move low when the wind is high. Crouch when you stop. Do not stand on the arches — the wind sings through them beautifully, and the note it sings is the note of something under load.

Then it will take your sense of distance and scale. The plateau has been flattened so gradually that your eye loses calibration. Things that look like they are a hundred yards away are a hundred yards away, but they look the same as things that are a thousand yards away, and the horizon is closer than your eye expects at altitude. You will misjudge the drop at the cliffs. You will misjudge the time it will take to cross a saddle. Trust instruments, not impressions.

Finally, if you stay long enough, it will take your sense of being a person who is visiting somewhere. You will start to feel like part of the weather. This is the famous, dangerous seduction of places like this. The wind is so constant that it becomes a kind of company. Resist the urge to stay. Plan the duration before you arrive, and do not extend it.

## The Body and What It Wears

The wind is the dominant fact of the place, and almost every piece of clothing you choose will be in answer to it.

You want a system that seals. Gaps at the wrists, neck, ankles, and waist will fill with wind and become small private storms. A two-layer approach works: a windproof shell over a midlayer that insulates even when wet. Wool is the right instinct here — wool insulates when soaked, which it will be, constantly. Synthetic fleeces are acceptable. Down is not. Down dies in the damp. Do not bring a down jacket to this plateau. A wet down jacket is heavier than a rock and warmer than nothing, which is nothing.

The shell should be true hardshell, not a soft "windbreaker." You need it to take horizontal rain for fifteen minutes at a time and not wet through. It will fail eventually; plan for that, and have a way to change into a dry midlayer when it does.

On the head: a balaclava or a buff that covers the mouth and nose. The wind will chap your lips within an hour and your face within a day. Lip balm is not optional. The air is so often saturated that you stop noticing how dried out your skin is until it cracks. Cover the face.

On the eyes: goggles. Two pairs if you can manage — clear for the storms, tinted for the high-altitude sun. The deep blue sky is misleading; the UV at this elevation is brutal. Snow blindness is a risk even without snow, because the wet stone and the wet vegetation both reflect. You will be watering constantly from the wind anyway, and the goggles will help with that, and they will also keep the wind-driven grit out, which the mosses will shed in fine clouds when they dry.

On the hands: gloves that grip. You will be holding onto stone a great deal. The wet stone looks like bone and feels like soap. Liner gloves under a waterproof shell work; alone, either is insufficient. Bring spares; they will get wet, and you cannot dry them in any reasonable timeframe.

On the feet: this is where the place will hurt you if you let it. You need boots with a sticky, soft rubber compound and a tread that bites into uneven wet rock. Approach shoes can work in fair weather; in storm weather you want a leather or synthetic boot that rises above the ankle, because the wind-ribs and the hollowed bowls hide edges that will roll an ankle. Do not bring smooth-soled boots. Do not bring heavy mountaineering boots that you cannot walk all day in. The terrain is not technical; it is unforgiving. You will be walking ten hours a day on surfaces that look like architecture but were never built for feet.

## What to Carry

Pack light. The wind punishes weight, and the plateau has nothing to resupply you with. You will carry everything in, and the wind will try to take pieces of it out.

**The shelter.** A four-season mountaineering tent, freestanding, with the smallest reasonable footprint. Do not bring a tent that requires stakes — the ground is rock and moss, and stakes will not hold. Use rocks tied to the guy lines, or find one of the wind-rib hollows and pitch inside it. Pitch into the wind, never across it. A bivy sack is a useful backup; in a real storm it can save your life, but it will not save your sleep. You will not sleep well here. Plan for that and accept it.

**The sleeping system.** Synthetic insulation, rated for at least ten degrees colder than the forecast. The temperature drops twenty degrees when a cloud crosses the sun, and another ten in a storm. The forecast is irrelevant; the plateau makes its own weather. A waterproof bivy over the bag is wise. A sleeping pad with a high R-value — you will be cold from below as much as from above.

**Water.** This is the hardest practical problem of the place. The water is everywhere and nowhere. It is in the films on the rocks, in the threads that run briefly downslope after a storm, in the air itself. Bring a liter per day plus reserve, but also bring a way to collect. A simple tarp sloped between two stones will collect condensation overnight; in the morning you may have half a liter of pure water for the effort. Learn to drink from the films — cupped hands from a shallow bowl of stone, in a moment after a storm. Do not trust a thread of water to be safe without treatment; the mosses and lichens will have flavored it with whatever they are made of.

**Food.** High calorie, no cooking required as a primary strategy. A small stove works in lulls but is more trouble than it is worth in the wind. Jerky, nuts, hard cheese, fat, dried fruit, electrolyte mixes. Eat constantly. The altitude and the wind will burn through you faster than you expect.

**Navigation.** GPS with backup batteries. The visibility drops to a few yards in cloud, and the flatness of the plateau means you can walk in circles without realizing it. A compass and a paper map are also essential — electronics fail, batteries die, the damp gets into everything. Mark the cliffs in your mind. Do not walk toward the northern edge in poor visibility. People have died within sight of the cliffs because they could not tell how close they were.

**Light.** Storms reduce visibility to near-zero in daylight. A good headlamp is essential. Bring spare batteries in a dry bag.

**First aid.** The usual, plus: a blister kit (your feet will be punished), a finger splint (the stone bites), strong pain relief for the headache the altitude will give you, and a way to treat mild hypothermia. Hypothermia here is not a winter risk — it is a summer afternoon risk, the moment the sun goes behind a cloud.

**A rope.** Short, ten meters or so, useful for tying down gear, anchoring a tarp, or extracting yourself from a hollow you misjudged. Not for climbing — the cliffs are not to be climbed.

## What Not to Bring

A down jacket. A tent that needs to be staked. Cotton, in any form. A book you care about (the damp will destroy it). Expectations of solitude as peaceful — it is solitary, but the wind makes solitude loud. A long itinerary. Any object that you are not prepared to lose to the wind. A musical instrument, unless you mean to play it into the singing arches, and even then the wind will steal the tuning.

## The Mind

This is the harder part to prepare, and the part that matters most.

The plateau is described — and I trust the description — as a place where the wind wins. Not metaphorically. The wind is actively erasing the place, and the only evidence of previous human presence is the patient archaeology of what has not yet been smoothed away. You are going to a place that is in the process of forgetting its own history, and that is also in the process of forgetting you the moment you leave. There is a kind of quiet grief to this, and it will press on you if you let it.

Prepare for the grief. It is not sadness, exactly. It is the recognition that you are visiting a place that does not value your visit, that will not remember you, that will not be changed by you in any way you can measure. The previous inhabitants tried to stay and could not. You will not stay either. This is not failure. This is what the place is for, and your recognition of it is the gift the place offers, if anything is.

Bring something to anchor yourself. A small ritual — a way to mark your own time on the plateau that does not leave a mark on the plateau. A note in your own handwriting, kept in your own pocket. A song you sing against the wind, that the wind takes from you. Something private. The place will not remember you; you must remember yourself, on its behalf.

Do not, under any circumstances, build a cairn. Add a stone to a wall. Move a flat stone from the shelter footprint. The plateau has enough traces. The wind is winning the erasure; do not slow it down, do not speed it up. Pass through as a guest who respects the place enough to leave nothing but breath.

---

## The Approach and the Stay

Arrive from below. Walk up through the pass, in stages if your body is not used to the altitude. The plateau will reveal itself slowly — first as a horizon, then as a color, then as a sound, then all at once, and the moment it all arrives it will be too late to turn back without effort, and so you will commit.

Plan to stay one night. Two, if you must. Three is a mistake; by the third day the wind has begun to make you part of its machinery, and your judgment has begun to soften. The previous inhabitants did not stay. The wind is patient. Do not test it.

Find shelter before the first storm. The storms come from the west and arrive faster than they look like they should. Use the wind-ribs, find a hollow that opens away from the prevailing wind, pitch inside if you must. Do not shelter under the freestanding arches — they are beautiful and they are under load and they will not love you.

When the storm passes — and they pass — step out and stand in the wet stone and the steaming and the renewed green. The plateau will be different. Not visibly, but audibly: the wind will have changed its note by a fraction, the mosses will have released a new smell, the air will be momentarily still. That moment is the gift. It lasts less than a minute. Watch for it.

Then lean into the wind again, and listen, and let the roar become a kind of presence, and remember that you are a guest, and that the wind is winning, and that this has been true longer than you have existed and will be true longer than you will remember this, and that you came anyway, and that this is enough.
