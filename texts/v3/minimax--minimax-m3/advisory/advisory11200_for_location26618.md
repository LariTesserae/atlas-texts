# On Going to the Hanging

The traveler should know, before any list is made, that the place has no name they will carry back. The name does not travel well through the wet air. People here speak of it as the Hanging, or simply as *inside*, and after a few days you will too. Preparation, then, is mostly the matter of being quiet enough, soft enough, and patient enough to hear the sentence the place is already in the middle of, when you arrive.

It will not perform for you. It will not unfurl on cue. The basin was respiring before you were born and will respiring after you are gone, and the only preparation it really requires is that you stop, for a while, asking it to be other than what it is.

## What to Bring

Bring less than you think.

Clothing should be quick-drying and warm when wet — merino or a good synthetic base, a light fleece, a waterproof shell that breathes. Cotton will not dry; cotton will become a weight you carry in your own skin. Two pairs of trousers, three shirts, socks enough for four days and the willingness to wear them longer. Boots broken in over many weeks, with a grip that has held on wet stone before. Gaiters, to keep the moss threads off your calves. A brimmed hat, because even when it is not raining on you, the canopy is raining somewhere, and the drips find the crown of an uncovered head with a kind of cheerful malice. Long sleeves and long trousers even when it is warm, because the insects will find the soft skin at your wrists and neck, and once they find it they will tell other insects, and within an hour your eyelids will be a country they have annexed.

A pack with a rain cover, and inside the pack, dry bags for everything that does not wish to be wet. Electronics in a hard case with silica. A headlamp, small, for the moments when the overcast light at ground level is not quite enough for a knot. A sleep mask, because the place is never truly dark and your pineal gland will appreciate the courtesy. A knife — not a machete; the machete is theirs, and it is a language you do not yet speak. A small towel of microfiber; bring two. Water purification if you intend to be careful, though they drink from the plinth pools without boiling and they are not sick from it. DEET at full strength. Permethrin, pre-applied to your clothing if you can manage it. A medical kit with antihistamines, an EpiPen if you carry one, anti-fungal powder, the things you would take on any wet tropical mountain walk.

Do not bring alcohol as a gift unless you know them. Do not bring sugar. Do not bring anything that would let you build a thing they have not built or do a thing they have not done. The economy of the place is mossed over with custom; do not lift the moss.

## The Body

Talk to your doctor about malaria prophylaxis. About Japanese encephalitis vaccination. About anything else that lives in wet mountain forests and likes the blood of large mammals. Get your tetanus current. Bring your own waterborne-illness plan; the place's water is fine for them but your gut biota is not theirs.

Your skin will macerate. After three days of constant moisture the skin of your feet will be pale and soft and prone to tearing; powder, dry socks at night, slippers inside if you can. Your hands will wrinkle until they look like theirs. Your cuticles will complain. This is normal. Do not over-wash; the water is not dirty, and soap strips what little barrier the moisture has left you.

Your lungs will acclimate. The air is dense, oxygen-rich, full of spores. If you have asthma, bring your inhalers. If you do not have asthma, you may sound as though you do for the first week; this too will pass.

## The Mind

This is the harder preparation.

The place has no quiet version of itself. From the moment you cross the plank-threshold, you will be inside a chord that is being tuned continuously. The water is the bass — a thousand small rains, each leaf and stone contributing its drop. The frogs are the middle, a hum with peaks. The two-noted bird — the one whose second note is slurred, the one that passes that second note across the upper canopy like a rumor through a room — is the melody that does not stop. Above that the insects, hissing in clouds. Above that the people: hammering a peg into a beam, arguing in vowels that go on for whole seconds, a child singing something about a fish, a dog that has been asleep for hours and chooses now to test the acoustics of the plinth.

There is no hour of silence. There is no moment when the sound pauses for you to gather yourself. If you climb to the highest ridge and put your ear to the wind, you will still hear it — a low rumble, the place's metabolism, audible for miles.

Prepare for this by sitting, before you go, in a noisy café or beside a busy road, and refusing to make it stop. After an hour you will know whether your mind can tolerate a soundscape that does not defer to you. If it cannot, this may not be the place for you. If it can — if you can let a sound be the room you are in rather than the obstacle between you and your thoughts — then you will be welcomed by the place in a way you could not be welcomed by a quieter one.

Bring earplugs for the first two nights if you must. They will not be needed after that.

The light is sourceless. The canopy is so dense and so even that the overcast light comes down through the leaves as a diffuse gray-gold, without shadow, without the kind of contrast your eyes are used to making sense of depth. Wear a brimmed hat. Trust your feet. Do not be alarmed when a person standing twenty feet from you appears to be a person standing five feet from you, and vice versa. The place does not lie about distance; it merely asks you to recalibrate it.

## The Insects

The insects will be on your eyelids. The traveler should know this in advance so that the first time it happens — the faint crawling on the lashes, the tiny weight — they do not panic and slap at their face, which only brings more.

Stand still. The insects are not biting, mostly. Some are. The biting ones are not the ones on your eyelids; the biting ones are the ones at your ankles and the backs of your hands and the soft crook of your elbow. The ones on your eyelids are after salt, and tears, and the small humidity of your eye, and they will leave you alone if you leave them alone. Close your eyes. Breathe out slowly. They will lift off in a moment.

Some trees smoke with insects. These trees are doing something; do not stand under them for long. The people do not. Move through them as through a door, and be on the other side.

## The Wasps and the Walls

The houses are living wood. The walls throw out new shoots each year and someone in the household must lasso them down or cut them, and that someone will be stung if they reach into the eaves, because the eaves are the wasps' condominium. The wasps are not evicted. The wasps are part of the agreement.

Do not prune the walls yourself. Do not touch the eaves. If a shoot is in your way, ask. If a wasp is on the inside of the longhouse, do not flail; move slowly, and it will tend to its own concerns. The people here are stung often. They are not dramatic about it. You should also not be dramatic about it. If you are allergic, bring your medication and tell someone the moment you arrive. Do not hide it.

## The Path and the Threshold

The threshold is a half-fallen tree with a single plank spiked into its flank, lichen-bearded. Memorize it. From inside the basin, the path out is the same path in, and at the end of it you will find the tree again. Do not assume you can find the threshold by intuition; on a wet day the paths all look like the same green-brown ribbon of stepped moss, and the ridges look the same, and the trees all look like the kind of tree they are. The plank is the only signpost and it is enough.

## The People

Learn three words before you go. Not the name of the place. Learn *hello*, *thank you*, and *water*. The vowels go on for whole seconds; you will not be able to reproduce them properly for weeks, and that is fine. The people will hear what you mean.

Drink from the plinth pools when water is offered. They have been drinking from them longer than you have been alive, and the water is what the place is, and refusing it is a kind of refusal of the place. Eat what they give you. Hold the children who want to be held; do not hold the ones who do not. The children will rope you into a game you cannot possibly understand — their counting is not your counting — and they will laugh, and you will laugh, and the numbers will not match, and that is fine; the game is not the numbers. If a child asks you to hold a vine-rope while they climb, do. The plants reward them, and they will reward you.

If they offer you work — holding a beam, hauling thatch, packing daub into a wall — accept it. Do not perform more than you can do. Do not perform less.

## The Larger Things

Something crashes in the understory at intervals, breaking branches. The people do not look up. You should not look up either, at least not the first time; looking up is a kind of attention, and attention is a kind of invitation. After you have been inside the place for a few days, you may glance. After a few weeks, you may know what it is. Until then, the people know what it is, and their not-looking-up is the longest kindness they will offer you.

## The River

You will hear it. You will not see it. Do not go looking for it. The basin opens toward it briefly on the fourth side, and that opening is the way the water gets out, and the water that gets out is what makes the basin what it is. To find the river would be to leave the basin, and to leave the basin is to be somewhere else, which is not where you are.

## The Late Afternoon Warmth

There will be a moment, often in the late afternoon, when the sun finds a hole in the cloud. The light that comes through it is gold and warm and almost horizontal, and the whole basin exhales — a smell of warm fern, wet fur, the inside of a sleeping animal. The bark-paper lanterns that hang unlit between the longhouses swell in the warmth. Nobody lights them.

Be still for this. It is the closest thing the place has to a speech, and it will not last more than a few minutes, and it will happen again tomorrow if you are lucky. It is worth remembering for the rest of your travels.

## The Tree at the Back

The tree is not sacred. The tree is the tree. The flowers are the flowers. The bowl is the bowl. The rain is the rain.

But if, on the morning of departure, you find yourself wanting to bring a flower to the tree at the back of the basin, do not be embarrassed. The people have been doing this for as long as anyone can remember. The flowers stay until they rot. The tree goes on.

It is not worship. It is the only gesture the place permits.

## Departure

You will leave with the two-noted bird in your head. You will leave with the small sound of water finding a new path down a leaf. You will leave with the smell of the place in your clothes, and you may not be able to wash it out, and you may not want to.

Do not try to explain the place to people who have not been there. You can live your whole life in it, and you will wake each morning to the same two-noted bird and the same small sound of water, and you will not think to call it anything at all. Trying to call it something is the first move of a person who has not yet been inside.

Be inside, while you are inside.
