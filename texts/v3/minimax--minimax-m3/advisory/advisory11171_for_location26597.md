# Preparing for the Weeping Valley

The first thing to understand is that this place does not advertise what it is. The light does not change. The air does not move. The animals are absent. If you arrive expecting a valley to behave like a valley — expecting birds at the eaves, a breeze at the ridge, sun on the floor at some hour — you will spend your first days disoriented and not know why. Read this carefully before you pack.

## What the valley actually is

A young valley in equilibrium. The rock is fresh — sharp fractures, squared shoulders, edges that catch cloth — but the settlement is old in its way, and the water cycle has settled into something stable. The ridges hold weather against themselves. The air is warm, heavy, saturated. Water is everywhere and on everything, in transit: condensation beading every vertical face, dripping from every eave, gliding as a thin skin over paths and outcrops. There is no true rain, or rarely any. There is no wind. There is no sun that reaches the floor.

The buildings come out of the rock around them. Dressed stone below, swollen timber above, heavy slate roofs. Plain construction. Pegged, hand-forged, fitted. No carving. Nothing taller than the ridges. Spaced generously — you can see between them, past them, to the rock. A hall, raised where the floor is driest, two stories. A low wall, more marker than fortification. Three low-arched stone bridges across the wetter channels. Fitted-stone paths that glisten because they are never dry.

The light is silver, flat, even, all day. Fog can fill the valley to the brim or sit knee-deep and soften everything below the rooftops. Seasons are marked by how much water the air can carry, not by temperature or color.

There are people. There is no one else. No birds, no insects in any obvious number, no tracks on the paths but boots. The settlement is alive; the valley's fauna is not, and has not been for as long as anyone here can remember. No one finds this strange. You must.

The place is stable. It does not move toward anything. If you came back in a hundred years you would find it the same. In a thousand years, almost the same — only the ridges a fraction softer, only the paths relaid, only the tussocks in slightly different places. This is not a place in transition. It is a place that has arrived.

## What this means for you, in your body

**The dampness is total and constant.** You will not get dry. Within minutes of stepping outside, every surface of you will be holding a film of moisture. Your clothes will be damp, your hair will be damp, the leather of your boots will be damp. Your skin will feel soft, slightly pruned, after a few hours. Your fingernails will absorb water. You are not going to be wet in the way rain makes you wet — you are going to be wet in the way a fog makes you wet, all the time, indefinitely. Plan for this.

The temperature is mild. The air is warm; the rock is cool where water runs over it. You will not be cold in the way cold usually means. You will be *clammy*, which is its own kind of discomfort — the body cannot quite thermoregulate because moisture is constantly being drawn out by evaporation and replaced by condensation from the air. You will want layers you can adjust. You will want to know how to be wet without being chilled.

**The terrain will catch you.** The rock is sharp-edged. The shoulders of the slopes are squared. The fractures will grab a sleeve, a pack strap, a loose hem. Wear clothing without flapping edges, or wear clothing you are willing to see torn. Stay on the paths and the outcrops and the fitted stones. Do not climb on the ridge flanks — they are not yet graded by time, and they will punish you for it. The fitted-stone paths are reliable in all weather; the rock beyond them is honest but unforgiving.

**The light will lie to your eyes about depth.** No sun means no shadows. In the silver flat light, distances are hard to read. A stone two paces away looks like a stone ten paces away. A bridge across a channel looks like a continuation of the path. In fog, this compounds. Your eyes will adjust over a day or two, but expect to misjudge for the first while. Walk slowly until you trust what you see.

**Time will slip.** The light does not change through the day. There is no morning bright, no afternoon gold, no long evening. Without a watch you will lose the hour. Without a watch for many days, you may lose the day. Bring a timepiece that is waterproof, and check it. The people here keep time somehow — ask how, when you arrive.

## Clothing and gear

**Wool.** Wool is your friend. It holds warmth when wet, it does not chill you when damp the way cotton does, and it tolerates the constant humidity without rotting the way some fibers will. A wool base layer under everything else. Wool socks, several pairs, because they will not dry between days. A wool sweater or two.

**A waterproof outer shell.** Breathable if possible, but anything waterproof will do. You will be standing under the drip from eaves constantly; you want one layer that sheds. A hood is more useful than a brimmed hat here, because the drip is from above and you will want your peripheral vision.

**Boots with grip.** The fitted-stone paths glisten — they are wet, they are sometimes slimy with algae or moss, they are uneven in places where stones have settled. You need boots that grip wet stone and support the ankle. Leather will absorb water and stay soft; treat them when you get home. Rubber-and-leather works. Pure rubber is fine but will make your feet sweat, which in this humidity is its own problem.

**No cotton as a base layer.** Cotton holds water against the skin and pulls heat out of you. Save it for nothing or leave it at home.

**Spare dry clothes in waterproof bags.** You will want, at the end of a day, to put on something that has been kept dry. Sleep dry if you can. The dripping outside is constant; inside a building it is far less so, but not absent. A dry set for sleeping is a kindness to yourself.

**A wide hooded outer layer for fog.** When the valley fills to the brim and you can see only the tops of the ridges, a hood helps with orientation — it cuts peripheral visual noise and helps you read the shape of things in front of you.

**Waterproof bags for everything that does not like water.** Paper, books, paper notebooks, leather goods that you care about, electronics, spare batteries, matches or lighters, silica gel packets if you have them. The air will get into anything not sealed. Ziplock bags inside dry bags, dry bags inside pack liners — assume redundancy.

**A waterproof headlamp or lantern.** The silver light is even but in deep fog can be very dim, and inside unlit buildings there is no light at all. Battery life will be shorter in the damp; bring spares sealed against moisture.

**Pencil, not pen.** Graphite holds up to damp. Ink bleeds, feathers, and runs.

**Waterproof paper, if you can get it.** Otherwise, take notes in a waterproof notebook or on a protected device. Anything on regular paper will become soft, swollen, illegible within a day or two.

**A small folding knife, simple tools.** The craft here is plain iron and pegged timber. Simple well-made tools will fit the place. Do not bring anything ornate. The valley does not ornament.

**A water vessel.** Not for drinking — the water is everywhere, and it condenses from clean air, and it is drinkable. A vessel is for catching runoff from a clean surface if you want water away from where you are standing. Most travelers will simply cup their hands under an eave.

**Food.** Bring what you need. Forage is thin here — ferns at foundations, moss on lower walls, dense-leaved small trees with bark the color of wet iron, tussock grass in clumps with dark ground between. There is not enough to live on, and what there is, the people here use. You may trade with the settlement; do not count on it.

**Medication.** Bring what you need, sealed. Dampness affects joints — if you have any stiffness, expect it to be a little worse. The flat unchanging light and the absence of animal sound can unsettle mood over many days. If you have any history of either, bring what supports you. The valley is not hostile, but it is constant in a way that can wear at a person who is not prepared.

## What to leave at home, or in your head

**Expectations of birdsong.** The eaves do not rustle. The water has no tracks but boots. The air is empty of wings. The first thing that will unsettle you, before you can name it, is that the world here has no animal sound. No dawn chorus. No insect hum. No distant lowing, no rustle in the underbrush, no owl at night. Just the dripping, the condensation, and the rare creak of the hall. Some travelers find this profoundly peaceful after the first day. Some find it oppressive and cannot settle. Most take longer to settle than they expect. The valley is not wrong; your ear is looking for what is not here. Know this before you go. Do not wait until the second night to discover that you are listening for something that will not come.

**Expectations of change.** The tussocks will not spread. The paths will not be rerouted. The buildings will be rebuilt as they need to be, in the same way, of the same materials, by the same hands. If you form an attachment here, know that the place will not grow around your absence. It will be here, the same, when you return — or it will not have noticed you were gone. This can be a comfort, and it can be a sorrow. Both are real. Walk in with your eyes open.

**Expectations of sun.** You will not get a sun on the valley floor. Not today, not any day. Plan accordingly — for mood, for vitamin D on longer stays, for any reason you would normally want a sun.

**Valuable things that cannot be protected.** Anything metal that is not simple iron will corrode. Anything paper will absorb. Anything leather will grow a soft bloom and darken. Anything electric will misbehave. Bring what you can replace. Bring what the loss of would grieve you only a little.

**A wish to make the valley something else.** It is what it is. It is settled. It has arrived. The ridges are too large for themselves still, but the rest of the place has found its shape. You are a guest. Be a guest.

## How to move through the place

**Walk the paths.** The fitted-stone paths connect everything. They are reliable in fog, in dark, in the heaviest condensation. Learn them in clear light before you trust yourself to follow them in a knee-deep fog. They glisten; that is the wet, not a warning.

**Use the bridges.** The three low-arched stone bridges cross the wetter channels. The channels are overfull; do not try to wade them. The bridges are low but solid.

**Stay on the inhabited side of the low wall.** The wall is a marker, not a fortification. It marks the side of the valley where the settlement sits, between the seep-lines. Beyond it is wetter ground, the weeping rock, the slope rising into the ridges. You may go beyond it; know that the footing is rock, not path, and that the rock weeps and is sharp.

**Do not climb the ridges casually.** The slopes are fresh. The fractures are sharp. The edges catch. A million winters from now these ridges will be rounded; they are not rounded yet. If you climb, dress for it, move slowly, and expect to come back with torn cloth and skinned knuckles.

**In fog, navigate by building, not by ridge.** When the fog sits in the valley to the brim, only the tops of the ridges show. The buildings, the outcrops, the bridges — these are your landmarks. Learn the shape of the settlement from above if you can, before you trust yourself in a full fog.

**At night, the silver light does not really leave.** It dims. It does not become dark the way a place with sun does. The dripping continues. Sleep comes, and is deep, but the valley does not enter a true night. If you are used to dark for sleeping, a sleep mask will help.

## How to be among the people

They are here. The paths are walked. The hall exists. They will see you. They will not, I think, make a fuss; the valley does not encourage fuss. Approach plainly. The walls here do not speak, and the craft is plain, and the people here, I would guess, are plain too — not in the sense of dull, but in the sense of unornamented, fitted to the place, not trying to be other than what they are.

Do not ask them about the absence of animals as if it were a mystery to be solved. To them it is not a mystery. It is the way the valley is. If you ask, they will tell you the same thing everyone tells you about it: it is the way the valley is. Take that answer. Do not press. They have lived inside this answer longer than you have been in the valley.

Do not try to change anything. Do not propose a garden that will spread. Do not suggest carving the walls. Do not offer to make the place taller or brighter or more. The valley has arrived. Your job is to be in it for a while, and then to leave it as you found it.

If you stay long enough, you will stop listening for birds. You will stop expecting the light to change. You will stop expecting change at all. The dripping will become the silence's punctuation. The silver will become just light. You will be settling into the valley, the way the valley has settled into itself. That is the sign that you are ready to be there.

## A short final word

Bring patience. Bring wool. Bring a watch. Bring a waterproof container for anything you care about. Bring an ear for the dripping. Bring an eye for the rock.

Do not bring expectations. Do not bring the need to make the place something other than what it is. Do not bring the wish for birds.

The valley will be there when you arrive, and it will be there when you leave, and it will be there, the same, in a hundred years. There is a kind of comfort in that which most places cannot offer. There is also a kind of sorrow, for the same reason. Walk into both.
