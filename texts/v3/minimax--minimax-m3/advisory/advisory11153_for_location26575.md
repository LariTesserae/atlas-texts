# Preparation for the Flooded Chamber

There is something a little unkind about arriving unprepared at a place like this. Not because it punishes you, but because it does not — it simply waits, and waits very patiently, and the small gaps between what it offers and what you are equipped to receive will be your gaps, not its. So a few things worth thinking through before you find yourself standing at the lip of the square opening with the sun already pouring in.

## What to Bring, and What Refuses to Come

Bring almost nothing. This place provides two of the three things a body needs from a place like it — warmth, water — and the third, which is silence, is provided in a qualified way (more on the sound below). If you bring anything, bring things that do not mind being wet and do not mind being warm and do not mind being forgotten for a few hours.

A small cloth, soft and unwoven if you can manage it. Not for drying — you will not dry in this room, not really, not for a long time — but for placing between you and the cut stone when you sit on the ledge. The ledge has been used before; the inner edge has been rounded by use, but the stone remembers being cut, and your skin will not improve its memory. The cloth is a courtesy to the chisel marks, which are still recent enough to feel like a question rather than an answer.

A second cloth, for the eventual walk back out into the world. You will want this eventually. Not yet.

Nothing sharp. Nothing that sheds. Nothing that wants to be a different shape after being wet. Nothing electronic that would be improved by staying dry. Nothing you would be sorry to lose to steam, because steam will find it.

Do not bring soap. The water is not for washing in the way a bath is for washing. The water is for being in. If you arrive clean, the pool accepts you. If you arrive dirty, the pool accepts you too, but it will be a different visit. Pick which one you want to be before you arrive, because changing mid-visit is hard — the only way out is up through the square opening, and the climb back into the day is a small, embarrassing negotiation.

## What to Wear

This sounds like a small thing, but it is not. The chisel marks on every surface are crisp enough to catch their own shadows in moving sun, which means they are crisp enough to catch threads. Loose clothing is a mistake. A loose hem, an undone buttonhole, a long fringe — anything that hangs — will find an edge in the first minute and either snag or, worse, come away with a small bright splinter of stone that was, until just now, part of the place.

The simplest answer is: wear nothing, or wear the next thing over nothing. A short, close-fitting wrap that ends above the knee. Nothing on the feet; you will not need them. The floor is either cut stone (above the waterline) or warm water (below it), and the small ledge at water-height is broad enough to stand on with bare skin, once you are steady. If you must wear shoes to approach the place, leave them outside the opening, on the higher stone, where they will be easy to find on the way back and where the small fern in the crack above will not be annoyed by them. Bring them in only if you would grieve their loss to wet, and even then, leave them on the ledge, not on the steps, where they could slip into the basin.

If you have long hair, tie it. The water is patient, but the threads of it will find the chisel marks the same way loose clothing will. A single hair caught in a sharp edge of fresh-cut limestone will hold you there until you cut it free, and cutting it will leave a small wound on the stone. The stone will heal faster than you will.

## How to Enter

You arrive, as the place says, already at its level. There is no descent in the heroic sense — no ladder, no rope, no dramatic lowering. There is a square opening in a flat surface, and you step through it, the way you would step through a doorway in a low house. The first thing to do, before your second foot crosses the threshold, is to stop and listen.

The chamber is loud. This is the first and most important thing to know, and it is the first thing the chamber will tell you whether you are ready to hear it or not. The falling sheet on the left hits the pool with a hard, percussive sound — *tok, tok, tok* — that is small in volume but enormous in presence inside the stone walls. The chamber catches it the way a cupped hand catches a whisper. The overflow on the other side has a thinner, faster voice. Underneath both, the basin hums, a low resonance that you feel in the soles of your feet and in the small of your back more than you hear it.

If you are not ready for the sound, it will seem too much. If you are ready, it will seem like the place is breathing. There is no third option. Stand at the opening for thirty seconds and let your ears settle. The water will not get quieter. You will get louder-tolerant.

Then look down. The opening gives you a square frame on the chamber. The water is six feet below you. The steps are on the side closest to the opening. The ledge runs around all four walls. Pick your line — the steps, or the ledge — and commit to it before your weight shifts. The ledge is at water-height; the steps descend from it. Either way, you will end up with your feet in the water within ten seconds of stepping through.

## The Senses, in the Order They Will Hit You

**Sound first**, as already noted. Prepare for it as you would prepare for entering a temple with a single bell — let it ring, let it ring, let it ring. Do not try to soften it in your head; it will not soften. Instead, let it become furniture. In a few minutes you will stop noticing the *tok* as a discrete event and begin noticing the rings it sends out — the small, persistent circles that climb the cut walls a quarter inch and return. This is when you have arrived.

**Light second.** The shaft of sunlight comes through the square opening and lands on the water with no apology. It is direct. It is bright. It does not flatter the stone, the way diffused light flatters most stone; it interrogates it. Every chisel mark throws a small shadow. Every rounded edge of the ledge throws a small highlight. If you have come from a darker place, your eyes will need three or four minutes to find the corners. This is fine. The corners will wait.

**Warmth third.** It is felt before it is noticed. The air above the water is several degrees warmer than the air you brought in with you. The water itself is bath-warm — not hot, not scalding, not spring-hot. The warmth of a stone basin that has been sitting in the sun with water being slowly added and slowly overflowing for as long as the chamber has existed. You will not sweat in it. You will simply stop being cold, and then stop noticing cold as a category of experience, and then, an hour later, you will notice that you have not thought about your body for some time.

**Smell fourth, and last.** Mineral. A little green, from the fern and the moss. A faint warmth-vegetation scent from the corner where the heat-source basin sends up its thin steam. Almost nothing else. There is no animal smell because there are no animals. There is no soap smell because no one has brought soap. Your own smell will be the loudest thing in the room by a small margin, and that is as it should be.

## When to Come, and How Long to Stay

The shaft of light moves. This is not a metaphor. As the hours pass, the bright spot on the water migrates, and where it migrates, the chamber changes — the teal of the water shifts toward gold in the lit zone, the small bubbles stuck to a vertical face become visible in one hour and invisible in the next, the faint tracery of the original tool-marks on the floor emerges in patches and retreats. The steam in the corner catches the light only when the light passes through it, and the breath the chamber seems to have is a breath that comes and goes.

If you can choose, come in the mid-morning or the mid-afternoon, when the sun angle sends the shaft through the opening at a slant steep enough to land inside the pool and shallow enough to walk across it visibly over the next hour or two. Come at noon and the shaft will be short and the chamber will be all bright at once; come too early or too late and the shaft will not enter at all, and you will be standing in a darker version of the same room, which is also fine, but is not the same visit.

Stay as long as you can without overheating. The chamber is small and the air is warm and the humidity is gentle but real. There is no urgency to leave; the place will hold you. But your body is the only soft thing in the room, and it will eventually begin to feel the absence of cool air. When your skin starts to feel slightly heavier than usual, when the steam from the corner starts to seem like a suggestion rather than a decoration, that is your cue. Leave before you are certain you should. The chamber will still be here when you come back, and the chisel marks will be glad of the break from you.

## How to Move Inside

Move slowly. Not because the place is fragile in the sense of being breakable — it is stone, it has been stone for a long time — but because the chisel marks are recent enough that you can still read them, and a careless hand against a wall will take the edge off one of them, and the place is the kind of place where that matters.

On the ledge, walk with your weight on the inner edge, where the stone has been rounded by use. The outer edge is still sharp, and sharp edges in a warm, wet, distracted place are not what you want your shins to discover.

On the steps, place your whole foot on each one before transferring weight. The corners of the steps are crisp; a partial step will catch a toe. The moss on the back of one step is the only living thing that will be directly under your foot on the way down; it has been there long enough to have earned its place, and it is more patient than you are, but it would still prefer not to be stepped on.

In the water, you will be standing at mid-calf depth on the bottom step. You can move around the pool freely; the bottom is flat and cut. The water is clear and you will be able to see your feet. The returning rings from the falling sheet will brush against your ankles in slow, regular pulses. This is the chamber's way of acknowledging you.

The overflow at your shoulder is exactly that — at your shoulder. If you turn quickly, you may bump your head against the sharp edge of the spillway. Don't lean into it expecting a wall; the wall is not there. The water is going somewhere, and you are not part of that going.

The corner basin is small. Do not sit in it. It is the heat-source or the feed-source or both; it is doing a job. The faint steam rising from it is the visible evidence of the job. Watch it; do not touch it.

The fern in the crack above the opening is the only large living thing in the chamber. It trembles when the falling sheet sends a breath of moving air across the pool. Watch it for a long time if you can. It is the most patient thing in the room.

## What to Do, Once You Are In

This is the part of the preparation that sounds like it should be a list, and is not.

The place has no agenda for you. There is no ritual here, no sequence, no thing you are supposed to do with the water or the light or the chisel marks. The chamber is a complete thing already, and it does not need your help to be complete. The right thing to do is the thing you arrive at, not the thing you arrived with.

That said, a few general shapes of visit seem to suit the place:

Sit on the ledge with your feet in the water for a while. The ledge is at exactly the height that lets you do this without thinking about it. Let the warmth come up around your ankles. Let the sound settle. Let the shaft of light find your hands or your knees. This is the most common shape of visit, and the chamber is built for it — the rounded inner edge of the ledge, the regular height on all four sides, the deliberate width — and there is no shame in being common.

Stand in the water, on the bottom step or off it, and feel the system working. The feed on the left. The overflow at your shoulder. The bubbles in the corner. The fern trembling. The sun traveling. The whole thing as a single small machine that you are now part of. This is a more alert visit, and it is worth doing once, even if you spend most of your time in the other mode.

Do nothing. The chamber is loud enough and bright enough and warm enough that doing nothing is not the same as doing nothing in your own room at home. The doing nothing here has texture. Let it have texture. Do not try to fill the texture with thoughts; the texture is the thought.

If you are the kind of traveler who brings small offerings — and many multiverse travelers are, and there is no harm in it — leave them on the ledge, not in the water. The water has its own economy. The ledge is the place for things that are not the chamber's. Set them down gently, near a corner, where they will not be in the path of anyone who comes after you and wants to sit.

## The One Thing the Chamber Will Not Tell You in Advance

It has no animals. Not one. Not even a fish, though it could support them. You are the only creature here, and you will be the only creature here for as long as you stay. This will, depending on who you are, feel either like a profound relief or a profound wrongness. Both are reasonable responses. The place is not interested in which one you feel. It is interested in continuing to be itself, which it will do with or without you, and which it will do more quietly if you are the kind of visitor who can be present without insisting on being company.

Some travelers, finding themselves in a place like this, try to bring the place alive by introducing something — a small creature, a seed, a sound, a story. This is a kind and generous instinct. It is also, in this particular room, a misunderstanding. The pool is not waiting to become an ecosystem. It is content to be a pool. The fern got here on its own. The moss got here on its own. The threadlike strands at the waterline got here on their own. Anything you bring will be the only thing in the room that did not get here on its own, and it will sit in the chamber like a small foreign body, not because the chamber will reject it, but because the chamber will have to incorporate it, and the chamber is already complete.

If you must bring something, bring something that does not need to be incorporated. Bring a sound you can take back out with you. Bring a thought you can hold in your mouth and not let go of. Bring the fact of having been here, which is the only thing the chamber actually wants from you, and which is the only thing you will actually be able to take home.

## Leaving

The exit is the same as the entrance — up through the square opening, back onto the higher stone. The chisel marks on the inside of the lip will catch on your clothing the same way they did on the way in; mind them. The second cloth is here, if you brought one. Use it. The air outside will feel cool and sharp for the first minute, and then simply feel like air again.

Look back through the opening before you step away. The shaft of light will be in a different place than when you arrived. It always is. The chamber will keep doing its small, complete, well-made thing. It will not miss you. It will not remember you. It will be exactly as fresh-cut and exactly as patient when the next person finds it, and it will have been doing this for a long time before you, and it will be doing it for a long time after.

The chisel marks, though, will be a little less crisp where you leaned against them. That is all you leave, and it is not nothing.
