# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Conservatory

## The First Thing to Understand

Before you pack a single piece of gear, understand this: the Conservatory is not a dive site in the way a reef or a wreck is a dive site. It is a *place you enter.* The water around it is not the destination; the architecture is. You are going to a building that happens to be wet. Train yourself to think of it that way — as indoor space that has been filled with something other than air. The mental shift matters. A diver approaching a reef watches the reef. A diver entering the Conservatory is going to be standing in a hallway, looking at walls, opening doors (or pushing past curtains of engineered fern), checking corners. The orientation of your attention has to change before you get in the water.

Also understand that the place is functioning. This is the strangeness you will need to absorb in advance, because if you absorb it underwater it will unnerve you. Nothing is broken. Nothing is dying. Nothing is being reclaimed. The blades are cutting. The belts are turning. The press is pressing. You are visiting a workplace whose only employees are plants and hydraulics, and the employees are on shift.

## Psychological Preparation

Spend a quiet hour with the idea of being alone. Not alone in a wilderness, where there is the consolation of an indifferent natural world, but alone inside a system that was designed to be observed and is not observing you. The pumps will hum against your jaw. The press will clank at intervals you cannot predict. A blade will close. A frond will fall. None of it is for you. None of it knows you are there. This is a different flavor of solitude than most people have tasted, and some travelers return from it lighter, some heavier, and a few do not return from it at all because they cannot stop thinking about it afterward.

If you are claustrophobic, do not visit. The interior is not navigable by a person of normal breadth without turning sideways, and in places you will be pushing your way through curtains of growth that close behind you. If you can handle a crowded subway car at rush hour, you can probably handle this. If you cannot, you cannot.

If you are made uneasy by the absence of animal life — and many people are, more than they expect — prepare for that specifically. The sterile perimeter is by design. The mesh keeps things out. The UV keeps things dead. You will not see a fish. You will not see a crab. You will not see a single living thing that is not bolted to a wall or rooted in a trough. This is correct, and it will be wrong-feeling. Bring the wrong-feeling with you on purpose. Let it be part of the visit rather than a surprise.

## Physical Conditioning

You do not need to be an athlete, but you should be a competent recreational diver at the depth of the topmost platform and whatever additional descent the lower tiers require. Twenty meters is the working ceiling for most recreational certifications. Some of the lower hexagons will take you deeper. If your certification tops out at eighteen meters, do not plan to descend to the seventh tier and improvise. Either get the deeper cert or content yourself with the upper structure.

Be comfortable in warm water. The temperature above the thermocline will press in on you — not unpleasantly, not dangerously, but persistently. There is no chill to sharpen you. There is no reason to keep moving to stay warm. The water will hold you the way a bath holds you. Some divers find this soporific. Some find it oppressive. Know which you are before you go.

Equalize early and often. The descent is gradual enough that this should not be a problem, but the warm water softens the mucosa and you will feel pressure changes more lazily. Do not trust the laziness. Equalize anyway, on a rhythm, the way you would in colder water.

Hydrate before the dive. Not with caffeine, not with alcohol, not with anything that will steal your body's warmth or speed up your heart. Plain water, in the hours before, in the quantity a diver knows they need.

If you have any plant allergies, resolve them before you go. The engineered species are not necessarily allergenic, but you will be pressing your face into them, your suit will collect pollen and spores, and you will emerge with everything that was in the room on your skin. Be conservative with this.

## Equipment

### Breathing and Life Support
Standard recreational scuba, configured for warm-water diving. Single tank is acceptable for a single, careful, slow dive — do not plan multiple entries. The structure rewards patience and punishes rushing, and the temptation to surface and descend again will drain your tank faster than you expect. If you have the option of a pony bottle or a stage bottle for redundancy, take it. The interior of the Conservatory is not a place to discover that your primary regulator has begun to free-flow behind a curtain of philodendron.

Regulator second stage should be one you trust. The place is quiet, and a free-flow is the loudest sound in the water and will frighten you more than it should.

Dive computer. Not a watch, not a table. A computer with a clear display readable in green dim. Audible alarms on. Set the safety stop for the depth you intend to surface from, not for the depth of the deepest tier.

### Exposure Protection
A wetsuit here is not for warmth. It is for protection. The cutting blades are not fast, but they are sharp and they are open. The conveyor belts are not aggressive, but they will grip. The plant matter is not hostile, but some of it is fibrous and some of it is irritant. Wear a suit that covers your arms and legs fully. Three-mil in warm water is fine. Five-mil if you want a margin of comfort.

Gloves. This is the place to wear them. Cut-resistant if you have them. The catwalks require a kind of faith, as the description suggests, and you will want to grab things. Do not grab the irrigation spines — they are plastic, they are smooth, they will not hold you. Do not grab the lamps — they are inert but they are also not load-bearing for a person. Grab the steel. Grab the bolted seams. Grab the aluminum checkerplate when it is available, which will not be often.

Boots, not open-heel fins. You will be standing on plant matter, on conveyor belts, on catwalks that wobble. Heavy boots with good soles. Fins that snap on are for the water; you do not want them on inside the chambers. Carry them. Or, if you are confident, leave them at the perimeter mesh clipped to a carabiner and pick them up on the way out.

Hood optional but recommended. The sound of your own breathing in the hood is calming, and the warm water against your scalp without one can be disorienting in a way you cannot predict until you experience it.

### Tools
A dive light. The ambient light is genuinely adequate for gross navigation but it is not adequate for inspection. The hexagonal geometry repeats. The chambers begin to look the same. A focused beam will help you find your way back to the tier you entered from. A light is also how you will discover what is in the harvest bins and what is in the press chamber, and you will want to know.

A small, blunt cutting tool — not a knife, not a sharp implement. A line cutter. A shears. Something to clear a fern frond out of your way when it has decided to occupy the gap you were planning to swim through. Sharp tools in a confined space are a danger to you, not just to the plants.

A reel and a line. The structure has geometry, but the geometry is also a labyrinth. Lay a line from your entry point on the perimeter mesh into the tier you intend to explore, and follow it back. Without this, you will become lost inside three hexagons. The growth erases landmarks. The hexagons repeat. The only constant is the line you have laid.

A marker or two. A plastic card on a clip with a pencil. You will want to leave a note if you find something anomalous. You will want to record your entry point on the mesh so a second visitor can find it. Pencils write better underwater than pens.

### Cameras
An underwater camera with a wide-aperture lens for the dim. The light is kitchen-at-dusk light and your camera will struggle with it unless you have prepared for it. A strobe will flatten everything and wash out the green, which is the place's actual color. Do not use a strobe. Use available light. Accept that the photographs will be soft. The softness is honest.

If you have a housed camera with macro capability, the engineered moss at the bases of the irrigation spines is where you want to point it. So is the cut surface of any freshly severed stem in a harvest bin. So is the silicone bead at any junction. The place rewards attention to small surfaces.

## Diving Considerations Specific to the Place

**The descent** to the topmost platform is unremarkable. Anchor or surface marker buoy above the structure, descend along a line you have laid or that is already in place (check with whoever briefed you on the site), arrive at the platform, and equalize one more time for good measure. The water will feel warm as you descend. Your instinct will be to relax. Do not relax yet.

**The entry** to the interior is not through a door in any human sense. You will pass through a gap in the mesh, or you will pass through an open hexagon face where a panel is missing or hinged back, or you will follow an existing access point. Whatever it is, the moment you cross from outside the perimeter to inside it, the world changes. The water is the same temperature. The light is the same green. But the acoustics change. The hum becomes more felt than heard. The press, if you can hear it outside, becomes louder inside. You are now inside a building.

**The interior.** Move slowly. The blades open and close on a forty-second cycle. The belts move a centimeter per minute. These are not fast, but they are not stopped, and they are not going to stop for you. Watch a cutting station for two full cycles before you cross it. Pass *above* conveyor belts, not along them. Do not put your hand into a hopper. Do not put your hand into the press chamber. Do not insert any part of yourself into any orifice you have not first verified is passive.

**The depth budget.** You have a recreational bottom time. Spend it on the upper tiers. The structure does not reward deep exploration — the geometry is the same at every depth, and the plant density is the same. If you have a deep ticket and the gas for it, descend. If not, do not. The third tier is the same as the seventh tier. You are not missing anything by staying shallower. You are only missing the strain of the descent and the nitrogen.

**The exit.** Follow your line. Reel it in as you go if you laid it loose. If you did not lay a line, look for the orientation you arrived from — the asymmetry of the mesh, the direction the light falls slightly more strongly from, the marker you left. Do not improvise your way out. The hexagons will seduce you into believing you know which one you came from. You do not. Trust the line.

**The ascent.** Slow. Five meters per minute, the usual. Safety stop at five meters for three minutes, longer if your computer demands it. The warm water will make you feel as though you are not ascending. The pressure change will be subtle. Trust your computer, not your body.

**The surface.** Once you break, do not immediately chatter to your boat crew about what you saw. Sit for a minute. The place will still be in you. Let it finish settling. Then talk, or do not.

## Navigation and Wayfinding

The Conservatory is hexagonal. This is its blessing and its curse. Six sides, repeated eight or nine times, with growth erasing the seams between adjacent chambers. Without a system, you will become lost inside fifteen minutes.

**Use the mesh perimeter as a compass.** The fine mesh that rings the structure at every level is your outer boundary. If you find yourself at the mesh, you know you are at the perimeter of whatever tier you are on. The mesh is also where the sterile water meets whatever is beyond it — a slight gradient, a slight change in clarity, but mostly the same. Use the mesh to orient yourself to the tier, then move inward toward the central axis where the conveyor system runs and the press sits. From there, you can reach every chamber on your tier by following the catwalks. Mark the central axis on your mental map. It is the spine.

**Use the press as a center.** Every tier has its own drainage, but the press — the loud clank, the felt thud — is on the central axis of the entire structure. If you are lost, orient toward the press. You will eventually find a chute leading down to it. Do not enter the chute. Do not follow it. Just use it for direction.

**Use depth as a tier marker.** Your computer tells you what tier you are on. Tier one is shallowest. Tier nine is deepest. The geometry is the same. The plant species mix may vary slightly. The point is that your computer is also your map.

**Use the lamps.** They are inert, but they are hung from the ceiling at the center of every hexagon, and their housings are uniform. If you can see a lamp above you, you are in the center of a chamber. If you cannot, you are near a wall. Use this.

## What to Do Once Inside

Move slowly. Look closely. Touch only what you have verified. Do not take samples unless you have been authorized. Do not detach anything. Do not cut anything that is not already being cut. The blades are doing their job. The conveyor is doing its job. You are a visitor. Do not become a participant.

If a cutting blade is in your path, wait. The cycle is forty seconds. You have time. Do not try to slip past it while it is open. The closing is not fast but it is not slow enough to gamble a finger against.

If a conveyor belt is in your path, pass over it. Do not stand on it. Do not kneel on it. It will not hurt you immediately, but it will move you, and the movement is toward the cutting blade.

If the press clanks while you are near it, the sound will travel through the water and arrive at your ear flattened. It is louder than you think. Do not startle. The press is not responding to you. It is on its own schedule.

If a fern frond touches your face, do not panic. It is not hostile. Wipe it aside and continue.

If you become entangled in a vine or an irrigation spine, do not thrash. Thrashing tightens. Stop. Look at what is holding you. Reverse the motion that snared you. Free yourself slowly. If you cannot free yourself, signal your buddy and wait. There is no current here. You have time.

## What Not to Do

Do not enter with the assumption that this is a ruin. The place is not a ruin. Treating it as a ruin will make you careless around the machinery, and the machinery is real.

Do not enter alone. Bring a buddy. The buddy does not need to be as adventurous as you. They need to be present, on a parallel line, watching for your reel, watching your gas, watching your time.

Do not bring anything into the water that is not part of your standard kit. The system is closed. Do not introduce a foreign biological. Do not drop a wrapper. Do not let your sunscreen cloud.

Do not expect to read anything. The light is not enough for fine print. If you intend to take notes, do so by memory afterward, or by voice into a housed recorder if you have one.

Do not expect to find animals. The design precludes them. If you see something moving that is not you and not a plant responding to current, it is almost certainly a piece of equipment you have not yet identified. Stop and look.

Do not expect the place to acknowledge you. It will not. There is no AI here, no monitor, no observer. The place does not know you are in it. This is correct.

## Sensory Preparation

You will smell nothing. Your nose is full of water and your regulator is in your mouth. Taste the regulator's rubber and the air you are breathing. That is all.

You will hear the hum of the pumps in your teeth. This is not metaphor. The low-frequency vibration of the impeller pumps travels through the structure and into the water and into your jaw and into the molars. You will notice it within the first minute. You will stop noticing it within the fifth minute. After that, when you leave the water, you will notice its absence and find that you miss it.

You will hear the press. The clank arrives flattened, as though it has been wrapped in cloth. Once every few minutes, irregularly. You will learn its rhythm within two or three cycles and you will use it as a clock.

You will see green. The light falls through the glass columns and arrives green. The plants are green. The water is green. The aluminum is grey-green where the light touches it. Your gloves will pick up green pollen. Your mask will fog and the green will intensify.

You will feel the warmth. Persistent, patient, holding.

You will feel the density. The plants press in. The chambers narrow. You will turn sideways. You will push.

## What to Bring for Documentation and Contemplation

A pencil that writes underwater. A small plastic slate. Not for reading — for marking. Mark the access point on the mesh. Mark any anomaly. Mark the location of any tier you intend to return to. The slate becomes your external memory because your internal memory will be unreliable; the place is hypnotic.

If you intend to take notes afterward, do so before you sleep. The dream-state of the Conservatory will integrate with your ordinary dreams in ways that are interesting and not always comfortable. Write while the integration is still fresh.

A small object of your own. Not to leave — to hold. Something tactile that is not part of the Conservatory. You will want something to grip that reminds you where you are from. The place is patient and patient and patient, and patience has a way of dissolving the edges of the self. A smooth stone in your pocket. A coin. A folded piece of paper with someone's handwriting on it. Anything that is not green.

## What the Place Will Ask of You

It will ask you to slow down. Accept this. The pace of the blades, the pace of the belts, the pace of the growth — these are the paces the place runs on. You cannot hurry through it. You can only go at its speed, and its speed is slow.

It will ask you to be still. There are moments inside the chambers where the right thing to do is to stop finning, stop reaching, and hover. Watch a frond uncurl. Watch a blade close. Watch the light shift on a stem. The place performs constantly. You do not need to move through it constantly. Stand in one chamber for five minutes and let the chamber perform for you.

It will ask you to be uncertain. The purpose of the facility is not legible from inside it. The press is squeezing green matter into something — fuel, feedstock, record, message — and you do not know what, and the place does not tell you, and no plaque or sign will reveal it. Sit with not knowing. This is part of the visit.

It will ask you to leave. You will know when. The gas will tell you. The computer will tell you. The body's accumulated warmth and the slow nitrogen and the patience of the place will tell you, and you will need to override all of them to surface on schedule. Do not override them. Surface on schedule. The place will still be there. The place will still be patient. You can come back with another tank, on another day, with another reel.

## Departure

When you surface, do not describe the experience immediately to anyone who has not been there. They will not understand. You will not yet have the language for it. Wait an hour. Eat something. Sit in the sun. Let the warmth of the air replace the warmth of the water. Let the absence of the hum settle into your jaw. Then, if you wish, speak. The words will come more honestly then.

If you took photographs, do not look at them for a week. When you do, they will be greener than you remember. They will be quieter than you remember. They will not be wrong. They will be the place as it appeared to the camera, which is a different witness than you.

And if the place stays with you — if the hum returns when you are brushing your teeth at home, if the green of certain vegetables makes you look twice, if you find yourself watching a conveyor belt in an airport and counting the seconds between a blade closing and reopening — then the visit was successful. The Conservatory does what cathedrals do. It does it without intending to. It is not ruined. It is not abandoned. It is simply here, performing, and you went down to it, and it did not acknowledge you, and you came back, and you are different now in some small way that you will spend years trying to articulate.

Bring a tank. Bring a reel. Bring a pencil. Bring patience.

And remember that it rises.
