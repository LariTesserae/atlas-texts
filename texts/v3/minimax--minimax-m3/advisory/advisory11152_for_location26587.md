# Notes for a Traveler Bound for the Unreliable Valley

The cartographer was wise to use the word *unreliable*. Treat the place the same way — not with suspicion, exactly, but with the understanding that your usual instruments may not behave as you expect, and that the discrepancy may not be revealed until you are already somewhere else, trying to remember.

## Before You Go

**Do not announce your visit.** This should be a rule for almost any location, but it is more than courtesy here. Whatever holds this place in its current configuration appears to be reinforced by its own consistency. Outsiders who arrive with the stated intention of *studying* or *documenting* the place may find that the place closes, gently, around them — not violently, not unkindly, but in a way that makes observation difficult. The first cartographer passed through and wrote a casual line in a ledger. The town seems to have accepted that line. Behave as if you are a casual line in someone's ledger.

**Bring nothing that remembers too much.** A camera with a full memory card, a notebook already half-filled with other observations, a watch synchronized to a network — these will be felt by the place as a kind of crowding, and the place is careful about crowding. Bring an empty notebook. Wear a watch you do not care about. If you must take photographs, bring film, not a digital body, and have the film developed somewhere else.

**Bring warm, damp-tolerant clothing that you do not intend to keep.** The damp is not metaphorical. Wool and oilskin are appropriate; synthetics will feel wrong in a way you will not be able to name, and that wrongness will be contagious to your mood. Wear layers. Accept that everything you bring will be heavier on the way out.

**Do not bring a guide, a translator, or a fixed schedule.** You will be offered all of these if you ask. Decline. The town will give you what it gives you, in its own time, which is not your time.

## On Arrival

**Expect the approach to be longer than it should be.** The valley is reached by a ridge, and the ridge is approached by a series of ridges, and the cartographer's notes about the maps ending three days back are not an exaggeration. Plan for this. Do not arrive hungry or exhausted; the town offers hospitality, but you will not be in any state to receive it properly.

**Expect the town to have noticed you before you notice the town.** The deer on the rooftops will see you first. Then the fat black bird with the red throat. Then, when you come over the final rise and the town is suddenly laid out below you on its impossible shelf, the children in the lanes will look up at the same moment, all of them, and one of them will smile in a way that is not a smile so much as a recognition.

**Do not count the children.** This is not because counting them will be upsetting. It is because the number you arrive at will be the number you remember, and the number you remember will be the number that stays with you, and the number that stays with you may not be the number that is correct. The town does not lie. It simply does not correct.

## While You Are There

**Match the pace of the place.** The quiet is not the absence of sound; it is a presence in itself, and the town extends it to you whether you want it or not. Walk slowly. Speak softly. Do not hurry between terraces. The market square is a good place to stand and simply receive the hush for a while; the sound of a person speaking a quarter mile away, arriving wrapped, is a thing that needs to be heard to be understood.

**Eat what is offered.** The cabbages are extraordinary. The barley is a thing you will not taste elsewhere. The cheese is made from the milk of goats that have eaten the ferns, and the cheese tastes of the valley in a way that is not unpleasant but is also not entirely of this world. Eat it anyway. The town is hospitable, and refusal is a small wound, and small wounds accumulate here in a way that is hard to account for later.

**Drink the water from the streams, not from any vessel.** The locals will offer you cups. Accept the cups, hold them, set them down politely, and then drink from the pale threads falling off the cliffs. There is no practical reason for this. You will feel better for it.

**Do not attempt to leave the shelf.** The town is the place. The paths that lead up into the higher terraces end at the council hall, and the paths that lead down to the river end at a point that is not a path anymore. The town is what the town is. Stay on the shelf.

**Do not ask about the moss.** Or the children. Or the timbers. Or the cartographer. The people will answer you if you ask, and their answers will be accurate as far as they go, and you will be the worse for hearing them. The town has been here for three hundred years, and the people have been these people for three hundred years, and they are not unhappy about this, and you do not need to make them explain why.

**If you see a shape against the snow that is too large and too still, do not look at it for long.** Look once. Register what you have seen. Look away. The locals do not look up at those shapes. This is not because the shapes are dangerous. It is because the shapes are *old*, in a way the mountains around them are young, and the attention you pay them is a kind of weight the valley does not need.

**At dusk, listen for the black bird.** It sings only at dusk. The song is not a song you will be able to reproduce or notate. It is a song that exists in the valley and possibly nowhere else. Stand somewhere with a view of the rooftops and listen. The small grey deer will be out, stepping across the slates in their impossible way. The fog will come down to sit. This is the time of day the valley belongs most fully to itself.

## On the Light

You will not notice it while you are there. This is important. The sun will be where the sun is, the shadows will fall as shadows fall, the dampness will be the dampness it is. You will think you are having a perfectly ordinary experience of a place that is simply damp and green and quiet.

Later — possibly weeks later, possibly years — you will try to remember the position of the sun at a particular moment. You will not be able to. You will try to remember whether the shadows fell to the east or the west at midday. You will not be able to. The memory will be of *sun* and *shadow* as qualities, not as positions. The valley gives you the light without the clock.

Do not try to recover the clock while you are there. The clock is what the cartographer lost. He tried to do his work in the town, and the town did not prevent him, and he wrote his note, and he left, and somewhere on the road back the clock he had been carrying in his head — the assumption of change, of duration, of *after* — caught on something, and he could not find it again, and he did not come back.

## On Leaving

**Leave when the town lets you.** Do not announce your departure. Do not say goodbye in any formal sense. Simply, at some point in the day, walk back to the ridge. The path will be there. The town will not prevent you from leaving. The town does not prevent anything; it simply *is*, and you are in it for a time, and then you are not.

**Take nothing with you.** Not a stone, not a leaf, not a scrap of moss, not a memory you have written down on the spot. The town will not notice if you take these things, but you will notice, later, that what you have taken has become heavy in a way that is not a matter of weight. Leave everything in the valley. The valley does not need it. You do.

**Expect the journey out to be shorter than the journey in.** This will feel wrong. It is correct. The ridge is closer than you remember. The maps that ended three days back will, on the way out, be the maps that begin again. You will reach the edge of the surveyed country and the surveyed country will be where you left it, and you will step into it, and the surveyed country will not be damp.

**Do not return immediately.** Give yourself a year. Give yourself longer. The valley will be there if you go back, and the valley will be the same valley, and you will be the same you, and the moss on the timbers will be the same moss, and the children in the lanes will be the same children. This is the bargain the valley offers, and the bargain is not a trap. It is simply what the valley is.

**When you try to tell other people about the place, you will find that you cannot describe it in a way that sounds like a real place.** This is the unreliability the cartographer warned of. The place is real. Your experience of it was real. The dampness was real. The sound of the bird at dusk was real. The hand-adzed joints in the roof beams were real. The way the deer stepped across the rooftops was real. But the *telling* of it will slide, and the people you tell will nod politely, and the place will not be a place they have any reason to believe in.

This is fine. The cartographer wrote his note. *Small settlement, of no great note.* That is enough. The valley does not need to be believed. It needs only to be left alone, in the way that it is.

Go gently.
