# Preparing to Enter the Forge-Vein

The first thing to settle in your body is the honest answer to a question you will be tempted to dodge: *you do not belong here, and the place will not pretend otherwise.* Everything that follows is built on that admission. A traveler who arrives thinking they can "handle" this chamber is a traveler who has already begun to make the kind of small error that, in here, becomes the last error.

So preparation begins before the first piece of equipment is checked.

---

## I. The Work You Do in the Days Before

This is not a place you visit on the back of another difficult visit. Your lungs, your hearing, your skin, and your mind should be in good working order when you approach it. If you are recovering from any respiratory illness — even a lingering cough — postpone. The air inside does not negotiate with damaged tissue. The sulfur and chloride compounds here will settle into irritation and turn it into injury inside a few breaths.

In the week before, do not load your body with new sensory demands. Do not spend the day before in a loud concert or a smoky room or a hot yoga studio. Your reserves of tolerance are finite, and you will need every bit of them.

Drink more water than you think is reasonable for the two days prior. The chamber is ferociously dry, and you want to arrive slightly over-hydrated rather than merely adequate. Your skin and mucous membranes will thank you for the buffer.

Spend a few minutes, in the days before, simply *thinking about heat*. Not endurance training. Not acclimatization. Just sitting with the memory of a very hot day, or a sauna, or pressing your hand against a sun-warmed wall, and letting your body remember what heat feels like as a sustained fact rather than a passing discomfort. The chamber is a place where heat is a permanent resident. Arriving in a state of mental friendship with heat is worth more than any single piece of gear.

---

## II. The Air You Will Carry In

The chamber's atmosphere is not air in any rescue-relevant sense of the word. It is a hot, dry, mineral-charged breath that contains gases which can disable you faster than you can name them — hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen chloride, very likely carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide, and an aerosol of metallic salts. The phrase in the description, that it would kill a man in two breaths, is not metaphor; it is a warning written into the place.

You will bring your own atmosphere with you. This is not optional and it is not a place for filter masks or simple respirators. You need a self-contained breathing apparatus — the kind firefighters wear into structural fires, the kind divers wear underwater. A full-face mask, a regulator, a cylinder of compressed air or, ideally, a mixed gas optimized for high-heat work. The air you breathe from the cylinder will feel cool and damp against your face, and that contrast is itself a small mercy you should anticipate gratefully.

Bring redundancy. Carry a full primary cylinder and a full backup. Calculate your time-on-station against your consumption rate with a comfortable margin; the chamber will tempt you to stay longer than is wise, and you need a hard personal limit, not a calculated one. The chamber's air does not smell worse as it gets worse — many of its worst gases paralyze your sense of smell before they paralyze you. So your decision to leave must be made by clock, not by nose.

Check every seal. Test the regulator with the mask on, in clean air, breathing hard. Make sure the exhalation valve does not stick. Make sure you can swallow and clear your ears with the mask on; the chamber is a small space and you do not want the first hint of claustrophobia tangled up with equipment malfunction.

The face shield of your mask should be rated for high heat and should be wide enough that you can see the floor at your feet without tipping your head dangerously forward.

---

## III. Heat, and What to Wear Against It

The chamber's air is "bread fresh from an oven." That is roughly the temperature of a working pizza oven, which puts ambient air somewhere between 90 and 120°C depending on where you stand and how near the central vent you are. The central vent itself is the place "where the heat crosses over and becomes something that does not forgive," and you treat that as a radiant heat source well above 200°C, capable of causing a thermal burn through ordinary clothing in seconds.

You will wear an aluminized proximity suit — the kind foundry workers and volcanologists use. The outer layer reflects radiant heat back into the room. Beneath it, a vapor barrier to keep your sweat from cooking you inside your own insulation. Beneath *that*, a layer of aramid or wool that can take sustained high temperature without melting into your skin. The suit should be loose enough that air can move between the layers; a skin-tight suit in this environment is a steam-cooker.

Your boots matter more than they usually do. They need thick, insulating soles — the floor is the throat of the mountain and heat conducts upward through it. Leather soles will not do. You want soles rated for high-temperature contact, with metatarsal guards, because you may step on mineral edges and because the floor's fibrous mat can hide sharp protrusions.

Your gloves need to allow dexterity for your mask regulator and your light controls, but they need to be rated for contact heat. They will get hot. That is acceptable; what is not acceptable is contact burn.

Your head and neck are the most vulnerable. An aluminized hood that integrates with your jacket, leaving only your faceshield exposed, is what you want. If you have a secondary neck shroud, wear it. The heat comes from above as well as around — the ceiling hangs low, and the fibrous tendrils there will brush against the back of your hood.

You will be hot. There is no version of this visit in which you are not hot. The question is not whether you are hot but whether the heat is moving *through* your layers or staying *outside* them. Every piece of gear is a vote in that question.

---

## IV. The Sound, and What It Will Do to You

The chamber has a continuous roar — not loud the way a bang is loud, but loud the way a held note is loud. It is the sound of pressure with nowhere to go. It does not echo; it accumulates. It presses on the eardrums and on the chest and on the mind, and it does not stop for the duration of your visit.

You wear double hearing protection: foam earplugs seated deep, *and* over-ear muffs rated for industrial noise. You will still hear the roar; it is too fundamental to the place to be silenced. What the protection does is keep the roar from damaging your hearing over a stay of more than a few minutes, and it reduces the sense that the sound is *inside* your head. That distinction matters more than you might expect.

Do not plan to communicate with anyone by voice inside the chamber. Even shouting in your own ears will be a muffled, useless thing. If you are traveling with a partner, agree on hand signals before you go in. If you are traveling alone, accept that you are alone in a way more absolute than ordinary solitude.

The deeper, occasional concussions — the thermal fractures propagating through the wall — are part of the sound. They are not, in themselves, signs of immediate danger. But if one is followed by a sustained change in the air pressure at your ears, or by a sudden shower of grit from the ceiling, treat that as the chamber's way of informing you that a piece of itself has just rearranged, and that you should be elsewhere while it settles.

## V. Light, and How to See

The only natural light inside is the dull red of the central vent. It is enough to read the room by, in the way a single ember is enough to read a familiar room by — but you are not in a familiar room, and the room itself is not stable.

Carry a headlamp. Carry two: a primary and a backup, each with fresh batteries, each sealed against the heat as much as your batteries allow (heat destroys batteries faster than almost anything else). Mount the primary on your hood where it illuminates the floor at your feet.

Use the red-light mode if your lamp has one. The chamber is already a red-lit world, and the creatures inside are eyeless and navigate by heat, chemistry, and vibration — not by light. Preserving your own dark adaptation and minimizing visual disturbance to the room is worth a small investment in lamp function. If your lamp does not have a red mode, tape a layer of red plastic over it. If you do not have red plastic, accept white light and move gently.

Do not use a flame. There is no telling what the atmosphere will do with an open flame, and you do not want to find out.

---

## VI. Water, Food, and the Clock

You carry all the water you will need. There is no water inside. There has perhaps never been water inside. You will be losing moisture to sweat at a rate that will surprise you, and your intake needs to be planned as carefully as your air.

A well-insulated bottle on your hip or in a chest pocket, with a tube you can sip from beneath your mask, is ideal. If your mask does not allow this, plan a single brief removal-and-drink window at the halfway point of your visit — but know that every time you unseal your mask, you are taking a small dose of the chamber's air, and you do that as few times as you can.

Eat a substantial, slow-burn meal in the two hours before you enter. You will not be eating inside. The digestive cost of being in this environment is high; you want fuel that is already being processed.

Set a hard time limit before you enter. Forty-five minutes is generous; thirty is sensible; twenty is wise. The chamber is hypnotic. It will not show you its danger as it accumulates. You must keep the clock, because your body will lie to you about how long you have been in there.

---

## VII. How to Move, and How Not To

The chamber is small. You will be crouched or hunched the entire time. Your shoulders will ache before the visit is over. Plan for this: stretch before, accept it during, recover after.

The floor is the most dangerous surface in the room. The central vent is the throat of the mountain, and standing on it is death by radiant heat; the felt of fibers around it may conceal the vent's true edge, and it may conceal nothing, and you will not know which until your weight tells you. Stay near the walls. Move along the perimeter. If you must approach the vent for any reason, do so on your belly, slowly, with the side of your face turned away from the radiant heat, and reverse out the way you came in.

The walls are "working." They are crusted with mineral blooms, weeping with half-solidified glassy flows, hung with pale tendrils. You do not touch them. The curls of yellow mineral are described as middens — the excreted residue of the small creatures that live in the walls. The dark glassy flows are substances that were fluid at higher temperature and are not fully solid now. Neither is a surface you want your glove against. If a tendril brushes your hood, leave it; if one catches in a joint of your suit, free it carefully without jerking.

The ceiling is hung with the same fibrous mats as the floor. Do not look straight up while standing; the heat radiates from above and the tendrils descend. Walk with your head slightly bowed, eyes on the floor and lower walls.

Move slowly. The mats on the floor part around your weight and resettle; they are not threatening in themselves, but a slow pace gives you time to feel the floor under each step, gives the mats time to settle, and gives the creatures time to register your presence as a thing that is not food and not threat. Quick movements in this room are the kind of small mistake that becomes the last mistake.

---

## VIII. The Creatures, and What to Do About Them

You are inside an ecology. The small white grains on the walls are not decoration; they are workers, taking the rock apart. The mats of pale fiber are not carpet; they are a tissue. The mouse-sized shapes in the folds of the wall-felt are not props; they are animals, or their moral equivalent, feeding and riding the thermal gradients of their home.

None of them, as far as the chamber is concerned, are you. The description is explicit: *you are not a thing they have language for.* They will not attack you. They will not flee you. They will, with any luck, continue about their business as if you were a particularly clumsy piece of weather.

This is the right relationship. Maintain it.

If a creature moves across your path, stop. Wait. Let it pass. Do not touch it. You do not know what temperature it is, what its surface secretions are, what it will do when handled. The risk is not aggression; the risk is the unknown chemistry of a body evolved for this environment and no other.

If you feel something moving on your suit, do not panic. Move to the wall, lean against it carefully (avoiding the worst of the crusts), and let the thing find its way off. Most of the creatures are slow. Panic will injure you faster than the creature will.

You are a visitor in someone else's home. The home does not know you are there. That is the safest version of this visit.

---

## IX. What You Will Not Be Able to Do

Accept these in advance, and the visit will be easier:

- You will not have a conversation.
- You will not eat.
- You will not have a clear line of sight for more than a few feet.
- You will not be able to sit comfortably, and you may not be able to kneel.
- You will not be able to take a meaningful photograph without specialized equipment; if a camera matters to you, bring one rated for high heat with sealed housing, and accept that the images will be unflattering.
- You will not be able to take a sample without contaminating it and possibly yourself; the chamber's chemistry is not forgiving to outside materials.
- You will not be able to leave anything behind. Anything you drop becomes part of the chamber, and the chamber will eat it in time, but in the short term, dropped gear is a foreign object in a sealed system, and the system will respond.

---

## X. The Way Out

Before you enter, be certain of your exit. In a multiverse context this may mean a personal device, a time-window, a portal keyed to your return — whatever your method, verify it before the suit goes on, verify it again after the suit is sealed. The chamber is not a place to discover that your way home has drifted.

If you are exiting under your own locomotion, the path you came in by is the path you leave by. The mountain will not have rearranged itself in the time you were inside — not in any way you would notice — but you may have lost your orientation in the red dark, and a familiar return path is a small mercy. If you have a tether, use it.

---

## XI. After

When you are out, and the mask comes off, the first breath of any other air will taste impossibly clean. That is real; do not be embarrassed by the relief of it.

Do not eat heavily for the first hour. Drink steadily. Check yourself, or be checked: lungs (any cough, any tightness), hearing (any muffling or ringing), skin (any place where heat got through the layers — look at the backs of your hands, the front of your neck, the area behind your knees). If anything is wrong, it is small and it will pass; if it is not small, address it now rather than later.

Do not describe the visit to anyone for at least a day. The place needs to settle in you before you try to put it into language. If you describe it too soon, the words will be the wrong ones, and the wrong ones will stick.

---

## XII. The Last Thing

The Forge-Vein is a sentence the mountain is in the middle of speaking. You are a single consonant in someone else's word, briefly sounded, and then gone. That is not a diminishment. It is the honest shape of the visit. You go in as a witness to a thing that has been making itself for longer than you have a unit of time for, and you come out carrying, if you have been careful, only the memory and the sweat and the knowledge that such a place exists and that it does not need you and that you were, for a small while, allowed inside anyway.

Be still. Be quiet. Be quick. Be kind to the rock that is doing you the honor of letting you feel it think.
