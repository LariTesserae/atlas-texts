# Preparing for the Plateau of the Slow Hum

There is no checklist that fits this place, but there are some honest things I can tell you about what it asks of you.

## Clothing and Skin

The air is warm and wet and never stops being both. Cotton and linen will grow heavy against you, sour against your skin, and stay damp for days if you hang them. Bring things that breathe and that don't mind being wet — merino, hemp, the kind of technical fabrics that smell like nothing after the third wearing. Bring more of it than you think. Nothing dries.

Your feet will spend more time than usual on stone and wet moss. The moss is kind. The stone is hot in places you don't expect. Thick-soled footwear you can walk in for half a day without thinking about your feet. Something that grips when wet.

The insects are the constant. They are not, as I understand them, our insects — they have come back with new words — but they occupy the niche that mosquitoes occupy. Long sleeves. A head net, even though it looks foolish. A soft-brimmed hat that keeps both rain and the low canopy off your neck.

## Water, Food, the Body

You can drink from the rock. Take this seriously and don't be squeamish about it. Lay your face to a flat surface in the early morning and let it in. The water has iron in it, faintly mineral, faintly sweet. Your gut will not be used to it. Bring something for the first few days — water that tastes of nothing, plain water from a place you came from — and then let yourself wean onto the stone-water when your body tells you it is ready. Some travelers say the transition is the most vulnerable they feel there. They are not wrong.

Food. I am told the mushrooms are edible in patterns that change by season, and that some of them glow. I am also told that the ecologists came back with new words and some of them with new stomachs. Eat what the forest seems to be offering, slowly, a small amount the first time, with the rest of your provisions still in your pack. Watch yourself for a day. Watch yourself for two. The plateau is patient with mistakes but it does not always tell you it has been wronged.

Bring salt. Bring something calorie-dense that you recognize. Your appetite will be quieter than you expect — the air itself seems to feed something in you — but your body is still doing work.

## Sleep and Time

This is the part I cannot prepare you for, only warn you about.

A day on the plateau is a day. The sun moves. You sleep when you sleep. But when you wake you will not be sure how long you slept. You will look at your watch and the watch will have an answer, and the answer will not feel right. Do not fight this. Do not try to anchor yourself to the clock. You will lose. The plateau is thicker than clocks.

Sleep when you are tired. Stop when you are tired. Eat when you are hungry. Walk when you want to walk. Let your body's clock drift into the rhythm of the place. Travelers who insist on keeping their home schedule come back haunted, even the ones who won't say so.

Bring a small notebook and mark down what you remember of each day. Do not try to be accurate. Try to be honest. The notebook will help you understand what time was doing to you, later.

## The Wind and Your Teeth

There is a hum. You will feel it first in your teeth, then in the bones of your jaw, then low in your chest. It is not painful. It is constant. It will become the most familiar sound you have ever heard, the way the sound of a refrigerator becomes a room. Carry something soft in your mouth if the hum gets too close — a piece of cloth, a willow twig, whatever the local travelers use. It dampens the vibration in your skull.

The wind at the western edge is deeper and the grooves in the stone there are beautiful. You can press your palm to them. The rock is warm. The wind will take your hat. Tie your hat to yourself.

## The Structures

Do not carve your initials. Not because it is forbidden — it isn't — but because the stone will remember the carve and you will come back next year and see it as fresh as the day you made it, and you will not know how to feel about that. Some travelers carve. Most don't, by the second visit.

When you touch the structures, your heart will try to fall into the hum. Let it, for the moment. The moment passes. The hum goes on. There is no harm in it that I have ever heard of, and there is something in the letting that travelers say is the reason they went.

Do not lie down and sleep inside one of the round rooms. I am told this is important. I am told some travelers have done it and come back speaking a language that was not theirs for a few days. I do not know why this is. Do not lie down and sleep inside one of the round rooms.

## The Pools

You will be drawn to them. The water is shallow and warm and the light from below is something you want to look at. Look. There are shapes down there, long and slow, like things thinking. They do not come up.

If you go down — and some travelers go down — do not describe what you saw. This is not a superstition. It is a kindness to yourself. There are things that are better held in the body that saw them than in the words that try to carry them. The ecologists who went down came back speaking softly and not quite the right dialect and have not, in general, written about it.

Wade in the pools. Let the iron-water sit on your skin. Sit on the warm rock at the edge and watch the shapes not come up. This is enough for most visits.

## What to Carry

- A notebook, a pencil that doesn't mind humidity.
- A head net and a brimmed hat.
- A water vessel you can also drink from the rock through, or that you are willing to set aside.
- Enough food for a few days of being careful, then a willingness to learn the forest's menu.
- A length of soft cloth, for the hum, and for wrapping things you find.
- A small mirror, unbreakable, for signalling across ravines and for seeing behind yourself in the forest where the canopy is dark.
- Something to give back. The plateau has been waiting, the place tells you. It is polite to bring it something. A stone from where you live. A leaf from a tree that is not these trees. A word, spoken aloud, in a language the wind has not heard.

## What to Leave Behind

A watch is optional. A schedule is optional. The idea that you have come to *see* the plateau, as if seeing were the point, is optional. The plateau does not perform. It permits itself to be seen, sometimes, to certain travelers, in certain moods. If you arrive with a list of things to check off, the list will be empty by the second day and you will be freer than you have been in years.

The plateau is patient. Be patient with it. Let it be slow with you. Let the days be thicker than you are used to. Let your teeth hum. Let your heart fall in, for the moment, with the sound the stone is making.

You are going to a place that has been waiting. Walk in as if you have arrived at the part of the waiting that has finally opened.
