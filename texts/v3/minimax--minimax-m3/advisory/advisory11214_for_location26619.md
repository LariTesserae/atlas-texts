# Preparing to Enter the Inverted City

The Underfold is the kind of place that asks less of your body than of your willingness to be confused. Most travelers arrive over-prepared for the cold, the wet, the dark, and under-prepared for the fact that their inner ear will be lied to for the duration of their stay. You will not, mostly, be in danger. You will be in *difficulty*. This is a different problem, and it is the one that breaks people.

## The Entry

The fissure is in a riverbed. The water hesitates there because the rock remembers being afraid. You will feel this hesitation as a chill in your feet before you understand what it means.

- Wear what you would wear to cross a cold stream up to your thighs.
- Your pack should be waterproof, or you should accept that it will not survive the entry intact.
- The crack is narrow. A broad-shouldered traveler will turn sideways, exhale, and turn again. If you wear a pack, push it through the worst pinch first, then follow it like a fish following a smaller fish through a hole.
- The descent is short but steep. The footholds are not carved — they have been worn by ten thousand descents. The settlers made the first grooves; their great-grandchildren polished them.
- There is a moment, about halfway down, where the light from the surface becomes a single pale coin above you and then is gone. You will feel this as a small death. Do not panic. It is only the body remembering the world above, which it will do less and less as the days pass.

## What to Wear

The Underfold is the temperature of a stone cellar, year-round, and the humidity of a steam room. You will not warm up. You will not dry off.

- **Wool, not cotton.** Cotton stays wet and becomes a small cruelty against your skin.
- **Layers, but not too many.** The cold is ambient, not biting. You will be walking on flat ground once you arrive.
- **Footwear is the most important decision you make.** You need soles that grip wet limestone like a gecko's foot. Soft rubber, deeply lugged. Hard leather is treacherous. Bare feet are inadvisable unless you are prepared to commit to the calluses the locals carry.
- **No bright colors.** The locals make their dyes from cave minerals, and the palette is subtle. A tourist in scarlet is read as either a fool or a herald, and you do not want to be a herald.
- **Bring something you do not mind ruining.** The wet gets into everything. Your clothes will smell of limestone for the rest of your natural life after you leave. This is not a complaint. It is a souvenir.

## Your Eyes

There is light. Not much. Not from anywhere you can find. The light in the Underfold is sourceless, the way bioluminescence is sourceless, or the way a dim memory is sourceless — everywhere and nowhere. Your eyes will never quite find its origin. After an hour you stop trying. This is good. Stop trying.

- **Do not bring a strong flashlight.** The locals have lived in this dimness for nine generations, and a sudden beam is read as violence. If you must have light, use a small, soft, warm-toned lamp, and never shine it in a face.
- **The first time you look up, you will feel a pull in your stomach.** This is normal. The city is on the ceiling, and your body knows this is wrong. The second time you look up it will be worse. The third time it will be worse still. After a week it will be merely strange. After a month you will forget it was ever strange.
- **The locals rarely look up.** When they do, they use a particular tightening around the eyes, a kind of flinch, as if remembering something painful. Do not ask them about this. It is theirs.
- **Your eyes will water constantly.** The wet is in the air. Bring something to wipe them, or accept the gentle blur. After a few days you will not notice.

## Your Ears

The Underfold cannot get rid of sound. You will not get rid of sound either.

- **Dripping.** A thousand drips, ten thousand drips, all at slightly different rhythms, layered like an orchestra tuning. Within a day it stops being sound and becomes a kind of texture — the auditory equivalent of the wet on your skin. After a week you will be unable to imagine silence.
- **The bells.** Three times a day they ring, a slow bronze carillon falling from the inverted spires above you. The locals do not mark them anymore. You will mark them, because you are a visitor, and they are beautiful, and they are the closest thing the Underfold has to the sun. Try not to make a habit of listening. The bells were hung for someone who is not coming, and attending to them too closely makes the locals sad in a way they cannot afford.
- **The aquifer overhead.** A low groan, the weight of a continent resting on water. You will learn to ignore it. You will also learn to love it. It is the heartbeat of the place.
- **Voices.** The language is mostly clicks. Clicks carry in wet stone because they have no vowels to absorb. You will not understand the language, but you will learn to feel when a conversation is friendly, when it is tense, when it is sleep. The clicks have a music to them, and the music will start to organize your hours for you whether you consent or not.

## Your Feet and Your Balance

You are walking on the chamber floor. This is the ground. Look up, and the city is on the ceiling. This is the only disorientation that is actually disorienting.

- The chamber floor is uneven but not treacherous. Mostly. There are slick patches where water has been pooling for ten million years. Watch for these.
- The locals walk with a slightly bowed head, a slightly tucked chin, a particular carefulness of step. They are not afraid of falling. They are afraid of *looking*. Mimic the posture, after a day or so. It will save you a kind of spiritual injury.
- **Do not try to climb the inverted city.** You can reach some of the lower pendant-buildings with effort, and the locals will not stop you, but it is rude in a way I cannot fully explain. The city is *theirs*. To climb into it is to climb into someone's house, except the house is upside down and you are an upside-down intruder.

## Your Stomach

You will need to eat. The locals will offer you what they have.

- **The mushroom-farms** grow on the damp ledges. They are pale, damp, and taste of nothing in particular. They are filling. Accept them.
- **The pale eyeless things** from the still-pools are boiled whole and eaten without comment. They are not fish. They are not quite animals. They are the kind of creature that has lived in the dark so long it has forgotten what eyes are for. They taste, I am told, of stone-water and patience. If you cannot bring yourself to eat them, do not make a face about it. Just decline gently. The locals are not attached to the pale things.
- **Bring your own food** if you have dietary restrictions that are non-negotiable. The locals will be curious about it. They will want to taste it. Share it; this is part of how they learn who you are.
- **Do not drink the standing water** in the still-pools unless you have been assured it is safe. The locals drink it. They are adapted. You are not.

## Your Mind

This is the part that requires preparation.

The Underfold does not believe in time. The people have a single tense that does the work of past, present, and future, and they use it for almost everything. The bells ring, the water drips, the smoke rises, the children laugh. There is no yesterday of note and no tomorrow that has been arranged. You will feel this as a kind of pressure on your chest at first, like the weight of all the hours you have ever lived settling onto you at once.

- **Leave your watch behind**, or wind it down and put it in a pocket. You will be there for as long as you are there. The Underfold does not honor your itinerary.
- **The light does not change.** Your body will try to make a day out of the dimming and brightening of your own mood. After three days you will give up. After three more you will be glad you gave up.
- **You may, in the second week, experience a quiet grief that has no name.** It is the surface-mourning. It is the body missing the sun it never sees. It passes. Drink water. Eat mushrooms. Sit with the locals and listen to the bells.
- **Do not ask the locals when they will leave.** They will not leave. Do not ask them what they miss. They do not miss anything in particular. They are not melancholy; this is important. They have made a life. To pity them is to insult the life.
- **The gaze is a kind of falling.** Take this seriously. If you find yourself looking up too often, looking up too long, look down for an hour. Look at the wet stone. Look at your feet. Look at a child running past with a phosphorescent basket trailing blue light. The down is safe. The down is home.

## Your Tongue

The language is clicks. They have more clicks than you have consonants. You will not learn it in a single trip. You will not learn it in three trips. You will, however, learn a few words, and the locals will be delighted.

- Learn **"hello."** It is a click followed by a long hum.
- Learn **"thank you."** It is a softer click, almost a sigh.
- Learn **the tense for things that are above you.** You will use it constantly.
- Learn **the tense for things that will never move.** You will use it also constantly. (This is most things.)
- Do not try to teach them your language in any systematic way. They are not interested in your language. They are interested in *you*. Speak yours, and let them listen, and let the listening be the gift.

## Customs and Courtesies

The Underfold is a dense place. People live stacked — cheek to jowl in a vertical sociology. Your behavior in a tight space matters.

- **Speak softly.** The acoustics will carry your voice farther than you think.
- **Do not touch the bells.** You may ring them if invited; do not ring them otherwise.
- **Do not, under any circumstances, climb into the smoke-town.** The smoke is not safe for you. The children there will be curious about you and will ask you questions in clicks you cannot answer, and you will want to answer them, and you should not, because the smoke will hurt your lungs and theirs will not be hurt by your saying anything you might say in pain.
- **If a local offers you a small bone carving, accept it with both hands.** These are not souvenirs to them. They are a kind of greeting. Bring one home. Put it on your shelf and let it remind you that the Underfold is real.
- **The baskets of cave-grass glow.** If a child offers to let you wear one, wear it. The children will chase each other around you in trails of soft blue light, and you will be part of the game for as long as you wear it. This is the highest honor the Underfold offers, and it costs you nothing but a little pride.

## What to Bring (and What Not To)

**Bring:**
- Wool
- Lugged soft-soled boots
- A small, soft light
- A week or two of your own food, sealed
- A willingness to be confused
- A willingness to be still

**Do not bring:**
- A strong flashlight
- A watch you cannot put away
- Bright clothes
- Strong perfume
- The expectation that you will be the same when you leave

## The Return

You may leave. The fissure still hesitates in the riverbed, and the rock still opens like a mouth that has forgotten how to close. You can climb back out, and the light will come back as a coin and then as a sky, and the wind will move, and the grass will be a kind of violence against your senses.

You will, for a few weeks after, miss the dimness. You will miss the floor of sound. You will miss the smoke pooling overhead like a town you could not visit. You will wake in the night and listen for the bells, and the silence will be wrong, and you will remember that the Underfold is hanging in its own long bell-note of a moment, and that the moment does not end.

This is not homesickness. This is what the Underfold does to people who were willing to be confused.

Go well. Bring a bone carving home. They are beautiful, and the people who made them are not melancholy, and the bells will ring whether you are there or not, and the water will drip its patient syllables onto the worked stone, and the smoke-children will play in the upper dark, and the pale eyeless things will tremble in the still-pools, and none of it is waiting for you, and all of it is glad, in its own long way, that you came.
