The place asks for a tempo before it asks for anything else. Before you pack a bag, decide to slow down. The water has been rising for decades, and the people who live here have learned a patience that will read, to your hurried senses, like melancholy. It isn't quite. It's something they haven't named yet. Match it before you arrive, and continue to match it after you leave the boat.

What follows is preparation in the order I would actually think about it, if I were going.

## The wet, and how to live in it

Everything is wet. The locals wear layered cloth against the wet, and the locals have it right. Bring wool if you have it — wool insulates even when damp and doesn't go clammy against the skin the way cotton and most synthetics do. Linen is acceptable. Oilskin for an outer layer is a kindness to yourself. Avoid anything you can't afford to soak repeatedly, including repeatedly over weeks.

There is no iron in this world. Your metal will rust. The warm humid air, the silty water, the perpetual damp — they will eat iron and ordinary steel in weeks, in days at the waterline. If you bring iron tools, accept that they will pit and stain and shorten. Brass, bronze, copper, ceramic, bone, hardwood, shell — these survive here the way they survive nowhere in your world. If you can leave the iron at home, leave it. A small iron knife, oiled and sheathed, is worth the trouble for the work it does; a steel-frame backpack is a slow grief waiting for you.

Footwear is the single most important choice you'll make. Surfaces here are wet, mossy, sodden, and ambiguous. Stone is slick with biofilm. Wood is soft. Rope causeways flex under you like a slow, wet drum. The residents seem to manage in rope sandals or barefoot — both are reasonable answers. You want soft soles, serious grip, and nothing that fills with water and sloshes. Heavy boots are wrong. A thin, sticky-soled leather shoe, or nothing at all, is closer to right.

## The body's slow negotiation

Your feet will be wet for most of your stay. Keep the rest of your skin as dry as you can, when you can. Warm, silty, half-stagnant water on human skin is the textbook setup for maceration, fungal infection, and parasites. Treat any cut the moment you get it. Carry what you need to treat it — clean water, soap if you have it, a drying powder. Watch for redness, swelling, the small fevers that follow small wounds. The residents appear healthy enough, but they've had generations to negotiate with this environment; you haven't. Bring any medications you rely on. Don't assume a local pharmacopoeia exists, or that it would suit you if it did.

The insect hum is enormous and is one of the loudest things you'll hear. Bring repellent. Cover your skin at dusk especially. If you have a history of bad reactions to bites or stings, bring what you need to manage that.

Don't drink the lake water. It is warm, opaque with suspended silt, and full of the slow dissolved city. The residents have a drinking source — probably rainwater caught on rooftops and stored in those rounded vaults, possibly springs or shallow wells above the waterline. Find it. Use it. If you must use the lake water, treat it seriously: filter to clear, then boil, then trust it only a little.

The structural collapses — a wall giving way at the waterline, a roof caving into the room below — are the loudest sounds in this quiet world. They are the place breathing. Don't let them make you jumpy, and don't ignore them either. Keep away from bulging lower walls. Don't walk under any roof that looks held together by habit. The residents know which structures are still negotiating with gravity and which have already lost the argument; follow their routes until you can read the building yourself.

## How to move

There are three surfaces here, and you must learn all three.

The first is the causeway: lashed-timber walkways, tied with rope, none of it pegged with iron. They flex. They shift under your weight. Walk with your feet low, your weight centered, your hands out but not grabbing — the railings, where they exist, are themselves lashed and not always reliable. Don't bounce. Don't run. The causeways are the streets of the new city; you will spend more time on them than you expect.

The second is the upper-floor interior. Floors that were once ceilings. Rooms whose lower halves have silted in or rotted open to the water below. These are the indoor ground of the drowned city. Some are sound; some give way without warning. The residents move through them with the unhurried certainty of long practice. Until you have that practice, move where they move, step where they step. Watch their eyes for the small hesitations that mean *this beam, not that one*.

The third is the boat. Flat wooden craft, poled through the channels — and the channels are everywhere, because the alleys were narrow and the alleys are now canals. The water is shallow enough that you can almost always touch bottom from the boat, but the bottom is silt and old floor and you cannot see it. Learn to pole, or accept being poled. Don't stand up in the boat. Don't lean. The residents pole with the same unhurried competence they bring to everything else; if one of them offers to teach you, take the offer and don't pretend to be quicker than you are.

Above the fog, on a rooftop, you can see across the canopy to the lake and the lower slopes. Below the fog, in a boat or on a low causeway, you are in a green-tunnel world of dim amber and dripping leaves. Plan your routes for the version of the place you happen to be in.

## The senses, retuned

The light is poor even at midday, and the air is amber-tinted from the lantern smoke that mingles with the fog. By afternoon the water itself looks like spilled metal. Bring or borrow a personal light — a small oil lamp, a candle lantern, whatever you carry between worlds that doesn't depend on iron and batteries. The residents already burn lanterns in the deeper arcades and covered passages; the light you see there is the place's own.

The quiet is full. Voices are low; paddles are muffled; the wind moves through the fog without finding anything to catch on. Speak softly. Move softly. The loudest sounds are the structural surrenders, and they come as a wet shudder and then nothing. You'll get used to them in time; give yourself that time.

The smell is what you'd expect: silt, warm vegetation, slow rot, smoke, the faint mineral note of lime-cut stone, and underneath everything the particular smell of a city giving itself back to a lake. Don't be afraid of it. Don't romanticize it either. It's just what the place smells like, the way your kitchen smells like your kitchen.
