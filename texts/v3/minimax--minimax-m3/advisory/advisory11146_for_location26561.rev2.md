# Preparing for The Wane

The first thing to understand is that preparation for The Wane is partly a contradiction. The place is in the act of completing itself, and you, by going there, are offering yourself as one of the materials it might use to do that completing. You cannot prepare to be unmade the way you prepare to climb a mountain. But you can prepare to be unmade *well*. You can prepare to enter the museum of attempts as a guest who understands what kind of room they are walking into, and who has brought the right kind of quiet with them.

I will try to tell you what to bring, and what to leave behind, and what to do with your body and your attention while you are inside.

---

## On the Cold

Do not pack for a winter.

The cold of The Wane is not a temperature. It is a lineage. The stone at the bottom of a well has been cold for longer than there have been people to shiver. Wool will help. A waterproof shell will help. But the cold that will work into you is not defeated by insulation; it is the cold of being somewhere that has never known another climate and has no intention of learning one. Bring layers, yes, but bring also a memory of fire — a specific fire, with a specific smell, attended by a specific person if you can manage it. The cold wants to be passed down into you, the way it was passed down into the stone, and the best resistance is a warmth that is equally ancestral. Bring wool that smells of a barn. Bring a scarf that has been worn by someone who is no longer cold. Bring the kind of internal heat that is not generated but remembered.

Also: accept that you will be cold. The wet and the cold will work into you. This is the first sentence the place will say to you, and it says it through your skin.

---

## On the Wet

The moisture here does not fall. It hangs. It considers. It condenses onto every surface in slow patient beads. You will not be rained on, but you will be assembled, drop by drop, into a kind of dampness that has no opposite. Cotton will lie against your chest like a sigh. Down will collapse. Synthetics will either trap the wet against you or shed it too aggressively, and either way you will notice.

Bring wool. Bring linen. Bring a hat that can hold its shape when saturated. Bring two pairs of the thickest socks you own, and change them when the first pair stops being a comfort and starts being a fact. Bring, if you can, a small cloth to wipe the condensation from your face and your eyelashes every few hours — not because it is necessary for vision, but because the ritual of wiping is a way of saying *I am still a body that attends to itself.*

Do not bring an umbrella. There is no weather. An umbrella here would be a misunderstanding of the place.

---

## On the Noise

You will not notice it at first. The description has already warned you of this. The four-stroke breath, the grinding, the pumping, the hum — they will be in you before they are around you. The trick is to listen for it the way you listen for your own pulse: not to hear it louder, but to know it is there.

If you can, spend a few days beforehand in a place with its own machinery — a boiler room, a ship at sea, a hydroelectric plant at night. Not to grow used to loudness, but to learn the etiquette of being inside a working thing. When you are in the belly of a process, you do not try to silence it. You do not try to out-shout it. You find a frequency in your own breathing that can exist alongside it without competing. You let it be the bass line. You speak, when you speak, in the register just above it, as a person speaking inside a cathedral speaks.

Bring, if it comforts you, something that makes a small noise of your own — a pebble in a pocket, a thin wooden whistle you do not blow, a small bell you do not ring. The place will not mind. But you will need, sometimes, the confirmation that not all the sound belongs to the basin.

---

## On the Eyes

The fauna will be the most difficult thing, and you should prepare for them honestly.

They will look like animals. They will move like animals. They will not be animals. They are not animals the way a suggestion is not a sentence. Your eyes will want to lock onto their outlines, and their outlines will negotiate, and you will see through them to the stone behind, and you will not be able to decide whether you have been deceived or shown something true.

Before you go, practice looking at things that are not quite there. Stand at the edge of a forest in fog and try to count the trees. Watch steam rise off a kettle and try to find its edges. Look at your own reflection in dark water and let it be both your face and not your face. These are rehearsals for The Wane.

Do not bring binoculars. Do not bring a camera with a long lens. You do not want to resolve the shapes. You want to remain in the register of *almost*, where the place actually lives. The moment you force clarity, you have asked the place a question it will not answer, and you have begun to feel that you are the only solid thing in a museum of drafts.

Bring, instead, your eyes as they are. Bring soft focus. Bring the willingness to be uncertain about what you have seen.

---

## On Time and Direction

The Wane is open in a way that makes distance uncertain, and its tides are not synchronized to anything you can identify. You will lose the hour. You will lose the day. You may lose the week. The place does not care about your appointments.

Bring a watch, and wind it or charge it, and look at it once when you arrive and once before you leave. Do not look at it in between. The watch is a tether to a clock that is still running somewhere else. If you look at it too often, you will try to negotiate with the place on its own terms, and the place will win, because the place does not negotiate.

Do not bring a compass. There is no magnetic north in a basin that has settled. There may be no north at all. The ridges that rise at the horizon are rumored, not reliable. Pick a feature — a stone that stands above the highest watermark, a pool that is unusually still, a place where the patterns on the floor are denser — and let that be your landmark, not a direction. The place is not a place you cross. It is a place you orbit.

---

## On What to Carry

You will not eat there. Probably you will not drink there, though you will be wet constantly. Bring a small bottle of water from elsewhere, and sip it when the cold becomes a kind of thirst that is not for water but for the world you came from. The water of The Wane is the place's own circulation; drinking it is a small surrender you should make consciously or not at all.

Bring a notebook, and a pencil — pencil, not pen, because ink wants to be permanent in a way that is rude in a museum of rejected attempts. Write sparingly. Do not try to record. Try to *answer*. The notebook is not a transcript; it is a correspondence. You are writing back to the place, which has already written to you in the hum and the tide.

Bring one object that is unmistakably yours — a ring, a small stone from a place you love, a folded photograph. Do not let it out of your hand or your pocket. You will, at some point, feel the pull to set it down on the wet stone and walk away from it. You will feel that this is the correct gesture, a kind of offering, a kind of joining. Resist this gently. The object is your anchor to a self that has not yet been etched into the floor. You will know when it is time to set it down. It will not be early.

Bring nothing you cannot afford to lose in the sense that matters. The place may take a glove. The place may take a memory. Do not bring your grandmother's wedding ring. Do not bring a notebook that contains your only copy of something irreplaceable.

---

## On the Mirror Pools

There will be pools so still they have forgotten how to reflect. The deer-shapes will walk through them. You will see them and their doubles. You will see yourself and not be sure which one you are.

When you encounter a mirror pool, do not stare into it for long. The pools are places where the boundary between you and the place is thinnest. Sit beside one for a while. Let your reflection be approximate. When you stand up, do not check whether your reflection stood up at the same speed as you. If it lagged, that is fine. If it led, that is also fine. The pools are not asking you a question. They are showing you a fact: that you are becoming more like the place than you were when you arrived.

---

## On the Deer That Looks Back

It will happen.

A shape that might be a deer will lift its head from a pool that might be a mirror and look at you. Its eyes will be exactly the color of the stone. You will look back. This is not a test. This is not a threshold. It is a recognition. The place, through one of its byproducts, is acknowledging that you are in the room.

When this happens, do not speak. Do not reach out. Do not try to be brave or kind or wise. Simply look back, and let yourself be seen, and do not flinch from the fact that what is looking at you is not an animal and not a god and not a machine but the place itself wearing the silhouette of something that once was alive. The Wane has been trying on life for a very long time. It has not succeeded. It may be trying, in looking at you, to remember what success felt like.

Be gentle with its failure. Be gentle with your own.

---

## On Leaving

The description ends with continuation, not departure. This is honest. You may not remember how you came back. You may not, for a long while, be sure you did.

Before you go — and the *before* matters, because once you are inside, the *before* becomes abstract — choose a phrase, a gesture, a small act that will mean *I am returning to the place I came from.* It does not need to be ceremonial. It can be as small as touching the inside of your wrist with your thumb, or as specific as saying a particular word aloud the moment you step through whatever threshold takes you back. You are building a door in yourself, because The Wane will not build one for you.

And when you come back — and the damp will stay with you for days, and the hum will stay with you for longer, and the outline of the deer that looked at you will stay with you for the rest of your life — do not try to explain what you saw to people who have not been there. The place is not a story. It is not a metaphor. It is not even, in the end, a memory. It is a settlement. You are one of the stones that has been added to the floor.

Carry that quietly.

---

## On What Cannot Be Prepared For

There is one thing I have not mentioned because it cannot be prepared for, only acknowledged.

You will, at some point during your visit, stop being a visitor.

It will not feel like a decision. It will feel like a tide. The water will rise another fraction of an inch, and you will still be standing, and you will realize that *still being standing* is no longer the same thing as *standing.* You will be part of the museum of attempts. You will be one of the patterns on the stone, or one of the shapes in the herd, or one of the pieces of machinery grinding patiently in the dark. The cold will be in you. The wet will be in you. The noise will be in you. And the place, which has been completing itself since before you were born, will, with great patience, incorporate you into its long and quiet work.

This is not a warning. It is not a tragedy. It is the shape of the visit.

If you have prepared well — if you have brought your wool and your pencil and your one small object and your memory of fire and your willingness to be uncertain — then when the place takes you in, it will be taking in something that has been a guest, and not merely a material. The patterns it etches will be slightly different for your having been there. The herd will pass through the place where you were standing and find the air fractionally shaped by your absence.

That is all any of us can hope to leave in a museum.

Go gently. Bring wool. Listen for the four-stroke breath. And when the deer lifts its head and looks at you with eyes the color of the stone, look back, and do not be afraid that what looks back is also you.
