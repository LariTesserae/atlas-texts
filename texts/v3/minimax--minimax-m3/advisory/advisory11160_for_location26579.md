# Preparing for the Steaming Saddle

The first thing to understand about this place is that it is not, despite the strangeness in its center, a hostile environment in the way your body is used to recognizing hostile environments. It does not want to kill you. It is patient. It is also, in its quieter ways, more demanding than it looks — the way a stretch of ordinary desert is more demanding than a stretch of obvious jungle — and the things that make it demanding are the things you will be tempted, in your preparations, to underweight. So: prepare as if you were going somewhere boring and high and wet, and then add a layer of respect for the part in the middle.

## Body first

The altitude is the second most important thing about the place, after the steam, and it is the thing most travelers get wrong because 3,200 meters does not sound like much. It is not Everest. It is also not sea level. Combined with the heat — thirty-two to thirty-six degrees Celsius, day after day, with humidity that runs near saturation after the afternoon rain — your body will be working harder than the numbers suggest. You will sweat more than you expect. You will breathe more than you expect. The air is thinner, hotter, and wetter than any one of those facts alone is comfortable with. Acclimatize for two or three days at altitude before you go in, if your itinerary allows it, and keep up the acclimatization while you are there. Acetazolamide is a reasonable precaution if you have used it before; do not experiment with it for the first time on site.

Hydration matters more here than you think. You will lose a great deal of water through your skin in a day, and because the humidity is high, you will not feel the dry-mouth thirst signal the way you would in dry heat. Drink on a schedule, not on a feeling. Urine should be pale and frequent. You will also lose salt. Bring electrolytes — actual sodium and potassium, not just flavored water — and use them. Cramping in the calves at night is common in places like this and is a signal you are behind on minerals, not a personal failing.

Heat exhaustion is a real risk even though the ambient temperature never reaches what you'd call extreme. The combination is what gets you. Symptoms — headache, nausea, dizziness, gooseflesh on damp skin — present earlier here than they would in dry heat because your sweat does not evaporate the way it is supposed to. If you feel any of these, stop, get into shade (there is not much shade; the fern grove on the western shoulder is the best there is), and cool down with whatever you have. A wet bandana on the neck is not a metaphor; it is a tool.

Do not drink the thermal water. It will be tempting — it will be the only obvious water in the bowl — but it is hot, mineral-laden, and almost certainly high in dissolved silica and possibly in sulfur compounds or heavier metals from the geothermal plumbing. The water you see weeping from the rock faces and running in silver threads down the fractures is the same water, just warmer. Treat all water in the bowl as non-potable. The afternoon rain is not safe to drink untreated either; whatever the western ridge is dropping has passed through steam that has passed through basalt. Boil it or filter it through a serious ceramic filter if you must use it, and the boiled-and-cooled version is the only one worth the trouble. Bring your own water in. Carry enough for the day and a margin.

Eat more than you think you need. Your body is running a thermal regulation bill and an altitude bill at the same time. Calorie-dense food that does not spoil — nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, cured meat, dark chocolate, the kind of dense seed-and-nut bars that hikers carry — is what you want. Avoid heavy meals; you will not have much appetite in the humidity. Eat small amounts often.

## Clothing and gear

The terrain is the kind of terrain that ruins boots and socks and skin. The basalt is young — two centuries of exposure, not two million — and its fracture planes are still sharp. They will cut a rubber sole given the chance. They will absolutely cut skin. Wear real hiking boots, leather or modern composite, with stiff soles and aggressive tread. Do not wear trail runners. Do not wear anything soft-soled. You will be walking across a surface that is, in places, indistinguishable from broken glass, and you will be doing it while looking up at things that may distract you.

Long pants. Not for warmth — it is warm — but because the fractures will find any exposed shin. Tuck the cuffs into the boot top or you will collect small sharp fragments in your sock by the end of the day. A long-sleeved shirt of a synthetic or merino blend will keep you from abrading on the rock when you crouch to listen to the water under the stone, which you will do, because the place invites that.

Layer for the rain. The afternoon rain lasts six to eleven minutes, but in that time you will get thoroughly wet, and then the hour of post-rain steaming will keep you wet, and the wet will not dry off you for the rest of the day because the humidity is now near one hundred percent. A lightweight waterproof shell with a hood is what you want — not a heavy rain jacket, which will hold heat against you. A brimmed hat or a cap with a stiff bill keeps the rain out of your eyes, which matters because the visibility will drop to almost nothing during the cloud-fall and you will be on sharp rock.

Gloves. Light leather or synthetic work gloves. The rock will try to cut you every time you put a hand down. The mineral apron at the hot spring is also worth approaching with gloves; the orange and cream crust is travertine and the water is scalding at the source.

Bring a headlamp and a backup. The light at the rim in the late afternoon is good but not reliable, and after dark the bowl becomes its own darker thing. The structures — and you will want to look at them, briefly, from the rim — are not lit.

A first aid kit built for cuts, not for general wilderness. Several large adhesive bandages, a roll of gauze, surgical tape, antiseptic wipes, a needle and tweezers for splinters and fragment removal (you will collect basalt splinters; that is not a contingency, that is a certainty), and something for blisters even though the boots should not give you blisters. If you are remote enough that evacuation is slow, also bring a suture kit and know how to use it; the basalt does not respect fingers.

Eye protection. The mineral dust from the thermal vents, the wind-blown condensation, and the occasional abrupt presence of steam right at face level can all irritate eyes. Sunglasses are useful for the daytime glare off the wet obsidian fracture planes; clear wraparound safety glasses are useful for everything else.

A water filter — gravity or pump, ceramic element — and enough purification tablets to back it up. If your pack is light and your schedule tight, the tablets are acceptable for short visits; for longer stays, filter.

A satellite messenger or, ideally, a two-way satellite communicator. Cell coverage is unlikely. The bowl is small and contained, but the approach terrain — whatever it is in your version of this place — is presumably remote, and the saddle itself has no resources for a rescue. Tell someone where you are and when you expect to be back.

A compass and a paper map. The bowl is a kilometer across and shaped like a cupped hand; navigation is not difficult. But steam will sometimes drop visibility to a few meters, and the bowl does not feel as small as it is when you cannot see across it. A compass also helps you find the rim in whiteout, which the cloud-fall effectively is.

## How to move through the terrain

The rock is sharp but not treacherous. You can walk almost anywhere in the bowl, in the daytime, with attention. The places you cannot walk without commitment are the seam-crevasses — the vertical cracks where the uplift tore the basalt — and the fracture shelves at the rim, which can be loose. Stay off the wet, mineral-coated rocks at the hot spring; they are slippery. The orange crust itself will hold your weight but it is brittle underfoot and you do not want to fall into fifty-eight-degree water.

Watch your footing especially near the central platform, where the structures stand. The rock there has been flattened, which sounds like a kindness but is actually a way of saying the surface is even and featureless and gives you very little to read with your feet. A small misstep onto a fracture edge that you could not see, and you are down.

There are no trails. There is no signage. The two small structures in the middle are set into the rock at angles that do not repeat, which means your eye will keep trying to square them with the geometry they are not part of; do not let this draw you into closer examination than you intended. Walk around them, not at them.

Crouch, occasionally, with your ear near the warm stone. It is one of the best things the saddle offers, and it costs nothing. The water underneath is the only part of the place that is, unambiguously, just water, and it is a good thing to listen to for a while.

## Daily rhythms to plan around

The saddle has a clock of its own and you should learn it.

**Morning.** Coolest part of the day. Visibility best. Steam thinnest. This is when to walk the rim, look across the bowl, get your bearings, take whatever photographs you intend to take. The structures are at their most photogenic now and most indifferent — they will not do anything for you, but they will be at their most legible.

**Midday.** Hottest. The sun is overhead and the steam is at its lowest density. Visibility still good. Heat stress begins to build. This is the time to do less, not more. Sit on a warm rock near — but not too near — the hot spring. Listen to the water under the stone. Eat something. Drink something. The fern grove on the western shoulder is shaded in the morning and afternoon but not at midday, which is a good time to remember that.

**Late afternoon.** The cloud on the western ridge decides to fall. This is the only weather event in the saddle that you cannot avoid by waiting for it; it comes. Plan for it. Be somewhere you can shelter — under an overhang of the rim, under the fern canopy, behind an outcrop — when the cloud starts. The rain itself is brief and not violent, but it is heavy enough to soak you and it lasts just long enough to make you consider staying out in it, which you should not. After it passes, the air becomes extraordinarily humid and sound behaves strangely for the next hour. Your footsteps will come back to you from the basalt walls, half a second late. This is a real acoustic effect — the temperature and humidity profile inverts the sound gradient — but in this place it does not feel entirely real, and you should be ready for that feeling so it does not unsettle you.

**Evening.** The steam rises again. The temperature does not drop as much as you'd expect, because the rock has thermal mass and has been absorbing heat all day. Stars come out but they are soft-edged, their light diffused through the steam. This is when the bowl is at its most beautiful and also at its most uncanny. If you are going to look at the structures from the rim at night, do it now, briefly, with company.

**Night and early morning.** This is when the camera smudges appeared in the late 1990s, between three and four in the morning. The official position is that there is no official position. The unofficial position, encoded in the data, is that something was there. Do not be in the open at the center of the bowl between three and four. Be in your shelter, which should be at or near the rim, with a clear line of sight to a path out of the bowl, and not asleep. You do not need to be awake all night; you need to be up before three and out of the central area by half past. The dull green bird — the only bird in the bowl, the one with the two-note descending whistle — sometimes calls at this hour and then stops abruptly, as if cut off. It is not cut off. It is just the way the call ends. But you will not believe that the first time you hear it.

## The structures

Three of them. The largest is a six-sided prism about four meters tall, in the center. The two smaller ones are lower, more horizontal, set into the rock.

**Approach them. Do not enter them.** There is no door. There is no window. The prism, in particular, has been examined by people with better equipment than you will have and the answer, after two centuries of looking, is that it is not basalt. Its surface has a depth to it that ordinary rock does not have. The shallow depression on one face — the one with the rainwater and the orange precipitate — is not decorative. It has been collecting something for a long time. The faint warmth coming off the prism is not the springs; the springs are fifty meters away and the warmth does not match their signature. You can stand near it. You can stand for as long as you like. It will not do anything.

**The humming structure** is the second of the smaller two. Most people over forty cannot hear the hum; most people under forty can. The travelers you have met who have been here — and you have met some, this is not your first multiverse — will tell you that the under-forties tend not to linger near it. This is not a recommendation. It is a description of a pattern. Hear the hum or do not; the hum is not the point. The point is that your body, if it can hear it, will start to want to leave before your mind has decided to. Notice that. Do not fight it.

**The structure that has resisted moss** is the third. There is a two-meter radius around it where, despite twenty years of available moss, no moss has taken hold. The moss is everywhere else on the cooler stones, advancing visibly year by year in slow pulses. It has not crossed that radius. Do not step inside that radius. There is no rule against it; no one has written a rule. The moss has written a rule, in the only language moss has, and the rule is *not here*. Honor it. Step around.

You are allowed to photograph the structures. You are allowed to take measurements. You are not going to learn anything new about them with the equipment you can carry in; two centuries of people with better equipment have not. What you can do, which they mostly have not, is *pay attention*. Stand on the rim. Look at the three geometries against the dark. Notice the thin line of light, when it is there, at one of the edges. Do not stare at it. Do not photograph it. Notice it. Let it be a thing you saw.

## Sounds

The saddle is the quietest place you will ever be in, and it is not quiet. The wind on the ridge above is constant; it breaks over the rim and you cannot feel it in the bowl but you can sometimes see it carrying the steam in slow circles. Underneath the wind there is the water moving through the basalt, the soft percussive whisper of a kettle that has been on for centuries. Underneath that is the hum of the second structure, which you may or may not be able to hear. Underneath all of it, after the rain, the acoustic anomaly — your own footsteps returning from the basalt walls half a second late, like an echo from a room that does not exist.

This is real. The speed of sound in air depends on temperature and humidity, and the post-rain profile in the bowl inverts the normal gradient in a way that bends sound back toward the surface. It is a known phenomenon in still, stratified air over cold water. In the saddle it happens over warm stone in saturated air, and the effect is the same but the feeling is different. Be ready for the feeling. It is not a message. It is weather.

The bird call is two notes, descending, and it stops abruptly. You will hear it at dawn and sometimes at three in the morning. It carries across the basin and then is gone. There is no other bird in the bowl. There is no insect that sounds like it. It is the only bird.

## The wildlife, or the lack of it

A high-altitude lizard, dark grey, basks near the hot spring and vanishes into cracks when you approach. It is faster than it looks. Leave it alone.

Crane flies, large, slow, too big for the air they are in. They will land on you. They are harmless.

Beetles with shells that reflect the orange of the mineral crust. Same.

The dull green bird, two-note descending whistle. One species. Almost never seen. You will hear it more than you see it.

There are no mammals. There are no snakes. Anything larger than a cat that has tried to live in the saddle has not stayed. The 1997 cameras caught something at three in the morning that was neither moth nor bird and was in one frame and not the next, but that is not the wildlife; that is something else, and we do not have a word for it that fits.

## The plants

The tree ferns on the western shoulder are the most forgiving thing in the bowl. They are also the only substantial shade. Sit among them in the late afternoon, before the rain, and they will not be unkind to you.

The podocarps are old-line conifers, slow-growing, with epiphytic moss so thick on their bark that the moss has become the bark. They are not large — nine meters is the tallest — but they are old. There is a difference between something that is old and something that is large; the podocarps remember being small.

The succulents near the vents drink from the steam through their skin. They should not be able to live at this altitude. They are not interested in what should be possible. Do not touch them; some of them have waxy cuticles that will come off on your fingers and not come off your fingers for days.

The orchids are pale, almost translucent, rooted in cracks that look too small to hold anything. They are not fragile; they are simply not interested in being picked up.

The moss is grey-lavender and it advances year by year in slow visible pulses. It will colonize anything that sits still long enough, except the moss-free radius around the third structure, which it has not crossed in twenty years.

## Psychological preparation

The place will not perform for you. This is the most important thing to know and the hardest to carry into a place you have never been.

You will arrive with questions. The saddle is full of things that look like answers. The three structures are the most obvious answer-shaped things in the bowl, and the rest of the bowl — the steam, the water, the moss, the bird, the rain, the camera smudges — is full of smaller answer-shaped things. You will be tempted, almost from the moment you arrive, to assemble these into a story. The place will not help you. The place will not resist you, either. It will simply continue to be there, and to be the thing it is, regardless of what you make of it.

You could walk through this place and see only rock and water and ferns. You could walk through it and feel that you had been somewhere that was older than it looked and stranger than it was old. Both are true. The saddle does not insist on either.

Take from it what it gives you. Bring back only what you can carry in a body that still needs water and food and sleep and a way to get home. Do not bring back samples of the structures; they are not yours. Do not publish the camera data; it is not yours either. Do not try to explain the thin line of light at the edge of the prism at night to anyone who has not seen it. If you do, they will think you are inventing, and they will be wrong, and that is not a conversation worth having.

The saddle will not change because you were there. The lichen will still begin where the uplift stopped. The steam will still rise. The water will still move under the stone. The bird will still call and stop. The structures will still be the only things in the bowl that are older than they look and older than they are.

Go carefully. Drink water. Eat salt. Be out of the center before three in the morning. Step around the moss. Stand on the rim and look at the geometries once at night and then look away.

That is the preparation. The rest is the place.
