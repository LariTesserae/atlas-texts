# A Traveler's Preparation for the Weeping City

The first thing to understand is that this place will not meet you halfway. It has no gate, no threshold, no moment of arrival, and so the work of preparing must be done entirely on your own body, in advance, in the place you are coming from. You will arrive wet. You will arrive cold. You will arrive out of breath on a plateau that does not declare itself. The city will not adjust itself to you. So you must adjust yourself to it, carefully and for a long time before you go.

## The Body, First of All

The plateau is high. Not the killing altitude of the great peaks, but high enough that the air has manners — it comes into the lungs as if it has considered the matter and decided not to give itself fully. If you have any history of breathlessness at altitude, take it seriously. Spend days, ideally a week, in thinner air before you arrive. Walk up stairs carrying weight. Sleep at elevation if you can manage it. If you have access to altitude medication and your constitution allows it, bring a course. The coolness here is not the dramatic cold of a mountain summit but the persistent, polite, drawn-out cold of a stone surface that has been holding the night for hours. Your body will be working harder to keep warm than you expect, because the cold is not coming from the air so much as from every surface you touch. Every wall is a cooled stone. Every floor is a cooled stone. Every seat, every ledge, every step. You are resting on the city's exhalation all day long.

Your feet will be the most important part of you. The stones are rough and dark and they drink water, and they are slick with the constant condensation that assembles on them from the air. There is no hour in the day when a stone surface here is reliably dry. The natives wrap their feet — you saw that, in the description — and you should do something similar, though you will not want to copy them exactly because their feet are accustomed in ways yours are not. You want grip. You want warmth. You want something that will not slide on a wet, moss-touched terrace edge when you step out of a doorway at dawn. Break in whatever you bring before you arrive. Walk miles in it. If the soles are smooth, discard them and start over. A fall here, on stacked stone, in the gray light, can be a long fall.

Your hands will be cold, often. Bring liners you can wear and still work your fingers in. You will want to touch things. The dew forms in slow silver lines, the lichen takes the shapes of continents, the mosses are almost black — there is much to look at, and you will be tempted to handle the stones. Let your hands be warm enough to feel them properly.

## What to Wear

Layer. The sun is sharp at midday and gives little heat, but your own body heat, trapped against the damp stones, will matter more than the sun. A base layer that holds warmth even when wet — wool, or the modern equivalents — is essential. Cotton will betray you. The middle layer should breathe, because there are moments, particularly in the long rain, when you will be inside a small room with a body or a fire and the air will grow close. The outer layer should turn water but not trap it, because you will be in mist and condensation as much as in actual rain, and a fully sealed shell will soak you from the inside with your own sweat. The natives are wrapped in what looks like layered cloth and skin. You can do better, but the principle is the same: many thin things rather than one thick thing.

A hood is more useful than a hat. The wind comes from many directions along the folds of the plateau, and you will want your head covered in the mornings especially, when the city is a low white sea and you cannot see where to put your feet. The wind is thin and cold and smells of nothing, and it will find the gaps in your collar. Cover the gaps.

Color is a small thing, but consider it. The city is dark stone, gray mist, pale lichen, silver water. A bright color will mark you. This is not necessarily bad, but be aware of what you are doing. The inhabitants move in muted tones. You may want to be muted too, at least until you understand the etiquette of being seen.

## What to Bring

Everything you bring will be damp, most of the time. Accept this now, in the place you are packing, and pack accordingly. Waterproof bags inside waterproof bags. A small dry bag for anything that must truly stay dry — a notebook if you keep one, a small electronic device if you carry one, any medicines. The city's capillary system is not a metaphor; water moves through the joints, and if there is a path into your pack, the water will find it. Bring fewer things than you think you need. A heavy pack on a wet terrace is a tipping hazard.

Light. The city's natural light is gray, often dim, and the rooms open onto courtyards that are lit only by the gray light from above. Bring a small, reliable, warm-toned light source. Candles are part of the local culture, presumably — there is mention of fires, of a faint heat of bodies and flames — so a candle may not be out of place, but a small modern light will let you read the joints of the stones, the channels in the upper courses, the small deliberate troughs the masons have left. Bring spare fuel, or spare batteries, or both. Do not rely on a single source.

Food. The city is not a place that feeds many bodies. The mosses are sparse, the grass grows only along the channels, there are no herds. The natives live on what they can carry in and what the city offers in the way of small pale invertebrates, dark quick things, the occasional bird from the higher ridges. You will not live like that. Bring food that is dense, that keeps, that does not need cooking if you can avoid it — because you may not want to build a fire, and the smoke will behave strangely in the still, damp air. Bring enough. Bring more than you think. A half-day's walk between districts means you cannot run back to a cache.

Water is, paradoxically, not a problem. The cisterns are everywhere, fed by the city's own weather, and the water is clean because it has only just come out of the air. A small vessel, something you can carry in your hand, will let you drink often. You will need to drink often. The coolness is deceptive — you will not feel the thirst you would feel in a hot place, but the air is pulling moisture out of you constantly, and the stones are pulling heat out of you constantly, and you must replace what is lost.

Navigation. This is the hardest thing. The city has no linear streets. The route is a braid. You pass under lintels, along ledges, down short flights, across courtyards, and from a courtyard you may go up again, or through a low door, or along a wall that is also a roof. The natives know the braids. You do not. Bring a method of marking where you have been. A small piece of chalk, a soft stone, something that will leave a sign on the dark stone and not be mistaken for the city's own working. Do not use anything permanent. The city tends itself, and you should not leave marks it will have to tend. Bring a method of orienting yourself when the city is a low white sea in the morning, and you cannot see the next ridge. The ridges are all the same height, the passage says, so you can never be quite sure you are on the highest. Do not trust a single vantage. Climb twice if you must. Note the direction of the runoff in the channels — the water moves with intent toward the lower terraces, and so down is always findable, but up may not be.

A small repair kit. Tape, thread, a needle, a little wax. Anything that breaks on you here will stay broken, because there is no metal, no smith, no shop. You are your own maintenance.

## How to Move

Slowly. This is the first and last rule. The natives move in the slow hours. They do not speak above a murmur. The city, as a thing, operates on the time of stone and water, and stone and water do not hurry. If you arrive at the pace you kept in the place you came from, you will be loud in a way that is not just audible but visible — you will be a smear of motion against a place of long lines and slow drops and the patient fitting of stone to stone. Slow down before you arrive. Practice it on the way. Walk the way the dew assembles: not the way rain falls.

The braids are not optional. You will be tempted to cut across a courtyard, to take a straight line from one point to another. Resist this. The architecture is the city. If you go around instead of through, you are not seeing the city. If you go up and over, you are seeing it differently than the natives do, and you will not understand the way the water moves, which is the way the city breathes. Walk the braids. Take the short flights. Pass under the lintels. Sit in the dim rooms lit only by the gray light from the courtyard, and do not rush out of them.

Watch where you put your feet. The lower stones are wet and slightly greenish where the light catches them. The joints drink. The moss in the joints is dark, almost black, and slicker than the stone. The channels the masons have left — the small deliberate troughs — will be running with water after rain and during the heavy condensation, and you must not step in them. They are the city's veins. Step around them.

When you climb to a higher terrace, remember that you are also climbing onto someone else's roof. Some of these are working surfaces. Some are yards. The natives will be moving across them in the slow hours, carrying water in clay vessels, replacing the stones that have finally given up, clearing the moss where it has begun to choke a joint. They have been doing this for a very long time. They do not seem in any hurry about it. Do not interrupt the work. If you must pass through a working space, wait until the worker has set down the stone or the bone tool, and pass quietly at the edge of their attention. They will see you. They will not necessarily speak to you. That is all right.

## How to Be With the Inhabitants

They are there. You will see them. You will not hear them. Their feet are wrapped, their tools are bone and stone and sinew, they carry water in clay vessels they have shaped and dried in the wind. They do not speak above a murmur, and the silence of the city is not a silence of absence but a working sound, and your voice, if you use it at your usual volume, will be an intrusion the way a shout in a library is an intrusion — not forbidden, exactly, but graceless.

Match them. Speak low, or not at all. The murmur is not weakness and not timidity; it is the city's scale. When the stones settle, microscopically, you can hear it. When the water moves through the joints, you can hear it. The few birds speak briefly in the morning and that is the loudest thing for most of the day. If you can lower your voice below the birds, you are in the right register.

Do not offer your tools. The natives use what they use, and your bright metal and your sharp edges will be foreign in a place where there is no metal anywhere. You will not find an iron nail or a copper pin or a hinge. The stones are fitted, fitted very well, in a way that requires time and patience and the ability to look at a stone for a long while before deciding where it goes. Your efficiency is not their efficiency. Your materials are not their materials. Bring your tools, use them on your own things, but do not assume they will be wanted. If you are asked to help with the stonework — and you may be, in time, in some small way — match their pace. Watch how they lift. Watch how they look at a stone for a long while. Do not try to teach them anything for at least a season. They have been doing this for a very long time.

The wrapped feet. You noticed them. The bone and stone and sinew. The clay vessels. These are not poverty. This is a place that has made its peace with what is local. The natives are not waiting for you to bring them better things. They are, in some way you may not be able to see at first, more in balance with the city than you will ever be. You are a visitor. The fact that you can leave is the proof of it.

If you are given something — a piece of bread, a drink of water, a place by a small fire — accept it with the same slowness it was offered. The long rain comes, and you sit in a room that is slightly warmer than outside, and you listen to the water finding its way through the stones above. If you are sitting in such a room with a native beside you, that is the only social register you need.

## The Mornings

Plan for them especially. The city is a low white sea in the very early morning, with only the upper walls and the highest posts emerging, and you will not be able to see the next terrace, let alone the next ridge. The sun touches it and the sea lifts and the city is revealed again, wet, waiting. This is the moment when most travelers hurt themselves — they step out into what they think is a courtyard and find it is a roof, or they walk confidently along a wall that ends in a drop of a body's height into the next terrace down. Wait. Watch. Let the mist lift. Drink something warm if you have it. The natives will be moving in this hour, but they will be moving along the channels and the braids they know, and they will not expect you to be moving at all. Sit and watch a single drop form on the corner of a worked stone, swell, hesitate, gather a neighbor, and trail down the face of the wall in a slow silver line. By the time the sun has lifted a hand's width above the ridge, every flat surface in the city is moving with water, and you will be able to see where the wet stone ends and the next wet stone begins. Only then move.

The long rain will come. Sometimes for a day, sometimes for three. You do not go out. You sit in a room that is slightly warmer than outside, and you listen to the water finding its way through the stones above. Bring something to do in such a room. A book, if you read. A length of cord to knot and unknot. A way to make the time feel less like waiting and more like being. The sound in such a room is the smallest possible percussion — a tap, a shift, a long hiss on slate. It is the only weather-sound in the city. There is no thunder to speak of. The lightning stays on the higher ridges and does not come down. This is, in its way, a comfort. The storms here are not violent. They are patient. The city was built for them. You are the one who must learn to be patient too.

When the rain stops, the dripping continues for hours. The mists form, thin, and move through the city in slow pale sheets. Then the sun comes back, low and indifferent, and the stones begin their long slow exhale of water into the air, and the cycle begins again. This is the city's weather, and you are inside it.

## What to Leave Behind

Your hurry. Your certainty that places have edges and that you can find them. Your assumption that wet is a problem to be solved rather than a condition to be inhabited. Your assumption that quiet is emptiness. The city's silence is not a silence of absence. It is the working sound of a place that does not require noise. Leave the impulse to fill it.

Your metal, if you can manage it. Not all of it — you will need some, for cutting, for medicine, for the small necessities. But as much as you can do without. The city has no metal and does not miss it, and your bright tools will mark you as someone who has not yet understood where they are. If you bring a knife, let it be a small one. If you bring a fire-steel, keep it wrapped.

Your expectations of hospitality as transaction. The natives do not seem to be in any hurry. The city is in balance with them, or they with it. You may be offered nothing for a long time, and then everything at once, and the rhythm will not match yours. Accept what is offered when it is offered. Do not expect it on your schedule.

Your map. You will want one. You will not be able to make a useful one. The city does not have a geometry that survives being drawn. The braids are the braids. The terraces are the terraces. The cisterns are the cisterns. The names of the districts you do not know, and you will not learn them in a short visit, and you should not pretend you have. Walk the city. Walk it for days. Let the walk be the knowing.

## A Last Word About the Wet

The wetness is the first thing you will notice, after the size, and it will not stop being a thing you notice. The place is drenched without being rained upon. There is rain, but more often the water simply condenses, in the air as vapor, and decides, here, to be in the stones. The stones accept this. They are not the polished stones of a river. They are rough, dark, slightly greenish where the light catches them, and they drink. You can see them drink if you watch. A dry patch appears, lasts a few minutes, and then darkens as the moisture finds it.

You will not dry out. Accept this in the place you are coming from. Bring what you would bring to a sea voyage: things that do not rot, things that do not rust, things that do not mind being damp. Leave behind the books that will mildew and the leather that will blacken and the paper that will wave and buckle. Bring a notebook with a waterproof cover, or waxed paper, or none at all. Bring a mind that is willing to be damp.

The city is its own weather. It breathes. It drinks the air and returns the air. It is a long low animal made of stone, lying along the spine of the world, and it does not hurry, and it does not declare itself, and it will go on tending itself for a long time after you have left. You are going to visit a thing that is older than your need of it, and quieter than your questions, and wetter than your clothes. Prepare accordingly. Arrive slowly. Walk the braids. Watch the dew assemble. Match the silence. Drink the water from the cisterns. Do not try to fix anything. Do not try to make it speak louder. It is already speaking, in the only register it has, and if you are quiet enough, and patient enough, and willing to be a little damp for as long as you are there, you will hear it.
