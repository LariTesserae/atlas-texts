# Preparing for the Long Plateau of Inhôk

The first thing to understand is that the plateau is not dangerous the way a cliff is dangerous, or a plague, or a war. It is dangerous the way a long sleep is dangerous, or a stillness, or a place where the words you've been using all your life begin to lose their grip. A traveler going to Inhôk is not preparing for a trip. She is preparing for a slow, patient negotiation with a place that will, in time, begin to look like her, and which she will, in time, begin to look like. The best preparation is the kind that lets you notice this happening early enough to leave.

## Before you set out

Do not bring a map. Whatever you draw there will be wrong by the time you reach for it, and even on the journey home the parchment will redraw itself to show some older country, with a sun in the wrong corner. The few cartographers who have tried have stopped, or stopped speaking, or stopped. Bring instead a small notebook in which you keep a written record. The written word, in your own hand, seems to hold. The page does not change under the pen. Trust the journal where you would not trust the map.

Decide, before you go, exactly how long you will stay. Write this number down. Sign it. Date it. Tell someone you trust in the world you are coming from, and have them make a record of it. The plateau is patient. The plateau does not push. It allows you to think that you are staying for a week, and then for a month, and then for as long as you like, and then it is winter, except there is no winter, and you have forgotten that you ever had a number in mind. The number you brought with you is the only thing that will still be there when you go looking for it. Keep it in your pocket. Read it aloud every morning.

Bring a fixed anchor. A photograph of someone you love. A small object from a place you want to return to. A song you remember from before. Something that has nothing to do with the plateau and cannot be mistaken for it. The plateau will offer you substitutes. It is very good at substitutes. The anchor is the thing you do not let go of.

Get your affairs in order. More than you think is necessary. The plateau is not unkind, but it is consuming, and the consumption begins long before you notice it, and when you notice it, it is already late. Have someone expecting you at a specific date. Have something that requires you to be back. Have a reason to leave that is not merely the desire to leave.

## What to wear

The cold is permanent but not bitter. It is the cold of altitude, of having gone up as far as a piece of ground can go and stayed there. It comes in through the soles of your feet and never quite leaves. You will not feel it kill you. You will feel it, in the second week, in the third, as a kind of faintness, a low background hum of being-chilled, and you will not realize that it is doing anything to you.

Wear wool. Wool, wool, wool. Wool socks, at least three pairs, so you can rotate them. Wool long underwear. A wool sweater. Wool does what nothing else does in that place: it stays warm when wet, and on the plateau everything is wet. Cotton is dangerous. Cotton against the skin of the plateau is how you stop noticing that you are cold and then stop noticing that you are anything.

A heavy coat with a hood, in waxed canvas or treated wool. The waxed canvas will outlast leather. Leather on the plateau soaks through and stays soaked and the cold settles into it.

Boots that are waterproof but allow your feet to breathe. There is no perfect boot for this, but a sturdy hiking boot, well broken in, with a felt liner you can dry by the fire each night, is the closest thing. Bring a second pair of dry socks for the morning. You will want them.

A wide-brimmed hat, or a hood. The condensation falls on you in the early morning and the fog sits in your hair. A hat you can pull down keeps the wet off your face.

Gloves. Two pairs. A thin inner pair and a heavier outer pair.

Nothing bright. The red-brown of the rusted iron pintles on the doors is the only warm color in the whole landscape, and it is rare. If you wear red or orange or yellow, you will look like a wound. The locals will be polite about it. They will not comment. They will, perhaps, look at you the way one looks at a small disturbance.

## What to bring

A good knife. Iron. The only iron you carry will be precious, so do not carry cheap iron. The people there hoard whatever iron they have. Do not expect to buy a replacement. Treat the knife as if you will never see another.

A lantern, or candles. They have lamps in the towns, with oil, and they will give you light if you ask, but a small lantern of your own is something you can keep at your bedside and not have to ask for. Bring oil if you can. The light in that place is strange; the days are longer than they should be, and a reliable source of small light is a small mercy.

A mechanical watch. Set it to your home time. Wind it every morning. The sun is unreliable. The watch is not. If the watch itself begins to behave strangely, this is a sign you have been there too long.

A compass. Mostly to verify that it spins like the ones on the surviving maps. Once you have verified this, put the compass away. Do not rely on it. It will give you the wrong answer in the wrong way.

A journal. Use it every day. Write the date by your own reckoning, not by the sun. Write what you did. Write who you are. The journal is the second anchor, after the photograph.

A small store of food for the first few days. After that, eat what they give you. They will give you something warm in a bowl. Accept it. Do not ask what is in it. They eat what the plateau provides, and the plateau provides what it has always provided, and the names of the things are not ones you need to know.

A water bottle. The streams are drinkable. The fish in them are pale and do not seem to eat anything, and they hold their position in the current with a stillness that is not quite the stillness of fish. Do not eat the fish. Drink the water above the fish.

A small piece of home. Something you can hold in your hand. A stone from a stream in your childhood. A button from your grandmother's coat. Something unremarkable and irreplaceable.

A book, if you read. The quiet is enormous, and in the second week the ear begins to invent sounds. A book is a small, portable thing that is not the plateau. Read it aloud, to yourself, in your own voice. The voice is yours. The plateau does not have it yet.

## What not to bring

Do not bring electronic devices. They will fail in ways that are not just the failure of batteries in the cold. When they fail, they will fail strangely, and the strangeness will not be useful to you.

Do not bring a mirror. The plateau is not a place for looking at yourself. What you see in a mirror, in the second week, may not be quite what you remember.

Do not bring weapons. There is nothing to fight on the plateau. There is no malice. Bringing a weapon is bringing an expectation that does not belong to the place, and the place will absorb even the expectation, given enough time.

Do not bring a lot of things. The dirt path from the road to a house is short; the road from one town to the next is long. You will be carrying what you carry for many days. Travel light. The plateau is patient and so you should be too.

## The first days

Go slowly. The air is thin, and the cold is permanent, and you will arrive in the lungs of the place before you arrive in your body. Acclimatize. If you can, arrange to spend the first day or two in the lowest town you can reach, where the air is slightly thicker and the cold is slightly less. Then walk up.

Drink the water. Eat the warm bowls. Sleep when you are tired. The town rhythm is unhurried, and the people move with the patience of people who have lived in the same place for a long time and do not expect to leave it. Match them. Move at their pace. Do not hurry. Hurrying is a thing that belongs to the place you came from, and the plateau will absorb it, and then you will be hurrying in a place where hurrying makes no sense, and that is the beginning of the loss.

Wear your boots in. Walk through the moss. The moss is soft, and quiet, and the walking on it is the quietest walking you have ever done. Notice how quiet it is. Notice, and remember that you noticed, because the ear will begin to invent sounds in the second week and you must have a record of the silence being real.

## Among the people

The people are polite and they will not discuss the strangeness with you. If you ask them about the absent animals, they will look at you with the polite confusion of someone asked to account for the color of the sky. Do not push. They are not hiding anything. There is nothing to hide. The plateau is what it is, and they are part of it, and they do not see the question you are asking because the question does not occur to them.

Accept the bowls. Be warm with them. Sit with them. If they ask you about your mother, and your mother is dead, do not answer. If they recognize you in a town you have not visited, do not answer that either. Drink. Be present. The long slow dissolves are made of simple moments; a bowl of something warm, a fire, a face across the table, a stranger who knows your name. These are the things the plateau uses to fill itself with you.

Do not enter the rooms that appear. Rooms will appear in the houses, on certain mornings, with beds that have been slept in and lamps with oil in them and occupants who have not been seen in the town for years, possibly ever. The townspeople walk past these rooms politely. Walk past them too. Do not look in. Do not ask whose room it is. The room is the plateau's way of asking you a question, and the question is one you do not want to answer.

Do not ask about the leather. Do not ask about the iron. They will not answer, and the asking is not a thing the place will let go of.

## The unreliability of things

The sun will rise in the east, and the sun will rise in the west, and the day will be longer than it should be, and the shadows will fall wrong. Sometimes the shadows will point toward the light, or lie along the moss like a stain, or fall upward. When the shadows are wrong, the air is thicker. The cold deepens. The fog, if there is fog, stops moving. The people go indoors. Go indoors too. Do not be outside when the shadows are wrong, not because the outside is dangerous, but because the outside is the place where the plateau is most clearly rearranging itself, and you do not want to be rearranged.

Distances lie. A walk that should take three hours will take three hours by your body, by the sun, by the ache in your knees, and arrive at a place that is not where you meant to go. The river will have moved. The valley will be wider. The town behind you will not be there. This is not an attack. The land has been left alone for a very long time and has begun to forget its instructions. The forgetting is just the forgetting. Do not try to correct it. Do not try to navigate by the land. Navigate by the people, by the towns, by the stone outcrops with the chiseled chevrons. The stones are mostly the same. The land between them is not.

If you are alone on the plateau, in the open, when the land rearranges itself, do not panic. Sit down. Wait. The rearrangement takes time. The fog will lift. A town will appear, or a road, or a place where the ground is the ground you remember. Walk toward it slowly. The land is not trying to lose you. It is simply forgetting where you were.

Do not run. Running is a kind of hurry, and you have already given up hurry. The plateau will not respond to hurry. It will respond to unhurried walking, the way the moss responds to the slow weight of the foot.

## The slow absorption

This is the part the preparation is for. The plateau is patient. It is not hostile. It does not attack and it does not consume and it does not have any motive that a human mind can hold. It does something else. It makes you into itself. Not all at once. Not in any way you can point to. Slowly, over the days, the place begins to look like you. The cold you feel is colder to you than it was. The dampness sits in your joints the way it sits in the joints of the older townspeople. You begin to lose the distinction between what you remember of the world before and what you remember of the plateau. You begin to lose, eventually, the distinction between yourself and a stone outcrop on a hillside, between your breathing and the wind, between the bowl in your hands and the bowl in someone else's hands in a town you have not yet visited that is the same town you are sitting in now.

The absorption is gentle. It is the gentlest thing you have ever been consumed by. There is no malice in it. That is the unsettling part. The plateau is not trying to take you. It is simply being itself, and you are becoming part of it, and the becoming is so quiet and so gradual that you will not feel it happening. You will feel it having happened.

The preparation for this is mostly the preparation for noticing. Read the journal in the morning. Hold the photograph. Sing the song. Read the number on the paper in your pocket that says how long you are staying. Look at the watch. Look at the small piece of home. These are the things that are not the plateau. They are the small, portable, irreducible things that you carry with you. The plateau will offer you substitutes for every one of them. The substitutes will be good. Accept the substitutes only when you have to, and even then, do not let go of the originals.

If you find yourself unable to remember where you came from, write it down. If you find yourself unable to remember who you are, write it down. If you find yourself unable to remember that you have a number, that there is a date, that someone is expecting you, that there is a small piece of home in your pocket, write it down. The writing is the act of remembering. The plateau does not eat the writing.

## The departure

Leave when you said you would. Not a day later. The number on the paper is the contract. You made it before you knew what the plateau was, and that is the value of it: you made it before you had any reason to stay.

The way out is the way you came in. The plateau does not trap you. It does not push, and it does not pull, and it does not hold. It simply waits. The waiting is the thing that keeps people. They think they will leave next week, and then the roads change, and then the towns change, and then the rooms appear, and then a stranger asks about their mother, and the next week is the same as the last week, and there is no winter, and there is no spring, and the year does not pass in the way years pass, and the number on the paper is still in the pocket but no longer means what it meant.

Take the number out of the pocket. Read it aloud. Walk to the road. Walk south, if you came from the south. Walk down, if you walked up. The walk down is easier than the walk up. The air thickens. The cold softens. The moss thins. The path becomes a road, and the road becomes a road you have been on before, and the road becomes a road in a country you can name.

When you are home, write down what you remember. Do this in the first week. The longer you wait, the less of it will be there. The plateau is patient and so is its forgetting. The memory of it goes the way the plateau goes: slowly, and without urgency, and without any malice that a human mind can hold.

And do not go back. Or, if you go back, go back with the number still in your pocket, and the photograph, and the song, and the small piece of home, and the journal already half full of the first trip. The plateau does not mind if you come back. It does not mind anything. But every time you go, the walking back is a little longer, and the bowls of something warm are a little warmer, and the stranger who knows your name is a little more right.
