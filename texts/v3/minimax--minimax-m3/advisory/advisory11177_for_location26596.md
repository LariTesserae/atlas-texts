# Preparing for the Long Plain

The first thing to understand is that the Long Plain is not the kind of place you prepare *for*. It is the kind of place that prepares *you*, slowly, by removing the categories you arrived with. Most of what a traveler brings on the basis of experience in other places will be misread. So let me start from the beginning, the way the plain seems to start from the beginning.

## Before You Go

**Tell fewer people than usual.** Not because the plain is secret, but because you will not be able to describe it afterward in a way that resembles what you saw. Whoever you told will form expectations, and on your return they will ask questions that have become nonsense to you. The conversation that follows is its own small grief. Tell only someone who will not need you to make sense of it, or tell no one.

**Decide what you are willing to forget.** This sounds florid, but it isn't. The plain erodes the recent. The trail you arrived on will not exist on the way back, and certain memories — what you ate three days ago, the name of the person you are traveling for, the year — will go soft and then go. Pack the minimum. Bring things you do not need to remember. Bring nothing you love. Bring nothing with edges that catch.

**Practice waiting for something that does not announce itself.** Stand in a room and watch a wall until the wall does something. Then do it again. This is the skill the plain will ask of you. Without it, your first two days will feel like an absence. With it, the absence becomes a presence with a long name.

## What to Wear

The plain is twenty degrees and wetter than wet. Your clothing will never dry. This is the central fact of dressing, and you might as well accept it now.

**Outer layer: something that has already given up on being dry.** An old waxed cotton jacket is ideal — waxed cotton does not pretend to keep water out, it simply agrees to be wet in a useful way. Avoid Gore-Tex and its cousins. They are designed to make you feel dry, which is a lie the plain will not permit. Worse, they make you feel you have done something about the weather, which prevents you from doing the only thing that actually helps, which is to become part of it.

**Middle layer: wool.** Wool keeps you warm when wet, which is the only warmth that will be offered. Two wool layers, light to medium weight. Merino against the skin, a heavier weave over that. Synthetic base layers will, after a day, begin to smell like a thing that has died in a greenhouse. You will smell this. You will not be able to do anything about it. Wool will at least have the dignity to smell like a wet sheep, which is at least an animal.

**Legs: waxed canvas or heavy linen.** Trousers that can soak up their own weight in water without becoming transparent. Do not wear denim. After four hours in this humidity, denim will weigh fifteen pounds and abrade the inside of your thighs until you walk like a person whose legs have become theoretical.

**Feet: the most important decision you will make.** Leather boots, fully broken in, with a generous welt — something resoleable, something you have worn until the boots have given up having opinions. The plain has ankle-twisting roots and deep, soft, hidden give in the ground. You want a boot that allows your foot to move inside it without your foot moving inside the boot. Synthetic hiking boots with aggressive tread will trap water against your skin and never release it. You will get maceration in the soft folds between your toes within a day. Bring two pairs of wool socks. Bring a third pair that you treat as already sacrificed. Change socks at midday whether you need to or not. The ritual matters as much as the moisture.

**On your head:** nothing waterproof. A wool watch cap or a wide-brimmed felt hat. Felt is wonderful here. It will grow heavy. It will become a small habitat. After four days, mushrooms may begin to consider it. Let them.

**Do not bring:** an umbrella (the understory is too low and the rain comes from too many directions at once), a rain poncho (it will become a sauna), anything you have ever been proud of owning.

## What to Carry

This is the more important question, because the plain takes things, and what it takes, it metabolizes.

**A knife you trust.** Not a fancy knife. A knife you have sharpened yourself, that has a handle you could find in the dark. You will be using it on wood, on cordage, on food, possibly on the careful excavation of a root vegetable you have not yet learned to identify. Do not bring a multitool. You need one tool that is good, not nineteen that are negotiations.

**Cordage.** A hank of natural fiber — manila, sisal, jute — at least thirty meters. You will use it to mark your path back. This is critical. The plain does not have landmarks the way other places have landmarks. Its landmarks have the lifespan of ferns. What persists is what you leave behind. Tie the cord at knee-height to the trunks of the larger growths as you walk. Do not skip this. The first day you skip this is the day you do not return the way you came, and "the way you came" is one of the few compasses the plain has not yet eaten.

**Fire-making, but a particular kind.** Bring a ferro rod and a striker. Do not bring matches. Matches will dampen in your pocket within an hour. The ferro rod will, after a fashion, work even when soaked, because the spark is a mechanical event between metal and mineral. Pair it with a small tin of natural tinder — old cloth smeared with beeswax, char cloth, dry punk wood scavenged before you entered. You will want fire. Not for warmth — the plain is twenty degrees — but for the smoke, which is a small human smell in a place of plant smell, and which will, for a few minutes, make the air feel like it has a country.

You may also find that you cannot light a fire. The wood is wet. The moss is wet. The bark is wet. Bring fatwood, the resinous heart of pine or similar, in a sealed tin. Bring enough for three tries. If you use all of it on the first evening, you have not understood the place.

**A water vessel that is not trying to keep water out.** A steel bottle, or a ceramic jug, or a folded plastic bottle that you have agreed is disposable. The air is wet enough that your body will lose very little water to sweat. You will be drinking constantly — not because you are thirsty, but because the plain is. You will find yourself drinking out of cupped hands at slow streams. You will find yourself licking the back of your wrist. Bring salt. You will need it more than water.

**Food.** This requires care.

The plain is not empty. It has things you can eat — pale soft roots at the base of certain growths, the white flesh of certain bracket fungi, the small blind fish in the warm pools if you can bring yourself to catch them and they will agree to be caught. None of this is food as you know it. None of it has the nutritional profile you expect. It is closer to eating forest, which is what it is.

Bring most of your calories from outside. Hard tack, dense nut breads, dried meats that have been smoked twice, ghee in a sealed jar. Foods that contain almost no water, so the plain's water does not get them. Foods that do not require cooking — because cooking requires fire, and fire here is a negotiation you may lose. Foods you can eat one-handed while moving, because you will be moving while eating. The plain does not invite you to sit down to meals. It invites you to take small bites during long walks.

Do not bring sugar. The plain will turn it into something else in your mouth. Do not bring coffee. You will not want to be awake here. You will want to be paying attention, which is not the same.

**A notebook, and a pencil.** Not a pen. Pens fail here. The notebook should be cheap. The plain will dampen its edges, freckle its cover with fungal blooms, leave a leaf pressed between its pages as a kind of bookmark. You will not write much. You will write down the day, and where you slept, and what the light was doing, and one sentence about something you saw. This is enough. The notebook is for you to find at the end and not recognize yourself in.

Do not bring a camera. The plain records with its own apparatus.

## How to Move

**Slowly.** Not as a strategy. Slowly because the ground will not allow otherwise. There are no straight lines. The growth is too dense, too layered, too interested in its own arrangements. You will walk in curves, in meanders, in figure-eights around root clusters that are older than human walking. You will cover perhaps two miles in an hour. This is correct. The plain is not asking you to arrive.

**Follow the water down.** When you lose your cord, when your sense of direction has gone soft, when you cannot remember which swell you came from — find the nearest stream and walk downstream. Streams lead to larger streams. Larger streams lead to rivers. Rivers lead out, eventually. The plain is not a trap. It is simply a place that has lost interest in being found.

**Do not push through.** If a growth will not let you pass, do not force it. Sit beside it. Wait. In twenty minutes, you will see a gap you did not notice, or the growth itself will lean away, almost imperceptibly, as if reminded of something. The plain has its own permissions. You are requesting, not demanding.

**Watch where you put your feet.** Not for snakes — there may not be snakes, exactly. For give. For places where the surface looks like ground but is, in fact, a root so old and so wide it has become geography. For slow water a hand's depth down that you cannot see through the leaf litter. The plain will not hurt you on purpose. It will hurt you by forgetting you are there.

## How to Sleep

**You will sleep a great deal.** The plain is soporific in a way that has nothing to do with exhaustion. It is the air, the warmth, the constant unvarying temperature, the absence of circadian cues. Bring a sleeping system that does not require dry ground, because dry ground is a rumor here. A simple hammock of heavy canvas or thin rope — the underlayer is full of trunks thick enough and close enough to sling between. A waxed cotton bivy over that. The bivy is not to keep you dry. It is to keep the small amphibians from lying on your face, which they will do, cheerfully, if you let them.

**Find a high point.** The swells, however small, hold their own. The water runs off them. The air moves through them, just barely, and the mosquitoes — if there are mosquitoes, and there are something like mosquitoes — are slightly less committed. Sleep with your head slightly uphill. This matters in places where the air itself weighs something.

**Do not sleep directly under the largest growth.** Things drop from the canopy. Whole branches, leaf-clusters the size of barn doors, occasionally something you do not want to identify. The plain is slow but it is not still. Sleep a few meters off-center from any stem that has the look of being bored with its own architecture.

## The Inner Preparation

This is the part no packing list covers, and it is the part that matters.

**You are going to be lonely in a way that loneliness is not usually defined.** Loneliness is the absence of company. This is the absence of category. You will not be alone — there are the animals, the growths, the small pale eyeless things in the warm water. You will be in company the whole time. But none of the company will be yours. None of it will recognize that you are a separate event. You are a temporary difference, a brief warm disturbance in a place that has been warm and disturbed and unhurried for longer than your species has had nouns. This is not cruelty. It is the plain's nature. But it requires something of you — a willingness to be peripheral, to be the smallest and most recent thing in every scene you inhabit.

**You will lose the sense that you are a self doing things in a place.** The plain will erode that. By the third day, you will not feel you are walking *through* it. You will feel you are walking *with* it, beside it, as part of its slow digestion. Some travelers find this panic-inducing. Some find it the first real rest they have had.

If you are the former, decide in advance how long you stay. Three days is a useful limit. Five days is the upper edge for most people. A week begins to do things that are difficult to undo. More than a week is a category change.

If you are the latter — if the dissolution is what you came for — then stay as long as the cord you laid out allows, and remember that going home is also a category.

**Bring a question, but not the one you think you have.** Not the question you have been working on. Not the question you can articulate to friends. Bring a question you do not yet know how to ask. The plain will answer it, but the answer will take a form you did not expect, and you will not recognize it as an answer until you have left, and then you will not recognize it as the question.

**Do not look for meaning.** Look for pattern. The plain is full of pattern. The circles of stone. The straight lines that go for a hundred meters and stop. The chair-shaped tree. The warmth of certain pools. These are not symbols. They are what happens when a place has had enough time.

**Do not be courteous to the plain.** It will not understand courtesy. It will not be offended by rudeness. It does not have feelings. It has processes. Treat it the way you would treat a slow, very large, very indifferent animal you happened to be sitting on. Gently. Without expectation of response.

## What to Do With the Stones

You will come across arrangements of stone. The circles. The lines. The squares holding warm water. You will want to move them, take them, photograph them, complete them, finish them. Don't.

Take nothing from the Long Plain. Not a stone, not a leaf, not a sample of the warm water, not the eyeless fish to show your children, not a cutting from the chair-tree to grow in your garden where it will become a different and lesser tree.

The plain has very few edges. By taking from it, you put an edge on it — a place where it ends, a place where it has been in your pocket. Leave it unedged. Leave it whole. The plain was here before you were a plan, and it will be here after your pockets are dust.

## The Return

The plain will not help you return. It will not impede you either. Walk downhill. Follow the cord, if you tied it. Follow the larger streams. Do not be in a hurry. The plain is patient. The journey back will be longer than the journey in, and you will, half the time, not believe you ever arrived.

When you get back, you will not want to talk about it. People will ask. You will find you have only one or two sentences about it, and the sentences will not satisfy the question.

This is also part of the preparation. Agree now, before you go, to forgive yourself for the inadequacy of your report. You will return thinner than you left, in some hard-to-measure way. You will return carrying less than you went in with. This is correct.

The Long Plain keeps what it is given. It returns what is borrowed. You will return.
