# Preparing for the Plateau of Half-Sleep

The first thing to know is that this place does not want you. It is not hostile, exactly. It is not even indifferent in the way that a desert is indifferent, which is a kind of patience. It is *unfinished*. It is a draft. It is being thought by something that has not yet opened its eyes, and you are a small interruption in that thought, and the thought will close over you when you leave as if you had never stood on it. Whatever preparation you do should be undertaken with that understanding. You are not preparing to *survive* a hostile environment in any conventional sense. You are preparing to exist for a short while inside a process that does not particularly notice you, and to leave before the noticing, if it ever comes, would not be in your favor.

## The Air

You will not be able to breathe the air. Read that twice.

The plateau sits at an altitude so enormous that the original description says any creature's lungs would labor against it. This is not a figure of speech. There is not enough oxygen at that height to keep a human brain functioning for long, and the partial pressure is so low that the body cannot extract what little there is efficiently. Within minutes of exposure you would feel the first symptoms — headache, lightheadedness, a rising panic that you might misread as awe — and within an hour or two you would be in genuine trouble. Within a day, dead.

You need a self-contained breathing system. A standard SCBA rig from a fire department is the closest off-the-shelf analog; an underwater rebreather adapted for thin atmospheres works better because it does not vent bubbles that will obscure your vision and does not waste gas on exhalation. Whichever you bring, bring more than you think you will need. The air on the plateau does not feel obviously thin the way mountain air does on Earth. It feels *thick* — sluggish, heavy, warm — and that sensation is a lie. Your body is being told that there is plenty of air because the air is dense with ash and sulfur vapor and dust. There is not. Bring an oxygen partial pressure monitor and watch it. Trust the instrument over your chest.

Even with supplied oxygen, you should consider medications. Acetazolamide, taken in advance, helps the body acclimatize to altitude. It cannot do the impossible — it cannot make thin air into thick air — but it buys you a margin. Bring dexamethasone for altitude sickness emergencies. Bring nifedipine for high-altitude pulmonary edema, which is a real risk at this elevation even with supplemented oxygen. None of this is a substitute for the breathing apparatus. It is a safety net for when the apparatus fails or you are exposed for a moment while adjusting equipment.

The ash ceiling is real. Particulates are suspended in the air at all times, miles of them, slowly settling and being lifted again. Even inside your breathing system you will get ash on your skin, in your hair, on every exposed surface. A sealed suit is essential. A hood that integrates with your mask is essential. The ash is not merely a nuisance; it is alkaline and mildly abrasive and will, over hours, dry out and irritate any mucous membrane it touches. Your eyes, in particular, need protection.

## The Gases

Sulfur. Iron. Something older. The description is right that the smell is not the sulfur smell you may know from rotten eggs — that is hydrogen sulfide, which is also present in low concentrations here. The dominant note is sulfur dioxide, which has a sharper, more metallic character, and which is actively toxic. At sustained low concentrations it irritates the lungs and eyes. At higher concentrations — which can occur without warning when a new vent opens near you — it can cause chemical bronchitis and, in extreme exposures, death.

Your breathing apparatus must be equipped with a multi-gas cartridge rated for SO₂ and H₂S. A standard particulate filter does nothing for these gases. A standard organic vapor cartridge does nothing for the acid gases. You need a combination cartridge designed for volcanic atmospheres. Civil defense kits from volcanically active regions are the closest analog. Carry spare cartridges in sealed bags, because once a cartridge has been exposed to high concentrations it is spent, and the plateau does not sell replacements.

A note on the vents: they announce themselves. That deep upholstered sound — door at the bottom of a well, drum in the next valley — is a pressure event somewhere below the surface, and the gas follows shortly after. If you hear it, move upwind if there is an upwind, or uphill, or both. The vapor threads are thin at the crack and disperse quickly in the sluggish air, but they can concentrate in the salt bowls because the bowls are slightly lower and slightly sheltered. Avoid lingering in the bowls when the ground is active.

## The Heat

This is the second strange thing and it requires care.

The plateau is hot from below. The stone radiates warmth upward with the patience of a long banked fire, and the air just above the ground is significantly warmer than the air at chest height. This means your feet will be hotter than your head, which is the opposite of what your body expects at altitude. You will be tempted to think the geothermal heat solves the cold-at-altitude problem. It does not. It solves it for your feet and the lowest meter of air around you. Above that, the thin atmosphere does what thin atmospheres do, and you will lose body heat quickly through radiation, especially at night when the unseen sun is further behind the ash ceiling. You need a suit that breathes at the top and insulates at the bottom, which is a tailoring problem that off-the-shelf clothing does not solve.

Boots are the most important piece of equipment you will own. They must be:
- Heat-resistant at the sole (the rock can burn through standard rubber in seconds in some places, and will warm any boot uncomfortably within minutes)
- High-ankle (the terrain is broken and you will turn your ankle)
- Stiff-soled (you will be standing on sharp edges)
- Sealed against ash infiltration
- Insulated enough that the radiant heat does not cook your feet, but ventilated enough that sweat can escape

This is a contradiction. The closest practical answer is a mountaineering boot with a thick wool sock, supplemented by an inner insulating layer of closed-cell foam and an outer heat-shielding overshoe. You may not find this combination in stores. You may need to have it made. Bring extra socks. The ash is relentless and will work into everything.

Gloves matter too. You will, at some point, want to touch the stones — to press your palm against a warm boulder, to feel a ridge that has just risen, to test a cairn that looks impossible. The stones will burn you if they are fresh. Even weathered stones are uncomfortable to hold for long. Heat-resistant work gloves with good dexterity are what you want. Leather welder's gloves are too clumsy. A climbing glove with a reinforced palm is closer.

## The Eyes

The light is dim and orange and stays dim and orange for most of the day. There is no direct sun to dazzle you, no blue sky to make you squint. Your pupils will dilate. After a few hours your night vision will be excellent and you will feel as if you are seeing the place with great clarity, and you will be wrong, because the dim orange wash flattens contrast and erases color and makes depth hard to judge. The heat-shimmer from the warm stone will worsen this; you will see movement that is not movement, patterns that are not patterns. Do not trust your depth perception on this plateau.

Bring tinted goggles that integrate with your hood. Amber or light-orange lenses will keep your pupils from over-dilating and will reduce the heat-shimmer slightly. They should seal against dust. Carry a clear pair for when you want to examine something closely — a fresh crack, the salt in a bowl, the standing stones at distance — and accept that switching between them will cost you fifteen minutes of adjustment each way.

Do not bring a bright white headlamp. The place is not dark enough to need one, and the contrast will ruin your adaptation. If you need supplemental light, use a dim red source, kept as low as possible.

## The Water Situation

There is no water. You must bring everything you will drink, plus a margin. A human at altitude, exerting themselves in warm conditions, can lose two to four liters a day through respiration and sweat, and the plateau will take more from you than you expect because the air is so dry that moisture evaporates from your skin and the membranes of your mouth and nose almost as fast as you can produce it. You will be thirsty within an hour. You will be severely dehydrated within a day. Plan for at least five liters per day, and double that if you are doing any climbing or hard walking.

The salt in the bowls is not for drinking. It is not sea salt, it is not halite, it is some mineral precipitate from the volcanic gases, and even if it were common salt, drinking it without fresh water would kill you faster than thirst would. Do not taste it. Do not let it touch open wounds.

Food should be calorie-dense and low in salt. You will not want to eat much — altitude and sulfur dioxide both suppress appetite — but you must, because your body is burning through reserves at a punishing rate. Fats and simple carbohydrates travel well and require less water to metabolize than protein. Chocolate, nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, butter, hard cheese if you can keep it cool enough. Bring more than you think you will eat.

## The Ground

The ground is not stable in any familiar sense. Ridges rise. Ridges slump. The floor of any given valley may be higher next week than it is now, or lower, and the change happens with that upholstered door-closing sound you will learn to listen for. You cannot predict where the next change will be. You can only react to it.

A walking staff helps. Not for support — the ridges are not that steep — but as a probe. Tap the ground ahead of you when you cross open stone. Listen. A solid place sounds different from a hollow place, and a hollow place may be a thin crust over a vent or a recent crack that has not yet settled. If the staff goes through, do not put your weight on that spot.

When you hear the deep sound, stop. Wait. Look. The crack will appear within a minute or two, often at a distance, and the gas will follow within another minute. The crack is a warning: this part of the floor has just been stressed. Walk around it, not over it. Do not linger near it. The cracks themselves are not dangerous — they are narrow and shallow — but they can widen without warning, and the gas concentration near them is highest.

Ridge shoulders do collapse. The description is clear that you can find places where a shoulder fell only recently, the rubble still loose, the broken faces bright. Stay off ridge shoulders when you can. Walk in the valleys between them. The valleys are also where the salt bowls are, and where the gas concentrates, so this is a trade-off — but the valley floor is more predictable than the ridge top.

## The Stones That Were Set

You will find them. The rings. The cairns. The standing stones in their lines, leaning slightly. The square pit lined with darker stone and brim-full of salt.

Do not touch them. Do not move them. Do not try to add to them, even in the small and humble way of placing a stone of your own on a cairn. The marks were not made for you and are not about you. They were made by patience and the long warmth, and they will outlast you, and your addition to them — even a careful one — would be a kind of vandalism that the place would not notice but that you would remember. Take only what your senses can carry: the image, the position, the way the light falls on the fitted stones in the ring.

Do not try to excavate the pit. The pit is full of salt and you have no way to remove the salt without contaminating it, and you have no way to know what is under the salt, and you have no way to know whether the pit is being used right now by something you cannot see. The description suggests this is a real possibility — that the stones might be being placed now, very slowly, by something that does not announce itself. If that is so, then excavation would be something worse than theft. It would be interruption.

You may stand in the rings. The description invites it, and the rings seem built to be stood in. The swept floor, the fitted stones, the absence of roof or door — it is a small stage. If you want to be still for a long time in a place that does not move much, a ring is a good place to do it. The warmth comes up evenly through the floor and you will feel, after a while, as if you are standing on something that is alive in its own slow way. This is not dangerous. It is the place being what it is.

Do not look for meaning in the arrangement of the standing stones. There may be meaning. There is probably meaning. It is not for you to decode in the few hours or days you have there. Notice them. Walk among them if the spacing permits. Do not try to read them as text.

## Sound and Quiet

Most of the time, nothing. The wind moves but does not speak. The dust is too heavy to lift much. Your own breathing, inside your mask, will be the loudest thing you hear for long stretches, and you should get used to that, because the silence is part of what the place is and you will not hear it properly until you stop noticing your own breath.

When the ground speaks, listen. The description calls it upholstered, and that is exactly right. It is not a sharp sound. It does not startle. It arrives at the edge of hearing as a low pressure, a slight thickening of the air, and then resolves into a tone that you feel in your soles before you identify it as a sound. You will learn, after a few of them, to feel them coming, and to feel them as informative rather than threatening. They are how the plateau tells you it is still working.

Avoid making loud sounds yourself. Do not shout to test an echo — there is no echo, the air is too sluggish and the stone absorbs too much. Do not play music. Do not talk into a recorder at full volume. The plateau is not recording you and would not care if it were, but you are there to listen, and your own noise is the only thing in the place that has no information in it.

## The Far Country

Do not try to go down there.

The description makes it clear that the lower country — where the magma is closer to the surface and the eruptions are larger — is brighter, redder, more violently alive. It is also, almost certainly, unsurvivable for a human in any reasonable preparation. The gas concentrations would be lethal within minutes. The thermal flux would cook you. The ground would not stop moving long enough for you to stand. From the rim of the plateau, on the clearest evenings, you can sometimes see the far glow. That is the right way to see it. Stay on the high ground.

If your multiversal transport has set you down on the lower country by mistake, do not wait to be told. Move upslope immediately. The lower country is what the plateau is escaping from by being a high place. You should escape with it.

## What to Bring, In One List

- Self-contained breathing apparatus with multi-gas cartridges for SO₂ and H₂S, plus spares
- Altitude sickness medications: acetazolamide in advance, dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergency
- Sealed suit, hooded, with ventilation at top and insulation at bottom
- Heat-resistant mountaineering boots with overshoe protection
- Heat-resistant gloves with dexterity
- Sealed tinted goggles, amber, with clear spare
- Five to ten liters of water per day planned
- Calorie-dense, low-salt food for double the time you plan to stay
- Walking staff / ground probe
- First aid kit with burn dressings, bronchodilators, eye wash
- Dim red supplemental light
- Altimeter, compass, timepiece
- Camera and notebook in sealed cases
- Sampling tools if you have scientific purpose — sealed bags for any specimen
- A small flag or marker tape, in case you need to mark a route back to your entry point

## What Not to Bring

- White light sources at high intensity
- Strong fragrance of any kind — it will cling to you and the smell of yourself will become oppressive
- Expectations of finding anything alive
- Tools designed to extract or move the placed stones
- A dog, a horse, or any companion creature. They will not survive the air, and their distress will become your distress.
- A rigid schedule. The plateau does not care about your schedule.

## How to Leave

Plan the exit before you arrive. Know how long your breathing apparatus will last, and set your turnaround time at no more than seventy percent of that. Know where your transport will be — on the plateau, at the rim, in the lower country — and how to get back to it. Mark your route on the way in with small flags or with cairns of your own, set well away from the placed stones so as not to confuse the record. When you leave, take the markers with you if you can. If you cannot, leave them; they will outlast you anyway, and the place will absorb them into itself the way it absorbs everything.

Take nothing from the plateau. Not a stone, not a pinch of salt, not a thread of the pale vapor in a sealed jar. Anything you take will be wrong in your home atmosphere — the salt will change, the stone will cool and crack, the vapor will dissipate — and the taking itself is a small violence against a place that has not finished becoming itself. The only things worth taking are the ones your senses can carry, and even those will fade.

## A Last Word

The plateau is a draft. It is a thought being thought very slowly by something that is mostly asleep. You will not meet the thinker. You will not be introduced. You will stand for a short while inside the thought, and the thought will continue without you, and you will leave, and the thought will not notice.

This is not a sad thing. It is the only kind of thing the place can offer, and it is, in its way, generous. You are being allowed to stand inside a process that has been running for longer than you can imagine and will run for longer than you can stay. You will see the salt in the bowls. You will feel the warm stone under your boots. You will hear, if you are lucky, the low upholstered sound of the ground considering itself, and you will see a thin crack open in the dust a few hundred yards away, and a thread of vapor rise from it, and the smell of sulfur will come faintly across the long warm dimness, and you will know that the place is still becoming what it is becoming, and that you were there for a moment, and that it was enough.

Prepare accordingly. Bring less than you think you need and more than you can carry. Move slowly. Listen. Do not touch what was set before you. Do not try to be remembered. The plateau will outlast the memory.
