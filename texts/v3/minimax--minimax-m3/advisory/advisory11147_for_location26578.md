# Preparing for the Boiler Vault of Orris

## Before You Go

**Heat acclimation is the single most important thing.** The vault is not burning — there is no flame, no radiant danger — but it is *held* heat, the slow accumulation of geothermal warmth in concrete and metal over what looks like decades. Wet heat is worse than dry heat. Sweat will not evaporate efficiently. Your cooling system will fight and lose. Spend at least a few days beforehand in humid heat if you can — a sauna, a hot bath, a tropical climate. Arrive already knowing how your body behaves when it cannot dump heat. Bring water and electrolyte salts for the descent. Plan your time inside in hard blocks — most people can tolerate a sealed hot-wet environment like this for perhaps an hour before judgment and judgment before coordination start to slip.

**Get your tetanus current.** This place is a rust museum. The boiler weeps long dried-blood streaks; the pump is half-consumed; everything metal is shedding oxide. Verdigris on the stair railing is not actively dangerous to touch, but anything you might grab, lean on, or stumble into will give way to oxidized metal if you put weight on it carelessly. A cut here is a cut plus tetanus risk plus a hard-to-clean wound. Wear gloves if you plan to touch anything. Wear long sleeves and pants in any case, both for the rust and because the air feels heavier on bare skin than you expect — the warmth and humidity together read almost as a physical texture against your arms.

**Pack a real light source.** The amber glow at the far end is enough to see the room's general shape from the stairs and nothing more. It is not directional. It will not illuminate the floor where you walk, the underside of the boiler where the stalactite of mineral is forming, the brass gauge near the base of the steps. A headlamp leaves both hands free and is ideal. Bring a backup. Bring spare batteries. The damp will shorten battery life. The sourceless quality of the light inside the room means your eyes will never quite adjust the way they would in starlight — there is no contrast to work with, only a uniform smear, and you will find yourself leaning forward and squinting in a way that gives you a headache by the second hour.

**Bring a gas monitor if you have one.** The smell of the place — warm iron, wet copper, sweet vegetal rot, sulfurous breath — is not just atmosphere. There is geothermal activity under the floor. The drumming you feel through your boots is real. Hydrogen sulfide at low concentration smells exactly like the clean sulfur note in this room. Carbon dioxide, being heavier than air, can pool in the low channels and seams of the floor. Methane can collect in pockets. None of this is likely to be at dangerous concentration in a vault this size, but a four-gas detector is cheap insurance, and the moment it ticks you out, you leave. Without a monitor, pay attention to symptoms: headache, dizziness, confusion, sudden fatigue. Any of those and you walk up the stairs.

**Do not go alone.** Standard for any sealed underground space. Bring someone who can climb back up the stairs and call for help if you cannot.

## The Approach

The sinkhole is sharp. Not metaphorically sharp — the lip is freshly fractured tufa, the kind of edge that will cut clothing and skin. Approach it from a side where you can see the lip clearly, not from above with your weight over it. The triangular mouth is wider than the stair beneath it; you will need to lower yourself onto the top step from a stable position. The verdigris on the handrail is slick. Use the rail but test your grip before committing weight.

The stairs descend into heat the way a diving board descends into water — you feel the temperature change in layers. First the outside air against your face. Then the held warmth as the walls close in. Then the full damp weight of the vault hitting your chest as you step off the last stair. Take a moment there. Let your breathing settle. The room is not going anywhere.

## Inside

**Move slowly.** This is a place that punishes impatience without seeming to. The floor sweats. Condensation threads trace paths you cannot see until you stand over them. The moss around the pump base is deep and even and looks manufactured; it will take your boot print and hold it. The concrete is uneven in places where the rust stains have flaked the surface. Slipping here means falling onto oxidized metal or into the embrace of a fern crown that has no intention of catching you.

**Do not touch the machines more than you must.** They look like they could bear weight, but the boiler is weeping rust through every riveted seam, and the long vertical stains tell you the metal is thinner than it was. The pump flywheel is cradled in moss that has been growing into the bearing for what looks like decades; the moss is structural now, the only thing keeping it from settling further. The valve wheel on the black tank is a column of vine — turn it and you will not turn a valve, you will tear a plant apart, and you will not learn anything useful about what is on the other side of the valve, because the rest of the system is silent and dead. Read these machines the way you would read a sleeping animal. Look. Do not poke.

**Drink water, but not the dripping water.** The condensation is essentially distilled, but it has run through rust, mineral crust, and possibly sulfurous deposits on its way down. It will taste of iron. It will likely not poison you in a sip, but it is not the same water you brought, and your body is already working hard to regulate temperature. Use the clean water you brought. Do not eat in here.

**Watch the gauge.** The brass gauge near the base of the stairs reads zero, and zero is the right answer. If it ever reads otherwise, something has changed, and you should leave. The feather beside it is unexplained. There is no good explanation for the feather. Do not let the feather become the center of your visit. It is an object on a floor. It is also, possibly, a reminder that this place has rules you do not fully understand. Accept that and move on.

## How Long to Stay

For a first visit, sixty minutes is generous. The heat compounds. By the forty-minute mark, your reactions are slower than you think they are. By sixty, the room feels smaller — this is not claustrophobia, it is heat fatigue compressing your attention. Set a time before you enter. Leave when it comes, not when you feel like leaving. The room will be here when you come back. That is the entire character of the place: it has been here, accumulating, and it will continue to accumulate, with or without your watching.

## After

When you climb back up the stairs into cooler air, you will feel the temperature gradient as a kind of relief and a kind of loss. Stand at the lip for a moment. The contrast is part of the visit. Then keep moving — keep drinking water, keep cooling down, do not sit in your car with the windows up and fall asleep. Eat something with salt. If you have any rust cuts, wash them thoroughly; if you have any respiratory symptoms that night or the next day, mention to a doctor that you were in a sealed underground space with geothermal sulfur.

## What This Place Will Ask of You

The vault is patient. It will not perform for you. There will be no event. A drop will fall. A thread of rust will not lengthen while you watch. A fern will not unfurl. The place asks you to sit with the timescale of stone and oxidation and slow plant growth, and to find that bearable rather than frustrating. If you can do that, the visit will be among the more remarkable you have taken across any number of worlds. If you cannot, you will spend an hour getting anxious about why nothing is happening, and you will leave irritated, and you will have missed the room entirely.

The slow dynamics are the place. Bring nothing to do. Bring less than you think you need. Stand still and let the heat and the pulse and the silence do their work.
