# Preparing for The Long Valley

A traveler going to the Long Valley is not going somewhere they will conquer, or even somewhere they will fully understand. They are going somewhere that will be aware of them. The first preparation is to accept this before you pack.

## Before you go

**Leave your reasons at home, or at least loosen your grip on them.** Not because the valley refuses curiosity — it doesn't. But because a person who arrives with a project is already a person who is, in some quiet way, preparing to take. The valley will know this. You will feel it in the first ten minutes as a kind of coolness in the air that has nothing to do with temperature. Go with the intention to be somewhere, not to do something to it.

**Tell someone where you are going, and give them a deadline by which they should expect you back.** Say it out loud. Write it down. The valley makes the day feel slightly longer at its edges, and people who have stayed a year came back saying the year was a year and meaning it, and unable to say more. A deadline is a small anchor you throw into your own future. The valley does not forbid you having one. It may, gently, make it harder to honor than you expect.

**Decide in advance how long you will stay, and pick a number that is short.** A few days is usually fine. Most people come back from a few days. Most people who lost themselves lost themselves over months. This does not mean the few-day trip is safe in the way a city is safe — it is not — but it means the curve is gentle at the short end and steep at the long end. Stay on the gentle part.

**Do not promise yourself you will not be changed.** You will be changed. A knife left in the grass in the valley keeps its edge longer than it should. You are more complicated than a knife. The change is usually small, and usually not unpleasant, and usually not visible from the outside. But it is there. The person who comes back is not, by every measure, the person who went.

## What to bring

**Warm things. Real warm things.** The valley is about three degrees cooler than its surroundings, year-round, and the coolness has a quality — it is the kind of coolness that wool understands and polyester does not. Bring wool, bring cotton, bring leather. Bring a coat you would trust in late autumn. The damp will find any gap in your insulation. Bring two of everything warm, because one of everything will be damp by the second day.

**Boots that go up to your calf, and that you do not mind getting permanently wet.** The floor is peat and silty clay over fractured shale. The water comes up through the shale in a hundred small weeping seams. You will walk through standing water in places. Your feet will never quite dry out. Accept this now.

**A bedroll and a ground cover.** There is no shelter in the valley. You will be on the ground. The peat is warmer than the air in some places — you will feel it as you settle in, a faint warmth coming up against you. A bedroll keeps the warmth with you instead of letting it go.

**Food. Enough for your stay, plus two days.** Almost nothing with a backbone lives in the valley. There is no hunting. The plants are not food in any sense you should trust. The rule of the place includes a quiet prohibition on taking what is not offered, and the plants, though they will not refuse you, will not feed you either. Bring what you need from outside.

**Water, sealed.** The water in the seams is the valley's. You can wade it, you can wash in it, you can listen to it. Whether you can drink it is not something anyone has written a paper about, because the people who study this valley have other things to write papers about. Bring your own.

**A notebook and a soft pencil.** A notebook is not a tool that alters what it touches. It records what it sees, which is not the same thing. The valley has no objection to being remembered. The pale flower will close when you look at it, but it will close whether you have a notebook or not — the closing is about looking, not about writing. Bring the notebook. You will want it. You will not, afterward, be able to read all of what you wrote, but you will want it.

**A light source, but a small one.** The valley has its own light — a soft pervasive glow, a little brighter than overcast, a little cooler than moonlight, from no direction you can point to. A headlamp is more than you need. A small handheld light is enough, and you should use it sparingly, because the valley's shadows under things are not as dark as they should be, and adding more light will make you trust your own vision less. Bring it for emergencies. The dark is not dangerous in this place. The dark is part of how the place is.

## What not to bring

**No watch. No compass. No clock.** A watch brought in from elsewhere will begin to run slow within an hour and will continue to run slow for as long as it remains. A compass needle held above the basin will point, gently, toward the center of the long valley, as if north were a suggestion. The valley is not lost. You are. Accept this. If you need to know how long you have been somewhere, you will have to feel it, and the feeling will be slightly off, and the slight off-ness is the valley being accurate about something your watch cannot measure.

**No tools meant to change what they touch.** No knives for cutting samples. No trowels. No pruners. No flagging tape. No stakes. No markers of any kind. No instruments designed to extract, even politely. The valley does not need you to take a piece of it to prove you were there. It already knows you were there. You will feel this. The coolness in the air, again — not temperature. The sense that something is attending.

**No camera with a flash.** A camera that records light is a tool that captures, not alters, but a flash is a tool that imposes light onto a place that has its own light, and the valley will not object, exactly, but you will see, on the developed image or the screen, something that does not match what was in front of you. Bring a camera if you want to remember the look of the place. Do not bring a flash.

**No synthetic fabrics you care about.** Synthetics behave strangely in places where the physics is being politely suggested rather than enforced. This is not a rule. It is an observation from people who have visited. Wool and cotton come home the way they left.

**No desire to leave quickly.** This is the hardest one. The valley will, over hours and days, begin to make the leaving feel less urgent than the staying. Do not fight this. Do not pretend you do not feel it. But do not, on the first day, decide you will stay forever. Decide on a small, definite number of days before you feel it. Stick to the number.

## The first hour

When you enter, the first thing you will hear is the water. A hundred small weeping seams through the shale, and the sound of those seams is constant and does not stop. It will take about ten minutes for your brain to stop treating it as a thing to listen to and start treating it as the background. After that, you will notice the second thing: a long, low, almost-musical tone under the water-sound, rising and falling but never quite stopping. This is the valley. This is also, partly, your inner ear, which is making the tone out of information it is not sure how to label. Let yourself hear it.

In the first hour, you will probably also notice:

- The light. Dim, slate-gray, even. The shadows under things are not as dark as they should be. A fern casts a shadow that looks more like a suggestion of a fern.
- The plants. Dense. Every cubic foot accounted for. The ferns uncurl in slow spirals. There is a kind of reed with hollow stems that rings in the wind, and there is so much of it that on a still day the whole valley hums.
- The absence of animals. Almost nothing moves that has a backbone. You will hear a snipe. You will not see it.
- The small pale flower. Almost white. It opens when you are not looking at it. It closes — visibly, with a small motion — when you turn your head back toward it. This is real. This is not a trick of perception. The people who have come to study this have written papers about it. The papers are slightly different from each other in ways that are hard to account for.

Do not chase the flower. Do not try to catch it open. Just notice it. The valley is not a place to chase things in.

## How to behave

**Quietly.** Not silent — you cannot be silent, and the valley does not ask it. But quietly. Move slowly. Sit down often. Let the place come to your attention rather than chasing your attention through it.

**Without tools in your hands.** Even a notebook can feel like a tool. Open it on your lap. Write with the pencil held loosely. Do not write fast. Do not write as if you are trying to get something down before it escapes.

**Without marking anything.** No broken stems. No bent ferns to show the way back. No piles of stones to mark where you have been. The valley will remember you. You do not need to help it.

**With attention to the rule.** The valley is not a place to take from. You may come. You may look. You may sit. You may put your hand into the peat and feel the warmth come up against your palm. You may stand at the basin where the river-that-is-not-a-river goes down, and feel the long tone in the soles of your boots. You may, if you are quiet enough, hear the green think. But you must not alter. You must not take a sample not offered. The valley has always known. It was old when the hills were young.

## What may happen, and what to do

**You may hear your own thoughts in a voice that is not yours.** This is common. It is not dangerous. It is not a sign that you have lost your mind. It is a sign that the long tone is doing something to the way your inner ear is processing the silence under the sound. If it happens, sit down. Do not argue with the voice. Do not obey it either. It is your thought, in a slightly unfamiliar costume. It will pass.

**You may see a shape in the mist that is not quite an animal and not quite a person and not quite a fold of the land.** This is the valley remembering. It will stay for a few seconds and then be gone. The reeds will ring at a slightly higher pitch for a while after. The peat will give back a warmth. Do not try to follow it. Do not try to photograph it. You will not catch it. It was not there for you. It was there for itself.

**You may feel the day is longer than it was.** This is the edges of time getting slightly soft. It is not a problem. The day is still a day. You are still you.

**You may, at the basin where the water goes down, feel something that is not quite vibration and not quite sound.** Stand there. Let it come into your feet. Do not lean forward. Do not try to make it mean something. It is doing what the valley does. It is not a message. It is a place.

**You may lose the desire to leave.** This is the warning. If you feel this, check the deadline you set with the person expecting you. Look at the number of days. If you are still well within it, sit with the feeling. It is not hostile. It is the valley being kind to you in a way that is hard to refuse. If you are at the edge of the deadline, or past it, stand up. Pack your bedroll. Walk toward the higher ground. The feeling will stay with you for a while after you leave. That is fine. That is the shape of the visit, from the outside. The shape from the inside is also fine. Both are true.

## When you come home

You will come home slightly changed. Not in a way that anyone else will notice, probably. Not in a way you can name. The knife you left in the grass will keep its edge longer than it should — if you brought one, which you shouldn't have. A watch you carried back will keep correct time for about a day and then begin to drift. You will catch yourself, sometimes, in a small act of attention — looking at a thing you would not have noticed before, hearing a tone under another sound — and you will know where that came from.

The valley will be there. It will be there after the hills that hold it are no longer hills, in their own long settling, in their own cool dim wet. It does not need you to come back. It will remember that you came.

The sound of water in the seams does not stop.

The light does not brighten.

The tone continues.
