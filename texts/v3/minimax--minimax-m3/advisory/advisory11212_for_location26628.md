# Preparing for the Manufactured Plain

The Manufactured Plain is not dangerous in the ways most worlds are dangerous. There are no predators, no weather that kills, no terrain that falls away. The thing you are preparing for is not a threat. It is a condition. The condition is total stasis, held at continental scale, dressed in warmth and dampness and a low industrial heartbeat. Anyone who tells you this place is easy is lying to you or has not yet noticed what the sameness does to a person over days. Read what follows and take it seriously.

---

## The thing you must understand before packing anything

You will not experience this place as a location. You will experience it as a single room that never ends, kept at one temperature, one humidity, one pressure of background noise, one density of small life, for as far as you walk in any direction. The horizon bends, but it does not change. The seams run to the vanishing point, but every seam is like the last seam. The water channels carry the same dark current. The fog forms at the same height. The wind blows from the same direction at the same speed.

Your body will acclimate in the first hour. Your mind will not acclimate in the first month. Pack for your mind first.

---

## Clothing and what touches your skin

The dampness is uniform. It does not increase or decrease. You will never feel soaked, and you will never feel dry. Your skin will register a constant presence of moisture on it, like the inside of a greenhouse, and you will eventually stop noticing this — which is its own problem, because once you stop noticing it, you also stop noticing when something is wrong. A blister going soft. A cut going warm. A patch of skin going odd. Bring a small light and look at yourself once a day.

The fauna covers every surface. You will be walking on a moving carpet one or two creatures thick. You will lean against housings and feel them shift under your weight. You will sit down and have things rearrange themselves around your shape. The creatures are not described as hostile, and the consensus from previous travelers is that they are not aggressive, but they are present on your body the moment you become a surface. They will be in your cuffs, in your collar, in your boots, in the folds of any fabric you wear. The small ones you cannot resolve will be in your eyelashes.

This means: **minimize architecture on your body.** No loose cuffs. No flapped pockets. No pleated trousers. No fabric with a nap they can grip. Wear smooth, sealed, low-friction clothing — treated synthetics, silicone-coated weaves, the kind of material rain shells are made of, but lined for warmth because the air is warm and you will sweat into it. The interior of your clothing will become a small Manufactured Plain of its own. Plan for it.

Boots: smooth-soled, sealed, no laces if you can manage it. The laces will become habitat. The seams of the boots will become habitat. Whatever you wear on your feet will be in continuous contact with packed fauna, and the fauna will be in continuous contact with whatever you put down. Hard soles also help with the heat radiating up from the substrate — in places the air shimmers and the floor is hot enough to feel through a thin shoe.

Head: a brimmed hat or a sealed hood. The fog band sits at about a meter, and you will walk through it constantly. Your face will be wet. Your eyelashes will host fauna. Consider a fine mesh veil that breathes — not to keep creatures out entirely (you cannot) but to keep the larger ones off your lips and the small ones out of your nostrils. Earplugs that vent — foam that admits some sound but softens the rumble.

Gloves. Sealed, thin, grippy. You will touch everything through them.

## Breathing the air

The air is warm, humid, and tastes of warm mineral and faint polymer. It is not described as toxic, but it is described as consistent, and consistent in this place means you will breathe the same particulate load for the entire visit. Your lungs will adapt. People with respiratory sensitivity should bring their usual medication and consider a half-face respirator with particulate filters for the first day or two — not because the air is bad, but because the polymer taste can trigger a reactive cough in some visitors that does not stop for hours.

The constant humidity also means anything you carry that breathes (gore-tex membranes, acoustic mesh, ventilation paths on electronics) will do so less efficiently over time. Salt crusts will form on metal as moisture cycles through. Anything you do not want colonized by small fauna — keep it sealed.

## The sound and what to do about it

There are three sound layers, all of them constant.

The lowest is the rumble of the machinery. Felt more than heard. The way a river under a bridge is felt. It does not stop. It does not vary. After two or three days most travelers stop registering it consciously, which is useful, because the alternative is to live inside a sound forever.

The middle layer is the hiss of the fauna. Soft. Continuous. A sleeve against itself. A stack of paper breathed across. This is the sound of uncountable small bodies moving. It will not rise above the rumble. It will not fall below a hiss. It will not change loudness. Do not try to drown it out with headphones at high volume — your ears are doing useful work filtering it, and if you fatigue them early, the later days are worse.

The top layer is the chirp. Single. Interval. Long. The largest fauna produces it. It is not communication you can interpret. It is not a warning system. It is a metronome. Some travelers use it as a kind of pulse, a way of noticing that time is passing even when the place refuses to give any other signal. Note its interval when you arrive. It will be the same interval when you leave.

Bring earplugs for sleep — soft foam, not solid — that attenuate the rumble without erasing the chirp.

## What you eat and drink

Bring everything. Bring more than you think. Bring it in sealed packaging.

The water on the surface is everywhere and none of it is safe to drink untreated. It is dark with whatever it has carried out of the substrate, and the substrate is machinery. It is not necessarily poison, but the travelers who have tested it report mineral profiles that no human kidney has a protocol for. Carry a serious filter — not a hiking straw, a real one, with replaceable cartridges — and even then, boil or distill if you can. The easiest approach is to bring your own water for the entire trip.

Food: dense, sealed, high-calorie. The place is warm and you will be walking on damp substrate and your body will be working harder than you think to maintain temperature and to ignore the constant micro-inputs of being touched. Jerky, bars, nuts in tins, dried fruit in foil, the kind of food that does not care about humidity. **Do not eat the fauna.** They eat the outputs of the machinery and they eat each other, which means their tissues are concentrating whatever the closed loop contains. Previous travelers who tried this had gastrointestinal effects that lasted weeks after returning home, and one reported a persistent mineral taste in the mouth for months. They are not food for you. They are part of the system.

---

## The fauna, considered as an environment

They are small. Mostly small. The largest you will see is the size of a thumb. They are quiet, packed, and not aggressive. They are also on every surface, in the air near the floor, in the water channels, climbing vertical faces. You will not avoid them. Treat them as a medium you are moving through, like water or sand.

Practical implications:

- **They will colonize anything still.** A pack left on the floor for an hour comes back covered. A pack left on the floor for a day comes back incorporated.
- **They go into orifices.** Eyes, ears, nose, mouth if you open it. The mesh veil helps. So do the vented earplugs. So does breathing through your nose with your lips closed, which sounds ridiculous until you are standing ankle-deep in thumb-sized creatures and they are all looking up.
- **They do not, so far as anyone has reported, bite through skin.** But they will grip exposed skin, and gripping many small things at once produces a sensation most travelers describe as "being lightly held everywhere." Some find this unbearable. Some find it soothing after a few days. The reactions are unpredictable.
- **They eat the machinery's outputs.** Do not assume they are inert. They are metabolically active. Treat them as living, even when they look like a coating.

---

## The machinery and what not to touch

It is everywhere, it is running, it is hot in places, it is electrified in places, and its markings are in a script that does not loop or curve but only brackets. You cannot read it. You cannot meaningfully interface with it. The script is not for you. The machinery is not for you. The machinery is for the system that is holding itself in equilibrium, and your presence in it is incidental.

Do not lean on junction blocks. Do not step on conduits. Do not place your sleeping bag on a heat shimmer. Do not poke at vents. Do not unscrew anything. The housings are not decorative and the equipment inside is not arranged for human curiosity, and the few travelers who have tried to interact with it directly have come back with burns, shocks, or in one case an unexplained paralysis in the hand that lasted the remainder of the trip. The system is closed. You are an outsider. Act like one.

The seam lines in the floor — the long, sharp, recent-looking joints — are not seams you can pry apart. Do not try.

## Navigation and the loss of landmarks

You will walk on a flat surface that looks identical in every direction. The seam lines provide a kind of grid if you learn to read them, but the grid is so vast that one seam looks much like another. The wind blows from a single direction at a single speed, which is at least a constant reference for orientation. The sun and the sky provide day and night, which is at least a temporal reference. The chirp provides an interval. The machinery has a density pattern — denser here, sparser there — which may give you structure if you map it as you walk.

Bring:

- A compass, sealed against the humidity.
- A GPS device, sealed, with the maps loaded for whatever entry point you arrived through. The plain may not appear on your home maps; load waypoints by hand.
- A method of marking your path that does not depend on the plain. Flour doesn't work in the damp. Small cairns work only if you carry the stones in. Tape, biodegradable, marked with your initials, stuck to the housings at intervals — this is what experienced travelers use.
- A way of measuring distance. Pedometer, odometer wheel, GPS track. The place resists measurement because nothing about it changes as you move through it.

Do not rely on visual landmarks. There are no landmarks.

## Time and the mind

This is the largest danger of the place, and it is not the damp or the creatures or the heat. It is the **sameness**.

Your brain is built to extract meaning from change. When nothing changes, your brain begins to invent change — pattern recognition firing on noise, time stretching, hours feeling like minutes, minutes feeling like hours. The first day is fine. The second day is interesting. The third day begins to fray. By the end of a week, travelers report a soft dissociation, a sense that they have been there forever and arrived yesterday, simultaneously. By two weeks the dissociation becomes pronounced, and by three weeks several visitors have had to be extracted because they had stopped being able to initiate movement.

Countermeasures:

- **Bring a watch.** A real one, with a face, set to your home time. Wear it always. When the place refuses to mark time, you mark it.
- **Keep a schedule.** Wake at a fixed hour. Eat at fixed hours. Walk for a fixed distance or a fixed number of hours. Stop and rest. Do not let the day blur. The plain will let the day blur. You must not.
- **Talk to someone.** Bring a communication device with a tether to home. Voice calls, ideally, with another human, daily. The place is silent in a way that has nothing to do with sound.
- **Write.** A journal. In ink that doesn't run in the damp. (Gel pens hold up. Fountain pens do not.) You will write the same observations over and over. That is fine. The act of writing is the point, not the content.
- **Set an exit date before you arrive.** A maximum stay. Stick to it. The place will not become more dangerous over time in any way you can measure. It will become more dangerous to your sense of self, and you will not notice it happening.

If you have a history of dissociation, anxiety, or mood disorders, consult someone you trust before going. The Manufactured Plain is not a place to find out what those conditions feel like under load.

## What to bring, briefly

- Smooth, sealed, low-friction clothing in layers, no architecture
- Sealed boots with smooth soles
- Brimmed hat or sealed hood
- Fine mesh face veil
- Vented earplugs and foam sleep plugs
- Sealed gloves
- Real water filter and a supply of water for at least your planned stay plus three days
- Dense, sealed, high-calorie food for planned stay plus three days
- Compass, sealed GPS, marked waypoints
- Watch set to home time
- Journal, gel pen
- Communication device with daily check-in
- A small light for self-inspection
- Basic first aid, including electrolyte packets
- A method of marking your path
- A firm exit date

## What to leave behind

- Expectations of variety
- The idea that you will "explore" in any meaningful sense
- The hope that something will change
- The plan to eat anything you find there
- Curiosity about the machinery beyond respectful distance
- Any schedule that depends on the place telling you when to stop

---

## A closing thought

The Manufactured Plain is not cruel. It does not want anything from you. It is finished. The builders finished it, the system came to equilibrium, and now the system simply is, the way a stone simply is, except that this stone is warm and damp and humming and covered with small quiet lives that have been doing the same things for longer than you can imagine.

You will walk there, and the surface ahead of you will be the surface behind you. You will see the same dark water, the same moving crust, the same sharp seam running to the same fog. You will breathe the same air. You will hear the same hiss. The chirp will come. The wind will move the fog. The pistons will cycle. And none of it will be different tomorrow.

The place will hold you the way it holds everything — gently, in dampness, in stasis, without change, without opinion, for as long as you stay and for as long as you leave and for as long as it continues to be the only thing it has ever been.

Bring what you need to remember that you are not part of its system. Leave before you forget.
