The first thing to know is that this place does not have a horizon. The fog meets the water meets the distant lower slopes, and your eyes will not find a place to rest. You will arrive confused. That is appropriate. The body expects a horizon and gets an amber wash; the feet expect ground and get a causeway that flexes like a living thing; the lungs expect air and get air, but warmer, wetter, and slightly copper. Everything is present. Nothing is in the right place.

This is a place that has been mid-sentence for decades. Bring patience. Bring an awareness of pace. Don't ask it to answer you. Let it speak in its own time.

---

**The body you bring.**

Clothing should be loose, layered, quick-drying, and modest. The locals wear layered cloth against the wet, and you should follow their lead; bare skin here is an invitation to insects and a small social error. Synthetics that wick will keep you warmer and drier than cotton, which stays wet for days. Long sleeves and trousers are not optional — without them you'll be eaten alive inside two hours, and the residents will mark you as someone who doesn't understand the place. A wide-brimmed hat keeps the fog off your face and the insects out of your hair. Keep one dry layer sealed in a waterproof bag for sleeping; the warm fog cools at night, and dampness compounds.

Footwear is the most important decision you make before you go. The streets are canals. The canals are streets. You will spend half your time on slick timber, a quarter on boat floors, and a quarter on half-eaten stone stairs. You want boots that come at least to mid-calf — knee-high is better — with soles that bite wet wood and slick stone. Rubber, vulcanized, fully waterproof. Not leather. Leather rots here in a season, and the smell will follow you home. Bring a second pair sealed in dry storage, because the first will soften, leak, or be lost to the silt. If you have room, waders. If you don't, learn to love wet feet.

The water is shallow — you can usually touch bottom from a boat — but it is opaque, warm, and silty. Anything you drop into it, you may not find again. Anything you cut yourself on in it, take seriously. The lower floors of the old city are dissolved or silted, but the edges are still there, sharp in places, soft in others. A small cut in this water can become a problem. Bring a way to clean and dress wounds.

---

**The senses.**

The light will work on you first. Even at midday it is amber, dim, copper-tinted. Your pupils will dilate and stay dilated. Bring a headlamp with a low red-light setting and use it sparingly — the locals have lanterns, and the smoke from those lanterns is part of the air. You don't want to add your own smoke to their sky. A small, well-sealed LED with a warm white beam and a red filter will let you walk their causeways without blinding anyone or filling the air with insect-attracting blue.

Smell: silt, vegetal rot, smoke, water, the green-copper tang of plants in heat. It is not unpleasant but it is constant. Your nose will adjust by the second day. Don't bring perfumes or strongly scented products. They will be wrong here, and the locals will notice.

Sound: the quiet is full. Voices are low. Paddles are muffled. The wind has nothing to catch. The loudest sounds you'll hear are the buildings giving way — a wet shudder, a slow collapse into the water, and then nothing. You will hear these and your body will tense. Don't tense. They are normal. They are the sound of the place becoming what it is becoming. Bring earplugs if the structural sounds will keep you awake, but mostly, learn to listen past them.

---

**The air and what lives in it.**

This is the most important practical preparation, and the easiest to underestimate. The prompt describes "an enormous hum." Believe it. You will be eaten if you do not prepare.

Bring DEET-based repellent at 30 percent or higher, or picaridin if you prefer something gentler on synthetics. Treat every piece of clothing you bring with permethrin before you go, or buy pre-treated. Sleep under a mosquito net — a small, lightweight one you can rig from any roofbeam. Carry calamine or hydrocortisone for bites. Bring a non-drowsy antihistamine; the species here will be unfamiliar to your immune system, and the swelling may be fiercer than you're used to.

Talk to a travel doctor before you go. This is, in every meaningful sense, a tropical environment, even when it looks like a drowned city. Bring broad-spectrum antimalarials if your doctor recommends them. Bring antifungals — the warmth and wet will find you. Bring a gut antibiotic for traveler's diarrhea and a stronger one for wound infections. Bring electrolyte powder. Bring water purification: a small filter and tablets as backup. The lake water is silty, warm, and full of organisms your gut has never met. The locals drink it because they are adapted; you are not. Their adaptations took generations.

Consider leeches. Warm silty water with vegetation means leeches. Tuck your trouser cuffs into your boot tops. Check your ankles often. A small amount of salt in a tin will discourage them, and salt is also a gift the locals will value.

---

**Moving through the place.**

You cannot walk here the way you walk in a city. You move in three modes: causeway, boat, and stair. Each has its etiquette.

Causeways are hand-hewn timber, lashed with rope, never pegged with iron. They flex. They bounce. They will not bear running. Walk softly, one foot in front of the other, hands free, weight centered. If a local offers to lead you across a narrow span, accept. They know which ones are sound, which ones only *sound* sound, and which ones are due to give way next winter.

Boats are flat wooden, propelled by poles. Stand up only when the boat is still and the poleman has nodded. Sit on the floorboards or a low bench. Don't lean on the gunwales — they are not reinforced like ours. If you bring your own boat, a small folding kayak or an inflatable is polite and unobtrusive; it will also let you move without depending on the locals' goodwill at every turn. If you don't bring one, theirs will carry you for a fish, a favor, a story, or simply an afternoon. Accept the ride. They like watching visitors try.

Stairs are partial. They climb two, three, four steps, then stop at the waterline. Below the waterline the stairs continue, but underwater, and you cannot see them. Don't grope for them. The locals carry a different map in their heads, and they will laugh or worry if you go feeling for treads in the silt. If you need to move from a lower rooftop to an upper one, ask. There's usually a ladder, a rope, an internal stair in a still-standing building. Trust the locals.

---

**The buildings.**

You will be tempted to explore. The ruins are irresistible — a stairwell with ferns growing out of the risers, a half-room with the floor dropped out and a fig tree growing up through where the dining table used to be. It is a beautiful ruin and a dangerous one.

The lower floors are gone. The upper floors are the new ground floors, but they were never designed to bear the loads they now bear, or to stay dry in the way they must stay dry. The lime-cut stone holds. The fired clay holds. The fibrous woven material — bring some home if you can identify it later; the locals weave it themselves and it is their secret — holds. The timber doesn't, for long. The thatch is going in places. The roof beams are soft in the middle.

Stay where the locals invite you. Don't climb a chimney to see the view unless a child shows you the safe route. Don't lean on a balustrade. Don't put your weight on a wall until you've watched a local lean on it first. The place gives way slowly, not violently, but it does give way. If you see a building shuddering, move laterally, away, toward open water or a known-sound rooftop. The local children know the buildings better than the adults; if a child crosses a causeway without looking, the causeway is sound. If a child walks around a building, the building is not.

---

**The people and how to meet them.**

The residents are dark-skinned and patient. They speak a language that, from a distance, sounds like water running over stones. Up close it sounds less like that and more like itself. They watch from windows and doorways and the prows of their boats, not because they are unfriendly, but because they have not decided what you are yet.

Bring small gifts. Salt, if you can find it in coarse flake — they have no easy source, and salt is precious here. Cloth, undyed linen or cotton. Glass beads, small, not ostentatious. Rope of natural fiber, because they are constantly re-lashing their causeways, and rope is a working currency. **Do not bring iron.** They have none, and there may be a reason — cultural, geological, historical — that iron is absent. Introducing it could be transformative in ways you cannot predict. Even a steel knife, a tin can, a paper clip — all carry iron. If you can travel with ceramic, bone, stone, or copper tools, you will fit better. If you can't, accept that you are a small disruption. Try not to be a large one.

Do not bring seeds unless they are specifically requested and known not to compete with the rooftop gardens. They are growing half their food on rooftops, and the balance of that ecosystem is delicate. A seed you carry in your pocket could become a weed that displaces a cultivar they've spent generations selecting.

Do not bring electronics that announce themselves. A discreet camera is one thing. A drone is another. The place has its own quiet; do not fill it with buzz.

Learn a few words before you go, or as many as you can. The names of: water, fish, rope, fire, thank you, goodbye, *please*, and the word they use for the place itself. The act of trying will be received warmly. The locals are few, and a stranger who tries to speak their language is a stranger with a future, in their eyes.

Do not try to fix anything. Do not try to save anyone. Do not try to develop, convert, electrify, or modernize. The place is mid-sentence. Do not interrupt the sentence.

---

**The mind, which is the hardest to pack.**

You will arrive expecting a place that has either survived or failed. The place has done neither. It is persisting in a way that does not yet have a verb. The architecture was built for ground. The plants were evolved for soil. The people were built for stable floors. All three of those assumptions are now false, and all three of those things are persisting anyway, in the manner of a sentence that continues after the verb has been crossed out.

Watching it, you will feel the groundlessness in your own body. You will want to ask the locals whether they are all right. They are all right. They are a kind of all-right that has no clean word in any language you bring with you. Sit with them on their rooftops. Eat their fish. Watch the fog with them. They will not be impressed by your sympathy. They will be quietly pleased by your company.

Bring a notebook. Bring a pencil — graphite, not metal-tipped. Bring time. Bring less than you think you need. The place will provide.

---

**The entry and the leaving.**

Mark where you arrive. The fog here swallows landmarks; a particular rooftop, a chimney, a tree, a leaning wall. Note it on arrival, on paper, in your own handwriting, because if you forget it you may not find your way back out. Decide how long you mean to stay before you go, because the place is slow and the slow will take you in. Set a return for yourself, and honor it. Multiverse travel rarely punishes those who linger; it punishes those who forget they were ever somewhere else.

When you leave, take yourself and your memories. Take nothing physical unless it is given. Some travelers say no souvenirs at all, and here that may be the right answer. The place is becoming. To take a piece of it is to interrupt the becoming.

Bring back the light. Bring back the sound of water over stones. Bring back the patience. Bring back nothing.

---

You will come home changed. The fog will stay with you. The slow shudder of a wall giving way will stay with you. The quiet will stay with you. You will find yourself, in your own city, listening for horizons, and finding none. You will find yourself pausing at thresholds, checking if the step is sound. You will find yourself speaking more softly. You will find yourself, perhaps, mid-sentence, and staying there.

Go well. Bring rope. Bring salt. Bring patience. Bring back nothing.
