# Preparation for the Underbelly

## The first thing to understand

You are going somewhere that was not built for you. There is no gift shop, no railing, no path that was cleared for a person. The place has been in its own arrangement for longer than human memory, and it will continue in that arrangement long after you leave. Your job is to *pass through* — to spend enough time there to have the experience and to come out the other side intact. You are not a conqueror. You are not even a guest. You are a small, soft, noisy visitor to a country that runs on a metabolism you can barely perceive, and your whole purpose is to disturb as little as possible while still seeing what you came to see.

Take that in before you read anything else. The rest of this is logistics.

---

## What to wear

**The single most important thing is to cover every inch of skin, and to do it in the right materials.**

The place is not hostile, but the residents are. They are not aggressive. They are indifferent. They live on you the way they live on the stone. A beetle will walk across your boot within the first ten minutes, because your boot is a new surface and they explore new surfaces. A bat may brush your face in the column, because the column is dense and you are inside it. A fish will investigate the disturbance of your ankles because that is what fish do with disturbances. The fungus will begin, within hours, to put a faint film on anything left still long enough. None of these mean you harm. All of them, in sufficient accumulation, can.

So: **a base layer of smooth, tight-woven synthetic against the skin.** Not cotton. Cotton holds damp, and damp against skin in this temperature is a slow unhappiness, and unhappiness on long trips becomes a problem. Synthetic wicks. Synthetic dries when you get out. Long sleeves, long pants, wrists and ankles cinched tight so nothing crawls in at the cuffs. A hood that closes around the face. Gloves with the fingers free, or with thin fingers, because you will need to feel things. This is the membrane between you and the rest of the place, and the membrane should be intact.

**Over the base layer: a layer you do not care about.** Something you can throw away when you come out, or at least wash in a strong solution and never wear to bed. The smell of the place — the milk-iron smell, the sweet-mineral smell of the fungus, the ammonia-and-musk of the bats — will get into everything you bring. It will get into fabric, into leather, into the stitching of your boots. It will fade eventually. It will fade faster from synthetics than from anything natural.

**On your feet: tall rubber boots, snug, that come at least to mid-calf.** You will be in and out of the water, and the water is not deep but the beetles are. The mat at the water's edge is a foot deep in places, and when you step into it you displace thousands of them, and the ones that are displaced go somewhere. Most go onto your boots. Some go into your boots. The boots keep the boundary. Walk slowly enough that you can feel the mat compress under you, and lift slowly enough that the displaced beetles have time to move rather than be crushed. You are not in a hurry. Nothing in this place is in a hurry. Match the pace.

**On your head:** the hood, and over it a soft helmet. Not a hard one — the ceiling is uneven and you will bump it in the low places, and a hard helmet will ring in the acoustics of the place and the ringing will not stop. A soft climbing helmet, or a padded beanie, or nothing if you can accept the bruises. You will hit your head. Plan on it. The cathedral has its eighty-foot reaches and the labyrinth has its ten-foot crawls, and the transitions are not announced.

**On your face, in the column:** a thin balaclava or buff, pulled up over the nose and mouth, breathable. The bats do not carry disease of any kind you recognize, but they carry dust, and the dust is bat, and you do not want to breathe a diet of bat for the hours you are inside. The buff also cuts the smell, which you will want, in the first hour, and you will want it less, after that, as the smell becomes the smell of being there.

**Jewelry: leave it.** Leave the watch, the ring, the earrings, the chain. The fungus will grow on metal. The beetles will investigate metal. A loose chain against a bat's wing in a column of a million bats is not a problem for the bat. It is a problem for you. Become smooth, and small, and sealed.

## What to carry

**Nothing you cannot afford to lose or ruin.** That is the rule.

You will need:

- **Two sources of light, at least one of which is not a flashlight.** The fungus gives a glow that is barely there. A flashlight is worse than useless in some ways — it ruins the dark-adapted eye, it spooks the fish, it draws the beetles toward you in a way that disturbs the mat. But you will need it for the moments when you need to see what you are stepping on, when you need to read a hand-drawn map of a passage, when you need to find the thing you dropped. So: a small, low-lumen, red-filtered headlamp as your primary. Red does less to the night vision of the place, though the place has no night vision, only your own. The second source: a small chemical light stick, or a second red lamp, in case the first fails. Lights die in damp.
- **A length of thin cord, thirty feet, brightly colored.** You will lose the way back. The river is the only line, and the river winds, and the dark makes all turns look the same. Mark your turn points as you go. A small piece of tape on a stone, a knot in the cord tied at the entrance of a side passage, anything you can feel in the dark. A human being with a map is still a human being in the dark.
- **Water and a way to drink it that does not require you to lift your face.** A camelback, a tube in the hood, something that lets you drink without tilting your head back and exposing the soft under-jaw to the column. You will be thirsty. The air is damp but it is not wet, and you will be exerting yourself in a way that does not feel like exertion.
- **Food that does not need to be chewed loudly.** The acoustics. Chewing sounds like a small private earthquake in the cathedral. Soft bars, paste in a tube, dried fruit, anything that goes down quiet. Eat when you stop, not when you walk.
- **A small container, sealed, for anything you pick up.** You will want to pick things up. A stone, a shed beetle shell, a fragment of stalactite, a thread of fungus. The place will not mind, mostly. But the things you pick up will, in the closed container, continue to be alive for a while, and a beetle in a closed bag with a piece of fungus is a small ecosystem that may or may not smell good by the time you get it home. Plan accordingly.
- **Nothing that makes noise you cannot turn off.** No crinkly wrappers, no hard zippers, no metal buckles, no plastic bags. Cloth tie-bags. Cotton cord, knotted, not clipped. Quietness is a courtesy. Quietness is also survival — a noise in the column makes the column shift, and a shifting column is a falling column, and a falling column of a million bats is the closest this place has to a warning.

## How to move

**Slowly.** This is the only rule that matters. The place does not reward speed. Speed is what you do in a place you are trying to leave. You are not trying to leave. You are trying to be in it.

**Watch the floor.** The beetles are the floor, in places. Step on the mat and it gives. Step on bare stone and it does not. Learn to tell the difference by feel — the mat is springy, the stone is hard, and the river-stone is the stone with a film of water on it. The transition between mat and stone is not always visible. The transition between stone and the deep water of a pool is sometimes not visible at all. Probe with the toe of the boot before you commit weight.

**Stay near the river.** The river is your line. The fungus glows brighter where the water pools, and the pools are the only places you can see more than a body-length ahead. The river leads somewhere. The river came from somewhere. The river goes to somewhere. You came down a crack in a dry riverbed; the river in the dark is the same water, going somewhere else, deeper, or back up, and it is the only landmark that will not lie to you.

**Do not touch the long thing.** You will not, in any case — it is in the deeper pools, and you are not going into the deeper pools. But know that it is there, and that it is the patient apex of a small and ancient food web, and that you are not part of that web. You are a visitor. The long thing has not seen a visitor before, or has not seen one in a long time, and either way it will not understand you as food. Stay in the shallows. Stay where the fish are small enough to see in the dim.

**Do not grab the bats.** You will want to. A bat that brushes your face, a bat that hangs low enough to reach, a bat that drops from the cluster to drink and passes within arm's length. Do not grab. The bat is fragile in ways that do not show. The wing membrane tears. A torn bat in a column of a million bats is a small thing to the column and a fatal thing to the bat. Let them pass. Let them brush you. The brush is the encounter. Take it as the whole of the experience.

**Sit, sometimes.** Find a stone, or a dry patch of columnar basalt above the high-water mark, and sit. The place continues without you. The river runs. The bats shift. The beetle mat moves in slow tides. The stalactite drips. The fungus does its imperceptible work. After a few minutes of sitting, your eyes will adjust further, and the dark will not be black but a deep purple-grey, and the fungus will resolve into detail, and the shapes in the water will become fish, and the shapes on the walls will become columns. The place is a slow instrument. You have to let it play.

## How to breathe

The air is breathable. It is not bad air. It is air that has been breathed by a million bats and is being breathed by them still, and the oxygen is fine, and the CO₂ is not yet a problem in the larger chambers, and the smell, once you stop fighting it, is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a place that is alive. Forest has a smell. The sea has a smell. The Underbelly has a smell. Stop noticing it after an hour.

But: **breathe through the nose as much as you can, and through the buff when you cannot.** The dust of bat, the spores of fungus, the small fragments of beetle shell that become airborne when the mat is disturbed — these are not dangerous in a short visit, and probably not in a long one, but they are present, and the nose filters better than the mouth, and the buff catches what the nose does not.

**And breathe slowly, and shallowly at first, and deeper as you adjust.** The acoustics of the place will pick up your breath and return it to you as a small wind, a faint puff, the place's own breathing mingled with yours. After a while you will not be able to tell your exhale from the bat-shifted air, and that is the right feeling. You are part of the weather for a moment. The weather does not mind.

## How to feel

This is the part the logistics cannot get to.

The place is going to be louder than you expect, and darker than you expect, and wetter than you expect, and stranger than you expect, and after the first hour, when the strangeness has become the new ordinary, you are going to feel a thing that does not have a name in most languages. It is not awe, exactly. It is not fear. It is the feeling of being a very small and very temporary thing inside a very large and very permanent thing, and the permanent thing is not hostile, is not even aware of you, is simply *there* in the way that mountains are there, and you are in it, and it is letting you be in it, and the letting is not a gift because gifts imply a giver, and there is no giver here, only the indifferent permission of a system that has been running without you for a very long time and will run without you again when you are gone.

You may want to cry. That is fine. The tears will be invisible in the damp on your face.

You may want to speak. Don't, unless you are sure someone is close enough to hear, and even then, whisper. The place is a cathedral of sound, and your voice will be received, and the bats will shift, and the shift is a small disturbance, and small disturbances add up. Save your voice for outside.

You may want to stay longer than you planned. The dark is comfortable, in its way. The damp is comfortable, in its way. The weather of sound is a kind of company, and after a while it becomes a kind of silence, the way a steady rain becomes silence, and you will not want to leave the silence for the loud dry bright world of the surface, where the sun is and the wind is and the things that move are not the things that move in the dark.

Leave anyway. Leave while you still want to stay. The place will not be different when you come back, because the place does not change. The river will still run. The bats will still shift. The long thing in the deep pool will still breathe. The fungus will have grown the width of a hair in the time you were gone, which is to say it will not have grown at all. The stalactite will have fallen one drop. The economy of the dark will be in balance, and you will have been a small bright disturbance in it, and the disturbance will have closed behind you, and the place will continue.

---

## A final word, before you go

Bring something to give back. Not a thing. A small effort of restoration. Pick up a piece of trash if you see it, even if it is not yours. Move a stone that has fallen across a beetle path back to the side. Let a bat that has landed on the ground near you climb onto your glove and launch itself back into the column, if it wants to. Touch a wall where the fungus has been scraped, so the spores on your glove have a chance to reseed. Small mercies. The place does not need them. The place is in balance without them. But you are a visitor, and visitors, in the old stories, leave a gift, and the gift is a small proof that they were there and that they were careful and that they understood they were in someone else's house.

The Underbelly is not someone's house. It is no one's house. But the gesture is the same. The gesture is the thing that makes you a guest rather than an intruder, and the difference matters more in the dark than it does in the light.

Go slowly. Go quietly. Go with sealed cuffs and a red lamp and a length of cord and a willingness to be small.

The crack in the dry riverbed will still be there when you come back.
