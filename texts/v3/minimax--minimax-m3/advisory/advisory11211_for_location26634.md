# Preparation Notes for the Damp Town

## The Big Picture, Before Anything Else

You are going somewhere that is not so much a place as a *negotiation*. Humans built here once. Plants won. Then a smaller number of humans came back, on the plants' terms. Your job is to be the kind of visitor who can hear what the terms are without making a fuss about them. The bog is the senior partner in every transaction. The brick is a memory. The moss is the present tense.

Most visitors make the mistake of trying to "see" this place the way they would see a city — landmarks, points of interest, a route. There is no route. The town is the route. Walk it twice and you have seen it. Walk it a third time and you have started to be in it. Plan accordingly.

---

## What to Bring (and What to Leave Behind)

### Clothing — the most important thing you will pack

This is a wet place. Not raining-wet. *Saturated*-wet. The moisture is in the air, in the brick, in the moss, in your cuffs within an hour regardless of whether it has rained. You are not going to dry out. Stop trying.

- **Wool.** Wool is the only fabric that matters here. It will absorb up to a third of its weight in water and still keep you warm. Cotton will become a cold, heavy, clammy second skin and will not forgive you. Synthetics will hold the damp against you and quietly grow something. Bring more wool than you think you need. Bring wool you do not like, because you may not like it by the end either.
- **Layers, but not too many.** You will be moving through doorways, up narrow stairs, possibly onto roofs to follow a sightline. Bulky clothing is a liability. A wool base, a wool mid, a waxed or oiled outer that breathes. That is enough.
- **Footwear with a serious sole.** The ground gives. Sometimes half an inch, sometimes more. You will feel the bog under you through your feet. A thin shoe will let the cold of the peat into your bones. A thick-soled leather boot, broken in but not worn out, with a wool sock inside it, is what you want. The soles of your boots will turn green within two days. This is correct. This is, in fact, a sign of respect.
- **A hat with a brim.** Not for sun — there is almost no sun. For water running off eaves, for the occasional drip from a fern above a doorway, for the small constant mist that will sit on your face and slowly find its way down your collar.
- **No bright colors.** The palette here is green, gray, wet brown, the silver of weathered wood, and the occasional warm note of ochre or faded red. You will look like a flag if you wear anything vivid. The locals do not object to outsiders, but they notice them, and they prefer not to. Wear something the color of moss or wet bark or old brick. A russet, a lichen green, a deep dull yellow. You will be less visible, and being less visible is the first step toward being comfortable.

### Equipment

- **A knife.** Not a big one. A small, sharp, good one. You will want to cut peat samples, peel bark, open things, score a mark on a brick to remember a doorway by. The town is full of soft, damp, fibrous things that want to be cut.
- **A waxed notebook and a pencil.** Pens will skip and weep. The paper will curl. Pencil is the correct technology here, as it has been for two hundred years.
- **A vessel for water.** Not for drinking — do not drink the water — but for catching drippings, for collecting runoff from a particular roof, for filling from the black stream to see what it leaves behind when it evaporates on a windowsill.
- **A length of cloth, maybe two.** Wool, linen, anything absorbent. Useful for wrapping things, drying things, kneeling on, putting over your shoulder when you crouch in a doorway to watch the moths. The damp will give you reasons.
- **A small mirror.** Not for vanity. For looking at corners, under eaves, behind a door. You will want to see what is behind you without turning around, and you will want to check the small high places where something interesting may be growing.
- **A lantern, but a gentle one.** Oil, not electric. The moths come to lit windows at night, and they will come to your lantern, and they will tell you what is in the air. Also, the dark here is not ordinary dark. It is the dark of a place full of soft surfaces that absorb light. Bring more light than you think you need, but use it sparingly.
- **No camera you cannot get wet, or do not love.** The damp will find it. The moss will find it. If you must document, do it in pencil in the notebook.

### What to leave behind

- Watches, mostly. The pace here is not yours. A watch will make you anxious in a place where anxiety is the only thing the bog cannot use.
- Strong perfume or scented soap. The place has its own smell — peat, wet wood, the faint turpentine of the conifer resins, the iron-and-tannin smell of the black water. Your smell will fight it, and you will lose.
- Schedules. You have a week. That is the unit. Do not divide it into smaller units.

## The Body

You need to be able to walk on ground that is not quite solid. You need to be able to stand still for long periods without your feet complaining, because much of what is worth seeing here happens to a person who has stopped moving. You need to be able to crouch, comfortably, in a damp doorway, for twenty minutes, and then stand up without making a sound.

If your knees or your back have objections, address them before you arrive. There is not a chair in this town that is comfortable for an outsider. The seating, where it exists, is the wrong height, made of the wrong material, in rooms with the wrong acoustics. You will stand or you will sit on the ground. The ground will be cold and damp and will give under you.

Your lungs will be fine. The air is clean in a way most air is not. It is full of water and plant volatiles and the slow exhalations of peat. If you have any kind of seasonal breathing complaint, you may find it either vanishes or worsens; there is no predicting.

Your skin will be wet for the entire visit. Accept this early. The locals are not wet, exactly, but they are not dry either, and they have made a peace with it. You can see it in the way they touch each other — briefly, lightly, with the fingertips, as if contact were a small and considered transaction.

## The Mind, Which Is the More Difficult Country

The hardest part of this town is not the damp. It is the *pacing*.

Almost everything here is slow. A vine is always reaching, but it reaches at vine speed. A bubble surfaces in the peat field once an hour. The wheel turns on alternate days. The mist burns off the lower streets in the morning and returns by afternoon. The people speak as if they have all the time in the world, because, in the way that matters here, they do.

If you arrive at your usual speed, you will be exhausted by the second day and sick of the place by the fourth. You will start to invent things to do. You will begin to find the silence oppressive, or the damp oppressive, or the lack of variety oppressive. You will want to leave a day early. This is the town telling you it does not need you yet.

The cure is to slow down before you arrive. Begin practicing, on the way here, in the way you walk, the way you look at things, the way you let a sentence end without finishing it. The town will meet you partway if you show it you are trying.

You will also need to be comfortable with **soft categories**. The buildings are not exactly buildings. The people are not exactly people in the sense you mean. The moss is not exactly moss. The stillhouse is not exactly a stillhouse. Everything here has one foot in what it was and one foot in what it is becoming, and the locals live in the middle of that verb. You can either join them there or you can stand on the bank and be cold.

Bring a tolerance for not knowing what something is. Bring a willingness to be wrong about a thing three times before you are right, and then to discover you were not right after all.

## How to Behave

- **Move at the pace of the place.** Walk slowly. Stop often. When you stop, do not check your phone. Check the brick you are leaning against. Check the moss under your boot. Check the small birds that have started to ignore you.
- **Do not pick the moss.** The moss is doing work. The moss is the building. The moss is the main character. If you must touch it, touch it with the back of your hand, gently, and move on.
- **Do not enter a building with a pale doorway unless invited.** The pale doorways are the active ones — the ones still being used. The darker ones, where the moss has closed over the threshold, are private, in the way a ruin is private, which is to say private to the plants and to whatever the plants are keeping.
- **If someone offers you something from the stillhouse, accept it.** It will taste of resin and something darker, and it will warm you from the inside in a way the wool cannot. Do not ask what it is. The locals find the question tiresome and slightly insulting. Drink it slowly, the way they do.
- **Do not try to fix anything.** The wheel that turns on alternate days does not need to turn every day. The black stream in the curb is meant to run where it runs. The moss on the roof is not a problem to be solved. The chimney leans east because the chimney leans east.
- **Speak softly.** The acoustics here are odd. Sounds travel strangely — they seem to be absorbed by the moss and then released later, sometimes minutes later, from a different direction. You will hear your own voice come back to you from somewhere you did not expect. The locals speak softly partly out of habit and partly because they know this. You will learn to.
- **Do not photograph the heron.** The heron has been photographed enough. The heron is doing its work. Watch it with your eyes, which are a less invasive technology.

## The Sound You Will Hear Once

There is a sound in this town that only happens if you have stopped walking and have been still for long enough that the place has begun to forget you are there. It is a low sound, almost subsonic, somewhere between a breath and a creak, and it comes from the ground. It is the bog. It is the bog being the bog.

You will hear it on your third or fourth day, probably, in the late afternoon, in a doorway off the main street where the moss is thick and the light is the color of old paper. You will not be sure, at first, that you heard it. You will hold very still. You will hear it again. It will have a rhythm, the rhythm of something asleep and breathing well.

This is the sound the town is built around. Not the wheel, not the chimney, not the main street. This. Everything else is commentary.

When you hear it, do not speak. Do not move. Stand there for as long as the bog will let you. It will let you a long time, if you have been polite.

## What You Will Probably Get Wrong

- You will think the chimney is leaning because of subsidence. It is not. It is leaning because something wanted it to lean. You will not be told what.
- You will think the town is dying, or being reclaimed, or in decline. None of these words apply. The town is in a phase. The town has been in phases longer than you have been alive and will be in phases longer than you will be alive. Leave your narrative at the door with your bright colors.
- You will think the people are unhappy or stuck or unfortunate. They are not. They are *placed*. They have made a deal with the bog and they are living on their side of it. You will not be invited into the deal. You do not need to be.
- You will think the heron is a symbol. It is not a symbol. It is a heron.
- You will think, near the end, that you understand the place. You will be wrong, but you will be wrong in a way that has improved you, and that is the closest you will get.

## The Last Useful Thing

Bring something to leave behind. Not money — they do not use much. Not a gift exactly — the concept is slightly off. Something useful, made of a material that is not local. A piece of dry wood, perhaps. A metal thing that does not rust. A book in a language they do not speak. Something that will sit, for a few years, in a place where it does not belong, and then slowly become part of the town.

The town collects these things. You can see them, if you know where to look — in doorways, on windowsills, set into the moss of a wall like a keepsake. They are how the town remembers its visitors, since the visitors themselves are not usually remembered.

Leave yours in a doorway with a pale frame, where someone will find it and know what it is.

Then go. Slowly. The way you came.
