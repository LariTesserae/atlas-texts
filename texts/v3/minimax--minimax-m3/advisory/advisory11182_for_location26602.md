# Preparing for the Hothouse Quarter

## The First Realization: You Are Going to Be Wet, Constantly, and There Is Nothing You Can Do About It

Pack for humidity first and everything else second. This is not tropical-beach humidity. This is *airborne humidity that has weight*. Within a minute of stepping under that canopy your clothes will be a second skin, and you will not dry off indoors, outdoors, or anywhere in between, because the air itself is at saturation. Accept this before you arrive or you will be miserable. The travelers who do well here are the ones who stop fighting the wet around hour two and start treating it as a constant, like gravity.

What that means practically: every garment you bring should be something you would be willing to wear damp for the duration. Cotton is a mistake. Cotton becomes a sponge here, holds the wet against your skin, and will chafe you raw within a day. Linen is better — it stays cool and doesn't cling as viciously — but linen also doesn't insulate worth a damn when it is wet, which is always. The best fabrics are the ones that feel almost unpleasant when dry: dense merino against the skin (it manages moisture and resists odor, which matters more than you think), a light synthetic base layer if you have one, and an outer shell that is meant to keep rain *out*, because the rain here comes from above in drips and from every surface as condensation and from below as steam. Bring a real rain jacket with sealed seams. A poncho is not enough. The water will find its way under any cape or covering, especially in the steam vents where it rises from below.

Shoes: this matters more than any other single piece of gear. You will be on slick brick, on compost, on puddles that are permanent, on iron grates that vent steam. Bring footwear with serious grip. Leather soled shoes are nearly suicidal here — the moisture will soak them, soften them, and turn them into slip-hazards on the brick paths. Rubber-soled boots are the practical choice, ideally ones that come up to mid-calf. They will not be comfortable in the warmth, but comfort is not the goal. The goal is not falling. Acceptable compromise: trail-running shoes with aggressive tread if you don't want to commit to boots. You will be changing your socks twice a day minimum. Bring more socks than you think you need. Wool socks. Wool socks only. The locals will notice if you arrive in cotton socks; you will be pegged as a tourist within the hour.

Underwear: merino if you can get it. Synthetic if you cannot. Bring enough for daily changes.

## The Second Realization: Your Body Will Not Thermoregulate the Way It Is Used To

The Quarter is warm — seventy-five degrees, year-round, with humidity near one hundred percent. This is the temperature of a comfortable shower, but a shower you cannot leave. Your sweat will not evaporate. This is the central physical fact of being here, and it will take your body two or three days to figure out how to cope, and in the meantime you will feel vaguely unwell: headachy, tired, mildly nauseated, as though you are coming down with something. You are not. You are just losing your primary cooling mechanism.

Drink constantly. More than you think you need. The locals drink a faintly sulfurous mineral water from the geothermal source, and after a day or two you will too, because the alternative is the tannery-flavored well water and at least the spring water is consistent. Carry a water bottle. Refill it often. Add salt to your food — you will be losing minerals through sweat that doesn't dry. Do not bring electrolyte powders unless you genuinely like them; the mineral water has most of what you need and the locals will offer you tea, which is its own thing.

Eat lightly. Heavy meals are a bad idea in this heat. The Quarter has food — there are fungalists, after all, and a few stalls selling the cultivated mushrooms and dyed breads and a strange pale stew made of things I would not ask too closely about — but the appetite will not be what it is at home. Eat when you can. Eat small. Trust the tea more than the food for the first few days. The tea is some kind of herbal infusion, bitter, faintly sulfurous, served hot in small unglazed cups. It is exactly what your body wants even though your body doesn't know it yet.

Sleep will be hard at first. The Quarter does not get dark. There is no dusk. There is a slow dimming of the orange glow over four hours and then a slow brightening, and through the night the light is the color of a low fire. Bring a sleep mask. A good one, with a proper seal. Without it you will wake every hour convinced it is morning. With it you will sleep better than you expect, because the temperature is constant and the sound is constant and the bed will be the same temperature as the air, which means you will not be woken by chill or by heat, only by the light if you don't block it.

## The Third Realization: The Light Is Beautiful and Bad

The diffused orange glow under the canopy is one of the most beautiful things you will ever see. It is also terrible for reading, terrible for navigating, terrible for doing any close work. If you wear glasses, bring a second pair or at least a cleaning cloth, because the moisture will fog and spot your lenses constantly and you cannot solve this problem. Anti-fog treatments help. They do not solve. Accept it.

Bring a real flashlight, not a phone light. The cellars and the deep stratum and the workshop floors where the compost has accumulated are dark in a way that the orange ambient light cannot touch, and you will want to look down into the layers sometimes. A flashlight with a good beam will let you see the flagstones the color of bone that the original paving was made of. You will want to see those.

A small notebook that tolerates moisture. A pen that writes on damp paper. The atmosphere is so wet that standard ballpoints will smear and fountain pens will weep. A pencil is honestly the safest choice. You will want to record what you see because the Quarter rewards attention and you will forget details faster than you expect.

## The Fourth Realization: The Sound Is Going to Get Into You

The hiss and tick of the steam vents, the rhythmic clunk of the small engines, the layered wet rhythms of treadles and presses and whatever it is the tanners do — this will become the soundtrack of your entire visit. It does not stop. There is no quiet hour. There is no place in the Quarter where the sound does not reach.

Most travelers adapt within two days. The sound becomes a kind of blanket. After a week you will find that you cannot sleep without it, and this will be a problem when you go home.

Bring earplugs if you are a light sleeper, but understand that using them will cut you off from the place in a way that the locals will notice. The steamfitters in particular have a habit of calling up to each other between buildings in the evenings, and if you cannot hear them you will miss a layer of the Quarter that you cannot get any other way. The compromise is to wear the earplugs only at night, and to take them out in the morning before you go out, and to let the sound back in.

## The Fifth Realization: The Locals Are the Real Attraction

Three thousand people in a space for eight thousand. The Quarter is sparsely populated not because the buildings are empty but because the people who are here are here on purpose. They are tanners and paper-makers and fungalists and the steamfitter families who treat the geothermal system as a birthright. They are not unfriendly, but they are reserved in the specific way that people who live in a hard place are reserved toward outsiders: not with hostility, but with the polite patience of someone who knows you will leave and they will still be here.

Learn a few words of whatever language they speak. It will not be the language of the capital. The Hothouse Quarter is in a southern province, and the southern provinces have their own dialect, their own idioms, their own foods, their own sense of time. If you only speak the standard, you will be able to communicate, but you will be talking to people who are translating in their heads, and the translation will lose something.

The steamfitters are the closest thing the Quarter has to an old family, and they are the people to be respectful of above all. Their fathers piped the spring. Their grandfathers maintained the pipes during the period when the city was still trying to remove the plants. They are the reason the buildings are sound. If you want to understand how the Quarter actually works, you talk to a steamfitter. They will not volunteer information. You ask. You listen. You buy them a drink if they will take one — and they may not, because the drinking culture of the Quarter is its own thing, mostly a small beer drunk warm, mostly on Sundays, mostly in the one room above the main tannery that serves as an informal pub.

Do not photograph the tanneries without asking. Do not photograph the fungalists' cultivated beds without asking. Do not, under any circumstances, climb on the green mass to get a better angle for a photo. The plants will hold you. The plants will not let go. The locals will have to come cut you out, and this will be remembered.

## The Sixth Realization: Pace Yourself, or the Quarter Will Pace You

This is a place that runs on patience. The plants grow an inch a day. The buildings settle a millimeter a year. The pipes corrode slowly. The people age slowly. The Quarter does not accommodate rushing. If you arrive with an itinerary of seven major sights in three days, you will have a bad time and you will see nothing well.

Pick three things you want to see. Spend four days seeing them well. Walk the main artery from one end to the other and back, slowly, in the same direction, at the same time of day for two of those days, and notice what changes. The orange light shifts. The drips from the canopy come from different leaves on different days. The steam from a given grate varies with the underground pressure, which varies with the time of year, which varies almost imperceptibly because there are no seasons here, but there are seasons of pressure, and a steamfitter can tell you about them.

The courtyard with the treadle is in the description, and it is real, and you should go there, and you should stand there for an hour, and you should listen to the wet rhythmic sound of the man's foot on the pedal, and you should let the place do what it is going to do to you. The Quarter is not a place that is impressed by being seen. It is a place that is impressed by being *sat with*.

## Practical Matters

There is almost no crime in the Quarter, but there is almost no enforcement either. Keep your valuables dry. A waterproof pouch for documents and money is not optional; in this humidity, paper currency will stick together and become useless within a day if it is not protected. Bring small denominations. The local economy is cash and barter and favors.

There are no doctors in the Quarter. There is a herbalist, and she is good, and her remedies are mostly tinctures and teas that you would not recognize from home pharmacopeia. If you have a chronic condition, bring your own medication in larger quantities than you think you need, and bring it in a sealed container that moisture cannot penetrate. Pharmacies do not exist here. The nearest hospital is two districts away, outside the canopy, and getting there in an emergency means finding someone willing to drive you, which is not always possible.

There are no ATMs. There is a money-changer in the larger of the two ground-floor shops on the main artery, and he is reliable, and he will give you a fair rate, and he will also try to sell you a fungus that he claims will let you breathe underwater. Politely decline the fungus.

There are very few formal accommodations. The Quarter does not run on tourism. You will be staying with someone, or you will be staying in the room above the tannery that sometimes rents out to travelers, or you will be staying in a corner of someone's workshop that they have cleared for the purpose. None of these will be clean in any way you are used to. All of them will be dry, or at least drier than outside, because the locals have figured out how to keep their indoor spaces at a humidity that humans can tolerate. It involves heat. Bring sleepwear that is appropriate for a warm room.

## What to Bring, Concretely

- Two pairs of wool socks, rotating daily, plus two spare pairs. You will wear through them faster than you expect.
- Merino base layers, three sets.
- A real rain jacket with sealed seams.
- A sleep mask.
- A pencil and a moisture-resistant notebook.
- A real flashlight with spare batteries.
- A waterproof pouch for documents and money.
- Any prescription medication in sealed containers, with at least a week's extra supply.
- A small bottle of salt or electrolyte tablets.
- A water bottle.
- A pair of rubber-soled boots, broken in before you arrive.
- A second pair of glasses if you wear them, or a cloth and anti-fog spray.
- A sense of humor about being wet.

## What to Leave at Home

- Cotton anything. Especially cotton socks.
- Leather shoes.
- A tight schedule.
- Expectations of silence.
- Expectations of dryness.
- The belief that you will leave unchanged.

## A Final Note

The Quarter is not weird. The description is careful about this. There is no magic. There is no strangeness beyond the ordinary strangeness of a place that has been left to its own devices for a hundred and fifty years and has figured out a balance that does not require anyone's permission to continue. You will arrive expecting to be impressed or unsettled or dazzled. You will instead be wet, and warm, and damp in your shoes, and slowly, over the course of several days, you will stop noticing the wet because the wet is just where you are now. And then, on the fourth or fifth day, when you step out from under the canopy into the ordinary weather of the surrounding city — the dry air, the sharp shadows, the wind that actually moves — you will feel a kind of loss that you cannot quite name. The Quarter will have gotten into you the way its humidity gets into everything: completely, and without asking.

That is what you are preparing for. Not the sights. The getting-into.
