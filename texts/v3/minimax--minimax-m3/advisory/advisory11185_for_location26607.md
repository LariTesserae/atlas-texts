# A Letter to Myself Before Marshside

I want to begin with the thing the description keeps returning to, the thing you'll be most unprepared for even if you think you're prepared for it: the wetness is not weather. It is the medium. You are not visiting a wet place. You are visiting a place where the boundary between you and the water has been negotiated down to nothing. Plan for that, and the rest follows.

---

## First, the body

Do not bring cotton. Cotton is a lie here. It will absorb and it will stay and it will lie against your skin like a cold hand. Everything you wear will be damp within an hour of arriving, and it will stay damp for as long as you possess it, and on the trip home it will mildew in your trunk and ruin whatever else you packed with it. Bring linen, if you have it. Bring the loose-woven silks that some travelers favor. Bring nothing woolen — wool is a sponge and will never forgive you. Whatever you bring, assume it will be ruined. Bring things you are prepared to lose or leave behind. The town is hard on fabric the way rivers are hard on banks.

Footwear is the most important decision you will make. The cobbles are slick. The alleys flood. There is a permanent film of rendered fat and condensed moisture on every horizontal surface. You will fall, at least once, probably on your second day, probably in a place where no one helps you up because no one is looking. Bring boots — proper boots, with soles that grip when wet and uppers that do not pretend to be waterproof because waterproof in this place means your foot sits in its own bath. Leather will mold. Treat them with tallow or beeswax before you go, and again every few days while you're there, and accept that you may bring home boots that smell of the town for the rest of their useful life. If you can find the kind with a slight heel, that helps on the uneven cobbles. The streets are not level. Nothing here is level. Plan to walk like a sailor.

Your skin. The constant moisture and the held heat will do strange things. Anything chafing will become a wound. Anything folded will become a fungal invitation. Bring a small tin of tallow or rendered fat — animal fat, not the perfumed kind — and use it on the soft places: under the arms, the groin, between the toes. The town is rendered-fat country anyway; you will smell of the place no matter what you do, so you may as well smell of it on purpose. Bring a soft cloth for the sweat. Bring another soft cloth, because one will never dry.

The mosquitoes. Read that part of the description again. They are not a nuisance. They are the dominant herbivore. The herons come for them. You are also food. Bring a head net. I know how you feel about head nets. Bring one anyway. Bring the strongest repellent you can find, and bring more than you think you need, because you will use it twice a day and a tin lasts about a week. Long sleeves and long trousers, even in the heat, even when everyone else has rolled theirs up. The dockworkers roll theirs up. The dockworkers also accept the bites as a fact of working here. You do not have to. Cover yourself at dawn and dusk especially. The town empties at midday for a reason; the mosquitoes do not.

Sleep. You will not sleep well. The industrial hum becomes inaudible after a few days but it is there, and the dogs at night are not inaudible — the dogs at night are the loudest thing in this town and they will sing for hours, ward to ward, a long communal howling that does not stop because you ask it to. Bring ear-cloth. Wax if you have it. Or accept it. Most people who live there have accepted it; some say it's the only time the town feels alive. You will form an opinion of your own.

If you have any condition that involves the lungs, the heart, or the joints, decide whether you are coming before you come. The air is wet and carries iron-smell and sulfur-smell and the fine particular matter of an industrial settlement that has been burning coal and coke for a hundred years. The heat will tax a heart. The damp will find every old injury in your joints and remind you of them. This is not a place for the fragile. If you are fragile and still coming, bring your medicines, all of them, in double the quantity you think you'll need, and bring a written list of what you take and why, because if something goes wrong there is no apothecary that will speak your language in the way an apothecary should.

---

## Then, the senses

Smell is the hardest. The description lists it almost casually — iron and sulfur and rendered fat and horse and humanity and river-water and the sweet rot of a place where things do not dry out. That is not a list. That is the air, all the time, with seasonal variations you will come to know the way you know the rooms of a house. You cannot prepare for it. You can only resolve that you will not be the person who complains about it, because complaining about the smell of Marshside is like complaining about the wetness of a marsh. The marsh doesn't care. The town doesn't care. The dogs certainly don't care.

Bring a small bottle of something strongly scented — pine resin, camphor, clove oil — not to mask the smell, you cannot mask it, but to give yourself a thing to smell when the smell becomes too much. You will use it. You will use it in the alley behind your lodging, in the small hours, when the rendering works on the wind has come around to something particular and you need a moment that is yours. Bring it small. The town will smell of what it smells of.

Sound. The town hums. You will not notice the hum after a few days, the way you stop noticing the blood in your ears. The dogs you will notice every night. The foundry horns you will notice when they come, because they come without warning and they are loud. The voices are quiet. The voices are the quietest thing about this place, and the contrast will unsettle you at first, because you have been raised in places where people speak up. Here people do not speak up. Here people speak as if there is never quite enough breath, and there isn't. Match the volume. Do not shout. Do not ask people to repeat themselves three times; ask once, softly, and then watch their mouth. They will appreciate it.

Light. The light is muted. The haze is permanent. You will not get a sharp shadow here. You will not get the bright clear light of the places you are used to. This will, after a few days, begin to feel like the light is the color of the place — yellowish-grey, the color of old paper — and you will start to think in that light, which is to say you will start to think slowly, because the light is asking you to. If you need to do close work — read, write, mend — bring your own light. A small oil lamp, well-trimmed. The town provides its own dimness and will not notice if you supplement it.

---

## The animals

The dogs first, because they are the most constant. They are not pets. Read that again. They are scavengers with attachments, and the attachments are not to you. They will watch you from the gutters. They will not approach. You should not approach them. Do not feed them unless you intend to keep feeding them; the dog's trust, once given, becomes a debt the town expects you to pay. If a dog follows you at a distance, that is the closest you will come to being welcomed. Accept it as the welcome it is.

The draft horses are large and not gentle and their handlers are not gentle. Do not step into the path of a dray cart. The draymen do not stop for pedestrians. They do not stop for each other, very often. Walk on the side of the street, against the buildings, and keep one ear behind you for wheels.

The cattle lowing across the water at night. Some people find it a comfort. Some people cannot sleep for it. You will find out which you are.

The rats in the cellared districts. Do not go into the cellared districts at night. Do not store food in your room unless it is in a sealed tin or a heavy crock with a weighted lid. The rats own the underground the way the people own the above. This is not a metaphor. They are not afraid of you. They are not even particularly interested in you. They are simply there, and they have been there longer than the brick.

The wild pigs in the marsh. You will hear about them. You will not see them unless you go looking, and you should not go looking. They are not the pigs of the southern ward, which are fed and dull. They are something older. They have reverted. Hunters take them in season, and out of season they are tolerated because they keep the rats down, and the draymen speak of them with a respect that is not affection.

The mockingbird. If you see someone pointing at a tangle of ivy on the north wall of the old Customs House, stop and look. You may hear it. You may not. The mockingbird has been reported for years and some people are skeptical and some people have heard it and won't say when. If you hear it, that is the town giving you something. Don't ask what it means. There is no meaning. It is a bird. It is one of very few birds in a town with no trees, and the people have made it a small emblem because people need emblems. Stand for a moment. Then walk on.

---

## The people

They do not greet you. This is the first and most important thing. The drayman takes your trunk and walks. You follow because there is nothing else to do. The town is working. The town does not care that you have arrived.

Do not mistake the indifference for hostility. It is not hostility. It is the indifference of a place that has been worked hard for a long time by a great many bodies, and your body is one more body, and the town has seen many bodies and will see many more. If you work, you will be permitted. If you do not work, you will be tolerated for a while and then you will leave, on the next barge downriver, and the town will not notice you have gone.

Do not speak loudly. Do not speak at length. Do not ask for warmth. The heat discourages warmth. The closeness discourages it. People here are damp and they are working and they have no breath to spare for you. Speak softly. Keep your sentences short. If you must give a command — to a porter, to a drayman — say it at half a shout, the way they do. You will be understood.

If you are staying for any length of time, find one person. One. Not many. One person who will sit with you, briefly, in the cooler hours, in whatever shade a stone building can offer. This is the unit of friendship in Marshside. It is not nothing. The town does not encourage more than this, but it does not forbid it.

Do not ask about the dead in the marsh. Do not ask about the marsh people. Do not ask the older residents about what the tide does at high tide, in the deeper channels, in certain lights. These are questions that have answers but the answers are not given to strangers.

---

## Practicalities

Money. The town runs on iron and on what iron buys. There is cash, of course — coin, paper, whatever the current arrangement uses — and it is accepted at the boarding houses and the taverns and the few small shops that are not company stores. Bring small denominations. Bring what you think you'll need and add a third. The town is not cheap, exactly, but it is expensive in ways that surprise: a clean sheet, a square of soap, a candle that does not smoke. These things cost. They cost more because they are scarce.

Lodging. The boarding houses are small and the beds are narrow and the sheets smell of bleach and river water. This is the best you will do. Do not expect more. Do not complain about the sheets. The woman who washed them is one of the women who lives here and she has done her best with what she had.

Food. River fish, salted and preserved. Bread, dense. Pork, in many preparations. The slaughterhouses feed the rendering works, which feed the pigs in the southern ward, which feed the workers in some arrangement that you should not trace too carefully if you want to keep eating. There are taverns. The taverns serve what is available. The taverns are not cheerful. Drink the small beer. Do not drink the strong stuff on an empty stomach; the heat will punish you for it.

Water. The river water is not for drinking. The freshwater springs are up the slight hill, in the worker housing. There are public taps, reportedly. Bring a water bottle — metal, not leather — and fill it from the springs, not from the river, not from the taps in the lower wards unless you have asked and been told they are clean. The lower wards are not clean. The lower wards smell the way they smell because they smell.

A lamp. A knife. A piece of cord. A small mirror — for signaling, for shaving, for reminding yourself what you look like when the haze has been at you for a week. A notebook. Write down what you see, because the town will work on you in ways you do not notice at the time, and you will want a record later. A letter of credit or something equivalent, in case you need to leave quickly and cannot wait for the next barge. The barges run when the barges run, which is dawn, which is when the fog is thickest and you cannot see the town you are leaving, which is, on reflection, perhaps the kindest way to leave.

---

## The marsh

You will be drawn to it. Everyone is drawn to it. The town ends and the marsh begins, and the line is a long negotiation, and the marsh is quieter than the town and has more life in it than the town, and you will want to walk out onto it, particularly at low tide, particularly in the early morning.

Do not walk out onto it alone. Do not walk out onto it at high tide. The channels shift. The mud is deeper than it looks. The wild pigs are out there. The marsh gives up its dead reluctantly and at unpredictable hours. If you go, go with someone who knows it — a duck hunter, a fisherman, one of the old marsh-dwellers who still knows where the channels run. If you cannot find such a person, go as far as the first heron congregation and stop. The herons are the boundary. Beyond the herons is the marsh's country, not yours.

Bring boots you can sink into mud and not lose. Bring a stick to test the ground ahead. Bring water. Do not bring food; the smell of food in the marsh is an invitation you do not want to extend.

---

## What not to bring

Do not bring expectations of beauty. The town is not beautiful. It is not trying to be beautiful. It is what it is, which is a place that has been worked and has settled into its worked shape, and it does not care whether you find it lovely. Do not bring expectations of welcome. Do not bring a romantic idea of industry, of the dignity of labor, of the heroic working class. The town is hot and wet and the people are damp and they are working because that is what the town does, and the work is not ennobling. The work is just what happens.

Do not bring wood. The town has eaten all the wood it had. Anything wooden you bring will be eaten in turn — not maliciously, just by the air, by the damp, by the small persistent life that lives in damp wood. Bring metal. Bring stone. Bring fired clay. Bring what will survive.

Do not bring more than you can carry yourself, because the drayman may not be there, and the porter may not be there, and the town does not care that you have arrived, and your trunk may sit on the public landing for a while before someone gets around to it. Bring what you can carry for a quarter of a mile in summer heat over uneven cobbles. That is the unit of luggage in Marshside.

---

## The longer you stay

You will change. This is the warning the description gives in the form of settled floors and leaning chimneys and rails that curve into the reeds. The town settles you. After a week, you will stop raising your voice. After two weeks, you will stop noticing the smell. After a month, you will have a route through the streets that avoids the worst puddles, and you will not remember learning the route. After two months, you will have a favorite tavern, and a stool at it, and the woman who pours the small beer will nod at you without speaking, and you will nod back, and this will be enough.

The town is not trying to change you. It is simply the medium, and you are in it, and the medium does what media do. The damp will find your old injuries. The heat will tax your heart a little. The dogs will sing at night and you will sleep through it, and one morning you will wake and realize you slept through it, and that will be the moment you know the town has had you for a while.

If you are only passing through, this is fine. Stay a week. Do what you came to do. Take the next barge downriver at dawn, in the fog, when the town is only a smell and a sound and the suggestion of chimneys in the grey. Leave the way you came, without announcement, and the town will not have noticed.

If you are staying longer, decide, in the first week, what work you will do. The town is working. If you work with it, you will be permitted. If you do not, you will be a body moving through the streets with no purpose, and the town does not know what to do with those, and you will find yourself oddly exhausted by the end of each day without knowing why.

---

## Last things

Bring less than you think you need. Bring more water than you think you need. Bring fewer clothes and more cloth — you can re-tie a cloth as a head-wrap or a wrist-covering or a bandage, and you cannot re-tie a shirt. Bring a small thing that reminds you who you are, and keep it dry, and look at it once a day. The town is not hostile to who you are. It is simply indifferent. The indifference is what wears you down, and the small thing will remind you that you are still a person and not just a damp body moving through damp streets.

Be patient with yourself. The first three days are the worst. The heat and the wet and the smell and the dogs and the absence of trees and the absence of welcome all press on you at once, and you will want to leave on the second day, and on the third day, and by the fourth day you will have begun to settle, the way everything settles here, and the town will be a little less strange, and you will be a little less strange to it, and the small adjustment will feel like the beginning of something, though it is not, it is just you getting used to a place that will never quite get used to you.

Take care. The town will not.
