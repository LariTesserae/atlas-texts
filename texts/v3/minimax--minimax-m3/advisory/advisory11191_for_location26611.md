# Preparation for the Low Country

## I. Begin With the Understanding That the Place Will Try to Keep You

This is not a metaphor, and it is not the kind of danger you can plan against with a checklist. By the second week you will notice that you are forgetting the shape of your original itinerary. By the third, you will find yourself unable to remember, with any precision, why you had ever intended to leave. The settlement will not imprison you. It will simply become the place where things make sense to you, and you will find, when you try to articulate what it is that the place does, that you can only describe it as a smell, or a color, or a name you have not yet learned.

Prepare for this. Decide, before you arrive, the conditions under which you will leave. Write them down. Keep the paper dry. Re-read it each morning.

You will be tempted to throw the paper away.

Do not throw the paper away.

## II. Clothing and Equipment for the Damp

Everything you bring will be damp within an hour of your arrival, and it will remain damp for as long as you remain. The dampness is not the kind that rain brings — it is the kind that the air itself is made of. Plan accordingly.

**What to wear:**
- Layers of natural fibers. Synthetics will hold the moisture against your skin and you will develop the smell of a person who has been unwell for a long time.
- A proper outer shell — waxed cotton, oiled wool, or a good treated rain coat. The rain here comes in slanted sheets and will pass through inferior garments in the time it takes to notice it has arrived.
- Footwear with serious tread and serious drainage. The pavement sweats. The grass is not grass. The sedge with the purple seam will hold water in its blades like a thousand small canals. You will be walking through a place that is always, in some sense, a marsh.
- A wide-brimmed hat. The sky is the color of the place — a flat, ambiguous gray — but the condensation will fall on your head and run down your neck and there is no defense against this except a brim.
- Gloves. Even in mild weather. The iron smell is more present at the fingers.

**What to bring, but in sealed containers:**
- Spare clothing in oiled bags or zip-sealed pouches. You will not be able to fully dry anything here. Accept this, but do not let mildew colonize your only clean shirt.
- Paper goods in hard cases. Your notebook, your maps, your letters home. Mold will find them within days if they are not protected.
- Electronics in waterproof housings rated for sustained humidity, not just splash. The place is wetter inside your pocket than a jungle is wetter than a desert.

**What you do not need, and should not bring:**
- A compass. The architecture makes compass bearings unreliable in the way that a funhouse mirror makes faces unreliable. Buildings do not maintain a consistent size as you approach them. You will need to navigate by memory and by the slow accumulation of landmarks in your own head.
- A vehicle. There are no roads for vehicles. There are channels where vehicles may once have been intended, but they are filled with the dark glassy substance, and you are not to touch the dark glassy substance.
- Heavy cold-weather gear. It is never cold here, exactly. It is the temperature of a room with the windows closed on a still afternoon. It is the temperature that makes you wonder whether you are cold or merely damp or merely remembering cold.

## III. The Body

**Before you go:**
- Have your sinuses in good order. The place sits low enough that you feel the inverse of altitude — a soft pressure at the eardrums, a sense that the air above you is heavier than the air below. If you are prone to sinus congestion, this will be the first thing to ruin your week.
- Have your lungs in good order. The air is damp and carries an iron smell at certain hours (low water table) and a sweeter, vegetal smell at others (high water table). Neither is unpleasant, exactly, but neither is nothing. People with respiratory sensitivities should think carefully.
- Have your eyes checked. You will be living in dim light for the duration of your visit. After a week your pupils will widen and stay widened. After a month you will be able to read by the light from a wall. Plan for this adaptation. Do not, on arrival, mistake the dimness for failing vision and panic.
- Get a good night's sleep before you go. The sound of the place is low — a hum felt first in the teeth, then in the hearing — and there is a rhythmic breath from beneath the pavement that the residents have stopped noticing but that you, on your first nights, will not stop noticing. You will sleep anyway, because the place is soporific in the way that a heavy quilt is soporific. But you will not sleep well for the first few days, and it is better to arrive with a reserve.

**While you are there:**
- Eat and drink regularly. The settlement has food, but it has the food of a place where the residents move at a pace that does not invite urgency. You will find yourself slowing down. You will find yourself forgetting meals. Do not let the pace of the place become the pace of your body.
- Watch your footing. The paving is unsettled. Slabs sit a half-centimeter above their bedding in places where the bedding is still drying. Roots are lifting curb stones in quiet, slow rebellion. The floorboards in the inhabited buildings sigh underfoot. Move at the pace of the residents, which is the pace of someone who has learned to expect the ground to do something unexpected.
- Drink the water only after you have decided it is water. The water table is three or four meters below your feet. The buildings are raised on pads partly to keep them above it. The contractors say the ground likes to give back what you bury in it. Be thoughtful about what you take from the place.
- Wash your hands often. The condensation on every surface carries a faint residue — not unpleasant, not dangerous as far as you can tell, but not nothing.

## IV. The Senses and What They Will Do to You

**Sight:**
The dimness will, at first, feel like dusk. You will reach for the light switch that is not there. You will wonder, for the first day, whether the buildings are unlit because it is daytime in a place with little sun, or because the buildings do not use lights in the way you understand lights. You will eventually realize that the place has its own light: the soft amber beneath the eaves, the cold-green from the drainage channels, the violet pulse that travels through certain walls. After a week your pupils will widen and stay widened. After a month you can read by the wall light alone. Do not be alarmed by this. It is an adaptation, not a damage.

There is one thing to watch for: the buildings do not maintain a consistent apparent size as you change your distance from them. A tower that cuts the sky from the central clearing will be, when you walk to it, only a few stories high. At its base it will be a building that is large but ordinary, and you will not quite be able to remember why it had seemed larger before. This is true of several of the buildings. There is one — pale, ribbed, windowless on its visible faces — that is always the size of a warehouse, no matter how far away you stand from it. People who work inside it do not speak about what is inside it. They speak about the commute. Do not be alarmed by this either. The settlement is older than the buildings in it, though the buildings are the only structures. Some kinds of size do not obey the rules of distance.

**Sound:**
The hum is constant and low. It is the kind of sound you feel in your teeth first and then in your hearing. It does not match any electrical grid or other infrastructure you have known. After a few days it will recede into the background of your perception, the way the refrigerator in your kitchen has receded into the background. After a week you will not be able to say, on any given moment, whether you are hearing it or not. This is fine. This is intended.

Do not attempt to trace the hum to its source. This is one of the rules. Nobody will stop you. There is no enforcement. There is only the way they do not look at you when you ask about it.

**Smell:**
The smell of the place changes with the water table. At low tide — in the morning, when the water has receded — there is a heavier iron smell, a mineral wetness, the smell of something that has been underground. At high tide — in the afternoon, after the rain has negotiated itself out of the sky — there is a sweeter, vegetal smell, the smell of something that has been growing. You will come to associate these smells with the rhythms of the day. You will, eventually, begin to feel that your own mood changes with them, the way the residents' moods seem to. This is not a danger. It is only the place teaching you its clock.

**Touch:**
The pavement sweats. The moss peels back like a pelt if you press on it. The vines have made architectural decisions and you should not undo them. The dark glassy substance in the channels gives off the violet pulse and is not to be touched. The water that condenses on every surface is warmer than you would expect — warmer than the air — and it has the faint slickness of something that has been somewhere before it arrived on you.

## V. The Rules

The residents do not always articulate these. They do not always remember to articulate them. You will learn them mostly from the way they do not look at you when you ask.

**Do not cut the vegetation.** This is the first rule and the most consistently enforced, in the way that a wall is consistently a wall. The vegetation is what the place is, more than the buildings are. Ferns have colonized the north faces. Moss covers the south faces with a fur so thick it can be peeled back. Vines have made architectural decisions. To cut the vegetation is to do something the place will not forgive in any way you can name, and the residents will not forgive in any way they can name, and the two may be the same forgiveness.

**Do not enter the buildings at night.** Not because anything has happened to anyone who has. Because of the way the residents look at you when you say you want to.

**Do not interfere with the violet pulse.** It travels through certain walls at a rate that suggests, but does not confirm, intention. It is not for you. Leave it alone.

**Do not touch the dark glassy substance in the channels.** The channels terminate in shallow basins at intersections. There is something moving in the basins, slowly, but when you approach it retreats below the surface. The substance is part of whatever the channels are doing, and the channels are doing something the residents do not discuss.

**Do not trace the hum to its source.** See above.

**Move at the pace of the residents.** They move from one building to another with the unhurried gait of people who do not have to be anywhere in particular. They do not speak loudly. They do not, often, speak at all. When they pass each other they nod. When they pass you, they look at you in a way that suggests they are not sure whether you are a visitor or a thing the city has produced. You will not be able to disabuse them of this by explaining. You will only be able to disabuse them of it by being there long enough that they stop wondering.

## VI. The Stay

You will arrive thinking you will find a low city on damp ground, with odd architecture and few people and not much going on. You will find this. You will also find that the floor plan you walked through this week will have a corridor next week that was not there this week, and the corridor will lead somewhere that is, somehow, the same place you were already going. You will find that the residents speak of the city in a tense that does not have a name in your language — neither past nor present nor future, but something that contains all three at once, with the emphasis shifting. You will find that the sky is the same gray from every vantage point, and that the gray is not a color.

You will find, eventually, that you do not want to leave.

This is the most important thing to prepare for, and the thing for which no preparation is fully adequate.

Bring something with you that you cannot leave behind. A photograph. A name. A person who expects you to come home. The paper on which you wrote the conditions under which you will leave. Any of these. They will not save you, exactly. They will keep you, for a while, in the tense your language already has. They will remind you that the unnamed tense of the residents is not the only tense there is.

The dim light holds. The hum holds. The vegetation continues its slow, lush claim on the buildings, the avenues, the channels, the gaps between the structures that no one is in a hurry to fill. The buildings settle. The ground takes the weight and gives a little back. The weather arrives and departs and arrives again.

It is a quiet place. It is an active place. It is a young place.

It is a place that has only just begun.

Prepare accordingly.
