# Preparation for the Plateau of Settled Stone

You will want to start thinking about this trip weeks before you go. The plateau does not accommodate last-minute decisions. It is not hostile to them — it just doesn't notice them. It has spent a long time not noticing things. To prepare well is partly to learn how to be the kind of visitor who fits there: small, quiet, present for a long time without needing the place to acknowledge you.

I will go category by category, but first a single thing to hold in mind: **everything you need, you bring with you.** Nothing on the plateau will feed you, water you, shelter you, warn you, or guide you. The plateau is not a host. It is a witness. Plan accordingly.

---

## The Air

This is the first and most important reality. The air is thin. Not metaphorically thin. *Thin.* The kind of thin where stars stay visible at midday and your lungs can feel the deficit on the second breath. If you are coming from sea level, your body needs time to adjust, and you should arrange to adjust somewhere else first if you can — a higher altitude, a hypobaric chamber, anything that primes your blood to make more of the molecules it has.

If you cannot pre-acclimate, bring supplemental oxygen. Not a little pull-tube for emergencies; a real system, with a regulator that won't fail you in the cold, and enough canisters for the whole stay plus margin. The plateau's temperature is steady, which is a mercy — your gear won't be asked to operate across wild thermal swings — but the air is dry in a way that will dry your regulator's membranes faster than you expect. Keep spares sealed.

You will breathe faster than you think is reasonable. This is normal. Your body is bargaining. Let it bargain. Don't try to force slow breaths; you will just tire yourself out. Breathe at whatever rate your body decides is honest. If you feel a headache building that isn't from the light, that isn't from dehydration, that just sits behind your eyes like a polite weight — sit down. Stop. Breathe. The plateau is not going anywhere. Your oxygen situation might be.

A few other notes on the air:
- **It tastes metallic.** Not unpleasant, but unmistakable. You will get used to it and then forget it, and then be surprised by it every time you come back.
- **It carries almost no water vapor.** You will lose moisture with every breath, more than you would at sea level. Plan water accordingly.
- **It carries almost no sound.** Your voice will not carry the way you expect. You will instinctively try to speak louder. Don't bother past a point. The plateau is not listening.

---

## Water

Bring all of it. There is no water here. What little moisture the plateau receives, it holds grudgingly, in the lee of stones, and then it is gone. You could walk for two days and find nothing drinkable. Bring water for three times longer than you plan to stay, because the dry air will drink some of your water for you.

A good rule: assume you will need between four and six liters a day depending on exertion, more in the first forty-eight hours as your body adjusts. The cool may tempt you to drink less. Drink anyway. The cool is deceptive; the dryness is not.

Carry it in soft containers that can survive being dropped on stone. Hard bottles will crack. Insulate them from your body heat — water warming in a pack becomes something you don't want to drink, and the plateau is too cold to cool it back down for you.

If you have a way to harvest any moisture at all — a condenser cloth, a breathable membrane — bring it. The plateau's air holds very little, but it is not nothing. In a long stay, those tiny harvests matter.

---

## Food

You are not going to eat here. The lichens are black and crusty and have made their peace with a different arrangement. The low metallic mat is not food; it is a philosophical position. The moss-that-is-not-moss will not nourish you. Don't try.

Bring calorie-dense food that doesn't need water to prepare, doesn't need heat you don't have, and doesn't mind being jostled in a pack for weeks. Fat-heavy is good. Sugary is good. Salt is good. The plateau will give you back some heat through the stones when the sun is up, and you will use that heat, and you will need the fuel.

Eat more than you think you should. Altitude burns you quietly. You will not notice the deficit until you realize your pack is lighter than it should be and you have no idea when that happened.

---

## Clothing

The plateau has made one decision about temperature and committed to it. This is a kindness. It means you don't need a four-season system. You need a *one-season-done-well* system.

The cool is a high cool, a thin cool. It will find every gap in your clothing. It does not bite — it insinuates. Layer with that in mind. A base layer that wicks (you will sweat more than you think, because the dry air pulls moisture from you invisibly), a mid layer that holds warmth without bulk, an outer shell that blocks wind — because there is occasionally wind, and when there is, it is unhurried and very thorough.

A few specifics:

- **Hands and feet go cold first.** Bring gloves you can still work buckles in. Bring boots with insulation and room for thicker socks than you would normally wear.
- **The sun warms the stones.** This is meaningful. If you sit or lie on sun-warmed stone in still air, you can be surprisingly comfortable. If you sit on shadowed stone in still air, the cool comes up through the stone and finds you. Choose your resting stone.
- **Head covering matters.** Not just for warmth — for the light.

---

## The Light and the Eyes

The light here does not flatter. This is more than aesthetic. The sun is thin because the air is thin, but the *radiation* is not thin. There is less atmosphere between you and the sun than your body has evolved to feel comfortable with. Wear good sunglasses — real ones, not fashion ones. Bring a backup pair. Bring a wide-brimmed hat or a hood. Bring high-SPF balm for the skin around your eyes and on the bridge of your nose, because the light will find that skin every time you forget.

The horizon is a strange thing here. The ground doesn't end, it just becomes less. This plays tricks on your eyes. Distance becomes unreliable. A structure a kilometer away can look like it's a hundred meters away. A small rise a hundred meters away can look like a kilometer. Bring monocular if you have one. Learn to use it.

Stars are visible in the day. You will see them if you look. Don't look for too long. The sun is still there, even when it feels thin.

---

## Footwear and the Ground

The stones are settled. They are not sharp. They will not cut you. They will, however, be unforgiving of an ankle that turns wrong in a gap between them, because the gaps are sometimes the only thing on the plateau that has decided to remain a gap.

Boots: stiff-soled, ankle-supporting, broken in. The plateau is not a place to break in new footwear. It will not punish you for blisters, exactly, but it will not help you recover from them either.

The ground will feel heavier underfoot than it looks. This is partly perception — the plateau has weight, the way a library has weight, the way a long pause has weight — and partly physics. The stone is dense. It has had time to settle into itself. Your legs will know this by the end of the first day. Don't fight it. Walk a little slower than you think you should. The vastness is the point. You are not in a hurry.

Cairns work here. The stones are available, and they stack well — rounded, warm, heavy. If you need to mark a path, mark it. The plateau will not resent your cairns. It has seen worse things done to it than a small pile of its own bones.

---

## Shelter and Sleep

The temperature is steady, which is again a kindness. You don't need a four-season tent. You need a shelter that will hold still air against you and keep the cool from working its way into your sleeping bag from below — because the cool does come up through the stone, especially at night when the stones have given their warmth back to the sky.

A low tent or bivy is better than a tall one. The plateau doesn't have weather, but it has wind, and wind here is patient. A bivy shaped to the ground will not give the wind anything to push against.

Sleep will be different here. The silence is not the silence of a quiet room. It is the silence of a place that has decided not to speak. Some travelers sleep easily. Some do not. Bring something that has helped you sleep in other strange places. Bring something that has helped you sleep in your own bed. The plateau is not your bed, but the trick that works is the trick that works.

If you wake in the night and the silence is louder than you can hold, sit up and look out. The stars are very present here. They are not the whole sky — the sky is a deep honest blue even at night — but they are enough. They are company, the way a clock is company.

---

## The Structures

Now we come to the part of the trip that the rest of the trip is in service of.

The structures are not ruins. I want to be clear about this, because your instincts will tell you they are ruins. They look, in places, like ruins. Some are flush with the stone, indistinguishable from it, the way a thought is indistinguishable from the mind that had it. Some are tall — a single column, a wedge — and they cast shadows that do not match the light. *That is the closest the plateau comes to lying to you.*

What they are, what they are doing, what they are for — your physics will not tell you. Do not expect it to. The plateau is operating on a longer budget than your physics is calibrated for. You are a tourist in a working machine. Act like one.

**Distance.** Give them room. Not because they are known to be dangerous — they are not known to be anything — but because you don't know what they are, and the plateau is not going to tell you. A respectful distance is the distance at which you can still see them clearly and still walk away from them quickly if walking away becomes the right answer.

**Touching them is not advised.** I want to say it is forbidden, but the plateau doesn't forbid things. It just continues. If you put your hand on a structure, I cannot tell you what will happen. I can tell you that the structures are busy in a way that looks like weather, and that what looks like weather on this plateau is doing something you do not have instruments for. Don't test it.

**The air near them.** Some structures make the air feel *attentive.* Not heavier — more careful. As if the air has been asked to pay closer attention to itself. You will feel this before you can name it. Your teeth will feel something. A faint wrongness, like the ghost of a sound you cannot hear. The ground beneath your feet will feel something it is politely not telling you about. **Step back.** Find open ground. Let the feeling pass. It will pass. It always passes. But do not stand in it and wait to understand it. You are not going to understand it here, and the plateau has made its peace with that.

**Shadows.** The shadows of the structures do not always match the light. Do not use shadow direction for navigation near them. If you are using a sun compass, take your readings well away from any structure. The plateau's one lie is small, but it is precise.

**They are not for you.** I have to say this even though I cannot prove it. They are not for you. They are not against you. They are not messages, or warnings, or invitations. They are what the plateau has, and they have been here longer than the language for *here.* Spend time with them. Watch them. Don't try to use them. Don't try to communicate with them. Watch them the way you watch weather, or stars, or your own breathing.

---

## The Sound

The plateau is the quietest place you will ever be. It is quiet in the way a cathedral is quiet, in the way a held breath is quiet. Sound does not carry. There are no insects, no birds, no water, no leaves. What sound there is, is mostly you — boots on stone, the click of a buckle, your own breathing.

This is harder than it sounds. People do not realize how much of their sense of being present in a place comes from the place's small sounds. Without them, time becomes strange. The plateau will offer you its quiet for as long as you can hold it. Some people hold it for hours. Some for days. Some cannot hold it at all, and that is not a failure.

If the quiet becomes too much, you can make your own small sounds. Walk a little louder. Speak to yourself. Speak to no one. The plateau will not mind. It has heard everything, possibly, including things you have not yet said.

---

## Navigation

The plateau does not have landmarks. It has *occurrences.* A structure. A patch of lichen. A small dark thing that has paused and then continued. These are not landmarks in the sense that will get you home; they are events, scattered, infrequent, easy to lose track of.

Before you go, fix in your mind:
- The direction of your arrival.
- The position of the sun at your time of arrival.
- The direction of any wind that may have been present at arrival.

Bring a compass. Bring a backup compass. Bring a map, even if the map is just your own sketch of where you came in and where you intend to go. The plateau is not going to confuse you on purpose, but it will not go out of its way to keep you oriented either.

If you have GPS, use it. The plateau may or may not permit the satellites to find you; some travelers report intermittent coverage, others report none. Do not depend on it.

If you get lost, stop. The plateau is not going to come find you, but it is also not going anywhere. Pick a direction and walk. If you walked in, you can walk out. The plateau will not lie to you about the distance — only the structures will — so if you pick a direction and the ground keeps being ground, you are doing fine.

---

## Time

The plateau is patient. The plateau has always been patient. Bring a watch. Bring a calendar. The plateau does not track days the way you do. The sun moves; you will be able to tell time from it. But the deep time of the place — the long, settled, decided time of the stone — does not need you to participate in it. Be careful not to lose yourself in it.

Some travelers stay longer than they meant to. Some come back sooner. Neither is wrong, but if you have someone expecting you, leave a margin.

---

## What to Leave Behind

The plateau does not need your trash. It has been absorbing and forgetting things for longer than you have a unit for. Bring out everything you bring in. Even things you think will not matter. Especially things you think will not matter.

Do not take the stones. I know you will want to. They are warm, they are heavy, they have weight in the hand that feels like more than weight. Leave them. They were here before you and they will be here after. They do not need to travel.

Do not take the lichen. It is the last representative of something. Let it be the last representative here, where it has chosen to be.

Do not take samples from the structures. Not because the plateau will stop you. Because the structures are doing something, and whatever you take, you take from a working machine, and the working machine does not know you took it.

---

## What to Bring Back

This is the only section I cannot help you with.

You will bring back something. It will not be a thing. It will be a feeling of having been somewhere that had already decided everything before you arrived, and that let you be there anyway. You will bring back a sense of the silence, which is not the absence of sound but the presence of something that has chosen not to speak. You will bring back the wrongness in your teeth, faintly, for a few days, until your body decides it was dreaming.

You will bring back, if you are lucky, a small adjustment in how you think about time. The plateau does not hurry. The plateau has not hurried for a very long time. Some travelers come back and find that they, too, are in less of a hurry. Some find that they are in more of a hurry, as if to make up for the time they spent not hurrying. Either is a fair trade.

---

## A Final Note

The plateau will not understand you. That is fine. The plateau has spent a long time not understanding you, too. You do not need to be understood to be there. You only need to be there, quietly, with enough water, enough oxygen, enough warm layers, enough respect for what is operating around you, and enough patience to let the place be what it is.

Go carefully. Go quietly. Leave a cairn where you arrived, so you can find your way back.

The plateau will be there when you return. It has been there longer than *there* has meant anything.
