# Preparation for the Ridge of Engines

There is no way to overstate what you are about to do. Most travelers to difficult multiversal places are dealing with a hostile environment — heat, cold, vacuum, predation, gravity, atmosphere, disease. The Ridge is not, primarily, any of those things. The Ridge is the problem of *unvarying eternity*, and the human animal is not built to endure it. So the preparation you need is less like preparing for a mountain and more like preparing for a monastery that has been running since before your species had language.

Read what follows carefully. You will not get a second chance to prepare, because once you arrive, the Ridge will start to work on you within minutes, and the part of you that planned this trip will not be the part of you that is still standing a day later.

## What the place is, before you go

The Ridge is two miles down. It is a young spreading center, sharp basalt, the kind of geology that should still be smoothing itself but isn't, because the machines have been there longer than the weathering would have taken. The water is near-freezing, black, and dense. The pressure is around three hundred atmospheres. The light, such as it is, is a kind of residue — the last grey memory of a sun none of the things down there have ever seen. There is no day. There is no night. There is no current you can see, no weather, no season. The temperature does not vary by a degree you can measure. The sound does not vary by a decibel. Time, in any human sense, is not a property of the location.

This is the single most important fact, and you must understand it before you put on a suit or board a submersible. *Nothing here is responding to anything else.* The machines run because they have always run. The fauna drifts because the water drifts, or because the fauna was always drifting. The flame in the tower burns because a flame was lit in that tower and has not been extinguished. None of this is a system in the way you understand systems. It is a *condition*. It is the way this stretch of seabed is. You are about to insert yourself into a condition that has been running, possibly, for longer than complex life has existed on the surface of your own world, and the condition will not notice you in any way you can use.

## The pressure, the cold, the dark

These are the easy parts, because engineering can solve them. You will need a pressure vessel rated for three hundred atmospheres or better, with a margin, because the pumps in the Ridge generate pressure fluctuations in the water that ordinary deep-sea gear is not designed to absorb. Soft suits will not do. Hard suit, or submarine. Bring redundancy. Bring a second submersible on a tether, or, if you are traveling light, bring a hard-shell pressure refuge you can reach within sixty seconds of any breach.

Thermal: you will not warm up. Don't try. The cold here is not the cold of weather; it is the cold of the abyss, which is a different thermodynamic animal. Your body heat will leak out faster than you can generate it, no matter the suit insulation, because the suit will eventually equalize. The trick is not to fight the cold. The trick is to lower your core set point before you go down. Acclimate in a cold chamber for the week before. Eat heavily. Sleep heavily. Arrive at the Ridge already cold. Then the place will simply *continue* you, instead of dragging you down into itself.

Light: this is where most travelers make their first mistake. They bring floods. They light the place up like a stadium, because darkness frightens them. Don't. The fauna is sensitive to light in ways you don't want to discover experimentally, and the towers have their own illumination, which is the only illumination that belongs to this place. Bring low-light optics — image intensifiers, near-IR, the sort of thing a military operator would carry. Use them. Your own headlamp should be on a dim red setting, used sparingly, pointed at your hands, never at the water column. If you must illuminate something in the distance, use a brief pulse and let your eyes readapt. Treat the dark as a thing that is doing you the courtesy of not looking at you, and return the favor.

## The sound

This is the problem that will break you if you let it.

The sound of the Ridge is constant. It is the loudest continuous sound most humans will ever sit inside, and it does not have a rhythm, does not have a phrase, does not have a breath. It is a drone of grinding, of pumps, of sonar, of vibration through the basalt itself, and it is so steady that after about ninety minutes your brain will stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as the texture of silence. This is a lie your brain tells itself to keep functioning. The sound has not stopped. The sound will not stop. The sound is older than you.

You must dampen it, but you must also *not* dampen it completely, or you will be navigating the place blind to one of its central properties. Bring ear protection that you can remove at will, and a way to record. Listen for the first hour, fully, with dampening off, so you can feel the place in your chest. Then put the dampening on, and keep it on, and only take it off in short, deliberate sessions to check that you are still sane.

Bring a sound of your own. Not music — music has structure, and structure will be crushed by this drone. Bring something that breathes. A metronome. A human voice on a loop, speaking nonsense syllables. The sound of someone in another room, doing something ordinary. The Ridge's threat is not that it is loud. Its threat is that it has nothing to say. Counterprogram it with something small and alive.

You will be tempted, around the third or fourth hour, to turn all sound off, including your own breathing, and to sit in a kind of sensory vacuum. Resist this. That is the place starting to take you.

## Time

You must bring your own.

The Ridge has no internal clock that a human can use. There is no sunrise. No tide you can feel. The fauna drifts, but the drift has no direction. The sound does not pulse. The orange lamps do not blink. Within half a day you will lose the ability to estimate how long you have been down. You will, with certainty, misremember events as having happened in a different order, or as having happened to a different version of yourself.

Bring a watch. Bring a written schedule. Bring a list of things you meant to do on this trip, and check them off in order, in writing, every two hours. Do not skip the writing. The act of writing is what keeps you in linear time. The Ridge is not in linear time. The Ridge is in *duration*, which is a different thing, and duration will eat you if you let it.

Plan your visit for a fixed length — twelve hours is a sane maximum, twenty-four is heroic, forty-eight is something only a specialist should attempt — and have a hard exit time, written down, in two places, that you will obey no matter what you think you have discovered in the last hour. The discoveries you make in the last hour are the ones the Ridge has been saving for you, and you should not trust them.

## The fauna

Do not approach the fauna. Do not feed it. Do not attempt to identify it. Do not, especially, attempt to befriend it.

The fauna is pale, drifting, full of filaments and eyes, and some of it feeds on the pressure waves of the machinery in a way that no terrestrial biologist will be able to explain to you in advance. It is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is not curious about you. This is the part that will be hardest to accept. The fauna does not care that you are there. The fauna has been there in some form since before the machines, or has only been there since the machines, and it does not know the difference, and neither will you.

If a creature investigates your vessel, remain still. Do not run pumps. Do not extend arms. If something trails across your viewport, watch it pass. It is not reaching for you. It is reaching for the vibration you are making, because you are a new source of noise, and it is a thing that eats noise. Try not to find this beautiful. Try not to find it tragic. The Ridge does not trade in beauty or tragedy. The Ridge trades in continuation. You are, to the fauna, a small and very loud event. Be a quiet one if you can.

## The towers

You will see, scattered along the ridge, the towers. Cylinders, a long ship-like shape, single windows with a steady orange inside. Cables trailing into the dark. Pipes walking on stilted legs.

Do not enter them.

The flame in each tower has not flickered. This is the single most important fact about the towers, and you must hold it in your mind without trying to resolve it. The flame is not a sign of life. The flame is not a sign of automation. The flame is a sign that *something has been paying attention* for longer than you have a unit of time for. The flame has not flickered because whatever keeps it has not been interrupted. Do not be the interruption. Do not be the thing that makes the flame move, because if the flame moves, you will know that something on the Ridge has *noticed*, and the thing that has noticed has been waiting with the patience of geology.

If you must look, look from outside. If you must touch, touch the basalt near a tower, not the tower itself. If you must take a sample, take basalt. Do not take anything that was inside a tower. Do not take anything that a tower was protecting.

## What to bring

A short list, since you cannot carry much at three hundred atmospheres:

- Hard submersible or pressure suit, rated, redundant.
- Image intensifiers. Dim red work light. No floods.
- Variable ear protection. A recording device with hours of capacity.
- A watch and a written schedule. A second copy of the schedule, laminated, on a tether around your neck.
- A small notebook and a pen that writes under pressure. You will write in it, often.
- A breath-sound loop. Something human and ordinary. A kettle boiling. A child reading. A market. Pick one that does not make you weep.
- A single object from home that changes. A flower, if you can keep it alive that long. A candle that you will not light. A photograph of someone who is waiting for you. The object must be visibly, obviously, *temporally unstable* — something that will wilt, melt, fade — because you need to be reminded, hourly, that change is real.
- A piece of paper, in your own hand, that says why you are leaving the Ridge. You will read it at the halfway point of your visit. You will read it again at the end. You will not be embarrassed by this. You will be embarrassed by not having done it.

## What to do, in order

Arrive. Note the time. Write it down. Note the depth. Note the temperature inside the vessel. Note the sound pressure level at the hull, because you will want to compare it later, and the Ridge will try to convince you that the number has always been what it is now.

For the first hour, do nothing but listen with the dampening off, in five-minute intervals, and look through the viewport with the image intensifiers at low gain. Let the place introduce itself. You will feel, around minute forty, a strong urge to turn around and leave, or, equivalently and more dangerously, a strong urge to *stay forever*. Neither urge is information. Note it, and continue.

For the second hour, do your work. If your work is observation, observe. If it is measurement, measure. If it is recovery of a sample from outside a tower, do it now, and be quick. Do not improvise. The Ridge is very good at making improvisation feel like a good idea. It is not.

At the halfway point, read your paper. Look at your object from home. Look at the flame in the nearest tower. Note the time. Write down whether the flame has flickered. It has not. Write down that it has not, in the present tense, because the present tense is what you have.

For the remaining hours, finish what you came to do, slowly. Begin your ascent checklist three hours before your exit time, not one, because the Ridge will compress your sense of how long things take. The climb is the most dangerous part, psychologically. As you rise, the sound will change, and the change will feel like a violation. Resist the urge to go back down. The change is the world being the world again. Let it happen.

When you break the surface, do not speak for ten minutes. Note the time. Note the position of the sun, or the stars. Note the color of the water. Note that there is a wind. Write all of this down. You will not believe any of it for at least an hour. That is normal. That is the Ridge's last gift to you, the one that takes the longest to open.

## After

You will come back different. Not damaged — or not necessarily damaged — but *tuned*. The drone will live in your bones for a while. You will hear it in white noise, in idling engines, in the hum of refrigerators at night. This will fade, mostly. If it does not fade, if it gets louder, see someone. The Ridge is not a place that follows people home often, but it can, and you should not be ashamed of that, only attentive.

Carry the memory carefully. The Ridge is not a horror. It is not a wonder. It is a *fact*, a place that is the way it is, and was the way it was before you, and will be the way it is after you. You were, for a few hours, the only thing on that ridge that was going to leave. That is a strange thing to have been. You do not need to make it mean something. You only need to have come back.

Go gently. Write things down. Eat something warm as soon as you can. And the next time someone asks you where you have been, you will find that the words do not quite fit the place, and that is, in the end, the most honest report you can give: that you went somewhere the words don't fit, and you came back anyway.
