# Preparing for the Reed-Vale of Anrath

A note before I begin: most of what follows is not what you would put in a dive plan. A dive plan you can write yourself, or your operator can write for you. What follows is the other preparation — the part that no checklist covers, the part that determines whether, after a week in the Anrath forest, you find the place uncanny, or whether you begin to find it ordinary. That second thing is the goal. The first is what happens if you arrive unprepared.

## The water is the country

The first thing to understand is that you are not visiting a building or a settlement that happens to be wet. You are entering a *medium*. The water is not a barrier to be crossed; it is the place itself. It presses on you. It holds sound. It bends light. It conducts temperature against your skin in a way that no air-conditioning engineer could replicate. Your body, which has spent its life evolving in air, will resist this fact for the first day. Your mind, if you let it, will begin to accept it by the second.

Practical preparation: **certification to thirty meters**, with current diving practice. This is not a beginner's dive. A newly certified open-water diver can technically reach this depth but should not, and certainly not for an extended visit. You want at least advanced open water, ideally with some cold-water experience, ideally with at least one prior dive in low-visibility green water. If you have only dived in the clear blue tropics, find a quarry or a temperate lake first. Practice in green-grey. Practice in dim. Practice with your mask off briefly and your eyes open, to feel what the light does when the natural illumination is all you have.

The dive should be planned as **no-decompression**, with conservative limits. Thirty meters is not deep by technical standards, but it is deep enough that you will be carrying meaningful partial pressure of nitrogen, and you will be breathing harder than usual because of the work of finning, the cold, and the constant low-level excitement. Bring more gas than you think you need. Plan to ascend early and slowly. Three to five minutes of safety stop at five meters is the floor; consider a deeper stop as well, if you have the training.

## Cold, dense, insistent

The water is described as *cool*. That word is doing a lot of work. It means cool enough that, in a tropical wetsuit, you will be shivering within forty minutes. It means cool enough that the bodies of the Anrath, who have lived here for generations, are presumably adapted in ways yours are not. **Bring thermal protection appropriate to temperate water — likely a 5mm to 7mm wetsuit, or a drysuit with adequate undergarments.** A hood. Gloves, if you can preserve enough dexterity to handle a camera and not crush the kelp. (Thicker gloves are warmer but clumsier; in this place, clumsiness is worse than cold.)

The water is also *dense*. You will feel this not as weight on your bones but as drag on every movement. Finning will cost more. Reaching will cost more. Gesturing to communicate will cost more. The Anrath move through this water as fishes move; you will move through it as a visitor — slowly, deliberately, with a kind of stately care. That is fine. The slowness will serve you.

The pressure is not painful. The text is right about this. You will not feel the weight of thirty meters of seawater as a thing that hurts; you will feel it as a thing that is *there*, a steady, patient insistence on your eardrums, your sinuses, the air spaces in your mask. Equalize early. Equalize often. Equalize before you feel the need to. On the way down through the kelp canopy, before the town comes into view, you will have perhaps fifteen meters of vertical descent through forest. Do it slowly. Watch for the first sign of pressure against your eardrums and respond before it becomes the second sign.

## The light

You will be tempted to bring a powerful dive light. Do not bring a powerful dive light. Bring a small, dim one, for emergencies and for inspection of details. The light of the Anrath forest is its medium as much as the water is. It is *green-grey, lichen-filtered, moss-colored*. It reaches perhaps forty meters before the place dissolves into murk. It comes from above, through the kelp canopy, in shafts that move with the precession of the moon at high tide.

If you flood the place with white artificial light, you will learn almost nothing. The Anrath have lived for centuries in this particular light. The architecture of their town, the colors of their fauna, the very quality of the air inside the chambers, are organized around it. Your torch will read as a small sun, and they will tolerate it the way you would tolerate someone walking into a cathedral with a strobe light.

If you must photograph, choose ambient-light photography or a still camera with very gentle fill. Plan for the green. Plan for the grain. Plan for the slightly underwater feel even of shots taken at this depth — the slight haziness, the particulate matter drifting through the shafts.

Allow yourself at least two full days before you can really *see* in this light. Your eyes will adjust. Your brain will follow.

## The sound

This is the one that surprises visitors. I cannot overstate it.

Open-ocean silence is a myth, and the Anrath forest is not open ocean. The forest is **loud**. The dominant sound is the snapping of pistol-shrimp — *Alpheidae* — in their tens of thousands among the holdfasts. It is a sustained, crackling static. It is the sound of rain on a tin roof. It is the sound of a fire burning crisply. It is constant, omnidirectional, and will, after three or four days, become so familiar that its absence would feel like a hole in the world.

Beneath the crackle: the rhythmic grunting of territorial fish, low and felt as much as heard. The long moan of current through narrow channels in the canopy. The high chatter of small cleaning fish, almost birdlike, darting at parasites on anything that holds still. And, occasionally, the deep boom of something large passing along the ridge — a cetacean, a giant squid, something of that scale — felt in the chest before it is heard.

What you cannot do, in this medium, is speak. Your voice will be muffled, distorted, inaudible beyond a meter or two. The Anrath have their own ways of communicating — gestures, signs, perhaps sounds of their own that work in their medium. You will communicate mostly by hand. Agree on simple signals before the dive. Learn to point. Learn to wait. Learn to *not fill silence* — there is no silence here, and the Anrath will not feel the need to.

Plan also: **you will not hear a dive alarm. You will not hear your buddy call your name.** Visual contact is essential. The forest has folds and chambers; if you lose sight of one another, surface. The town is small but the forest around it is not.

## The forest

You will descend through the kelp canopy. This is the part that resembles, more than anything else, a slow-motion ride down through a very tall grove of trees. The stipes of *Thalassia magnifica* rise the full thirty meters from the ridge to the surface, anchored in the volcanic rock below and fanning into a tangled ceiling just under the tideline. Where the current favors it, they form genuine forest. Where the Anrath have lived longest, they have done something more than let the forest happen: they have organized it.

You will know you are approaching the town when the forest becomes *regular*. The kelp will be more evenly spaced, more deliberately pruned, more clearly bounded. The ridges of the Anrath shelf, at depth, are about three hundred meters long and eighty wide. Within that space, the forest thickens into settlement.

Inside the forest: do not fight it. **Do not thrash.** Entanglement in kelp is generally not dangerous, but in a place where the forest is also a town, where the kelp walls are also living rooms, the wrong entanglement could damage a chamber. Move with the forest, not against it. Frog-kick where you can. Pull yourself hand-over-hand along stipes only when you have to, and do so gently. The kelp is tissue, not steel cable; it can be bruised.

The fauna are abundant and **unafraid**, because the Anrath have not hunted them in living memory. Brittle-stars forage in the holdfasts. Anemones colonize the lower fronds. Eels sleep in the hollow chambers — tolerated, even welcomed, because they eat the creatures that would otherwise eat the people. Schooling fish move through the canopy in silver-blue clouds that darken the green light when they pass overhead.

Children here learn to read a school of fish before they read woven marks. You are an adult visitor. You will not learn to read fish in a week. But you can learn to *respect* the readings. A tight school is calm. A loose school is nervous. A school that fragments and disperses has been startled by something you have not yet seen. Stop. Look around. Stay calm. Do not put yourself between a mother fish and her shelter. Do not put your hand into a hollow chamber where an eel may be sleeping.

## The town

The town is **small**. A dozen or so structures cluster near the center of the ridge, with outliers — a watch-tower-kelp, a drying-rack-island, the great communal rookery — spaced more sparsely toward the edges. From above, looking down through the green ceiling, it would appear as a small constellation of denser shapes, woven together by rope and growth.

Each structure is built of living kelp. The technique is primitive in the literal sense — no metal, no concrete, no processed materials beyond woven reed-fibers and shaped shell. The labor, however, is immense. A house here is a hollow grown inside a single colossal plant, or across several. The kelp has been induced, by careful cutting and binding over generations, to form a chamber: the outer stipe thickened into a wall, the fronds woven into a roof, the holdfast spreading into a floor that breathes.

The interior is **dim**, lit by the same green-grey as everything else. It smells of iodine and the sweet rot of organic matter. Smoke does not exist here — there is no fire, no oxygen to feed it, no need. The houses are warm because the water is cool and the bodies inside are warm, and the warmth is held by the kelp walls in a way that stone could not replicate.

What this means for you:

- **Do not touch the walls.** They are not stone. They are tissue. They will bruise. They will also, over the slow time of months and years, *migrate* — the doorway you entered through today may not be in exactly the same place next year. The town is not built. It is *tended*.
- **Do not expect a door to behave like a door.** The slits in living tissue close slowly when the family does not want visitors, and open when they want trade. This is not a malfunction. It is a statement. Read it.
- **Do not enter a sealed chamber.** When someone dies, the chamber is sealed. A new one is cut. The sealed chamber is part of the substrate. It is also a kind of memorial. Respect it.
- **Expect to be received in the veth-kar.** This is the gathering-stomach, the great central hall, extended four times in living memory through careful grafts of new kelp onto old. It is the cathedral-scale space in a hamlet-sized town. You will be welcomed there, if you are welcomed at all.
- **Move slowly. Hover more than touch.** A clumsy fin kick can rip a woven roof. A heavy landing can crush a floor. The buoyancy control you practiced in the quarry is now the difference between courtesy and vandalism.

## The people

There are perhaps sixty, eighty of them. They are scattered, by the standards of a village, but they are bound tight by the structures they have made together and by the structures' density.

They speak a language with words like *veth-kar* (the gathering-stomach) and the saying *veth karu, karu veth* (the town is the garden, the garden is the town). They mean it literally. Their writing is **woven marks** — patterns of fiber, presumably, that read as text to those who know how. If you want to bring them anything that is not a stranger in the water, learn a phrase. Even a clumsy attempt will mean something.

The naturalist-divers come down from the drowned cities of the lowlands — Vorr, Saltreach, the long shelf-cities of the continental edge. They come to record. They note the kelp-architecture with the careful fascination of people who build in stone and steel. They note the sounds. They note the fauna, abundant and unafraid. They note the scale: small. What they do not note, because they cannot, is the way the place feels after a week. Plan to stay a week. The text is right about this. The green light becomes familiar. The shrimp-crackle becomes the sound of having a roof over your head. The slow growth of the walls becomes the rhythm of the seasons, of the year, of a life. You will not get there in two days. You will not get there in five. By the seventh day, if you are paying attention, the place will have begun to feel ordinary. That is when the visit begins.

You will not be the first visitor. You will not be the most welcome visitor. You will be, for a week, a clumsy stranger in a slow medium. Plan accordingly.

## The pace

This is the part that requires the most preparation, and the part that cannot be drilled.

Everything here is slow. The kelp grows at its own pace — perhaps a meter a month in good current, much less in winter-storms. The houses grow with it. A chamber carved this year will be a slightly different chamber next year, as the living walls thicken, as new fronds push through the woven roof, as the doorway slowly migrates.

Because of this slowness, the place has a quality of having been there forever. The kelp on the eastern flank of the ridge is older than the oldest living Anrath, and it has been part of the town-structure for as long as anyone remembers. When a storm tears a roof open, it is not repaired — it is regrown. When a child is born into a family, the family's house is extended in welcome. When someone dies, the chamber is sealed and a new one is cut. The town **accretes**, slowly, in the manner of coral or a termite mound, but on a scale of centuries rather than seasons.

What this means for you:

- **Do not expect responsiveness on your timescale.** A question asked in gesture may take a slow swim across a chamber to be considered. A doorway that opens may open at the rate the kelp grows — centimeters per day, perhaps, not at the speed of a hand on a handle.
- **Do not try to fix anything.** If a roof is torn, it is being regrown. If a wall has thickened, that is its nature. If a floor breathes, that is the holdfast doing what holdfasts do.
- **Do not rush the visit.** If you have only three days, do not come. If you have only a week, prepare for the fact that the first half will feel like waiting, and that the second half is what you came for.
- **Do not bring a schedule.** The Anrath do not have one, or if they do, it is written in the growth-rate of their walls.

## What to bring, what not to bring

**Bring:**

- Dive gear appropriate to 30m in cool green water. Redundant gas supply. Dive computer. Cutting tool. Surface marker buoy.
- Thermal protection matched to the water.
- A small, dim dive light for emergencies and close inspection. **Not** a primary illumination source.
- An underwater slate for notes, or a notebook for above-water notes afterward.
- A camera capable of ambient-light photography in green-grey.
- Patience.
- Time — at least a week.
- Modest gifts, if you bring any, of organic origin: woven fibers, shells, things that could in principle have grown or been gathered. They will not value processed materials. They may not value metals at all.
- A guide, if one can be arranged. The naturalist-divers who come from Vorr and Saltreach may be willing to introduce you, for a fee or a favor or simply for the company.

**Do not bring:**

- Fire. There is no oxygen to feed it and no need. Anything that requires combustion does not belong here.
- Hunting implements. The Anrath do not hunt. Bringing a spear into their town is an insult.
- Processed materials as gifts or as intrusion. Plastics, metals, concretes — these are the substances of the drowned cities above and below the Anrath shelf, but they are not the substances of the town itself.
- Expectations of land-based etiquette. Doors do not behave as doors. Furniture does not behave as furniture. *Greetings* may not behave as greetings — a slow approach, a hovering beside the kelp, a hand offered palm-out at the level of the chest, may be the closest thing.
- Anything that requires haste.

## A closing note

The text ends with the water holding it all: cool, dense, green-grey, loud with the percussion of shrimp and the low moan of current and the constant soft rustling of a forest that has been here, in one form or another, since long before anyone thought to call it a town.

It will be here, slowly, for a long time after.

That is the preparation you cannot pack. You are going to a place that does not need you and will not change because you came. You are going to a place that has its own time, its own weather, its own quiet, its own cathedral. The most useful thing you can bring, after the dive gear and the thermal protection and the patience, is the willingness to be a small and clumsy visitor in something that was already whole.
