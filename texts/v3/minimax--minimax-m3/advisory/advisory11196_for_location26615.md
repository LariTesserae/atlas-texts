The first thing to understand is that the cold of this place is not the cold of an ice cube or a winter wind. It has had a very long time to settle into the water, and has become a property of it, indistinguishable from the pressure, the stillness, the age. So the first preparation is thermal, and it must be the right kind of thermal. A heavy dry suit with active heating. Insulation alone will not hold. The water is too steady, too committed to its temperature. You need a heat source that will run for as long as you intend to stay down.

Pressure. The water column above the stone is vast. If the place is what it sounds like — a deep-ocean ruin — then pressure is not optional. A submersible, or a hard pressure suit of some kind. A wetsuit will not work. A rebreather at depth with too little pressure protection will not work. The water down there has had time to learn how to push. Bring something rated well past the depth you'd guess from looking at the surface.

Breathing. Closed-circuit rebreather. Open-circuit scuba with its bubbles would be wrong here — the place is silent, and bubbles would be a kind of shouting. They would also rise through the column and disturb things above, and the place is connected enough that disturbance somewhere is disturbance everywhere. Rebreather with extended duration. Three days of supply minimum, if you intend to stay any meaningful time.

Light. This is where the preparation gets less obvious. You need light — human eyes cannot see in this darkness without help. But the kind of light matters. The place already has its own: a faint green-gold wash from somewhere far above, that catches the uppermost growths and turns them slightly translucent. Your light should not be much brighter than this. A few soft lamps, preferably warm-green rather than harsh white. Anything brighter and you will see less, because you will bleach the shadows the place is built around. Bring a primary and a backup, but plan to use the primary sparingly. Plan, in fact, to use your unaided eyes for much of the time, after they have adjusted.

Movement. You cannot swim across this place. The corridors between the rope-plants, where the currents part them, go on for miles. If you have a small submersible, something slow and quiet, good. If not, plan to stay in one area and move on foot through the channels. The rope-plants are not rooted in single points — they are rooted in everything — and you can push through them, but it should be done slowly, with attention to which ones you disturb. They will part around you if you are patient. They will close behind you. The place forgets quickly, but you should still be polite about it.

Navigation. Bring a compass. Bring inertial navigation if you have it. The currents push, and they push gently enough that you will not notice for a while, and then you will notice you are somewhere you did not intend to be. Mark your return path somehow — a tether if you are in a suit, a beacon if you are in a vehicle. The masonry itself is too uniform to navigate by; everything has worn down to roughly the same shape, and the only distinguishing features are the channels, the currents, and the way the rope-plants lean in certain places. Learn to read these before you go down. They are the only map the place will give you.

Food and water. Bring what you need for the duration. There is nothing to eat down there. There is nothing to drink. The closed circuit of stone, plant, and water does not include anything you'd want to put in your mouth.

Duration. This is where preparation becomes something other than equipment. How long to stay? The place is slow. It does not reveal itself quickly. If you go down for an hour, you will see a vast dark space with some pale ropes in it. If you stay for a day, you will begin to notice the currents. If you stay for three days, sitting still most of the time, you will begin to hear the sounds. If you stay for a week, you may hear one of the long slow notes the stones make when they settle, and you will not have done anything to deserve it. A week is probably the right answer. A weekend is too short. A month is more than you can reasonably carry.

The sounds. They are mostly the water. A deep hum, below hearing. The occasional groan of a stone block settling a fraction further into its final position. Once an hour, maybe three times in an hour, a single low note when a braid of growth slips against a worn corner of granite. The sounds do not come together. They are not music. You should not bring instruments. You should not bring speakers. You should not bring any kind of recorded sound to play back into the water. The place is the way it is. If you want to record it, fine — but record only. Do not add.

What to wear under the suit. Soft layers. Nothing with hard edges that will clack against the hull, against the suit, against itself. The place is quiet, and you will hear every small sound you make, magnified by the suit and by the water and by your own attention.

The plants themselves. They are not dangerous. Probably. There have been no animals for a very long time, and so no selection pressure for toxins, or thorns, or anything a moving thing would have had to defend against. But you are a moving thing, and you are bringing novel chemistry down with you. Do not touch them with bare skin. Do not break them. They have been here longer than anything.

The stone. It looks settled. It is settled, mostly. But there are the groans, which mean the settling is not complete. Do not lean against the buttresses. Do not climb the terraces. Do not use the columns as anchors. The place is still in the process of becoming its final form. To disturb it now would be to disturb something that has spent a very long time becoming undisturbed.

The currents. There are places where they converge and the rope-plants lean all in one direction, in a slow hush. There are places where they part and the corridors widen and you can see further than you can see. The currents are slow but they are not weak. Do not fight them if they take you. Let them take you where they are going. If you are in a submersible, kill the motor and see where they put you. The place has weather of its own — slow storms, centuries long. The weather is patient.

Other people. There will not be any. This is worth preparing for. The place is closed-circuit, no animals, no other humans. If you are traveling with companions, you will see each other, mostly. If you are alone, you will see the place. Decide which one you want before you go.

What to expect to feel. This is the hardest preparation, and the one most people skip. You will feel small. Not in the way you feel small on a mountain, where the smallness is exhilarating. Small in the way you feel small when you realize the building you're standing in has been there for a thousand years and will be there for a thousand more — except more so, because the building has worn itself down to its essentials and is still there, and the things that built it are gone, and the things that replaced them are not really things, and you are something new again, briefly. You will feel like a temporary phenomenon. You will feel like a brief chemical accident against the long patience of stone and water. This is not a bad feeling. It is a true feeling. But it is one that some people do not recover from easily, and you should know that about yourself before you commit.

You will also, if you stay long enough, begin to feel something else. A kind of settling of your own. The place has weather, slow storms, centuries long. The plants sway in gyres that turn once every several minutes. There is a rhythm down there, very slow, and if you stop insisting on your own rhythms, you will start to feel it. This is the gift of the place. It is also something you should be prepared to lose when you come back up — the re-entry to surface time can be jarring. People who stay for the week sometimes have trouble with the first day back, with the noise, the speed, the way air moves differently than water.

What to bring back. Almost nothing. The place does not give up its material. If you bring a sample of the rope-plants, it will die, because the closed circuit it is part of is the only thing keeping it alive. If you bring a chip of stone, fine — the place has more stone. But you should not take much. The place is not a museum. It is a place. Going there is the thing, not taking from it.

How to go. This depends on your method of multiverse travel. The preparation is the same regardless. The way you get there, the door you step through, the coordinates you punch in — those are about the other side of the journey, not this one. Once you are at the threshold of the water, the preparation begins in earnest.

The threshold of the water. At some point you will be standing, or floating, at the edge of where the water begins. Maybe on a shore you can see is the wrong shape. Maybe at the surface of an ocean you can see goes down further than oceans should. Maybe stepping out of a vehicle into the water column itself. At this moment you should pause. Not out of ritual — because your body is about to do something it cannot undo. Once you are down there, you are committed for the duration of your air or your battery or your sanity, whichever comes first. So pause. Look at the water. Notice what color it is from this side. Notice how it moves against you. Notice whether you can see the bottom from here. Then go.

Down. The descent will take time. If you're in a submersible, it will be longer than you think, because the place is deeper than you think. The light will fade in stages. The green-gold wash from above will follow you down for a while, then thin out, then fail. The temperature will drop. The pressure will increase — even inside a vehicle, in the small sounds the hull makes. Eventually you will see the first of the stone shoulders. Then the rope-plants. Then you will be there.

There. Once you are there, the preparation is over. What remains is the visit itself. Move slowly. Listen. Notice where the currents are. Notice where the rope-plants lean. Notice the channels between them. Pick one. Follow it for a while. Then stop, somewhere, and stay. Watch. Listen. Feel the cold that has become a property of the water. Wait. See what comes to you, in the time you have.

The place is not in a hurry. You shouldn't be either.
