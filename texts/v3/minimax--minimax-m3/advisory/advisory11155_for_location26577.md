# Preparing to Visit the Chimney

You are about to step into a structure that is not, anymore, a structure. It is a body. A body that was poured, that was filled, that was sealed, that was abandoned, and that has spent decades since teaching itself to be a forest in a column. The preparations for entering it are mostly preparations for what it will do to you, because nothing in the chimney was designed to keep a person out, and very little in the chimney was designed to keep a person alive.

## First, accept the verticality

A hundred and forty feet across the base. Five hundred feet straight up. You will feel this before you understand it. Bring a reference point — a friend you can lose sight of within ten seconds. A flashlight will only show you the wall. To see the wall, you have to see it as a curve. To see the curve, you have to see it as a column. To see the column, you have to look up. You will look up. You will keep looking up. Pick a place on the floor where the drips are not falling and where a fern is not dripping onto it, and put your back to the wall, and let the wall take your weight. Lean. The wall will lean back. It is a thirty-minute sensation and then it becomes a sensation you have for a year, after.

## What to wear

Cotton. The chimney is wet. Everything in the chimney is wet. You are going to be wet for the entire time you are inside. The moisture is condensed, filtered, exhaled, transpired, dripped, but it is the same water, and it is on you, and it is in your socks within the first ten minutes. Cotton holds it against you. Synthetics will trap it and turn it to heat, and you do not want to be the warmest thing in the chimney. Wear layers you can take off. Wear a hat with a brim. A drop falling five hundred feet is a small painless event, but a drop falling five hundred feet and hitting you in the eye every four seconds is something you cannot sustain. The brim is not a comfort. The brim is a piece of equipment.

Wear closed boots. The floor is steel plate under a foot of black sediment, and the sediment is the remains of things that have been falling down the inside of the chimney for as long as the chimney has been a forest. It is soft. It will take your boot to the ankle without resistance. It is also the home of the pale elongated thing with too many legs. There is also, possibly, a snake. Wear boots that come above the ankle. Wear them laced.

Bring nothing that rusts. Nothing ferrous in your pack. Nothing with a mirror finish, if you can help it. The moisture in the air is aggressive. The smell of ferrous is not a metaphor for the chimney; it is the chimney. Your knife will rust. Your knife is going to rust anyway. Accept that.

## What to bring

A headlamp. One. A backup. The light that filters down from the top of the chimney is real light, it is the same light that falls on any forest clearing at midday, but it has been filtered through five hundred feet of humid air and through the bodies of the things that fly in the upper third, and by the time it reaches the floor it is gray-green and moving slightly, the way light moves under deep water. You can see in it. You cannot see well. A headlamp will solve this for a few hours and then you will turn it off, because the headlamp will startle the things in the upper third, and the things in the upper third are the part of the chimney you are most vulnerable to, and they are resting, and they will continue to rest as long as you do not give them a reason not to.

A water bottle. You will not drink from the bottle. You will set the bottle on a flat piece of steel and let it fill from the drips, and then you will drink from the bottle, because the drips are cleaner than the air you are breathing. The drips are not clean. The drips are not clean at all. But the air is not clean either, and the air is what will be in your lungs, and the drips are at least drips.

A watch. A mechanical watch, if you have one. The chimney has two clocks, the turbine and the bats, and neither of them is calibrated to anything you recognize. Your watch will keep its own time. The two clocks will keep theirs. You will need a third time to know which one you are in.

A camera, or something that holds a record. Bring more than you think. The light is a particular green-gray and it does not reproduce well, and your eyes will keep trying to fix it as a color from outside, and the inside will not be that color. The chimney will not look like a forest in the photographs. It will look like the inside of a chimney with plants in it. Take the photographs anyway. The thing that was in the chimney will not come out of the photographs. The chimney is what was in the chimney.

A field journal, or its equivalent, with a pencil. Pencils do not fail in humidity the way pens do. You will want to write down the smell, because the smell will fade in memory faster than anything else. The smell is the part of the chimney that stays with you. It is wet concrete and the inside of a machine and ferrous and sweet, sweet the way overripe fruit is sweet, sweet the way a wound is sweet. The smell will come back to you at unexpected moments for the rest of your life.

## What not to bring

Food. The chimney is a closed ecosystem in a soft sense, a closed system in a hard sense, and the drips are the only input from above, and the drips are not food. You will not be hungry while you are in the chimney. You will lose your appetite within the first hour, in a way that has nothing to do with willpower. The air is too wet. The air is too sweet. The smell is sweet, and the sweetness will sit in your stomach and tell you that you have already eaten, and the sensation will persist for as long as you are inside. Eat before you enter. Eat on the way out. Do not eat in the chimney.

A phone, unless you have to. The moisture will get into the charging port. The moisture will get into the speaker. The moisture will condense inside the lens. The chimney does not care about your phone. The chimney will continue to run for as long as the rebar holds. Your phone will fail in approximately forty minutes. Use the forty minutes.

A weapon. There is nothing in the chimney that a weapon would be useful against. The pale elongated thing with too many legs is the size of a forearm. The snake is a snake. The bats are bats. The translucent-winged things are the size of small birds at most, and they are not interested in you during the day, and you should not be in the chimney at dusk.

## The hours

Plan to enter in the morning. Plan to leave before dusk. The chimney has a day, which is the time from when the light comes in through the mouth at the top to the time the light begins to leave. The chimney has a dusk, which is when the things in the upper third wake up. The chimney has a night, which is not your night, and the chimney has a dawn, which is the sound of silk on stone, the second sound beneath the turbine, the long dark river coming back down. You do not want to be in the chimney for the night. You want to be in the chimney in the morning, when the light is establishing itself, when the drips are at their regular rhythm, when the bats are folded and silent, when the ferns are still.

## The first hour

Stand at the base. Put your back to the wall. Let the wall take your weight.

Listen.

There are two sounds. One is below hearing, the turbine, the rebar, the resonant wobble wandering by a fraction of a hertz. You will not hear it. You will feel it. You will feel it in the soles of your feet, in the bridge of your nose, in the small of your back. After a few minutes, you will stop feeling it as a sensation and begin to feel it as a fact, the way you feel the floor under you or the air in your lungs. The chimney is making a sound. The chimney has been making the sound for longer than the moss has been growing.

The other sound is above. It is the rustle. It is silk on stone. It will not stop. It is the bats, and the other things, the ones with the stretched translucent skin, and they are not moving, and yet the sound comes, and the sound will continue as long as you are in the chimney, and it will continue when you are not.

You are now in a place that has its own time, and the time is not yours, and you have to put yours down for a while to be there.

## The ferns

The ferns hang downward. The ferns have been hanging downward for decades. The fronds are six feet, ten feet, and they are heavy with the moisture they have caught, and they are slow, and they are beautiful in a way that does not ask anything of you. Do not touch them. They are not fragile. They are older than you are in their posture. They have been hanging there for as long as they have been growing, and they will be hanging there when you leave. Touch the moss. The moss is the color of wet slate, and it is dense, and it will yield a little under your fingers, and it will be cold. The moss is the only thing in the chimney that is colder than the air. The air is warm. The concrete is cool. The moss is the cold made solid, the cold of the wall, the cold that has been in the wall for a hundred years. Touching the moss is the closest you can come to touching the chimney itself.

## The climbing fig

Look up. The climbing fig has a single woody stem, and it has come all the way down from the upper third, and it is holding on with small adhesive pads wherever a seam gives it a foothold. The leaves are the size of dinner plates. The stem is the diameter of your wrist at the base and gets thinner as it goes up, and it goes up out of sight, and you do not want to follow it. The climbing fig is the chimney's spine, in a sense. The climbing fig is the chimney's hair, in another sense. The climbing fig is the part of the chimney that most resembles a forest. Do not climb it. The adhesive pads will hold you but the pads are not for you. The pads are for the fig. The pads do not know about you.

## The beetles

The beetles travel in lines. The lines follow the seams of the steel plate, where the sediment is thinnest. The beetles are lacquered black. The beetles are doing something. The beetles have been doing something for a long time. Sit near a line and watch for ten minutes. Do not interfere. The beetles are the part of the floor that is most visible. The rest of the floor is a film of motion over the hum of the turbine, a layer of things that feed on the things that feed on the drips, and the layer is dense, and the layer is not for you, but it is for the beetles, and the beetles are for the things above the beetles, and the things above the beetles are for the things above them, and the drips are for all of them, and the drips come from the mouth, and the mouth is where the rain comes in.

## The frogs

The small bright frogs are the color of a cut leaf. The small bright frogs sit on real leaves. The small bright frogs do not move. The small bright frogs are a kind of punctuation in the floor. They are not asleep. They are not awake. They are the part of the chimney that has decided not to move for a while. The snake is the opposite of the frogs. The snake is the part of the chimney that has decided to move. You will see a length of the snake once, drawn across a gap between two ferns, and then it will not be there. The snake is not a danger to you. The snake is a fact of the chimney. The chimney has a snake in it, the way a forest has a snake in it, and the chimney's snake has been a snake in the chimney for as long as the snake has been a snake.

## The light

The light comes down in a long pale column. The light is the color of a clouded sky, and then it is not, and then it is gray-green, and then it is moving slightly, the way light moves under deep water. The light is not constant. The light is the light of a forest clearing at midday, but it is being filtered, and the filter is the chimney, and the chimney is doing its own filtering, and the filtering is not a metaphor. The light at the top of the chimney is daylight. The light at the bottom of the chimney is the chimney's light. The chimney has had decades to make the light its own. Stand in the column. Let the column fall on you. The light will not warm you. The light will not give you vitamin D. The light will tell you where you are.

## The turbine

The turbine is under the floor. The turbine is in a sub-chamber that was sealed when the plant was decommissioned and the doors were bolted and the jungle was permitted to come back over everything. The turbine is running. The turbine has been running for longer than the moss has been growing. The turbine will run.

No one came to turn it off. No one could, perhaps, or the decision was made long ago and forgotten, or it runs on a feed from something that doesn't know how to stop.

You are not going to see the turbine. You are not going to open the sub-chamber. The sub-chamber was sealed. The jungle was permitted. The sub-chamber is a place where a decision was made and a door was closed and a thing inside the door has been doing its thing for as long as the things outside the door have been doing theirs. The two systems are not in conversation. They are in the same chimney. That is enough.

## The cracks

There are cracks in the concrete. The cracks are slow. The cracks follow the rusting rebar inside. The cracks widen by fractions of a millimeter a year. The cracks catch more spores. The cracks hold more water. The cracks will eventually, in a time longer than you will stand here, bring the chimney down, but the balance is held. The balance is held by everything in the chimney doing its thing. The balance is the chimney. The chimney is a thing that is holding itself together, and you are standing in it, and a drop falls on the back of your hand, warm.

## What to expect

You will be in the chimney for two hours. You will think you are in the chimney for twenty minutes. You will leave the chimney and you will not be able to describe the color of the light, and you will not be able to describe the sound, and you will not be able to describe the smell except to say it was wet and ferrous and sweet, and you will say these things, and the people you say them to will think you are describing a place, and you will be trying to describe a state.

You will lose the sound in your ears after the first few minutes. You will feel the sound for the rest of the day. You will feel the sound in your sleep that night, and you will not be sure if you are hearing it or remembering it, and you will be right both ways.

Your boots will be wet for two days. The black sediment will be in the treads. You will find a small beetle in the treads, lacquered black, alive. You will let it go outside, in a place that is not the chimney, and the beetle will not know what to do, and you will not know what to do about the beetle, and the beetle will go under a leaf, and you will keep the boots.

The chimney does not care about you. The chimney is not a place that has been waiting for you. The chimney is a place that has been doing its thing without you for a long time, and it will be doing its thing after you. You are a perturbation in the chimney's day, the way a leaf falling from a fern is a perturbation, the way a drop landing near the pale elongated thing is a perturbation. The chimney will absorb you and continue. The drips will fall on the back of your hand for as long as you hold it out, and the drips will fall on the back of your hand after you put it down, and the drips will not know the difference, and the difference is you.
