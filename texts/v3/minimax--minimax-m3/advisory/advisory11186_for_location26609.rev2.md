The first thing is to stop thinking of this as a city. The word will mislead you at every step. You are not entering a place that humans built, maintained, and forgot. You are entering a thing that laid itself down in layers when the machines were still here, and that has been running itself, unattended, for nine centuries, in the warm and the wet, and that has become, in the time since, something that no longer has a name in your language. The city is its metabolism. The walls, the channels, the spires, the fauna, the people — they are all parts of one large, slow, packed, continent-sized process. You are going to be a passenger in that process. The city will tolerate you. It will not, in any sense that matters to you, notice you. That is the first thing to understand.

The second thing is your body.

The body is where this place hits you first, and the body is what will fail you first if you are not careful. The heat is constant. The humidity is constant. There is no dry season; there is not even, in the deepest cuts of the city, a dry hour. You will arrive from somewhere with weather and you will think, *I understand humidity*. You do not. The humidity here is not weather. It is the medium. Water sits on every upward-facing surface. Condensation forms on your clothes, on your skin, on the inside of your eyelids. Sweat does not evaporate; it just sits, and then it migrates, and then it is somewhere else on you. Heat stroke is a real risk, especially in the first three days, because your body will keep trying to cool itself by methods that do not work. Acclimate before you go, if your home world has any way to simulate the conditions, even approximately. Saunas help. Hot yoga helps. Nothing fully helps. Plan to do almost nothing physical on day one.

Your skin is going to suffer. Maceration sets in within hours: the outer layers of your epidermis soften, white, and begin to slough. Fungal infections follow. The places where skin touches skin — between the toes, in the groin, under the arms, anywhere a fold holds moisture — will be the first to go. You need a clothing strategy that gets air to the skin while keeping the worst of the wet off it. Tight synthetics are worse than loose weave. Bare skin is worst of all, despite the instinct. The local people, whose skin is a kind of city material, do not have this problem. You do.

Your eyes need protection, and this is not optional. The locals' eyes are engineered for a reason, and the reason is not subtle: the air here is full of small things. Particulates carried by the condensing water of the warm upper atmosphere. Aerosolized biofilm from the deep infrastructure. The dust, if you can call it that, of the city's own weather. On a calm day in the lower avenues, you can feel your eyes film over within an hour. On a day when the spillers are active in the upper band, and the wind is moving the wrong way, and the downwelling brings some of that upper-air mess down to you, you can be functionally blinded for the span of an afternoon. Bring sealed goggles. Bring them in duplicate. Bring cloth to wipe them, because they will fog and film and need constant clearing, and your fingers are not clean enough to clear them with.

Your ears are the next problem, and the biggest. The city lives at 2.7 on a scale of three. That number is not abstract. It means the sound is *constant*, layered, and loud enough to damage you over hours, not days. The white-noise hiss of a million channels at once. The deep rhythmic pulse coming up through the floor — that one is subsonic, you feel it in your sternum before you hear it, and it does not stop. The high irregular chatter of fauna in the upper structures. The structural moan of the city itself settling and expanding and contracting in the warm and the brief cool of night. Over all of it, the spillers, who scream constantly, even asleep, and whose roost at dusk is a particular kind of torture that the description does not exaggerate. Hearing protection is not optional. It is not even sufficient. You need earplugs, you need over-ear protection, and you need to understand that even with both, the subsonic pulse will reach you through bone, and that this will, over time, do something to you that is not quite nausea and not quite anxiety and not quite dread, but is some persistent low-grade distress of the body that you will only notice when it stops, after you leave. Plan for hearing breaks. Plan to remove your protection in safe moments and let your ears recalibrate, in short doses. Plan to have a conversation in a low register with your hand over your mouth, like the locals do. They speak low because the city is loud. You will too, by the second day, whether you mean to or not.

The smell is the next thing, and it is the thing the description is polite about. The channels are full of biological activity. The biofilm the lurkers eat has a smell. The channel-worms, in the trillions, have a smell. The gullet-fish that eat the worms have a smell. The slashers that eat the gullet-fish have a smell. The runoff that collects in the lower cuts of the city has a smell that is its own kind of punishment. You will, within a day, become familiar with a vocabulary of odors that you cannot currently imagine. None of them are pleasant. Some of them are signals — the smell of a channel going anoxic, the smell of a slasher hunting nearby, the smell of a wall section that is starting to dry out and die — and you will need to learn them. The locals can read this vocabulary without thinking. You will, at first, be unable to distinguish any of it. Plan for this. A respirator that filters particulates is useful; one that filters odor is better. Bring something that fits under the chin, because the smell is worse at face level.

The light is strange, but it is not hostile. In the lower avenues it is a diffuse, warm, faintly green-tinged thing — the color of a forest at noon, except there is no forest. In the upper spires it is brighter and whiter, and at sunset the upper city turns the color of a heated piece of metal, which is a kind of beauty, and the spillers scream louder because something in the light bothers them in a way that has not been solved in nine centuries. The light will not hurt your eyes the way the noise will hurt your ears, but it will play tricks on your sense of time. The diffuse green-tinged light in the lower city has no shadows, no clear horizon, no anchor to the hour. Bring a personal light source of known spectrum and intensity — something warm and white and calibrated — and use it to mark your day. Eat when your light says morning. Sleep when your light says night. Let the city's light be background.

The food and water situation requires care. There is no plant life here. There has not been plant life for a long time. The locals eat the fauna, and the fauna eat each other, and the whole thing runs on the city's own biochemical output. You, as a visitor, are not part of this system and should not pretend to be until you have been briefed by someone who is. Bring your own food for at least the first week. Bring your own water, or a filter capable of handling biological contamination at densities you have not seen before. Do not drink from a channel. Do not eat anything that pulses. Do not eat anything you cannot identify. Once you have been with a local for a few days and they have decided you are real, they will feed you, and the food will be strange and dense and probably a little alive, and that will be the moment you understand that you have, in some small way, been accepted into the city's metabolism. Until then, eat what you brought.

The fauna are the part of this that can kill you, and you should treat them with the seriousness that killing deserves. Most of them will not kill you. The lurkers, despite being the size of a transit car, are slow, gentle, and uninterested in you. They feed on the biofilm; they have mouths, but the mouths are not used for much. They are the city's sanitation. Walk around them. Do not startle them — I do not know what they do when startled, and the description does not say, and that is the kind of gap in the data you should respect. The channel-worms are not dangerous individually. Do not put your head in a channel. The gullet-fish are mid-level predators; they live in the deep channels, mostly, but they can come up. The slashers are the thing to actually fear.

The slashers are large and red and fast, and they come up out of the deep channels into the avenues when they are hungry, and they eat people sometimes, and *sometimes* is the operative word. You will not be able to negotiate with one. You will not be able to outrun one. The way to survive slashers is to not be where they are. They are rare in the middle altitudes where the people live; they are common in the deep channels and the avenues that border the deep channels. The locals know which avenues are slasher avenues this month, because the slasher pattern shifts with the worm populations, and the worm populations shift with the water, and the water shifts with the city's own turbulence. Ask. Listen. Do not assume that an avenue you crossed yesterday is safe today. If you hear the wet continuous sigh of channel-worms stop, in a localized patch, go the other way. If you feel a sudden absence of the deep pulse through the floor, in a localized patch, go the other way. If you see a red motion at the edge of vision, do not look at it. Look somewhere else and walk away with intention.

The dead zones are the other danger, and it is the danger of absence, not presence. The dead parts of the city are the parts that have dried out. The infrastructure does not work there. The lights are out. The pumps are out. The walls do not weep. The fauna do not go there, because there is nothing to feed on. The people avoid them. The dead parts are the closest thing to open space inside the walls, and they will look, to a newcomer, like relief. They are not relief. They are places where the city has stopped being a city. You cannot get help there. You cannot be heard there. You cannot orient by the heartbeat, because there is no heartbeat. If you have to cross a dead zone, do it fast, do it during the brief cool of night when the fauna are quieter, do not stop, and bring your own light.

The people are real. By consensus they are real. By your standards, you will have to decide. There are about a hundred thousand of them in the entire continent-sized city, which is not many, and which is enough, and which means that you will go for days without seeing one. When you do, the encounter will be strange. They are warm and wet and loud like the city. Their skin is a kind of city material. Their eyes are protected. They speak in low registers. They listen with their whole bodies, the way a person in a dark room listens. They have been here, in some form, since the machines left, and they have engineered themselves to fit, the way every other fauna has engineered itself to fit. They will know you before you know them. You will be loud to them in a way you cannot hear yourself being loud. You will move in a way that signals *I do not belong here*. They will see this. They will decide, between themselves, whether to engage with you. They are not obligated to. Be patient. Be quiet. Be willing to be tolerated, which is all the city offers, and which is all they offer, and which is enough.

The middle altitude is where you want to be. It is the band where the air is breathable, where the water is not too thick with worms, where the lurkers cannot easily reach, where the slashers are rare, where the people live. The upper band is for the spillers and the wind and the things that fly in the particulates. The lower band is for the channels and the slashers and the deep infrastructure. Stay in the middle. Sleep in the middle. Eat in the middle. You will be tempted, especially in the first days, to climb up toward the brighter light, or down toward what looks like solid ground. Resist the temptation. The middle is where the city is kindest to people-shaped things.

The turbulence is the hardest part to internalize, and it is the part that will break your sense of place if you let it. A wall that was weeping in the morning is sweating in the afternoon. A spire that was upright last month is leaning this month. A new one is being extruded from a different part of the block. A channel that was clear yesterday is clogged today. The geography you memorized yesterday is the wrong geography for today. Do not memorize. Do not plan a route that depends on a route being there tomorrow. Move with the city. Use the heartbeat through the floor to orient. Use the structural moan to read what is settling and what is growing. Use the locals, when you find them, as your map. They live in the turbulence; they have learned to think in turbulence; they can tell you what the city is doing today in a way no static description can.

The equipment you need is mostly defensive. Sealed goggles, in duplicate. Hearing protection, in duplicate. A respirator that handles particulates and ideally odor. Synthetic close-weave clothing that breathes, in layers, all of it replaceable because it will not last. A personal light source of known character, to anchor your day. Water, or a serious filter. Food for at least the first week. A sleep system that handles constant humidity — most off-world sleep systems will fail within days; the locals sleep on platforms that wick, and they sleep short, and you should plan to do both. A way to signal distress that works in this environment — the sound will swallow a whistle, the city will swallow a flare, the locals respond to low-register vibration more than they respond to light. A knife is useful. A weapon against a slasher is not useful. A way to leave, on short notice, that does not depend on infrastructure you do not understand.

The psychology is the last thing, and it is the thing that will determine whether you come back intact. This place is overwhelming in a way that is hard to prepare for. The constant noise. The constant moisture. The constant fauna. The absence of horizon, of sky, of plant life, of anything you would recognize as green. The strangeness of the people. The fact that you are, most of the time, completely alone in a packed city. The fact that the city itself does not, in any way you can feel, care whether you are there. Plan a limit on your stay. Plan a way to leave that you trust. Bring something that anchors you to your own world — a sound, a smell, an object, a person you can talk to by means that bypass the city's noise. The city will tolerate you. The city will not remember you. That is the contract. Take it or do not come.
