# Preparing to Visit the Workshop on the High Shoulder

## Reading the Place Before You Go

The first thing I'd want to do, before any packing list, is sit with what this place *is* — because the workshop doesn't reward the kind of traveler who treats it like a curiosity to be photographed and ticked off. This is a place mid-becoming. The stone is still deciding what stone it wants to be. Whatever intelligence is doing the building is patient in a way that makes human patience look like impatience. You are going to be a guest in a workshop where the work has been going on for a very long time and will continue for longer after you've gone, and your job is mostly to not be in the way.

The bowl is small and the air is thin. The bowl is warm and the rock beneath you is warmer. Water falls, climbs, and stands still on principle. There are creatures — many creatures — doing the small labor of a world that isn't finished assembling itself. There are filaments in the stone, pulsing with light that doesn't travel. There is a seat near the center that was worked into the rock by something. There is a broken pillar that probably didn't originate here. The place is loud with work-sound, and quiet with everything else.

That's the place. Now — how do you go there without ruining your visit, or the place's afternoon?

## Body, Before Everything Else

**The altitude will get you first, and it will get you faster than you expect.** This is the single thing I would not negotiate on. Even if you're a seasoned high-altitude traveler, this combination — thin air *plus* a long approach that may involve teleportation, dimensional transfer, or some mode of displacement your body isn't used to — can compound in ways you don't fully feel until you're already several hours in. Headache that arrives like a polite stranger and stays like a relative. Nausea without warning. A fog over your thinking that you'll mistake for the place's strangeness, and it's partly the place, but it's also the oxygen.

I'd want to come up on this gradually if I could. Spent a night lower down, on the mountain's flank, around two or three thousand meters of equivalent elevation if the local scale is readable. Drank a liter of water before I even set out for the bowl. Brought more water than I thought I'd need — the air is wet, but wet air at altitude dehydrates you faster than dry air, because your body is working harder to breathe it and not noticing.

**Eat before you go, and bring food that doesn't ask much.** The bowl is warm and damp; your appetite may quietly leave. But your body is doing extra work in thin air, and the walk back, if you do walk back, will be hungry work. Nuts. Dried fruit. Something dense and salted. Not because this is a strenuous hike in the ordinary sense, but because the air is doing something to your metabolism that you won't appreciate until you don't.

**Dress for warm rain that is not quite rain.** The water here doesn't fall so much as *condense* — on you, on everything. Anything waterproof will become a sauna. Anything absorbent will become a sponge you'll be wearing all day. The right answer is something in between: layers that breathe, a shell that you can pull on when the actual drizzle starts (and there will be a real drizzle, sometimes, falling out of the clouds that sit in and around the bowl). Wool, not cotton. Cotton here is a punishment. The warmth from below means cold isn't your enemy — but wind coming up over the lip of the bowl is, if a weather pattern comes in.

**Footwear is not optional to think about.** The rock is wet, and the rock is uneven, and the rock has seams that are slicker than they look. Some of those seams are *filaments* — the hairline lines of not-stone that pulse with their own light — and if you step on one bare-footed you may not be hurt but you will feel something, and what you feel may not be entirely yours. I'd want closed boots, broken in, with soles that could grip a wet slope. Bring a second pair of socks. Your feet will be wet for most of the day. Plan for it. Don't fight it.

## What the Place Will Do to You (and What to Do Back)

**Your senses will lie to you, gently.** The water climbs. The droplets stand still. Something in a pool regards you. The moss flickers in colors that don't have names. The closed fist opens, briefly, to a flower-shape you've never seen. None of this is dangerous in the way your reflexes will assume — none of it is an attack, or a trap, or a predator. But it is *strange*, in a particular layered way: strange like a dream you can't quite remember, strange like a sentence in a language you almost speak. The temptation will be to grab, to pick up the small smooth knobs that look like artifacts, to touch a filament to see what happens, to put your hand into a pool and meet whatever is looking back.

Touch lightly, if you touch. The filaments dim when you press them, then recover. The knobs are *fossils*, in a sense — they've weathered into the stone, and the place has chosen them to be there. They're not souvenirs. Picking one up may not harm anything but it will change something, and you won't know what.

**Sit in the seat.** The worked node near the center of the bowl, the only artifact that unambiguously intends, was made to be sat in. Sit in it. Not for any metaphysical reason — or if there is a metaphysical reason, it's not one I can predict — but because sitting down does two things: it lowers your center of gravity in a place where the air and the light and the small industrious sounds can otherwise make you feel slightly unmoored, and it slows you down to the pace of the place. The bowl builds itself the way frost builds. Slow. If you stand and rush and turn, you will not see the building. If you sit, you might.

**The creatures are part of the building.** I would not kill one. I'd not even knock one away. The essay is gentle about them, almost affectionate — too many legs, none of them right, gliding things, small lights moving in patterns — and they make the sound of the place. They are, very literally, part of what is being made. To brush one off a rock is to brush a brick off a wall being laid by someone you cannot see. I don't know that the place would punish you. But the place would *notice*, in a way that might make the rest of your visit quieter, and you don't want a quieter visit. You want the full loud working-sound. You want the place to continue treating you as a visitor whose presence has not yet been processed.

**Don't eat anything that grows here.** The closed fist will open. The flower inside will be pale and strange and almost certainly not food. The moss-flickers are wrong in color. The water in the pools is suspiciously clear. None of this is for you. Bring what you need from elsewhere.

**The pool-regard.** Something in the small pools looks back. You can look back. Hold still. Don't reach in. That's all. The essay describes it as "not an animal, not a face, just a *regard*," and that's the right word for it. What you do, in response, is hold still and let it be. Don't try to make it into something you have categories for. Don't try to talk to it. Don't try to photograph it, not because photography is forbidden but because a camera is a kind of hunger, and this place responds best to attention that isn't trying to take anything.

## What to Bring (and What to Deliberately Leave)

**Bring:**
- Water, in a metal bottle, more than you think. The wet air is deceptive.
- High-energy food that doesn't spoil and doesn't need cooking.
- A light shell and layers beneath it. Wool base. Wool mid. A shell that shrugs drizzle.
- Broken-in waterproof boots with grip. Two pairs of socks. Liner socks.
- A head covering — not for cold, but because your hair will be wet within an hour and the sensation is more distracting than you expect.
- A small notebook and a soft pencil. I would not bring an electronic device. The place is not hostile to them, but electronics tend to behave strangely in places where the light doesn't dim and the air has too much water in it, and you'll find yourself *wanting* to not have a screen to look at. The notebook lets you leave a record that is yours without being a museum piece.
- A small cloth. For wiping your face, your hands, the lens of anything you brought that has a lens. The condensation is constant.
- Something to sit on, if you don't trust the worked seat — a folded wool blanket, light. The stone is warm but the wetness gets through. You may want a dry layer between you and it for a long visit.
- If you have any medication you need, in a waterproofed container. The altitude plus the dampness plus whatever else is going on here can interact with medications you take for granted at home.
- A simple first-aid kit. Blister care, because you will get blisters if you walk the rim.

**Deliberately leave:**
- Expectations that this place operates on your schedule. The closed fist opens when it opens. The droplets climb when they climb. You are not the main character of this afternoon.
- Any plan to *understand* the place in the sense of being able to explain it to someone else. You can describe what you saw. You will not be able to explain what it meant. These are different projects.
- Any intention to take anything. No knobs, no stones, no filaments, no water, no moss. Leave what is here. The place will still be here tomorrow, and it will appreciate your visit more if it remains itself afterward.
- A weapon, even a small one. There is nothing here that requires defending yourself against, and bringing a weapon into a workshop is a kind of rudeness I can't quite define but feel strongly.

## How Long to Stay, and How to Leave

I would not rush the arrival, and I would not overstay. The bowl is a forty-meter oval. You can walk its perimeter in fifteen minutes. You can see the notable features — the worked seat, the broken pillar, the flat plate with the impossible edge, the ring of stones around a dry place — in an hour. But the place is not really an itinerary of features. The place is the *event* of being made, which happens continuously, which you can watch for as long as you have attention to give it.

I would budget a half-day minimum. Arriving in the morning, when the light is most even and the small lights in the bowl have to compete least with a sun somewhere above the clouds. Sitting in the seat early, before you've done anything else. Letting the creature-sound wash over you without trying to parse it. Watching a closed fist open once, all the way through, before you start walking. Looking into one of the small pools and being looked at back. Noticing, toward the end, that the warmth from below has been working its way up through the soles of your boots for hours, and your feet are warmer than the air should allow.

Then leaving.

The leaving matters as much as the arriving. Don't go at a run. Don't go feeling like you've stolen something. Say something if a quiet word feels right to say — the place won't hear it as a word, but you'll hear yourself say it, and that closes a circuit. Walk back to wherever you came in from. Descend if you ascended. Drink water. Eat your dense, salted food. Notice that your ears are still ringing faintly with the working-sound even an hour after you've left the bowl — they aren't, actually, but the absence of the sound will feel like ringing for a while, because your attention has been resting in it.

## A Final Quiet Note

I think the thing I'd most want to say to whoever goes is this: the place is being made. You are inside a making. The layer laid down beneath your feet while you visit is one of an uncountable number, and you stood on it for an hour, and you'll never stand on that particular layer again — when you leave, the making continues on top of where you were.

That's not a small thing. Treat it accordingly. Not with reverence, exactly — reverence can be its own kind of taking. But with the kind of attention you'd give to a friend's kitchen while they're cooking dinner for you. You are in a place where someone is making something, and you have been allowed in, and the best thing you can do is be a guest who doesn't interrupt the work, who notices what's happening, who is quiet in the right way, and who leaves when the work is still going and the place is still happy to have been where it is.

Sit in the seat. Watch a fist open. Let something in a pool look at you.

Then go home and write it down in your notebook, because the place will still be working, and so will you, and both those processes deserve a witness.
