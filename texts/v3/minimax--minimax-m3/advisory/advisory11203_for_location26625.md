# Preparation for the High Plateau

## The Body First

Before anything else, a traveler should understand that this place is a low-grade negotiation between the body and the air. The elevation is the kind that doesn't kill you but doesn't let you forget itself either — temples, lungs, the backs of the eyes. A traveler coming from anywhere near sea level should plan for at least two or three days of acclimatization before attempting to sleep at altitude. The body wants water in greater quantity than feels reasonable. It wants salt, and it wants carbohydrates, and it wants them often, in small amounts. Appetite is unreliable. Nausea is common in the first twenty-four hours. None of this is a warning sign. It is the price of admission.

Clothing is the most important object you will bring, and it should be layered with a specificity that feels almost absurd until the wind finds the gap. The wind finds every gap. Layers should be:

- A base layer against the skin that wicks, because the body will sweat under effort and then chill instantly when the wind catches the damp.
- A mid-layer of fleece or wool that holds warmth even when wet, and the body will get wet, from sweat, from the dampness that beads on every surface, from crossing the meltwater channels in the morning.
- A shell that is not merely windproof but *abrasion-proof*, because the wind here carries grit, and grit will work its way through anything flimsy in a matter of hours. Zippers should be covered or baffled. Hoods should be adjustable and ideally helmet-compatible if you wear one.
- Gloves. Two pairs. A light pair for dexterity and a heavy pair for standing still.
- A buff or scarf for the lower face. The mouth dries in minutes at this wind. Breathing through the nose is the body's default mode here, and a traveler should practice it in advance — mouth-breathing on this plateau is a route to cracked lips, sore throat, and a particular kind of misery that compounds across hours.
- Headwear that covers the ears and doesn't have a brim that will catch the wind like a sail. Sun exposure is severe; bring sunglasses with side coverage and high-altitude UV rating, and accept that the eyes will still water in the wind.

Footwear should be waterproof but breathable — the dampness is everywhere, and the traveler will cross wet channels. Boots should be broken in. Blisters at altitude heal slowly and become problems fast. Bring your own socks, enough for a change twice a day, and accept that they will rarely dry fully.

Sleeping system: a four-season rating is not paranoia. The wind will not stop at night, and a summer night on a high plateau in steady wind is colder than forecast. A closed-cell foam pad beneath an inflatable gives redundancy if the inflatable is punctured by the chert flakes that lie in every streambed.

## Water, and the Question of Water

The plateau gives water and takes it back in the same gesture. The morning channels carry meltwater that evaporates by afternoon; the standing pools are drinkable but should be filtered or treated, both for sediment and for whatever the foxes and ibex leave behind. A traveler should not rely on finding water at any specific time of day — the gullies that run at nine may be dry at noon. Plan to fill bottles at every opportunity.

Paradoxically, the danger is dehydration in the presence of moisture. The wind strips water from skin and breath faster than thirst registers. A traveler should drink on a schedule, not on sensation. The sensation lags the reality by hours. Electrolytes help; plain water at altitude, in volume, washes salts out and brings on cramping.

## Food and the Slow Burn

The body at altitude wants calories and wants them steadily. Plan for more food than seems reasonable — perhaps double what you would eat at home. Dense, fatty, salty foods do best: hard cheese, cured meats, nuts, chocolate, dried fruit, butter, tallow. The marmots of this place would approve of your choices. Avoid anything that requires a lot of water to prepare. The mouth is dry; chewing is work.

Cooking in the wind is a small art. A sheltered stove setup behind a boulder in the lee of the wind is essential; an exposed flame will be beaten flat and useless. Bring a windscreen. Accept that hot food is a luxury, not a guarantee.

## Movement and the Body's Adjustments

Walking on the plateau is its own education. The footing is mostly stable — pale stone, packed sand — but the wind changes the cost of every step. Walking into the wind is uphill. Walking with it is downhill in disguise; the body wants to lean forward and the gait becomes awkward. A traveler should expect to slow down and should not interpret slowness as fatigue. It is wind.

The boulders that look placed are not placed, but they are slippery in a particular way — polished smooth on their windward faces, lichen-dark on their leeward, often undercut at the base where the wind has done its patient work. Hands go on rock often. Gloves save the knuckles.

The most dangerous stretch is the gullies in the morning, when they run. The water is shallow but the stones beneath it are the polished kind, and a fall in cold water at altitude is the kind of mistake that ends expeditions. Cross early, cross carefully, or wait for the channels to wane in late morning.

## Time and the Light

A traveler should set their watch by the light, not by the clock. The plateau has its own hours. The morning is the time of long shadows and marmot calls and the channels running and the ibex moving on the upper ridges. The midday is the time of flat light and the land showing its true color — dust and old bone — and a strange visual exhaustion where the eye finds no shadows to measure by. The afternoon is the time when the gullies fill with shadow and the place reveals its depth, and this is the best time to move across open ground because the shadows show the channels that were invisible at noon.

The dampness peaks in the early hours and again near dusk. A traveler should expect everything they own to be damp on waking. Tent flies, sleeping bags, clothing — all will carry a fine moisture that is not quite rain and not quite condensation but something the place manufactures from the air. Ziplock bags are not paranoia. Paper becomes limp. Leather becomes stiff and then soft. Electronics should be sealed.

## The Wind Itself

The wind is not weather here. It is the medium. A traveler should plan for it the way a sailor plans for the sea — not as something to be endured but as something to be read. The whistle through the spine of harder rock tells you where the harder rock is. The bark off a particular shelf tells you the shape of that shelf. The moan from a gully mouth tells you the gully is deeper than it looks. Listen for an hour and the wind becomes a map.

There will be moments when the wind pauses. These moments are startling. The body has been compensating without knowing it — shoulder turned, head lowered, breath routed through the nose — and when the wind stops, the body over-corrects and sways slightly. Enjoy the pause. It will not last long. Use it to take a bearing, to hear the marmots, to notice the fox you didn't see moving across the flats.

## The Slight Wrongness

The plateau has a quality that the description names correctly. It looks worked. The boulders are too balanced, the channels too evenly spaced, the pools too round. A traveler should not try to solve this. It is not a puzzle. It is not a sign. It is the way this particular place has worn itself into its current shape over a time that the mind cannot easily hold. The traveler should notice it, sit with it, and let the noticing fade. It will return at odd moments — at night, in the pauses between gusts, in the second or third day — and each time it returns it will feel slightly less strange and slightly more familiar, until eventually it just becomes the way the place looks, and the question of whether it was worked or just worn will stop mattering.

Do not try to take the chert flakes as souvenirs. The place has almost nothing left of the people who passed through, and what remains should remain. A traveler who lifts a flake takes a small thing from a place that has little to spare, and the absence will be noted in the stone.

## What to Bring, Concretely

- A pack that fits close to the body and does not catch the wind like a flag.
- A shelter that pitches fast and sheds wind — a floorless shelter in the lee of a boulder is often sufficient; a full tent is heavier but warmer.
- A water treatment system and bottles that seal well.
- Sunscreen of the highest rating, and lip balm with SPF, applied often.
- A small first-aid kit oriented toward blisters, sprains, and the headache of mild altitude.
- A headlamp with spare batteries; the cold and the wind sap batteries.
- A repair kit: tape, patches, spare buckles.
- A notebook. The light here rewards looking. The place changes by the hour and rewards the kind of attention that writing makes possible.

## What to Leave Behind, Concretely

- Schedules. The plateau does not run on them.
- The expectation that you will master the place. You will pass through it. It will continue.
- The desire to name what you are feeling. The body will oscillate between cold and warm, between exertion and rest, between hearing the wind and not hearing it. Let this happen.
- Anything that cannot survive the damp, the grit, or the wind.

## A Final Note

The place is content. The description is precise about this. Nothing is changing. The wind will still be wearing the rock long after the traveler has gone down to lower elevations and resumed the life that the plateau interrupted. A traveler who arrives prepared and moves carefully and sits in the lee of the right boulder at the right hour will not see anything dramatic. They will see stone, and wind, and dampness, and the small persistent signs of things that are alive. This is the whole of it. It is enough.
