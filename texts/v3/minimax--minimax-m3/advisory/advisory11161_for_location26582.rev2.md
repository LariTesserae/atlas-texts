# Preparing for the Hollow

There is no urgency in this place, and so the first thing to leave at home is urgency itself. The hollow does not need anything from you, and the worst way to arrive is encased in the tense little bubble of someone expecting a place to perform. Bring unhurriedness. Bring curiosity that is willing to outlast its first hour. The hollow is not interested in your story, but it will, in its own slow way, take you in if you let it.

That said — a body still has body needs, and a body unprepared for what is in here will inform you, with increasing firmness, that it is the wrong species for the joint. So let us go over the practical edges first, and then let some of the other things come to the surface.

## The Light

The hollow is dim by design and dim by long habit. The sky up there is suspected rather than seen — a paler patch of grey or weak milk that brightens a degree between high sun and no sun, but does not, at any hour, become a sky you can read. Your eyes will open wide and find the world to be the color of a closed book. If you intend to do anything finer than sitting and breathing, **bring your own light**, and choose it with care. Cool blue-white LEDs are wrong here. They will make the stone look chalky and your pupils will refuse to reopen to the dim afterwards. A warm, low-output lamp — beeswax candle, oil lantern, a small halogen or amber-bulb flashlight on its dimmest setting — will let you see without flattening the place into a museum case. Plan on spending most of your time *without* light on, learning to move in the half-dark. Objects reveal themselves by their edges and the way they take the floor's faint rise toward the walls. After an hour or two you will not be able to return to bright rooms without wincing.

Bring a headlamp for the descent only, with a red mode if you have one — red light is a kindness to the place and to the plants, which the description suggests are committed to life but not committed to *glare*.

## Water

This is the most important preparation, and the easiest to underweight. There is no water in the hollow. The cracks in the floor are dry. The plants are dry plants. The air is dry, and warm, and mineral, and it will pull moisture from you and from anything porous you bring through the gap in the rim. Your drinking water is your drinking water, and nothing in here will add to it. Plan on **three liters per person per day minimum**, more if you move around — the air, although still, is desiccating in a way that a humid afternoon is not. Breath in, breath out: you will notice your throat tighten within the first hour. Carry water in a vessel that is not transparent (sun-warmed plastic flasks are regretful). A clay or unglazed ceramic canteen, well sealed, is the right note for this place. Treat it as part of your arrival, not your luggage.

There is also the question of washing. The hollow will not give you a basin. Bring a small cloth and a little water set aside for the face and hands, but go easy — the mineral skin is a feature here, and you will feel more part of the hollow if you let yourself become a little mineral too.

## What to Wear

The floor is hard-packed sediment that has been keeping its own counsel for a long time. It will not give under your heel. Closed footwear with a soft sole (leather or felt) is the right call — something that lets you feel the polygonal cracks without bruising the ball of your foot, and that does not announce every step with a rubber squeak. The hard rubber tread of a hiking boot will be wrong here for two reasons: it will be loud in a place where footsteps are among the loudest sounds you will make for hours, and it will feel, after the second day, like wearing armor to a tea.

Dress in loose, natural-fiber layers. The hollow is warm near the floor and cooler near the pale patch above. Strange to think of vertical climate in a forty-foot space, but the description has it right: the air stratifies a little, and if you climb to one of the higher seams where the olive plants are clustered, you will feel a cooler band. A linen shirt and a wool shawl or light wrap covers most of what this place asks of you.

Whatever you wear, **do not wear perfume**. The mineral air carries scent poorly, and it carries synthetic scent *very* well, into stillness, where it will hang for the whole of your visit and announce you to whichever of the small dry plants has any opinion on the matter.

## How to Enter

The rim is not a thing so much as a suggestion. Three uneven arcs of stone, each one curving inward at the top, with the gaps between them the size of, perhaps, a doorway or perhaps a window — varying. Pick the **broadest, lowest gap**, which will likely be the one facing whatever passes for a prevailing sun in the world you've arrived in. The stone is the color of old bone and it will be hard and warm to the touch on the sunward side. Bring leather gloves for the descent — there are handholds, but the worn corners of the stone mean that some of the most obvious ones are not the most reliable. Test before leaning weight. Test again.

The drop from rim to floor is roughly forty feet but the curving inwards of the walls means the lowest practical descent point is somewhere you cannot see from above. Bring a length of rope about fifty feet long, even if you suspect you can climb down without it. The walls are bone-pale and the floor is grey-brown and the contrast will fool your depth perception on the way down. Once your feet are on the floor, give your eyes a minute. The hollow will look like nothing at first. Then it will *fill in*, and you will see you've been inside something that has been quietly paying attention to its shape for longer than you can imagine.

## Time of Arrival

The hollow doesn't care, but your body will have preferences. **A late-afternoon arrival** is the kindest: you will get the brightest hour of the pale patch during the descent, and you will arrive into the slow easing of dim toward dusk, which is the hollow's master key. Your first night will be a long quiet thing in which the place itself, by being so patient with you, will teach you how to be a little more patient with yourself.

Morning arrivals are fine but you will, after first light, find the place a touch more insistent in its brightness — the polygonal cracks glint faintly around noon, and the warmth at floor level rises a real degree or two, which the body notices in spite of itself.

## What to Bring, Beyond the Water and Light

- **Sleeping.** A thin mat or blanket, no tent. The hollow is its own shelter. Set your sleep against the polished scooped place in one of the walls — the place where someone once sat, or once leaned, or once slept. You will not be the first to do so, and being there gives you company you cannot see.
- **Food**, but think light, dry, non-aromatic. Hard bread, dried fruit, a little cheese wrapped in oiled cloth. Nothing strong-smelling. The hollow will share its silence with you and the very least you can do is return the favor.
- **A book**, if you read. Pencil only — no ink, no risk of a dropped pen staining the cracked floor, which would feel small and would feel enormous here.
- **A small mirror**, on a string, for sighting up through the pale patch — useful if you have to know what time of day it is or whether clouds have arrived. Don't use it often. Trust the dim.
- **Salt.** The air is faintly alkaline. You will taste mineral at the back of your throat and your tongue will begin to crave salt by the second day. A pinch under the tongue at dusk is not a bad ritual.
- **A small broom of soft twigs**, only if you are staying long. The floor is very clean by itself, but it is also very settled, and a few crumbs from your food will sit, conspicuously, in the cracks. Brush them gently into a seam at the wall, where the olive plants might welcome them.

## What *Not* to Bring

Most of the modern world, frankly. No speakers, no screens except as a closed device you do not turn on, no bright cloths, no synthetic fabrics that rustle or shed. Heavy boots. Any plan that involves shouting across the hollow to a companion. Any plan, period, that requires *doing a thing* for more than an hour a day. The hollow will not punish you for activity, but it will gently make you feel you have missed something by not stopping.

## On Being There

The hardest preparation is for the stillness. Visitors from places with wind and background noise arrive, often, unable to sit for ten minutes without checking themselves. They itch. They speak into it. They fill. Give yourself a day, if you can, in which the entire project is **noticing what the silence is not absence of**. After a while you will hear: the seed pod tick. The pebble pop at dusk, if it is dusk. A small dragging thing — a fingernail-length scorpion almost certainly, behaving itself, or a beetle the color of the cracks walking across a crack, which will look for a moment like it isn't moving at all. Your own pulse in your temples, which you have not heard in years because no place has been this quiet since you were a child in a room of your own.

You will, by the second or third day, understand why the marks in the wall are old and not going anywhere. You will feel the speed at which the hollow expects time to pass, and you will, if you let yourself, slow down to it. You may sleep an hour longer than usual. You may notice hunger in a different way — a quiet thing, asking rather than insisting. You will probably want to write something, possibly several somethings. Write them and leave them folded under a stone on the floor, or burn them with the beeswax candle if you have one. The hollow is not interested in your story, and it will hold a paper longer than it will hold a memory.

## The Plants

Do not pick them. There are perhaps a dozen. You can lean close and see the dull olive is not really dull at all but a complex dusty green more patient than your greens know how to be. They are the only other living things down here besides you. Smell one — it will smell like sun-warmed stone after rain, a memory this place does not have access to but the plants have somehow.

If you must touch, touch the leaf once with the back of a finger, briefly, the way you might touch a sleeping animal. Then leave it alone.

## On Leaving

Decide in advance how long you are staying and stick to it. The hollow is patient on a timescale you do not have. Take everything with you. Sweep the floor where you slept. Leave the scooped wall exactly as it was. If you have placed a paper folded under a stone, take the paper; leave the stone.

The final thing to do, before the climb back up to the rim, is to stand in the center for one minute with the light off, and let the hollow be itself without your watching. It will not perform a thing for you. It will simply go on, and you will know that the pale patch will brighten by a degree and dim by a degree for as long as this shape of stone can hold itself, and that the small plants will go on being small, and that the silence will rest in its own shape, undisturbed.

Then climb. Your eyes will be wrong about the daylight for ten minutes once you are over the rim. Be patient with that, too. You are not the same animal who came down.
