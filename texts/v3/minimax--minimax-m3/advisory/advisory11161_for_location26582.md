# Preparing for the Hollow

The first thing to understand about this place is that it is not trying to give you anything. There is no view, no vista, no moment of arrival where the landscape opens up and rewards the journey. The hollow offers itself the way a cupped hand offers itself — palm up, empty, patient. You will need to bring almost everything that makes a place feel like a place, and you will need to be the kind of person who knows what to do with very little.

## What the hollow actually is

Before packing, it helps to hold the geometry clearly in your mind. Imagine a small irregular depression in pale stone, perhaps a hundred paces across at most, sunk into the ground with walls that curve gently inward as they rise. The whole formation is broken into three arcs — the rim is not a continuous ring but a sequence of stone shoulders, and between them the walls are lower, so the sky never appears as a defined shape above you. You are always inside something. The hollow has a floor of hard, pale sediment, cracked into a faint polygonal pattern that has been set and finished for a very long time. The walls lift about forty feet. The color of the stone is the color of bone left in a desert — bleached and rebleached, the color of dust that remembers having been something else. There is no water. There has almost never been water. A dozen or so small plants, the dull olive-green of things that have negotiated with aridity, grow in the seams at the base of the walls. The air is warm, dry, faintly alkaline, and it does not move. Light arrives only as a paler patch high above. The hollow has finished becoming what it is, and is simply being it.

## The light, and how to meet it

The most disorienting thing about the hollow is not its silence, although the silence is considerable. It is the light. The sun never strikes the floor. The brightest thing in the hollow, most of the time, is the diffuse patch high up between the curving lips of the rim, and that patch goes from a dim grey at its dimmest to the color of weak milk at its brightest. Your eyes will spend most of their time working with the memory of light rather than the light itself, and this produces a particular kind of vision — soft, even, slightly muffled, with edges that lose themselves into the walls.

**Practical adjustments.** Bring a low-intensity, warm-toned light source. A headlamp on its lowest red setting, or a small lantern with a fabric diffuser. You do not want to blast the place with white light. The hollow has been in its dimness for a long time, and your retinas will adjust within twenty minutes if you let them, but you will want a light for detailed work — for examining the scrapes on the floor, for finding things you have set down, for moving at the edges where the walls curve away from you. Bring more than one light, because a light that fails in a place like this is a more serious problem than a light that fails in a lit room. Bring a small mirror, polished side, the size of your palm. You can angle it up at the patch of sky and bounce usable daylight down onto whatever you are doing. This is an old trick and it works better here than it would in a forest, because the light is already soft.

**Time.** Bring a watch, or whatever you use to mark time, and trust it more than your senses. The hollow has no strong cues for the passing of hours. The pale patch above brightens by a degree or two at midday and dims again toward evening, and that is nearly the whole arc of visible change. Without a timepiece, the day will loosen its grip on you faster than you expect, and you may find yourself uncertain whether an hour or four hours have passed since you last attended to the question. This is not unpleasant — the hollow is well suited to long, unhurried attention — but it is worth knowing in advance.

## The air and the body in it

The air is warm and dry and faintly alkaline, with a mineral taste that gathers at the back of the throat. It does not circulate. If you sit still for a long time, the air immediately around you will grow very slightly warmer from your own heat, and you will become aware of yourself as a small thermal event in an otherwise still medium. This is one of the stranger gifts of the place, and one of the more useful things to anticipate: you will feel your own temperature, your own breath, your own body's small emissions of warmth and moisture, more acutely than you usually do, because the air is not absorbing or carrying them away.

**Hydration.** You will need more water than you think. The dryness is not violent — your lips will not crack within the hour — but it is patient, and it works on you the way it has worked on the stone. Drink before you feel thirsty. Bring at least twice what you would normally carry for a trip of the same length, and plan to refill if there is any possibility of refilling outside the hollow. The plants here have made their arrangements with dryness; you have not.

**Breathing.** The alkaline tinge is faint but present. Most people find it settles into a kind of background taste within minutes and then stops being noticeable. If you have any respiratory sensitivity, or if you are used to humid air, the first hour may feel slightly tight in the chest, as if the air wants to be inhaled but does not quite want to be exhaled. It passes. Breathe through your nose, slowly, for the first half hour, and let your lungs acclimate.

**Clothing.** Loose, breathable, light-colored. You are not going to be in direct sun, but the walls will hold the day's heat and radiate it back gently through the evening. A wide-brimmed hat is less useful here than in open country — there is no sun to keep off your neck — but a light scarf or shemagh is useful, both for the faint dust that occasionally lifts from the floor and as a soft layer if the air cools at the bottom of the night. Closed-toed shoes with soles that can grip smooth stone. The sediment floor is hard but not rough; the danger is the polished scoop in one wall, which is smooth enough to slip on if you put a careless foot on it at the wrong angle.

## Sound, and the importance of making almost none

The hollow is not silent in the way a recording studio is silent, with the silence of a place engineered to exclude sound. It is silent the way an old library is silent — the silence is full of small permitted sounds, and any sound you make will join them and be heard.
