The place opens beneath the world.

Not gently. It is a thing torn — a rupture in the basalt that runs for kilometers, ragged and unfinished, walls still weeping their own substance as if the rock itself cannot decide whether to be wall or river. You look down into a long valley of cooling stone, where the heat rises in visible waves and the air is so wet it feels like breathing through cloth.

The floor is not yet a floor. It is a confusion — broken shelves of obsidian, pools of something that might be water or might be mineral soup, drifts of pale powder that have not decided whether to be ash or dust or fungal bloom. Where there is moisture, there is a thin crust of something cream-colored and veined, which may be the first lichen, or the last memory of lichen, or something that has no name because no one has yet found a language to put it in. It does not move. It does not need to.

Above this embryonic floor, the fauna.

It is the first thing you understand when you come here — there is more of it than of stone. The walls are not walls, they are carpets. Every surface is layered: a stratum of small pale things with too many legs, beneath them a deeper layer of larger pale things with fewer legs, beneath them the moving substrate of worms, or grubs, or something that has given up having a name. They pulse. They are warm because the cave is warm and they are warm because there are so many of them — a closed circuit of bodies.

The air is not air. It is a swarm. Winged things — pale, membranous, eyeless — move in columns and eddies, their bodies so numerous that the air has texture, has weight. Where they pause, the stone beneath darkens with their weight. Where they pass, the air is briefly clear and you can see the next column coming.

The sound is not a sound. It is the absence of silence. The constant susurrus of millions of small things breathing, crawling, scraping, chittering. The drip of condensation falling into the drip before it. Occasionally a deeper note — the groan of the cavern's unfinished settling, the pop of a thermal pocket giving way somewhere in the dark below the floor. The volume does not lull. It does not peak. It simply persists, the way a river persists, except the river is alive.

And among this — this is the strange part — there are marks.

Low walls of stacked basalt, some tumbled, some still standing. A platform chipped from an obsidian shelf, the chips still lying where they fell. A niche in the wall with something placed inside it: a stone, polished smooth, of no apparent use. Cords twisted from the silken threads the flying things leave behind — there are no plants, so the fiber is not plant fiber. Hammers of bone, the owner of which is unclear. Blades of obsidian, knapped with the patient wrongness of someone who learned the technique without ever seeing a hand do it.

Whatever made these — or is making these, or is in the process of making these, because it seems ongoing — does not seem to mind the fauna. The marks are placed in the middle of the swarms. The walls are not built to keep them out; they seem to be made partly of them. Some of the larger pale things have been worked into the lower courses of the walls, alive or recently so. The masonry is partly alive. The architecture is digesting.

This is the emerging thing. The cave is not yet finished. The fauna is not yet finished. The marks are not yet finished. They are all in process together, the way a city is in process — except a city has a plan, and this place has only the plan that emerges from the next moment.

The light, such as it is, comes from above. The rupture to the surface is a long ragged slash, and the light comes through at an angle, in dust-laden shafts that move as the wind moves outside, which is a wind you cannot hear. The light does not reach the floor in most places. It reaches the upper walls, where the densest swarms of the winged things circle in it, briefly gilded, then drop back into the warm dark.

In a few places, the light catches condensation on a wall and the wall becomes briefly a mirror, and you see the swarms reflected as if they were clouds. This is beautiful. It is also loud, and dense, and unfinished, and warm, and full of the slow patient work of something that is not yet sure whether it is alive or is being made alive by what surrounds it.
