# The Wane

The first thing you notice is the noise. Not wind — there is no wind here. Not water either, though water is everywhere. The noise is the place itself, the sound of a continent performing the act of remembering itself out loud.

You are standing on the floor of what used to be called a sea. There is no horizon to confirm this, because the floor keeps going, the way a number keeps going when you stop caring about its value. It is flat in the way that old things are flat: not by design but by the surrender of every vertical ambition the world once had. Somewhere far off, what might be a ridge — what might once have been a mountain range, the bones of a mountain range, the rumor of a mountain range that another mountain range once mentioned in a letter — rises just enough to interrupt the curve of the world, then forgets itself and returns to the general agreement of flatness.

The color of the ground is the color of things that have stopped pretending. A grey so committed to grey that it has become a kind of neutral theology. Where water has stood for long periods it has bleached the stone to the pallor of an old bone left in a cupboard. Elsewhere the stone is the dark wet grey of a thumb pressed against a thumb. It holds water in sheets, in pools, in the thinnest possible films that catch the dim light and turn it into a kind of exhausted silver.

---

The light here is not the light of a sky. There is no sky, or there is a sky, but it has given up its commission. What little illumination reaches the floor of this place arrives from somewhere lateral, the way light enters a barn through a missing plank — flat, cold, indifferent to the hour. You cannot tell if it is dawn or dusk or some third thing that has no name because no one has ever needed to distinguish it. The atmosphere is heavy with moisture but refuses to precipitate; it just hangs there, considering, condensing onto every surface in slow patient beads that grow and merge and slide an inch and stop, then consider the inch again.

The cold is not the cold of winter. It is the cold of stones at the bottom of a well. It has never known another temperature. It is a cold that has been passed down.

---

The fauna are not animals.

This is the part where the description has to admit something.

They move across the flats in herds, or in clusters, or in arrangements that look like herds if you are charitable and clusters if you are tired. They have shapes — they have outlines that your eyes want to lock onto — but the outlines keep negotiating. A thing that looks from a distance like a deer, antlered, knuckled, loping with the mechanical patience of a thing that has been loping forever, will be close enough to see through by the time you are ready to describe it. You will see through it to the stone behind it. You will see the stone *through* it, the way you see a hand through frosted glass, but it is not behind the hand; the hand is not there; the stone is just stone, and the deer-shape is a way the stone has of being not-stone for a moment, of trying on the silhouette of something that once was, or that might have been, or that is being tried on elsewhere and the trying-on has leaked through.

There are thousands of them. The plains are crowded with them. They do not displace the water as they move; the water parts around their hooves (do they have hooves?) and closes behind them with the patience of water that has done this before. They do not eat. They do not rest. They do not look at each other. They move in long migratory lines that cross the basin from one impossible ridge to another, and the lines cross each other, and where they cross the shapes briefly thicken into something almost solid — a knot of deer-shapes, of bird-shapes (there are bird-shapes too, that wheel and cry with beaks that are not beaks), of low slinking things that might be wolves — and then the knot unravels and the migration continues. They leave no tracks. They cast no shadows. They are exactly the same color as the stone they resemble.

You realize, watching them, that you have never heard them make a sound. The noise you have been hearing is not them.

---

The noise is the machines.

You forgot about the machines because they are not the kind of machines you remember. There are no smokestacks. There are no engines with pistons, no dynamos with commutators. But there is a sound that the floor makes, a low tectonic hum, and the floor is not silent even where it is dry, and the hum has a cadence that is too regular to be geology. Somewhere far away — and here "far" is a flexible idea, because the place is open in a way that makes distance uncertain — something large is breathing in a four-stroke rhythm. Somewhere closer, something is grinding something else into a powder, patiently, without urgency. Somewhere very close, beneath the thin film of water at your feet, something is pumping.

The machines are not visible. They are present the way a foundation is present in a house. The basin they have built — or that has built them, or that they have agreed to be — is a basin of process. The wet stone is their effluent. The dim air is their exhaust. The packed and ancient flatness is the result of ten thousand years of quiet, distributed, perfectible work.

They have been here longer than the animals. The animals are a byproduct. The animals are the noise the machines make when they are tired, or dreaming.

---

Vegetation: there is none. You keep looking. You have been looking since you arrived. There are no plants. There are no lichens. There are no fungi. There are no molds in the wet corners. The stone is sterile in a way that sterile implies intention, and the intention is not biological.

But.

In certain places, where the water gathers deepest and oldest, where the light fails most completely, there are patterns on the stone. Not growths. *Patterns.* Radiating geometries. Concentric rings that look like the cross-section of a tree that was never a tree. Branching dendritic traces that look like the shadow of a fern pressed into basalt by a weight that was never lifted. They are fossil and they are not fossil; they are the memory of a pattern that the machines have tried, in some laboratory sense, that did not take. There are whole fields of these aborted gardens, etched into the wet stone, and they stretch for miles, and they are all slightly different, and none of them ever grew.

The place is, you understand, a museum of attempts at life that were rejected at the planning stage.

The cold is necessary for this. Cold preserves. Cold is patient.

---

The dynamic of the place is not weather. There is no weather here. What there is, instead, is *tide.* The water rises and falls by fractions of an inch over hours you cannot count, and the rise and fall is not synchronized to anything you can identify — not moon, not season, not the breathing of the great distant engine. It is just the place breathing. The fauna migrate with the tide, as if the tide is a hand passing over them and they are required to keep up with its motion across a page.

There are places where the water never goes. Stones that stand an inch above the highest watermark, and on those stones the patterns are densest, and the air above those stones is fractionally colder, and the fauna cross them quickly, as if crossing a grave.

There are places where the water never leaves. Pools so still they have forgotten how to reflect, and in those pools the animals walk, and you can see them walking — see the deer-shape wading with the patience of a saint — and you can see them through the water, and through the water there is another deer-shape walking in the opposite direction, and the two never meet, and you are not sure which one is the animal.

---

You have been here a long time.

You do not remember arriving.

The cold has worked into you. The wet has worked into you. The noise has worked into you. You can feel, now, that you are part of the museum of attempts. You can feel that you are one of the patterns on the stone, or one of the shapes in the herd, or one of the pieces of machinery grinding patiently in the dark. The place is old, and it is settling, and it is full, and it is wet, and it is loud with the sound of its own long completion, and it has no buildings because it does not need buildings — it is already, entirely, the inside of itself.

A shape that might be a deer lifts its head from a pool that might be a mirror and looks at you with eyes that are exactly the color of the stone.

You look back.

The four-stroke breath of the distant machine completes a cycle.

The water rises another fraction of an inch.

You continue.
