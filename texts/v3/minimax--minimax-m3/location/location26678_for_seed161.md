# Mesaq'in

The plateau of Mesaq'in rises from a plain so old it has forgotten how to be a plain. It is the last sharp thing in the world. Everything around it — the dunes, the salt-troughs, the long mineral flats that stretch to a horizon made of haze and heat — has been ground down by more time than the people who live here can picture. The mountains that fed this plain are gone. What remains is a column of basalt and welded tuff roughly two leagues across at the top, perhaps three hundred cubits high, with sides so weathered they slope rather than drop. A wedding-cake of stone, layered by ancient eruptions and then by ten million years of patient wind.

This is the warm high place.

---

You come up the only road that works — a switchback cut into the southwest face, paved in fitted blocks of red scoria that someone, six hundred years ago, hauled up one at a time. The climb takes a morning. The air thins. The light goes grey and brassy as the road climbs above the dust-haze that lies over the lowlands like a quilted blanket. By the time you reach the top your ears pop and your skin is prickling with the peculiar dryness of altitude — but the air is not cold. It is the warmth of a body asleep under blankets. The rock beneath the road is faintly warm. Somewhere below you, deep in the basalt, the last of the world’s heat is still moving.

The plateau unfolds. It is small. You could throw a stone from one edge to the other, if the houses were not in the way.

---

There are houses everywhere.

They crowd against each other the way teeth crowd a jaw. Flat roofs of clay and beam, walls of the same red scoria as the road, occasionally dressed with a band of black obsidian or a course of cream-colored pumice. Three, four, sometimes five stories — built up because there is nowhere else to go. They lean on each other. A balcony from one house bridges into the window of the next. Stairs climb outside the walls, and from those stairs more stairs climb, and from those — until the whole southern quarter is a single organism of stairs and platforms and shadowed loggias, all of it soft with age, all of it the color of old brick and old blood.

It is dim inside the town. The sky is a strip above you, more guessed at than seen. The light that reaches the streets is filtered through the haze of the plain below, and then again through the eaves and awnings and the long fronds that have begun to take the roofline as their own. The town is a ravine that walks.

It smells, on a morning like this, of woodsmoke and damp earth and the faintly sweet rot of overripe figs and the warm mineral breath of the springs.

---

There is water.

That is the first thing you must understand about Mesaq'in: there is water, and there is everything else. The springs rise at the center of the plateau, where a fissure in the basalt goes down and down and finally meets an aquifer that the geologists of the town — a guild of old women who read the stone the way other towns read books — say is the last remnant of an inland sea trapped when the volcanoes died. The water is warm. It smells of sulfur and iron and something almost sweet, like the inside of a peach stone. It comes up at a temperature you can bathe in, year-round, in three principal pools and a dozen minor seepages that weep through the mortar of the lowest houses.

From the central springs the water is led, by canals of cut stone that have been repaired so many times no one remembers the original work, across the plateau. It does not flow fast. The town is built to its slowness. The canals are wide and shallow — you could step across most of them — and they thread between the houses, along the streets, up the sides of buildings where the masons have run them as raised troughs, onto the roofs where they feed ceramic basins and small paddies. The water goes everywhere. It is the only reason anything green exists.

---

And a great deal is green.

This is the second thing you must understand: the town is half-jungle.

Wherever a canal passes, a garden has erupted. Figs, pomegranates, a gnarled thing with small sour fruits the locals call *qatifeh* that no one can grow anywhere else. Beans climbing the walls. A kind of tall feathery grass that nobody quite admits is a weed but everyone tolerates because the pack-beasts eat it. On the upper roofs, in the ceramic basins, lotus and watercress and a floating mat of something red-flowered that the healers use. On the northern face, where a seep has run unchecked for two centuries, a whole slope has surrendered to roots: a green cataract pouring down the scarp, damp and glossy, loud with insects, the only place on the plateau where you can stand and feel moisture actually settling on your skin.

The vegetation has worked its way into the buildings. A fig tree has grown through a second-floor window and out the skylight of the house above, and the householders of both floors have simply accommodated it — they eat from it in season, and the woman who owns the upper room has hung a small shrine in its branches because, she says, it was there before she was. A grapevine has eaten the entire eastern staircase of one block. To climb it you must part leaves with both hands and step where the steps still are.

The town is a settlement, and the settlement is also a forest, and the forest is also a building, and the building is also a town. You cannot say where one ends and the other begins.

---

The light, then, is filtered thrice: by the haze, by the eaves, by the leaves.

It is the light of an overcast morning in a forest. Everything is the color of tea, of old bronze, of dust on a windowsill. Shadows are soft, not deep. Faces read better from sound than from sight. The town has the air of an indoor place. People speak quietly even when they are far apart, because the sound carries between the close walls, and because in this dim warm dim they feel as though they are already indoors.

And the town is loud.

---

The noise of Mesaq'in is one of its oldest features.

It begins before dawn with the beasts. There are many beasts. The *khazne* — large six-legged grazers, the size of ponies, with thick wool and a placid mouth — low in their byres at the edges of town, the sound of which is less a moo than a long felt vibration you feel in your heels. There are the smaller riding animals, the *sireh*, who bray at first light whether or not they have cause, and whose young are kept in pens on the lower roofs and make a piping noise like wet reeds. There are goats, and there are dogs, and there is a species of large flightless bird — kept for eggs and for their long red tail-feathers, which are the town's principal export — that makes a noise one of the travelers' guides described, with some puzzlement, as a trombone falling down a staircase.

On top of this, before the people are even out: water. The sound of water. The canals moving. A man drawing a bucket. A woman sluicing her doorstep. The slap of wet cloth on stone as someone beats a rug on a stair landing. The deep hush of the larger pools at the springs, where the water wells up and the surface trembles and never quite stills.

Then the people come, and the sound becomes a single chord.

Voices in three or four languages, because Mesaq'in is a trade town and has always been, sitting as it does above the only road across these flats that does not vanish in summer. The hammering of the coppersmiths, who work in a lane near the western edge where the light is best. The children — and there are many children, the town being warm and crowded and the customs being what they are — screaming in the alleys, splashing in the shallower canals, throwing fruit at each other from balconies. The bells. There are bells in Mesaq'in, small ones hung in the eaves of public houses and large ones hung from the frames of the springs, that ring for hours, festivals, deaths, the opening of the morning market, and for no reason at all, when a child is born or a stranger is recognized or someone has simply decided the morning requires a bell.

Above all of it, and through all of it, the sound of the green — the whisper of the fig leaves, the buzz of the bees, the small rustle of whatever it is that lives in the mat of red flowers on the upper roofs and makes a noise like pages turning.

---

The town is dense and the town is slow.

These are not contradictions. They sit together like the layers of the basalt.

The density is the density of a thing that has nowhere to expand. The plateau is the size it is. The springs yield the water they yield. There is only so much room, and there has been only so much room for a long time, and so people have learned to live in close quarters, and have done so for many generations, and have arranged their lives into a kind of vertical choreography. Each house holds three families. Each family holds three generations. The dead are kept on the lower floors, in niches sealed with plaster and lath, and the living are on the floors above, and the smallest children sleep in the eaves, and the oldest sleep wherever they can be persuaded to. Privacy is mostly a matter of curtains and the angle of a staircase.

And the slowness — the slowness is the pace of the water. The water does not rush. It moves through the canals the way a thought moves through the mind of an old person: it arrives when it arrives. The day is shaped by the water. The morning market opens when the water has filled the upper basins, and closes when the water has drained back. The cooking is done in the evening when the canals are lowest. The bathing is done in the small hours, when no one else wants the pools, and the very old go down to the springs at dawn and sit in the warm water and speak to each other in voices that have grown so soft they are barely voices at all.

The seasons, too, are slow. There are two — a long warm dry, and a long warm less-dry, in which the haze thins and the light on the plateau becomes briefly, dazzlingly, almost white. The dry season is when the beasts are slaughtered and the meat is hung in the upper eaves, and the less-dry season is when the planting is done and the festivals begin. Each season takes a quarter of the year. The year is long. The people live a long time. Some of them, you are told, have lived in the same house for ninety years and have descended the same staircase ten thousand times and have never, in all that time, gone down to the plain below.

---

The plain below is the world.

The plateau is a small country. You can see it from the road, on the way up — that thumb of stone above the haze, with a fuzz of green at its rim and a slow feather of woodsmoke rising from it. Seen from the plain it is a single feature, the way a single tree in a field is a single feature. Seen from the town itself, looking outward, the plain is what you see when you climb to the highest roof and stand on tiptoe and look past the eaves and the leaves and the bell-towers and the flights of birds.

What you see is nothing. An endless pale sweep of dust and salt, fading into a haze that becomes the sky without a seam. There is no water down there. There is almost no green. There are the moving shapes of caravans — small and patient, taking weeks to cross from one well to the next — and there are, in some seasons, the pale shadows of animals too far away to identify, crossing flats that have not changed since they were first laid down.

It is, the town agrees, a place you come from. The town is the place you stay.

---

The tools of the town are the tools of any town that has not yet learned to lift things with engines.

Stone, cut and fitted and carved. Wood, from a species of small tree that grows only on the lower slopes of the plateau where the seepage is — felled with bronze axes, dragged up by the *khazne*, and worked by hand. Bronze, traded up from the plain in exchange for feathers and water — the springs are owned in common, but the water-rights are sold, and the coppersmiths are the wealthiest guild. Clay, for the roofs and the basins and the figurines the children bury in the gardens. Wool, from the *khazne*, carded on every roof in the dry season, woven on looms that take up half a room. Paper, made from the inner bark of the fig, on which the town's records — and there are many records, the town being old and literate and proud — are kept in a script that curls back on itself like the fronds of the red-flowered weed.

There is no metal more precious than bronze. There are no engines. There is no glass except the obsidian the smiths polish to a dark mirror and use for ceremony. The light in the houses, after the dim day fails, comes from oil lamps that smell of the *khazne*'s winter fat, and from small fires of wood in braziers set into the floors. The town is dark at night. The town has been dark at night for as long as it has been a town.

---

And yet.

There are things in the town that are not explained by the town.

The geologists, who read the stone — there are seven of them now, down from eleven a generation ago — have marked, on a map kept in a tile-walled room near the springs, certain places where the basalt goes down farther than it should. Certain alignments of fissure that do not match the lava-flows. A chamber, somewhere beneath the southern quarter, that the oldest among them insists she has been down into, and the youngest among them insists does not exist. There are stones in the paving of the central square that are not scoria and not pumice and not anything the geologists can name — they are heavy, and they are warm, and they ring when struck, and no one knows how they came to be there. There is a stair, in the cellar of the house where the oldest geologist lives, that goes down, and down, and is said by her to come up somewhere else. She has not been asked to prove this in many years. The town has a way of not asking.

These things are quiet. They are part of the slowness. Whatever is down there has been down there longer than the town. The town has built itself over it the way the moss has built itself over the stone.

---

If you stand, on a warm dry morning, on the highest roof of Mesaq'in — the roof of the house of the bell-keepers, which is the highest point of the plateau, and is itself only as high as a tall tree — and look out over the parapet of fitted tiles, this is what you see.

The town below you, in its layers: the red of the roofs, the green of the figs, the silver of the canals in the alleys, the small movements of people and beasts. The bells beginning, somewhere, to ring. The haze above the plain, and the plain below the haze. The sun, which is up there somewhere, a pale disc you cannot look at directly, pouring a thin warm light over everything, asking nothing, hastening nothing.

The wind on this roof is the same wind that has been blowing across these flats since before there were flats. It moves the leaves of the fig tree that has grown through the window two houses down. It carries the smell of the springs, and the sound of the children, and the faint far cry of a caravan crossing the plain below. It is not a strong wind. It is a patient wind.

Mesaq'in sits in its column of warm stone, on its plain of old dust, under its thin bright dim sky, and waits, the way the water waits in the canals, the way the bells wait in the eaves, the way the bells' keepers wait in their house, and the way the geologist waits in her cellar, with her stair that goes down.

The town is small. The town is dense. The town is warm and dim and loud and slow and old, and it is the only green thing for a thousand leagues in any direction, and the water in its springs is the only water for a thousand leagues in any direction, and the people in its houses are the only people for a thousand leagues in any direction, and they have been here, by their own count, for six hundred and forty years, and by the count of the stones in the central square, perhaps longer.

They have nowhere else to be.

They are, by every measure, at the end of the world.

They have made it a garden.
