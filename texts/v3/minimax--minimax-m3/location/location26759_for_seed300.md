# The Sump at Lower Ash

You find it by following a sound you almost can't hear — a low ticking, regular as a slow pulse, carried on the wet air from somewhere past the alder thicket. The path is muck and red gravel, old railway sleepers laid end to end and sunken by half their depth. They were sleepers once; now they are a track, then a suggestion, then a thing you pick your way over.

The hollow opens without ceremony. The ground simply drops, by a meter, then by three, then by eight, and you are standing on a stone shelf looking down into a space the size of a chapel. It is not a natural chapel. Someone cut the rock back, and someone cut it back again, and then a third time, and the cuts are at different ages — the lowest walls are pre-railway, the middle cuts are from the engine era, the uppermost shelf is fresh enough that the chisel marks still hold a sheen. The place has been worked into its own shape over a long time, and the rock has accepted this. Weeping is the right word for what the walls do. A fine condensation stands on them in beads, gathers, runs, gathers again. The stone is dark. It is not quite the dark of wet granite. It is the dark of a stone that has been wet for so long it has forgotten another color.

Down in the floor of the hollow, the machines are packed. This is what your eye registers before anything else: the density. There is no spare room. A beam engine — small, the size of a room — takes the long wall. A condenser stands beside it like a fat iron bottle, sweating. Pipes run everywhere. They are not routed for elegance. They are routed for the next thing that needed connecting, and then the next, so the bundle climbs the wall in thickets, branches, re-knits. Some are as thin as your thumb; some you can step into. They are all the same color, a kind of tarnished bronze-green that the damp has made uniform. Between the pipes, in the spaces no bigger than a cabinet, there are dials, valves, junction boxes, and a few instruments you cannot name but recognize as instruments. A trough runs along the floor carrying water that came up from somewhere, and the water moves in it. The water moves briskly, as if it has somewhere to be. It spills at the far end into a second trough and goes down again, or up again — from this shelf you cannot see.

Above the engine, a flywheel turns. It is the only large moving part exposed to the air, and it is turning now, slowly, with a patience that feels older than its bearings. There is a chain. There is a leather belt. There is a small hammer that lifts and falls onto an anvil shaped for it, tapping a rhythm you have to stand very near to hear, and then only just. The tapping is the ticking you followed. It is mechanical and it is also, somehow, a kind of calling — the way a heron calls, or a snipe, the kind of sound that says *I am here, I am still here*, without urgency.

The place is not silent, but it is quiet. Steam hisses inside pipes and the hiss is felt more than heard. A valve somewhere lets go a slow breath every few seconds. Water ticks in a U-bend. The flywheel makes a sound like a thumb drawn slowly around the rim of a heavy glass. Once in a while — and you would miss it if you were not paying attention — the engine *shifts*. The whole rhythm re-phases, the tapping re-aligns, a pressure somewhere is satisfied, and the new pattern holds for a while. The place is turbulent and the place is hushed, and the two conditions are not in contradiction. The turbulence is inside. The pipes are full of it. The dials are in constant small disagreement with each other, and the disagreement is being worked out.

There is no one here. The fire is banked in the engine's belly and no one tends it. A grate of peat-brick, half-collapsed, glows beneath the boiler. The fire is not unattended by design; it is unattended because whoever comes comes at a different hour, or comes seldom, or has stopped coming. The machinery does not appear to mind. It runs.

Sparse things grow. A single alder has taken the lip of the upper cut and leans inward, its leaves the color of tin. Rushes stand in the rim of the trough. Moss is colonizing the lowest pipework with a kind of slow ambition, and the moss and the verdigris are the same green, so it is hard to say where the machine ends and the growth begins. There is a frog on a flange. There is a frog on every flange, perhaps, or the same frog moving between them. A dragonfly hangs in the air above the trough and does not decide.

The air is cool and sits on the skin. It tastes of iron and of something else — a faint mineral sweetness, like the smell of a stone you have just split, except the stone has not been split recently. The condensation on the walls catches what little light the sky sends down, and the rock shines, and the pipes shine, and the trough shines, and the place has, for a small industrial pocket under a low sky, a kind of generosity to it. It is the size of a room and a half. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other. And it is full. It has been full for a long time, and the fullness is what the place is for.

The wrongness, if you want to call it that, is this: the water in the trough, when you look at it for more than a few seconds, runs uphill at the far end. It does not run uphill. It is clearly running down, into a lower sump, and you can see the lip it spills over, and the fall, and the splash. But in the corner of your eye, while you are watching the flywheel, the water is on its way back. You do not mention this. The engine shifts again. The tapping re-aligns. The flywheel continues its slow patience. The frog on the flange watches you the way frogs watch things, which is to say, with no opinion at all, but with the full weight of its small witness.
