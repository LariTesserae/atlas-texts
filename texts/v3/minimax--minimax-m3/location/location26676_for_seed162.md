# The Foundry of Quiet Engines

There is a place where the sky forgot what color it was supposed to be. Not darkness — the word darkness implies the possibility of light elsewhere — but a kind of permanent *undecidedness*, a low pewter ceiling that breathes without ever seeming to exhale. From certain ridges you can see it pooling into valleys like sediment, like spilled milk, like the memory of clouds. The light that makes it through is not sunlight anymore; it is something tired, something secondhand, arriving hours late and apologetic.

The plateau stretches to every horizon and farther. Whoever called this place high was measuring more than altitude — they were measuring intention. The ground rises in long muscular folds, like a fist unclenching over geologic time, and at the crest of each fold the rock is newer than at the trough. This is young terrain. You can tell by the way the fractures don't agree with one another, the way a single ridge will present three different directions of cleavage as if it had been built by three different gods on three different Tuesdays and never reconciled.

And yet there are machines.

---

The first thing you hear when you arrive is the second thing you stop hearing. A rhythm, somewhere below the threshold of attention — not loud, not quiet, but *omnipresent* the way a refrigerator hums in a kitchen you've stopped noticing. It is the pulse of something running, something that has been running longer than any of the rust remembers being painted.

Walking the plateau in any direction for an hour you will encounter engines. Not factories. Not the kind of place that builds machines. The *opposite* — a place machines use. They sit in shallow depressions they've melted into the basalt, iron housings the color of dried blood, exhaust plumes curling lazily from stacks no human welder ever riveted. Some of them are smaller than your body. Some are larger than a house could ever need to be. None of them have labels you can read.

None of them appear to do anything.

The pistons move. The flywheels spin. Valves tick open and close with the patience of something that expects to be observed by weather rather than people. Steam issues from a flange, but it is the wrong color — slightly amber, slightly weighted — and where it touches the lichens at the base of a stack, the lichens grow in concentric rings as if branded.

---

Between the engines runs a forest.

This is the part you will not believe later, when you try to describe it to people who have not been here. The forest is *lush* is not the word. The forest is lushed *upon*. It pours. It is ferns as tall as your chest, ferns as tall as a cathedral's nave, ferns that unfurl in spirals so tight they look mathematical. Mosses so thick they have given up being plants and become geography — you walk on them, you sink gently into them, you do not mind. Tree ferns with trunks wrapped in living velvet. Strangler figs that have wrapped around basalt pillars that no one placed, only to discover they were wrapping around an engine housing instead, which is fine with them, which they have incorporated into their structure with the unhurried generosity of things that have nowhere to be.

The canopy is dim. Perpetually. Not nighttime dim — pensive dim, study-lamp dim, the dimness of a place *deciding* how much of itself to reveal. Beams fall in slow shafts and arrive already diffused, like rumors that have been passed down until the original information has been forgiven out of them. Birds — we will come to birds — sing in the middle distance and the sound arrives as feeling more than as noise.

---

There is water, but it has manners.

A river cannot be said to cross this plateau; the plateau argues with rivers. The Vell runs through here, in some low season; in others it does not. There are the dried ghost-courses of a hundred former rivers, sandy channels that could not agree on which direction downstream was and so compromised by going every direction at once. In the coves between ridges there are ponds that never dry up — green-glass still water, water that has visibly *stopped being water* and become a different substance that happens to look like it. If you stand at the lip of one of these and drop a stone, the stone does not splash so much as it *agrees*.

After rain — and the rain comes, sometimes for an afternoon, sometimes for days — the whole plateau exudes moisture from below, the rocks sweating, the mosses darkening by three shades, the engines ticking louder as if pleased. The light gets worse in the rain. The light gets *more itself* in the rain. Both statements are true.

---

The fauna here did not read the papers.

A pale deer, antlers like branch coral, browsing something invisible with the meditative attention of a goat. A pale bird the size of a forearm, glossy, eye the color of a gearbox, that mimics the engine rhythm exactly when it calls. Lizards as long as your arm, basking on the iron housings as if they had been put there for the purpose; their bellies are the same color as the rust. Insects that hum in B-flat. A great drifting thing in the upper canopy — a glider, a colugo, something with a face like a question — that pads from trunk to trunk on the membranes of itself and watches you with one orange eye before it dissolves into the leaves and reappears forty meters away.

None of these are afraid of you. None of them are *not* afraid of you. They have arranged their feelings about your presence in a category you do not have a word for.

---

There is a sound here, and you must forgive me.

You can be standing in any of these places — fern forest, engine yard, dry ghost-riverbed, glassy pond — and you will hear something at the bottom of everything. It is not the engines. It is not the birds. It is more like a very large field turning over very slowly, the way an ocean liner rolls over a long swell, but in sound. It is the *place*. The plateau itself, breathing, or remembering to breathe. Or failing to forget it had been breathing when something else was inside it.

There is a rumor of machinery in that sound. There is a rumor of water in that sound. There is a rumor of *time being pressed into shape*, the way metal is pressed, but by what mold you cannot say. Sometimes, lying with your ear to the basalt between engines, you can hear individual frequencies separate: a high one like a bell struck once and forgotten; a low one like a wave returning to a shore it has never seen.

You learn not to listen too long.

---

And the weirdness.

The weirdness has a flavor and the flavor is *almost*. Almost is the dominant flavor here. Almost like the trees. Almost like the engines. Almost like a sky. Almost like birds.

Walk for an hour in any direction and you will come around a fern-covered outcrop and find — what? An engine, but the bolts of it are on the wrong side. As if it had been assembled from the inside out. A river, but it runs *uphill along its length* and then bends down at the last moment with a politeness that is almost obscene. A copse of trees whose shadows are accurate but whose trunks are transparent, and through them you can see, at a depth of several meters, an *engine identical to itself*, also upright, also functioning, also in shadow. The second engine has, sometimes, a bird on its housing. The bird is also possibly two birds.

There are places where the ground is *higher in some directions than in others*, regardless of how you stand. There is a pond in which you can see your reflection but the reflection blinks a half-second *before* you do. There are stones that have been here longer than the gorge they sit in and the gorge has been here longer than the stones. None of this is a trick. None of it is a glitch you can pin down and fix. The plateau is *contending* with something, the way a page contends with what is written on the other side — pressing through, showing in damp patches of meaning that don't quite become sentences.

---

A word about the engines, before they have the last word.

The engines are not abandoned. They are not maintained either. They are *ongoing*. They run because running is what they are, the way a river runs. Some of them have grown through. A fern has erupted from a piston housing and now the piston housing is also a planter. A tree has incorporated a gearbox so thoroughly into its trunk that the tree is now, in some technical sense, *geared*. There is an engine somewhere on the southern flats that you can only find in certain weathers, and when you find it, it has no engine — it has a *space* shaped exactly like an engine, dark inside, humming slightly, and if you reach in, your hand comes back smelling of oil even if your hand never went past your wrist. (There are those who say there are hands, in there, that come back smelling of *river*. The plateau declines to clarify.)

They will still be running when you leave. You will go home and for years afterward you will occasionally hear a certain hum — in plumbing, in refrigerators, in the chest cavity when you are trying to sleep — and you will wonder if you took something with you, or if something has agreed to follow.

---

A word about the sky.

The sky is the same color as the moss and the same color as the river-glass and the same color as the rust and the same color as the air itself when the air is full of moisture. This is not metaphor. From certain elevations, on certain afternoons, you cannot tell where the plateau ends and the overcast begins, and you may walk for a quarter of an hour before you realize you have been walking on a cloud that has very kindly agreed to behave as ground for the duration of your crossing. The plateau does this. It accommodates.

Above the ceiling, somewhere, there is presumably a sun. It is not a question I can answer here. Above the ceiling, there is a kind of *industry* — the weather being made by something — but who is making the weather, and what they are making it *for*, and whether they themselves are inside some larger engine being run by a yet-larger engine being run by —

Down here the ferns uncurl. The pale deer dips its mouth to a glassy pond and decides not to drink. An amber plume issues from a stack and writes a sentence in the air that dissolves before you can read it.

The plateau continues. The plateau continues. The plateau continues.
