# The Cistern of the Listening Walls

You find the entrance as a fissure in the bedrock of a high mountain shelf, no wider than your shoulders, weeping a constant curtain of condensation. The rock is warm to the touch, warmer than any stone should be at this altitude, and slick with a film that smells of iron and something older — yeast, perhaps, or the inside of a beehive. The fissure breathes outward in slow, wet gusts.

You have to turn sideways. The stone presses against your chest and back, and the sound hits you before you emerge into the space.

It is deafening. Not loud like thunder, not sharp like metal — it is the sound of *many things*, layered, a chord built from a thousand individual notes that your ears can almost separate but never quite can. The hum of a transformer left in a field. The roar of water through a limestone gorge. The keening of insects in extraordinary number. A high, thin whistle like a kettle that never quite sings. It comes from everywhere. It comes *through* you.

You step out onto a curved stone ledge, and the chamber opens.

It is not large. The ceiling is maybe forty feet above you, sagging in places where ancient wooden braces have bowed under the weight of saturated rock. The chamber is roughly oval — perhaps a hundred feet across its longest axis, sixty across the other. It feels like a cathedral built for a very small god, or a cistern that was never meant to hold water. Everything here is *made*. There is no natural substrate anywhere, no exposed earth, no untouched stone. Every surface has been worked.

The walls are the first thing that holds your attention. They are not smooth. They are textured in a way that resists the eye — a relief carved or grown or both, in repeating motifs that you cannot quite resolve. Up close, in the light of the bioluminescent mosses that crust every horizontal surface, the patterns resolve into thousands of small hollows, each one the size of a coin, each one holding a glint of moisture and something else — a tiny lens, perhaps, or a tiny mouth. From many of them, the sounds emerge. You realize, with a small and pleasant horror, that the walls are *speaking* — or listening, or both, the distinction unclear. The hollows modulate. The chord shifts as you move. The chamber knows you are here.

The floor is uneven, stepped in terraces of worked stone that descend toward the center. Water stands on every level — shallow pools, an inch deep at the edges, deepening as you approach the lowest terrace, where a single circular basin perhaps fifteen feet across holds water the color of strong tea. The water moves in slow spirals. It is warm. Steam rises from it in thin threads. The air is so humid that you feel you could drink it, and the warmth settles on your skin like a second garment.

Vegetation is sparse but present, and strange. Long, pale fronds of a fern you do not recognize grow from cracks in the stonework, their leaves trembling slightly even when the air is still. Patches of the bioluminescent moss — a dim, blue-green glow, barely enough to see by, the reason the chamber is not entirely dark — colonize the upper ledges. There are small, stalked fungi growing in clusters near the water's edge, their caps a deep oxblood color, trembling in a rhythm that may be unrelated to any breeze. The plants are not natural. They are *cultivated*, you realize, even the moss. Something has arranged this ecology. Something tends it. The ferns grow in deliberate arcs around the central basin, as if they were a planting diagram drawn in chlorophyll.

The fauna is the second thing that holds your attention, and it holds it longer.

There are creatures everywhere. The chamber is dense with them, near-touching, layered on every surface in profusion. Most are small — the size of your thumb, or smaller. They are dark, segmented, vaguely crustacean in their outer form, and they move with a quick, deliberate purpose along the walls and ceiling. Some carry droplets of water in specialized appendages. Some carry tiny fragments of moss. They tend the place. They farm the fungi. They maintain the water. Their footsteps — thousands and thousands of tiny hooked feet on worked stone — contribute to the chord, a high, pattering percussion that runs beneath everything else.

There are larger creatures too. In the upper reaches of the chamber, clinging to the ceiling where the moss grows thickest, you can see shapes the size of dogs, eyeless and pale, hanging in clusters like fruit. They are sleeping, or in torpor, or some third state. Their breathing is part of the sound, a slow, deep bellows-rhythm that you feel in your sternum as much as hear. Occasionally one of them detaches and drops, catching itself on a lower ledge with startling grace, and moves down to the water to drink, or to the wall to feed on the moss, or to a nest of the small creatures, which it harvests with a careful, almost gentle motion of its many limbs. The small creatures do not flee. They are accustomed.

The air smells of warm water, of living stone, of the faint sweet rot of the fungi, of ozone — though you cannot place the source of the ozone. The light is dim and blue-green and comes from the moss, but there are other sources: a faint, sourceless amber glow from within the central basin, as if the water itself is faintly luminescent; occasional flickers from among the ferns, brief and unexplained, like heat lightning seen from very far away.

The technology here is everywhere and invisible. The walls sing. The water spirals in patterns that do not correspond to any drainage you can identify. The creatures have been shaped — you see it in the symmetry of their limbs, in the way their eyes (where they have eyes) are placed. The chamber is a machine, and the machine is a garden, and the garden is a choir, and all three are the same thing, and you are inside it, and it is aware of you, and the chord in the walls has shifted again — a new note, very low, almost below hearing, that you feel in the soles of your feet and in the roots of your teeth.

Somewhere in the lower terraces, water drips into the basin in a rhythm that is just slightly too regular to be accident. A cluster of the small crustacean-things pauses in its work, all at once, and turns to look at you with eyes that are not quite eyes. Then it resumes. The ferns tremble. The pale creatures breathe. The song continues.

You have the sense that you have been expected, though not for any particular purpose, and that the expectation is very old, and that the chamber will continue, undisturbed and undiminished, long after you leave — if you are able to leave — and that the song will not change for your going. It has not changed for the going of others. There have been others. You can see, on the upper ledges, in the dim blue-green light, small arrangements of worked stone that you choose to interpret as memorials, or offerings, or both.

The water in the basin spirals on. The light shifts, very slightly, as if a cloud has passed across a sun that is not there. The warmth holds you. The sound holds you. You have the thought, very clearly, that this place was not built for you, but that you are, for the moment, an acceptable component of its operation, and that this is as close to welcome as the chamber knows how to offer.

You stay. You listen. The chord includes you now, faintly, a single new note in a music that has been playing for longer than the mountain has stood above it.
