# The High Stillness

It takes half a day to walk from one edge to the other, and you are never quite sure you have moved at all. The plateau does that to the eye. From the rise where the path first comes up over the shoulder of the world, the land lies out like a vast pale dish, slightly tilted, the far rim rising again into a low broken horizon. Nothing moves in the obvious way. The wind moves, but you feel it more than you see it — a slow pressure against one cheek, a smell of wet iron and stone-dust, the patient smell of somewhere that has been waiting a long time for nothing in particular to happen.

The rocks are the oldest thing here, older than anything you have language for. They were mountains once. You can read what is left of them in the shapes that remain: long ridges that taper to nothing, shoulders of stone that slope down into the plain and then simply decide to stop being stone and become gravel, and then dust, and then a thin skim of pale soil that the wind keeps rearranging. The biggest of the outcrops are like the knuckles of buried hands — grey-brown, lichen-painted, warm to the touch even on the cooler hours of the morning. They hold the heat from the day before and give it back in the dark. In the dim, before the sun clears the eastern lip, the place smells of warm dust and something sweeter, almost mineral, almost like the breath of an animal that has been sleeping.

The damp is not rain, not most of the time. The sky here is a high overcast for weeks at a stretch — a thin pearl-grey ceiling that lets the light through but keeps the heat close to the ground. The moisture comes down in a fine almost-nothing, a mist that you only notice because your skin tightens and your hair curls and the rocks darken from tan to the colour of a bruised plum. In the deeper folds of the plateau, where the old ridges have left a kind of shallow bowl, the mist pools into something you can almost call water — shallow sheets across the flat rock, only ankle-deep, moving so slowly you have to kneel to see which direction they are going. Where it gathers longest, a skin of something green-black has colonised the stone: a felt of moss and lichen and a kind of liver-coloured slime that you only see when you are close. The plant life, such as it is, has a withdrawn quality. There are tussocks of a coarse grey grass in the hollows, spaced out as if planted by someone with a careful eye. There are low shrubs with small hard leaves and a smell like sage but darker. There are things that look like plants and may not be — pale fronds that wave without any wind you can feel, and seem to pull back when your shadow crosses them. Nothing taller than your knee. Nothing that makes a sound when you push through it, because there is almost nothing to push through. The openness of the view is what you notice; the openness of the ground, the way it is mostly stone with only the thinnest cover over it.

The animals are a different story. You do not see them, at first. You feel them, and then you see them, and then you understand that you have been surrounded the entire time. The place is full of life in a way that has nothing to do with how green it is. A square metre of stone here might be home to more creatures than a square kilometre of forest anywhere else. They are small. Most of them are very small. They live in the cracks and under the thin soil-membrane, in the felt of moss, under the loose flakes of rock that have not quite decided to become gravel. They come out when the light is low — the small hours, the cooler hours — and move across the stone in patterns that are too patient to call a swarm. They are the colour of the stone. They are the colour of the lichen. When they stop moving, you cannot find them again. There are things that flit across the surface in low arcs, pale as ash, and land without sound, and are gone. There are things that hang from the underside of overhangs in clusters, like fruit, and only when you pass under them do you hear the soft shifting, like the inside of a paper bag being crumpled very slowly. There are things on the ground that look like the dropped husks of seeds until you watch one long enough and it walks. There are birds — you cannot tell if they are birds — that hang at the edges of the thermals in the middle of the day and ride them in wide circles without ever quite beating a wing. Their shadows pass across the rock and the shadows have no sound.

The sounds that exist are small and patient. The wind in the grass-tussocks, which sounds like someone turning the pages of a thick book at the back of a library. The far-off cry of something that hunts — a thin, rising note, not quite a scream, not quite a call, more like the question the air asks itself when it has been still too long. The occasional tick and clatter of a rock cooling in the evening and giving up a piece of itself to gravity. And under all of it, so low you feel it in the soles of your feet before you hear it, a kind of hum — not mechanical, not biological, just the sound of the plateau itself, the slow tectonic complaint of something that has been holding still for so long it has begun to forget how to do anything else. When you stop walking, the silence comes back, but it is not empty. It is dense with all the things you are not hearing.

There are marks of a building, here and there, but the word is too grand for what they are. A line of stones set on edge, going nowhere in particular, that might be the remnant of a wall or the beginning of one. A scatter of dark slabs arranged in a half-circle on the lee side of one of the knuckles, with the sand drifted up against them as if it has been a long time since anyone leaned there. A single standing stone, taller than the rest, with a kind of shelf cut into its face near the top — too worn now to say for what purpose, but cut, definitely cut, before any of the living things here were born. There is no metal anywhere. There is no clay. There are no signs of fire. Whatever these marks were made by, they did not use much. The lichen has eaten half of them. The sand is finishing the job. In a few hundred years they will not be marks at all, they will just be stones, and in a few thousand the plateau will have taken them back into itself without remark.

What makes the place strange is not any one thing. It is the feeling of standing inside a thing that has finished happening. The mountains are gone. The river, if there ever was a river, has been distributed across the surface as a thin persistent damp. The soil is a rumour. The animals have arranged themselves around the long quiet of the stone and seem to know something about patience that the visitor has not yet caught. Even the strangeness of the fauna — the blind pale things, the things that are colour-of-stone, the things that may not be quite what they look like — does not feel like strangeness for its own sake. It feels like a place has been left alone for so long that the life on it has simply drifted a little away from the rest of the world's ideas. The light is the same warm pearl-grey it has been for as long as anyone could remember. The hum in the ground is the hum it has always made. The plateau is not waiting for anything. It has come to the end of what it was going to do, and it is content.
