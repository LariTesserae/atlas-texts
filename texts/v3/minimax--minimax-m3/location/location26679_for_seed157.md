# The Weeping Bowl

It is a dimple in the mountain. Not a valley, not a gorge, not a cirque in the proper glacial sense — something smaller, an accident of stone that has held its shape for so long it has become intentional. A hundred meters across, perhaps less. Open to the sky only along a single ragged tear in its upper rim, where the rock has peeled and cracked and the gap has never closed again. Inside, the air does not move the way air moves outside. It sits. It has the weight of poured water that has stopped being poured and forgotten how to flow. You walk into the bowl and the temperature of your lungs changes.

The back wall is the first thing to claim you. A sweep of dark stone — not basalt, something older and quieter, an amphibolite or a metamorphosed shale, dark green in one light and almost black in another, with a long grain running through it that gives the wall the look of sleeping muscle. The stone has been wetted so long that it no longer has the texture of stone; under the moss and the lichens and the continuous weep of water it is smooth, and where it is exposed it gleams. From every seam — and there are many seams — water emerges. Not in falls, not in streams, but in threads. Threads that begin as wet lines in the rock and thicken as they descend, joining and parting and rejoining, leaving silver traces in the dark patina. A dozen threads at any moment. A hundred more that have dried and dried again and left their calligraphy on the wall. The stone is not sweating — sweating is a bodily thing, a mammalian thing — but it is performing the same gesture, slowly, geologically, as if the mountain itself has run a low fever for a very long time and forgotten how to break it.

The floor is built of the things that have died here. A long slow compost, pressed into a living carpet that gives an inch under the foot before it holds. No path goes through it; every step is the first step; nothing in this bowl has been walked on twice in human memory, or in any other memory either. The surface is hummocked — raised in soft mounds whose centers are old trunks or stones or the buried bodies of larger things that came here and were not asked to leave. Between the mounds the water lies in thin black sheets that mirror the gray slit of sky and the inverted trees. If you stand still you can see things moving in these sheets, just below the surface — a slow pulse, a slight stirring, the pulse of a great many small lives packed into a thin film of cold water.

The mounds wear crowns. Fern — sword fern, hart's tongue, the small parsley fern, things smaller still that you cannot name without kneeling and parting them — grows so densely that it is almost a continuous surface at knee height, a soft canopy that closes over your boots when you push through. Under the fern canopy, the moss begins: sphagnum in the wetter places, broom moss, the kind of feather moss that walks with one bright green tip and a brown body, the haircap moss that puts up dark seed heads like tiny steeples. The moss is not one moss but a community of mosses, each with its preference, and the floor is the result of their long negotiation — a quilted surface of green and ochre and rust, set with the white stars of liverwort and the dull orange buttons of a fungus that has decided, for reasons of its own, to fruit here and not elsewhere.

The trees grow inward. Every one of them leans toward the center of the bowl, as if drawn by a faint magnet in the middle of the floor. They are alder mostly, and yew, and an occasional rowan that has seeded itself into a crack and committed to the dark. Their roots are sunk into the wet hummocks and drink from the same water that runs down the back wall. Their trunks are furred with moss to shoulder height. Their branches wear long beards of *Alectoria* and *Usnea*, the hanging lichens that take the cleanest air and give it back as quiet gray lace. The trees, leaning, meet overhead in a thin canopy that the light can neither penetrate nor properly strike. They are spaced close, and they have been close for a long time, and the result is a place that has never, in living memory, been properly lit.

The light here is a green dimness. It comes in sideways through the rim and the gaps between the leaning trunks, and is filtered through the saturated air before it arrives. There are no beams. There are no sharp shadows. Everything in the bowl is softly seen, as if a veil of gauze has been laid over the world and forgotten. At the brightest hour of the longest summer day, this is the quality of the light: the light of a shuttered room, of an aquarium, of the inside of a sealed jar full of cold water and green things. At other hours — and there are many other hours, more than there should be — the bowl is a dark place, a place where shapes resolve only when you have stood still long enough for your eyes to learn the new alphabet.

And the bowl is full.

The fauna here is not a presence but a saturation. Every surface is occupied; every surface is a city. The weeping stone wears a film of rotifers and diatoms, snails the size of pinheads, flatworms that move across the wet face like spilled ink. The moss is a metropolis of mites, springtails, and beetles no larger than rice grains, and they go about their errands in the dim green twilight as if the bowl were built for them and they alone had paid the architect. Under every fallen log — and the logs are many, half-swallowed by the moss, sunk into the floor until only the top curve is visible — are salamanders. Dozens of them in a single log, dark and slick and cold, stacked like wet coins, their gold eyes lit from within by some private sun. Push the fern aside and you will find newts. Move your boot an inch into the leaf litter and a slug the length of a finger will withdraw into itself with the patience of a thing that has nowhere to be. Lift a stone and the underside is a moving carpet of millipedes and woodlice and the small pale threads of worms that have spent their whole lives in the dark.

The water between the hummocks is dense with life. Tadpoles, mayfly nymphs, the larvae of caddisflies building their small cases from gravel, the larvae of stoneflies clinging to submerged stems. So many things move in this thin film of water that the water itself seems to pulse — a slow, irregular pulse, like a heart that has been beating so long it has forgotten it has a body. The salamanders come down at certain hours and add to the pulse. The beetles come down at other hours and add to it again. The whole floor of the bowl, water and hummock and fern and moss, is one continuous circulation, and you are standing inside a lung.

And there are larger things. A few small dark birds that fly in through the rim and never sing — they make only a single low note, repeated, like a question that has been asked so often it has worn a groove in the air. Something moves in the canopy that is bigger than a bird; you cannot see it; you hear only the slow, deliberate shifting of branches and the occasional careful sound of weight being redistributed. On the floor, in the deeper dimness at the foot of the back wall, something passes now and then — the size of a fox, or larger, but moving with the patience of a thing that has nowhere in particular to be. You do not see it. You see only the fern closing behind it.

All of these creatures watch. Not obviously — not with the alert regard of a mammal that has noticed you. But when you step, the soft rustle of the leaf litter pauses. The amphibians turn their heads a fraction. The beetles go still. The springtails stop their errands for a moment, as if a wind has gone through the room and every small flame has bent. Then, after a held breath, the bowl resumes — but slightly rearranged, slightly shifted in its pattern, as if a piece of music has moved to a new chord.

The sound of the bowl is mostly quiet. There is a drip from the back wall — a single drip, slow, steady, from a seam that has been dripping so long the stone beneath it has been worn into a small bowl of its own, a basin of black water that overflows once an hour and feeds another basin below it. There is the wet sound of small things moving through wet leaves, a continuous soft rustle that is almost white noise, almost not there, the sound of a great many small lives going about their business in the dark. There is the audible breathing of the salamanders, if you stand still. There is the thin hiss of water emerging from the rock — not falling, but being squeezed out, the way water is squeezed from a cloth, slowly, continuously, with a small pale sound. And there is, at long intervals, the single low note of the dark birds, repeated, unanswered.

The air is cool. Not cold — cool in the way a stone is cool in summer, the way water is cool when you cup your hands in it. It is saturated. Every breath tastes of cold stone, of cold water, of the green of moss, of the soft ammoniac breath of many small lives breathing together in a small place. It is air you could almost drink. It does not move. There is no wind in the bowl — the rim is high enough on three sides, and the trees close enough on the fourth, that the air inside has reached a rest, the way water in a sealed jar reaches a rest. Same temperature throughout. Same moisture throughout. Same stillness throughout. The atmosphere inside the bowl has, in some forgotten century, found its perfect equilibrium, and it has not been disturbed since.

There is a strangeness here that is hard to name. It is not that anything is wrong. It is that everything is *too right*. The ferns grow as if they had been arranged by a careful hand. The hummocks are placed with an eye for spacing that no accident would produce. The trails of water on the back wall form something like calligraphy, and the calligraphy is the same from year to year, the same characters, written in silver on the dark stone. The creatures have their niches, their ledges, their preferred stones and logs and corners, and they keep to them with a fidelity that has the air of a long agreement. The bowl has the look of a place that has been composed — not built, not made, but composed, slowly, over a long time, by a slow and patient hand that does not hurry.

And the hand has finished.

Nothing in this bowl changes. The drips fall in the same places. The trees lean in the same directions. The salamanders stack themselves in the same logs. The beetles run the same errands along the same fronds. The dark birds fly in through the same gap in the rim and ask the same low note. The thing in the canopy settles on the same branch and shifts at the same hour. The thing at the foot of the back wall passes at the same slow interval, and the fern closes behind it in the same way. The water does not rise; the trees do not fall; nothing is built and nothing is torn down. The creatures are born and die and are replaced, but the pattern of the bowl — the pattern that the bowl has spent so long composing — does not shift. It is a photograph in the world's album. You could leave this place for a hundred years and return, and the bowl would be exactly as you left it — the same drips, the same lean of the trees, the same congregation of small dark things on the wet stone, the same green dimness, the same cool saturated air, the same held breath, the same silence that resumes, slightly rearranged, like a piece of music moving to a new chord that turns out to be the chord it has always been.
