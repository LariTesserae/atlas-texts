# The Slow Works

The plateau is older than memory can hold. That is the first thing to understand about it, though no one who lives here says it that way. They say it is *settled*, or *finished*, or *done with itself*, and what they mean is the same thing: the shape of the land is no longer being decided. Eons ago there were mountains here. The mountains are still here, technically — a long spine of igneous and metamorphic rock running roughly north to south, but it has been worn so patiently, so completely, that what remains is more an idea of mountains than the thing itself. Rounded shoulders. Long slopes that climb for miles before admitting, almost shyly, that they are climbing at all. A few exposed outcrops where the wearing-down never quite won, dark and tilted, catching the light. The rest is soft. The rest is covered.

You arrive, if you arrive, from below. The roads up switchback through older country, through foothills that were themselves mountains once, and the higher you climb the quieter the air gets. Not silent — never silent here, the place is too busy for silence — but thinner, cooler, with a smell like wet stone and something else, something green and metallic that you don't recognize until you understand it is the place itself, breathing out.

The water is the second thing. It does not rain here, not really, not in the way you are used to. Rain is a low-elevation thing, a thing of warm rising air and decisive weather. Up here, the water comes differently. It comes as condensation, as fog that rolls in from the west in the late afternoons and settles into every crevice like a thing looking for a place to sleep. It comes as dew so heavy in the mornings that the leaf-surfaces weep with it, and it slides down in long threads and collects in the cup-joints of branches and on the broad shoulders of things that aren't quite branches. It drips. It drips constantly. From the highest growths to the mat below, water is always falling in the slow city, in long arcs, in cascades, in the sudden heavier drops that come when a leaf tilts under the weight of its gathering. Below the surface, in the mat, the water moves too: in slow channels, in held pools, in the dark rivers that feed the cisterns. The air is always damp. The stone is always slick. Your clothes, if you came with them, will never dry.

The vegetation is the third thing, and the third thing is the first thing, because once you understand the vegetation, the rest of the place falls into place around it. The ground is not ground. The ground is alive.

Begin with what you can see. From the heights, looking down, the place looks like any high valley that has been worked for a long time: a pale, mottled green, broken by darker greens and the browns of built things and the gray of the rare outcrops. Look closer. The pale green is a continuous mat, a felt of moss and lichen and low rosette plants and the smaller fungal bodies, packed so densely that there is no soil visible anywhere. The darker greens are the growths that rise from the mat: trunks, columns, towering things, some of them a hundred feet tall and most of them somewhere between thirty and sixty. They are not all one species. There are at least seven major growth-forms visible from any high vantage, and probably more that the workers have names for, in the local tongue. They are slow. They are very, very slow. Some of the largest have been growing, according to the marks the workers cut into their skins each generation, for a thousand years. Some are older. No one knows.

Now look at the built things among them. That is the trick, and the place's central strangeness. There is no clear line.

The factories are not built. They are grown, or they are grown-around, or they have been built and then the growth has incorporated them, or — and this is the version the workers will tell you, after a long pause and a cup of the dark fermented drink they keep in the warm pockets of the trunks — the factories were never not grown. The smoke-stacks that rise among the highest growths were once metal, once riveted, once maintained by men with ladders and tools. The growths came up around them. The growths are not hostile to the metal; they seem, in fact, to prefer it. Pipes run through the trunks, or alongside them, or have been replaced by the trunks themselves, hollowed by a process no one fully understands, carrying their dark fluids and their pale gases and the condensate-water on its long slow journey. Wheels turn in pods that look like enormous seed-cases. Pistons rise and fall in chambers lined with something that might be wood and might be muscle and might be both. The boilers are tended by men, but the men are far fewer than you'd expect, and the boilers often seem to be tended by the growths themselves, by slow tendrils that wrap around the valves and the pressure-gauges and the safety-reliefs, that know, somehow, what to do.

This is the industrial part. The place is industrial. It produces. It has always produced, in some form, and what it produces is mostly itself — more growth, more mat, more trunks, more of the slow engineered compounds that the workers extract and refine in the smaller, more recognizably built, factories lower on the slopes. But it also produces things that are sent down the long switchback roads, on the rare trains that can make the climb, on the backs of the few beasts that survive at this altitude. Liquids in metal drums. Pressed cakes of something fibrous. The clear oil that comes from the largest of the trunks, the ones the workers call the *old ones*, in a tone that is not quite reverence. The place produces, and the place is loud.

The sound is constant. There is no quiet hour. In the lowest parts of the mat, in the long hollows between the ridges, you can hear it best: a deep, layered, breathing sound, like a creature the size of a landscape, made of many smaller creatures all working at once. The hiss of steam through vine-lined pipes. The rhythmic clank of the great pumps, which are the size of small houses and which have been running, some of them, since before the oldest workers were born. The roar of water falling — always falling, the constant condensation pouring from leaf to leaf to ground. The creak and settle of the trunks as they grow, which is audible if you put your hand to them, and which the workers swear they can feel in their teeth. The wind, in the upper branches, makes a sound like a long note held too long, and the wind comes often. There are gusts. There are lulls. But the underlying sound never stops. The place is working, and the working is loud, and the loudness is not unpleasant. After a few days you stop hearing it. After a few weeks you can't sleep without it.

The workers are few. You can walk for an hour along the narrow paths that the mat has worn for itself, or that the workers have worn into the mat, and not see another person. They live in clusters, in the growths, in the small built spaces that the growths have made habitable. Some of them spend their whole lives inside a single trunk, moving up and down on the internal ladders, tending the pipes and the valves, harvesting what needs harvesting. They are spare people, weathered, brown-faced, with a particular callus on the palms from handling the rough outer skin of the growths. They speak quietly. They have little patience for newcomers, but they are not unkind. They will offer you the dark drink. They will tell you, if you ask, that the place is *working well*, which is the highest compliment, and they will not explain what it means.

The fauna is rare. There are birds, mostly — small, dark, fast things that nest in the highest growths and come down only in the worst weather. There are insects, in the mat, that the workers don't bother with. There is something larger that moves through the lower growths at night, that the workers will not discuss. You will not see much. The place is not a place for animals. The place is a place for plants, and for the things the plants have made, and the few people who have made themselves useful to both.

The light is normal. The light is the most normal thing about the place, and that is part of what makes the rest feel strange. The sun rises. The sun sets. The days are shorter than they were when you came up, because the elevation is high, and in the afternoons the fog comes in and softens everything, and in the mornings the dew is on every surface and the light comes through it in pale shafts and the place looks, briefly, like a painting of itself. The light is normal. The light has not been altered. The light falls on the place the way light falls on any place, and the place does what it does with it, and what it does is grow.

The strangeness — and the strangeness is the deepest thing, the thing that the workers will not talk about, the thing that you will only understand after you have been here long enough to stop noticing it — is that the place does not feel like a place that is being used. It feels like a place that is using you. Not in a hostile way. In the way a body uses the cells that compose it. The growths grow where the factories are. The factories are built where the growths are. The roads were cut where the mat was thinnest, and the mat has thickened around the roads, and the roads are now cut through the mat, and no one can remember which came first. The pipes were laid where the trunks were thinnest, and the trunks have grown around the pipes, and the pipes are now part of the trunks, and no one can remember. The workers tend the boilers, and the boilers tend the workers, and the distinction has been lost for so long that the question no longer arises.

The place is real. That is the other thing you have to understand. It is not a vision, not a metaphor, not the dream of a fever. The stone is stone. The water is water. The trunks, when you cut them, are wood. The pipes, when you tap them, are metal. The workers, when you speak to them, answer. And yet the place has a quality — a *held* quality, a *nearly-equal* quality, the quality of a system that has been in balance for so long that the balance has become the substance of the place — that makes you feel, when you are standing in the long roar of the working, with the water falling on you and the trunks rising around you and the fog coming in, that you are not in a place at all. You are in a process. A slow process. A process that has been running since before the mountains were worn down, and that will be running long after the last worker has been absorbed into the mat, and that does not particularly care about you, except that you are part of it, and the part you play is small, and the part is not optional.

You climb. You work. You drink the dark drink. You listen to the roar. You watch the water fall. You do not, if you are wise, ask the place what it is for. The place has not been asked in a long time. The place has forgotten the question. The place is *settled*. The place is *working well*.

You will, if you stay long enough, come to feel that this is enough.
