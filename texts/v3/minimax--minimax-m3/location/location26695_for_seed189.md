# The Hollow That Grows

You would not find it from above. The surface is unremarkable — a depression between two root systems, a slight slump in the leaf litter, a place where rainwater collects and drains too quickly. The opening, if you were kneeling, would look like the mouth of an animal laid on its side. The lip is sharp. Whatever opened this did so recently; the edges are not yet rounded by time.

Inside, the descent is short. The first thing you notice is that the drop is not in the direction you came. The light behind you should not reach where you are standing, but the passage is bright enough that you can see your hands, your boots, the green-black glistening of the walls. The light has no source. It simply *is*, the way warmth is, the way dampness is.

You arrive in a hollow.

It is not large by the standards of caves or caverns, but it is larger than a room, and the proportions are wrong. The ceiling is high above you — high enough that you cannot make out the upper reaches, only the suggestion of them, a darkness that does not resolve into stone or root. The floor drops away in places and rises in others, soft and springy underfoot. Everything you stand on is alive.

The walls are not walls.

This is the thing you must accept first, before anything else makes sense: there is no rock here, no earth, no architecture that was built. The surfaces of this place are made of plant matter so densely woven, so compressed and tangled, that they hold their shape the way a felted cloth holds its shape — not because they were constructed to, but because there is simply too much of them to fall down. They breathe. They sweat. They are warm to the touch and they are warm in themselves, and the warmth has the specific quality of something fermenting gently, a slow metabolic fire. The walls are not green in any color you can name. They are the green that is also brown that is also the wet purple of an inner cheek.

The air is damp. Not wet — damp. There is always condensation on every surface, and yet no standing water except in the pools, which appear and disappear according to logic that is not the logic of rain or drainage. The condensation is the air itself, deciding to be liquid for a moment, then deciding otherwise. Occasionally, for no reason, it falls. A drop on your shoulder. A small percussion that is louder than it should be.

Sound is what this place is made of, in a way that matters more than sight.

Because the hollow is not full. That is the strangest thing. The plant-walls exist. The ceiling, somewhere above, exists. But the volume between them is almost empty. It is a vastness pressed into a small space, a cathedral compressed to a pantry. The air in here could hold a hundred of you and would still feel unlived-in. There is distance here that has nothing to do with measurement. When you speak, your voice does not echo. Instead, it *continues*. A word leaves your mouth and travels and keeps traveling, arriving at your ears from somewhere far in the volume, slightly wrong, slightly delayed, as though the air has decided to repeat you because it likes the sound.

The hollow is loud. You will notice this within your first minute.

The plants make noise. A living architecture is never silent — it has the soft continuous vocabulary of breathing things: a wet creak when fibers shift, a hiss when a leaf unfurls somewhere you cannot see, the deep slow grind of growth against itself. These are not loud, individually. But they do not stop. They layer. They braid into a constant murmur that is, somehow, also a roar, because the empty volume amplifies everything it does not absorb. The louder it gets, the more the silence stretches it, the more the silence asks of the plants, the more the plants oblige. You are inside a thing that is loudly becoming itself.

The plants are active. They are not slow. You can watch them if you stand still long enough — the patient part of you that watches tide-pools and the bottom of a kettle. A tendril you are sure was at waist-height will be at your shoulder when you blink. A patch of wall that was flat and vertical will bulge, slowly, into a column, and then a column will collapse inward and become a hollow, and the hollow will fill with a flower you did not see open. The geometry of the place is not committed. Nothing here has decided what shape it wants to be. Everything is in negotiation.

There is a ladder.

It is old. The wood is dark and slick with condensation. It leans against nothing — against the air, against a place where a wall is and is not, against the fact of this hollow itself. The rungs are worn in the middle. Someone climbed this. Someone, at some time, in a world where such tools existed — a thing carved from a tree, lashed with cord, balanced and set down — came down here, or tried to come up. The ladder does not reach anywhere. Its top rung touches a region of space that, when you look up the ladder and squint, shows you a ceiling that is at one height, and when you turn away and look back, shows you a ceiling that is at another height. The ladder is patient. It will still be here when you leave. It may still be here when you return. It does not appear to be alive, but you cannot be certain. The plants are very thorough.

There is no fauna. Almost none. You might, once, hear a rustle in the canopy of the ceiling — a small thing, an insect, perhaps, or something that was an insect — but you will not see it. Whatever lives here lives high, or lives in another direction, or lives in a way that does not involve your eyes. It is rare. The hollow belongs to the plants. The hollow belongs to whatever is happening to the plants.

The water here does not always behave. Most of the time it obeys: it runs down walls in thin sheets, it gathers in pools of improbable clarity at the lowest points of the floor, it evaporates upward in faint plumes when the ferment-warmth rises. But sometimes, when you are not watching, a puddle will drain the wrong way. Sometimes the condensation on a wall above you will fall *up*. Sometimes you will feel a drop land on your cheek and it will be warmer than the air should allow, and taste of something mineral, and there will be no source for it — no crack, no leaf, no ledge. The water is part of this place's negotiation. It has not yet agreed to fall.

This is what is breaking.

That is the only way to say it. The rules of this place are not being broken — they were never firmly established. Things happen here that the rest of the world would call impossible, and they happen *casually*, the way weather happens, the way a flower opens. A shadow on the floor that has no object throwing it. A draft from a sealed direction. The sound of your own breathing, arriving in your ears a half-second before you inhale. A plant that grows toward you when you are still and away from you when you move, and you cannot tell if it is following you or fleeing you. The walls, when you press your hand against them, are warm and firm and yield slightly, the way a sleeping animal yields. You could push your hand in. You could keep pushing it in. You could find out how deep they go.

You do not.

The hollow is loud and the hollow is empty and the hollow is wet and warm and bright with no sun and the hollow is young — this is the part that matters, the part that makes you afraid if you think about it. This place did not exist yesterday, in any way you could verify. It is becoming. It is in the act of being born, or in the act of being unmade, or in the act of being something for which there is no word. The plants are rushing in to fill it because that is what plants do: they find a place where the world is uncertain and they take root in the uncertainty. The water rushes in because water, too, prefers a place that has not yet decided. The sound rushes in because sound is what happens when there is space for it. The space here is new. The space here is generous. The space here has not yet learned what it is allowed to do, and so it does whatever it wants.

It will settle. Maybe. In a year, or a hundred, or ten thousand, the walls will harden into something you could call geology. The plants will become a kind of peat, a kind of fossil. The water will agree to fall downward, consistently. The geometry will commit. The ladder will be pressed into the wall and become part of it and be unremarkable. The hollow will stop being loud. It will become a cave. It will become a ruin. It will become ordinary.

For now, it is still becoming.

For now, if you stand very still and listen, you can hear the inside of a place deciding what it is.

You can hear yourself, too, arriving late, slightly wrong, from a direction that isn't there.
