The city is small. You can walk from one edge to the other in twenty minutes if you walk the long way, in eight if you cut through the Pipes. Everyone knows this. The city is small the way a fist is small, or a heart, or a held breath. It is dense in the way that a fist is dense: not because of any single hard thing inside it, but because everything has been pushed close, has grown close, has pressed close over so many years that the pressing has become a kind of weather.

The Pipes are why anyone comes here, or why anyone stays. They say you can follow the Pipes down and reach the deep aquifer, and from the deep aquifer, the sea. But nobody follows the Pipes down. The Pipes go up, mostly. They rise from the marshy ground in clustered stands, like organ pipes in a cathedral that has been tipped on its side and half-buried. They are made of cast iron, mostly. Some of the older ones are made of something else—lead, or a lead-ish alloy that has gone pewter-gray and soft—and these ones weep constantly in the humidity, beads of condensation racing down their flanks and pooling at their feet in shallow, rust-colored saucers. The iron ones weep too, but slower. They sound different. The lead ones sing.

The light in the city is the color of a tarnished spoon. It comes from above through a permanent overcast, and from below through the grates—hundreds of them, set into the cobbles and into the plank-walks that bridge the lowest streets. Through the grates you can see a faint orange glow, and rising heat, and the suggestion of something moving far below. Nobody talks about what is down there. The grates are warm to sit on, in the cold parts of the year, and children do sit on them, and old people lean against the railings around them, and the warmth comes up through them like the memory of warmth. Some of the grates have grown small ecosystems of moss and a kind of pale, stretching fern that has never seen true sun and does not need to.

The sound is the first thing. If you arrive from outside, the sound is what you notice before anything else, because it is loud and it is everywhere and it does not stop. It is not a sound with edges. It is the sound of steam escaping from a thousand small valves, of pistons breathing in slow synchrony, of water running through pipes that are too narrow for it so it sings as it goes, of metal expanding and contracting in the heat, of hammers falling somewhere distant, of a great wheel turning somewhere beneath your feet. The hammers fall in a rhythm that is almost a heartbeat. The wheel is the city's, or the city's is the wheel's; it is hard, after a while, to know which is turning which. People speak to each other in shouts, or in the flat palms and slow gestures of people who have lived all their lives inside a sound. Visitors from outside cannot sleep. The citizens sleep like the dead.

The buildings crowd together. They are two stories, mostly, three in the old center, and they lean on each other the way tired people lean on each other—without apology, without much hope of standing up straight again. They are built of brick that was fired from the local clay, which is the color of dried blood, and the bricks have darkened over the years with soot and damp until the whole city is the color of an old bruise. Windows are small and thick-glassed and most of them are lit, even in the day, with a yellow-orange glow that you can see reflected in the wet cobbles. The doors are heavy and round-arched and they are never quite closed; the heat and the steam and the slowness of the citizens and the general agreement that air is for sharing mean that everything is always a little bit open. You can hear the sounds of kitchens through the doors, and arguments, and the small mechanical ticking of clocks and the larger mechanical breathing of the city itself.

Vegetation has not been defeated, here. It has negotiated. Moss grows on every north-facing surface, in thick green-black carpets. Ferns root in the gutters and the cracked eaves and the laps of statuary that nobody remembers commissioning. A kind of vine with small white flowers climbs the Pipes in some of the outer districts, and the citizens have stopped trying to remove it because the vine is what keeps the Pipes from sweating so badly in summer, and the small white flowers feed something—some insect, some other small invertebrate—that the city is fond of, in a way it does not talk about. There are no animals. There is a small pale moth that lives near the grates, but nobody calls it an animal. It is just part of the light.

The air is wet. It is always wet. The humidity is a citizen, and a heavy one. It sits on your skin. It beads on every metal surface. It makes the brick walls weep. It makes the wooden walkways swell and soften until they are almost sponge. After a rain, which is often, the lowest streets flood to the ankle, and the citizens wear tall boots, and the children put on tall boots even when there is no rain, because they like the sounds of the boots on the planks. The floods do not go away; they are absorbed by the ground, or they evaporate in the heat that rises from the grates, or they are simply lived with, the way the steam is lived with, the way the sound is lived with. The city is built on a slight bowl, and the bowl is filling, slowly, with the warm wet breath of the earth beneath it, and nobody talks about that either, in the same way that nobody talks about what is at the bottom of the Pipes.

The pace is slow. The citizens move slowly, and speak slowly, and do not seem to be in a hurry about anything. This is not because they are lazy. It is because the city is heavy, and hot, and wet, and loud, and a body in such a place conserves itself. The citizens are patient, the way the moss is patient, the way the rust is patient, the way the hammers are patient, falling and falling and falling in their slow rhythm. Things change here, but they change across long durations. A pipe is replaced over the course of a season. A street is repaved over the course of a decade. The vine on the Pipes has been climbing for as long as anyone remembers, and will climb for as long as anyone will remember, and the moss on the bricks has been darkening for longer than that. The city is not a thing that is being made. It is a thing that is being maintained, in the way that a body is maintained, or a sound, or a marriage.

The grates glow. That is the strangeness of it, perhaps, or one of the strangenesses. The light is not fire-light. It is not electric. It is something that comes up from below, in a color that the eye wants to call orange but that the mind, after a while, begins to suspect is its own thing. It pulses, very faintly, with the turning of the wheel. It is warm in a way that is different from the warmth of a stove or a sun. It is the warmth of something that is alive, or of something that wants you to think it is alive. The citizens do not look at it. They have made a life in the warm orange glow, and they have decided, collectively, over many generations, that it is better not to ask what makes the glow. The moss has not asked. The ferns have not asked. The small pale moths have not asked. The city turns, and glows, and steams, and is very old, and will be very old for a long time.
