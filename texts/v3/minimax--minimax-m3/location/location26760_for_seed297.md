A basin sits low, broader than a day's walk across, and warm. The light is the color of late afternoon everywhere at once, although there is no sun in the sky, or there is a sun and the sky is something else, or the light is simply how this patch of the world happens to be. The light is ordinary in tone, normal in warmth, but you cannot find what casts it. There is no shadow with a clear source. The shadows there are fall from the leaves above, but they arrive late, half a beat after the leaves that should have cast them, and they are darker than they should be, and they hold a kind of cool that lingers.

The geology is young. Ridges of pale, almost bone-colored rock rise in long spines from the basin floor, sharp-edged, unweathered, as if they had been pushed up this morning and would not be here tomorrow. They have been here a long time. They have not changed. The rock has veins of a darker mineral that catch the light in two colors at once, blue and orange, simultaneously, without flickering. The ridges run in directions that almost make sense; some converge when they should diverge, some curve back toward themselves, as though the ground were a page that had been refolded. They look like they are still rising. They are not. They have settled into the act of rising and will not come out of it.

Between the ridges, growth. Dense, layered, every kind of saturation. A soft carpet of something mineral-cool underfoot, almost gelatinous, faintly iridescent, that you would call moss if you were using your mouth before your mind. Out of it, thousands of stalks per square meter, standing, holding their shapes, in colors the eye wants to name green and indigo and sulfur but cannot quite land on — they are like a memory of those colors, or those colors seen through a thin film of something else. The stalks do not wave. There is no wind. They are not in wind.

Higher, the canopy. Thin plates the size and shape of leaves, but rigid, like beaten metal or sheets of mica, overlapping in dense layers. The light through them is broken into a patchwork of shadows on the ground that does not match the spacing of the leaves above. There is a delay, the same delay as everywhere here, the world's lag of half a second. You watch a leaf; a half-second later its shadow slides into place on the carpet, slightly darker than physics would suggest, and stays.

No water. No stream, no pool, no dew, no wetness. The air is dry, warm, still. The growth is lush without water. Touch a stalk: it is cool, slightly slick, but not wet; the slickness is metallic, faintly sweet, with a numbing aftertaste. The growth is solvent-fed by something the place offers in lieu of water, perhaps a vapor off the rock, perhaps a humidity of a different kind, perhaps the air itself, which is not quite air. The growth has reached its configuration and holds it. It has held it for a long time.

Fauna is rare. In an hour of walking you might see two, three small things. They move through the growth in ways that do not quite touch it — there is a fine clearance, a hair's width of gap, between the creature and the leaves. They are six-limbed or four-limbed or something else, their bodies folded along seams you do not see until they move. They make no sound. They leave, as they pass, a faint trail in the air, a line of slight disturbance like a heat shimmer, that closes behind them. They are not numerous. They are at the density they have always been at. The place is in equilibrium down to the last insect.

Mostly quiet. The sound is small: a low hum, everywhere, sourceless, that you stop hearing until you listen for it, and then it is the floor of the world. Occasional small sounds, clicks and ticks and single held notes, coming from the growth as if a stable system were reassuring itself — a leaf-plate adjusting, a stalk settling. Once, far off, something that might have been a call, except you never heard the end of it. The hum might be the light. The hum might be the rocks holding their just-risen state. The hum might be the place being the place.

There is no building. There is no machine. There is nothing made. But the place is engineered, in a sense you do not have words for, by a hand that did not build anything, that simply set the conditions and let them run. The way the rocks are arranged. The way the growth maintains itself without water. The way the shadows lag. The way the light comes from no source. The way time has a half-second seam. This is the engineering. It was done a long time ago, or it is being done always, or it is the local physics of this basin, and the rest of the world is different. There is no edge to it. Walk all day and the configuration ahead of you is the configuration behind you: young geology, dense growth, broken causality, frozen time.

The reality here is wrong in small ways that add up. Causality has a lag. Distant things do not shrink the way they should — a ridge at the horizon looks the same size as a ridge a hundred meters away, and only when you walk toward it does it grow, slowly, as if it had been thinking. The light at the edge of a leaf has a wrong thickness, like it is lit from inside. The growth seems to lean toward you as you approach, not from wind, not from growth, just from how the space is. You are not quite sure how long you have been walking. You are not quite sure the walking is over.

Nothing changes. The leaves do not fall. The stalks do not die. The rocks do not erode. The light does not shift. The hum does not vary. The basin is held in its configuration, a permanent state of just-having-risen, just-having-grown, just-having-become. It has been like this. It will be like this. The dynamic state of the place is its stasis.

You sit down in the growth. The carpet is cool under you. The stalks stand around you. A small creature passes, leaving its shimmer, and is gone. A leaf-plate clicks somewhere above. The hum holds. The light holds. The basin holds. The shadows, late as ever, settle into their pattern around you, slightly too dark, slightly too cool, exactly the same.
