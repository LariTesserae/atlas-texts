The town sits at 4,200 meters, give or take, though the altitude is one of those things you stop trusting here. The mountain is called — but it doesn't matter what it's called, because the name changes depending on who's holding the map, and the map changes depending on which day you unfurl it. By the surveyors' reckoning it has existed for nine years. By the geologists', maybe seven hundred thousand. By the lichen's reckoning, which is the most reliable, it has existed for as long as it intends to.

The cloud sits on the mountain like a hat that has been there longer than the mountain. Or the mountain sits inside the cloud like a fist inside a glove. Both are true; the cloud is the older thing in some readings and the younger in others. It doesn't move quickly, the cloud — it has a patience to it, a thickness, a habit of sitting. It is what gives the place its dimness, this light: a flat gray, almost silver, that flattens shadows until the town looks pressed into the rock like a coin. There are no sharp edges here. No strong silhouettes. The lanterns that line the slag-yard don't really light anything; they just mark where the air is. Sometimes you can see the light bend around them in a way it shouldn't.

The town has a name, but the name is one of those words that doesn't sit right in the mouth, the kind that suggests it's been translated from something that wasn't language. The locals have several pronunciations for it, and the disagreement is friendly. The town is maybe three thousand people, depending on who's counting and which census, and the census is done by a clerk who is also, by most accounts, the same person as the mayor, the postmistress, the woman who runs the smelter, the man who keeps the goats, and the goat. This is unusual but not, here, particularly noteworthy.

The buildings are stacked. They climb the slope in terraces, each one taller than the last by a fraction, so when you stand at the bottom you can see the whole town at once, like a staircase that has given up. They are mostly stone — rough-cut, locally quarried — and the stone is the color of a bruise after it has stopped hurting: pale violet, gray-green, a kind of lilac that shouldn't occur in stone. It weathers to something almost white where the mist touches it. The mortar between the stones is dark and has the consistency, when fresh, of cold honey. It sets slowly. There are buildings here that have been mortaring for twenty years and are still soft to the touch. No one worries about this.

The streets are paved with the same stone, set in patterns that look, from above, like writing on the side of the mountain — the kind of writing you can't quite read but can sort of feel. The patterns shift over the years, slowly, the way a glacier shifts. If you came back after a decade you'd find the streets had rearranged themselves into a slightly different version of themselves. The postmistress swears this is the same map. You can take her word for it or you can not.

The mine is the reason for the town. The mine is up the slope, past the last terrace, where the rock face has been cut open like a wound that won't stop showing something new. The ore here is — well. The ore is what brought the engineers, the company men, the railroad, the smelters, the rail yards full of car-loadings that go down the mountain in a slow, looping descent. The ore is heavy in the hand and has a faint grain to it, like very fine sand pressed into metal. It is the color of something you might see if you closed your eyes and pressed on them. When you hold a piece up to the dim light, you can see through it, faintly, in a way that suggests there is another side to it than the side you're looking at.

The miners say the mountain talks to them. Not in words — more in the way a clock talks to a room. A low hum that the stone gives off, mostly below hearing, but you can feel it in your teeth if you stand in the right shaft. Some of the older shafts have started, in the past few years, to give off a smell like rain on hot iron, even when it is not raining. The shafts have begun, in places, to give off small lights, the color of something between green and gray, that don't move and don't quite illuminate. They are sometimes mistaken for stars. They are not stars.

The smelter is on the south face of town, where the wind is least and the light is most dim. It is a low building, more of a hollow than a building, with three chimneys that don't quite reach the cloud line. The chimneys breathe a vapor that isn't smoke — it's heavier, more patient, more like breath. It spreads out across the terraces when the wind is wrong, and it hangs, and it doesn't dissipate for hours. The children play in it. The adults walk through it to get to the post office. The lichen loves it. There are varieties of lichen here that have not been classified and may not be classifiable, growing in colors that don't quite fit on a chart — the lichen is part of why the place is so lush.

Because it is lush. Despite the elevation, despite the rock, despite the cloud, despite the slow strangeness of the air — the place is lush. Water: there is water everywhere and almost nowhere. It condenses on every surface, a fine mist that beads on stone and runs down in tiny rivulets, gathering in pockets and pans that the masons have cut into the terraces for this purpose. These cisterns are full always. They are drunk from, cooked with, washed in, and the water has a faint taste like metal and like nothing. Moss grows where the moss can reach, which is everywhere. The stone walls are bearded with it. The roofs are furred. The steps between terraces are slick and soft underfoot, and you walk on them the way you would walk on something alive.

Ferns come up between the paving stones, and in some places the streets have begun to give up and become gardens. There is a kind of wild celery that grows in the cracks, and a small onion-relative with purple bulbs that the goats are fond of. There are flowers — white, mostly, but with a faint flush to them that isn't pink, isn't quite. They smell like perfume that has been left in a drawer for a long time. The florist has tried to cultivate them. The florist has given up. They grow where they grow, which is everywhere water gathers, and they don't transplant.

The fauna is the strangest part, and that is saying something, because the rest is strange enough. There are goats, and the goats are more or less normal, though they have begun, over generations, to develop a kind of luminescence in the eyes that makes them easy to spot in the dim light — they look, in the fog, like a constellation moving at hoof-speed. There are dogs. The dogs are mostly normal but have grown unnaturally long in the body, as if they have been stretched, and they move with a kind of liquid grace that isn't quite canine. There are birds — a few species, none of them migratory, all of them staying here year-round because where would they go. The birdsong here is not the birdsong of other places; it is closer to a kind of low whistle, a tuning, and it is mostly audible in the early morning when the cloud is at its thinnest.

And there are the others. The things that aren't quite accounted for. The miners see them in the shafts sometimes — a shape that is not quite a shape, a movement that is more like an absence moving. The children have named several of them. The postmistress has a ledger where she tracks their appearances, which has been going for eleven years and has become something more than a ledger. There is a beast, or a thing that has the form of a beast, that lives in the upper mine workings, that the miners leave offerings for — mostly cheese, sometimes flowers. It has been seen maybe nine times. The descriptions of it never quite match. It is bigger than you'd expect and smaller than it should be.

The sound here is the loudest thing about it, after a while. The wind, which is constant, which is not constant but feels constant, is a kind of high whistle. The ore in the crushers makes a sound like teeth. The smelter hums. The chimneys breathe. The fauna contribute a kind of running commentary — the goats, the dogs, the birds, the unnamed things. And underneath all of it is the mountain itself, which makes a sound that is felt more than heard: a kind of low pressure, a vibration, a hum that has been measured at various points and never quite nailed down. Some readings suggest it is slowing. Some suggest it is quickening. Some suggest it is doing both.

The town is slow. Time here moves at a rate that has been remarked upon by everyone who has visited and not stayed. The clocks run slightly differently. The bread takes a little longer to rise. The children grow a little slower. The lichen grows faster. There is a sense, in the town, of long processes — the mountain lifting, the rock wearing, the ore being smelted, the town building itself out of the same stone it is being built into. The terraces are old, but they are also new, because the stone they are cut from is new, is being made, is still in some sense becoming stone. The masons say they are working with a material that is still deciding what it wants to be.

At night — if there is night; the dimness doesn't really go away — the town looks like something seen through water. The lights of the smelter, the lanterns, the bioluminescent goats, the small green-gray lights in the mine shafts — they all smudge together in the cloud. The town doesn't disappear. It becomes something softer. The clouds muffle it. The sounds become one sound, which is the sound of the mountain breathing, which is the sound of the town existing, which is the sound of this place being what it is.

If you stand at the very top, on the new rock above the highest terrace, where the air is thinnest and the dimness is brightest, you can see, sometimes, the things on the other side of the cloud. They are like cities. They are like forests. They are like the sea. They are not any of these things but they have the quality of any of these things. The miners call it *the underview* because it looks, mostly, like something seen from underwater. The children call it *the ceiling.* The postmistress calls it *the survey.* It is the thing that the town is being built on top of, or underneath, or inside of, depending on how you measure it.

The ore, when it is smelted, becomes something. The something is shipped down the mountain, on the rail that loops down through the cloud for forty miles, into the lowlands where it is used in ways that the town doesn't really know about or ask. The townspeople don't ask because they know what it does when it is taken away. The townspeople don't ask because the answers would change the place, and the place is what they know. The mountain hums. The smelter breathes. The goats glow. The children play in the vapor. The lichen grows in colors. The shafts give off small lights. The postmistress keeps her ledger. The town goes on. The town is slow, and damp, and dim, and strange, and it is what it is, and it has been for longer than it hasn't been.
