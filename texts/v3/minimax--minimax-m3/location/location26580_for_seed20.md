# The Singing Stack

You do not arrive here. You rise here.

That distinction matters, because arrival implies a path, a direction, a place you came from, and this place has none of those. There is no approach. The water is on all sides at once, and below, and somehow — though your eyes insist this is impossible — at the level of your own throat when you breathe in.

It is warm. The water, the air, the stone under your feet, all of it warm in the way that something which has been absorbing the sun for a very long time is warm, deep-warm, the warmth of a body that has forgotten how to cool itself. Not unpleasant. Closer to the warmth of being held.

The light is small. There is a sky somewhere above — you can feel its weight, the way the air presses down — but it has been filtered through so many layers of weather that by the time it reaches the stone you stand on, it has become something like the inside of a closed eye. Grey-gold. Directionless. The shadows it casts do not behave as shadows should: they fall upward as readily as down, and the brightest patches sometimes appear under the overhangs of rock rather than above them. You learn, after some time — you cannot say how much; the clocks here are unreliable — not to trust where your eyes tell you to look.

The sound is enormous.

This is the first fact of the place, the fact the place is built around, the fact from which everything else extends. It is not that the world is loud. The world here is *audible*. There is no silence. There is no quiet. There is only the continuous, all-directions, no-source-identifiable roar of water in violent motion. Waves that have been breaking against this rock since the rock existed, and the rock is young, sharp-edged, still learning what shape it will be. Every wave that hits it makes a sound. Every wave that misses it makes a sound. The wind that is generated by the falling of the water makes a sound. The water that is generated by the falling of the wind makes a sound. There is the deeper sound too, the one you cannot quite hear because it lives below the threshold of the others — the slow groan of the basalt adjusting to its own weight, the tectonic sigh of something still being born. You stop trying to speak after a while. Your voice cannot find a gap.

The basalt itself is the size of a cathedral, perhaps two cathedrals, if cathedrals could be measured. It is not tall. It is *up*. A columnar thing, jointed the way young basalt is jointed, into hexagonal pillars stacked against each other with the irregular precision of a thing that has not yet decided whether it wants to be a ruin or a foundation. The pillars at the base are widest, fat and dark and undercut by the water, worn only at the line where the water's edge — there is no real edge, the water is everywhere — passes through them. As you climb, the pillars narrow, sharpen, become more vertical, more aggressive in their geometry. The very top of the thing — if it has a top; you suspect it does not, not really — would be a single point of stone the size of a fist, or a knife.

There are stairs.

You notice them late because they are part of the stone rather than cut into it. They might be cut into it. It is hard to tell with the young rock. They spiral up the column in a way that is not quite a spiral; the steps repeat, and repeat, and the same step is sometimes two steps, sometimes none, and you can never be sure if you are ascending or merely orbiting. They are wet. Everything is wet. There is no place here that is not wet, and yet the wetness is not the wetness of rain or spray — it is the wetness of having been underwater for a long time and only just now being above it. The stone weeps a thin mineral tear at the joints, and the stairs weep, and the pillars weep, and the sounds they make when they weep are folded into the roar of the water so completely that you cannot hear them but you feel them in the bones of your feet.

The water is the color of a bruise in late stage: violet-grey, the color of something that has been hit many times. It moves. It moves constantly. It moves in directions that should not be possible, given the apparent wind direction, given the apparent geography. Waves rise from places where there is no slope to generate them. Whirlpools open and close without leaving a trace. The surface is never flat. The surface is never still. The surface is the most active surface you have ever seen, and yet it is only the surface; below it, the water is darker, and deeper, and you can feel — again, not see — that the depth goes down much further than the height of the basalt goes up. The basalt is a needle in a floor of warm ink.

Around the basalt, scattered at distances you cannot reliably estimate, are other stones. Younger ones, smaller ones, some just barely breaking the surface, some fully submerged but throwing shadows up onto the underside of the waves as though the light were coming from below. They form a kind of archipelago, or a kind of constellation, or a kind of sentence whose grammar you do not speak. Between them, the water churns. The water between the basalt and its neighbors is louder than the water on the open side, a compression of sound in the throat of a narrowing gap, and when a wave comes through that gap it is squeezed into a shape that is more vertical than horizontal and it hits the basalt like a fist. This happens often. This is one of the things that is happening now.

There are no trees. There is no grass. There are no plants you would recognize as plants. On the basalt, in the cracks between the hexagonal columns, in the protected hollows at the lee of the higher steps, there are things that might be lichen, or might be coral, or might be something else entirely. They are the color of rust and the color of dried blood and the color of the inside of a shell you once held to your ear. They are sparse, and they are tough, and they cling to the stone with the determination of a thing that knows it is the only living thing on this rock and intends to remain so. They do not grow so much as endure.

There is no animal life. Not visible. Not audible. Not in any of the senses you have access to. The place is not silent with animal absence — it is *absent* with animal absence, a kind of negative space that has its own presence. Whatever lived here, or whatever would have lived here, has not arrived, or has already left, or was never going to. The basalt is too young for an ecology. It is the morning of the second day, and the second day has not yet populated itself.

The faint marks of human presence are older than the basalt, which should be impossible.

You find them halfway up, on the wider steps where the stair widens into something like a landing. They are carved into the stone with a tool that was not made for this stone, or was not made for any stone. A series of small circular pits, evenly spaced, connected by shallow grooves. They could be a game. They could be a tally. They could be a map. They could be the remains of a structure so old that it has become the stone it was built from, the way a tree becomes the soil it grew in. They are scattered. There are more of them lower down, at the waterline, half-eaten by the warm sea, visible only at low tide — but the tide here is not a tide, the water does not recede in any predictable way, and so the lower marks appear and disappear according to rules you cannot deduce. You have seen three of them. You suspect there are dozens more, lower, deeper, in the part of the basalt that is permanently underwater but somehow still legible.

There are no devices. There are no tools. There is nothing here that requires a hand to have made it, except those pits and grooves, and the stairs, and the stairs might be natural, and the pits might be natural, and you begin to suspect that everything you are taking as evidence of intention is actually evidence of geology's tendency toward pattern.

The light flickers. Not in a way you can predict — not lightning, not the strobing of a fluorescent — but in a way that suggests the source of the light is not a single source. Sometimes the entire scene brightens for a moment, as though something above the clouds has shifted. Sometimes a patch of basalt that should be in shadow becomes briefly lit from an angle that has no corresponding sun. Sometimes the color of the water changes — from bruise to pewter to the dull copper of a coin held underwater — and changes back without any reason you can identify. The light is unreliable. You do not trust it. You do not need to trust it, because there is nothing here that requires you to see accurately in order to survive it. The place is not interested in you. The place is not interested in anything.

And yet.

And yet there are moments, between waves, in the gaps where the roar drops by some small fraction, that you feel the basalt *attend*. Not to you. To itself. As though the stone is checking its own inventory, counting its own columns, verifying that it is still the shape it thinks it is. These moments are brief. They are also wrong. They should not exist in geology. They are moments when the place, briefly, considers whether it is a place at all, and finds the question difficult.

The water is warm against the basalt. The basalt is warm against your feet. The air is warm against your face. The sound is enormous against everything. The light is dim against the inside of your eyes. You are standing on a young piece of a young thing in the middle of a sea that has not decided what direction it wants to be moving in, in a part of the world where the rules are being tested by being obeyed too literally. The basalt is the size of a small cathedral. The basalt is the only dry ground for a distance you cannot measure. The basalt is not quite real.

It is also the only thing that is.

You do not arrive here. You rise here. You rise, and you stand, and the water is everywhere, and the sound is everything, and the place continues to be a place for as long as you continue to stand on it, and not one moment longer.
