The world here is drowning. Not violently — there is no surge, no flood pulse — but slowly, over the patient span of decades, as if a great warm hand has been laid across the valley's mouth and is gently pressing down.

You arrive at altitude, or what passes for altitude in this drowned high country. The peaks above are still sharp against the haze. The saddle between them is gone. Where a city once stood on that saddle and spilled down its shoulders, there is now a brown-green water, opaque as peat tea, warm to the touch, dim with the suspended silt of centuries. The water has not quite reached the ridgelines. It has reached everything else.

A city is here. It emerges from the lake the way a reef emerges from a sea — in fragments, in vertical stubs, in the recognizable geometry of things built for ground that have since been claimed by something else. Roofs of fired clay and thatch. The rounded backs of storage vaults. Chimneys. Stairheads that climb two steps and then stop at the waterline. The lower floors are gone, dissolved or silted, and the upper floors have become the ground floors. The residents move between these floating rooftops on hand-hewn timber causeways, lashed with rope, unpegged by any iron. There is no iron here. There is wood, there is lime-cut stone, there is a fibrous woven material I cannot identify — and there is water, patient with all of it, working through the joints the way a tongue works through sugar.

The buildings were tall. A dense urbanism: narrow, clustered, layered upward rather than outward, so that the alleys between them are now canals and the canals are now streets. The bridges are the streets. The plazas are lagoons. A second-story window has become a ground-floor entrance; a courtyard has become a pond; a stairwell, half-eaten by rot and warm silt, has become a fountain where the lake breathes in and out with the slow weather of the day.

Vegetation has come in. Lush, heavy, opportunistic. Ferns and broad-leafed things have taken the rooftops. Climbing vines — the residents tolerate them; the vines anchor the tiles — drop green curtains down the walls and into the water. Small trees have rooted in second-floor courtyards and grown up through window holes, their crowns spreading out over the dim air. The garden is no longer a garden. It is a forest that happens to be built on top of a city that happens to be built on top of a lake. Where biology ends and architecture resumes is not a line you can draw with any confidence.

The light is poor. The water is shallow — you could touch bottom from a boat in most places — but the surface is shaded by the canopy above, and a low, warm fog persists at chest height even in midday. The world exists in a kind of amber twilight. Lanterns are kept burning in the indoor arcades and the deeper covered passages. Smoke from them mingles with the fog and gives the light a copper quality. By afternoon the water itself looks like spilled metal.

There are not many animals. Birds — a few waders, a raptor or two on the chimney-tops — and fish in the channels, which the residents eat, and an enormous hum of insects in the warm vegetation, and a frog sometimes. Mostly the place is people, plants, and water. The fauna is thin because the place is new. The water is still too warm, perhaps, or the silt is still too thick, or the oxygen at depth is still wrong. There will be more, later. The slow clock is still turning toward fullness.

The people are few but present. Dark-skinned, wearing layered cloth against the wet. They watch from windows and from doorways and from the prows of their flat wooden boats. They speak a language that sounds, from a distance, like water running over stones. They do not seem to mind being half-drowned. They were born into it, perhaps, or they have simply decided to stay. Their tools are hand tools — axes, adzes, knives of chipped stone and bone. They cut the causeways and re-lash them when the rope rots. They pole their boats through the new lanes. They tend the rooftop gardens because the rooftop gardens now grow half of their food. The whole economy has shifted, gently, around the water's preferences.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It is the wrongness of a place that is committed to neither land nor water. The architecture was built for ground. The plants were evolved for soil. The people were built for stable floors. All three of those assumptions are now false, and all three of those things are persisting anyway, in the manner of a sentence that continues after the verb has been crossed out. There is no clear horizon. The fog meets the water meets the distant lower slopes. You cannot tell where the city ends. It does not end. It is becoming something, slowly, that does not yet have a name. The geologists of some future age will find the foundations under the silt and call it a lake village, and they will be wrong.

It is very quiet. The warm water absorbs almost everything — voices are low, paddles are muffled, the wind moves through the fog without finding edges to catch. The loudest sounds are the structural surrenders: a wall giving way at the waterline, a roof caving into the room below. Even these are softened, swallowed, becoming a wet shudder and then nothing. The quiet is not empty. It is full, the way a held breath is full — it is the silence of a place in the middle of becoming.

This is what the place is. A city, half-swallowed. A garden, on the rooftops. A warm green sea, still rising by inches over the decades. A slow, embryonic world assembling itself around the ruins of an older one. The water is patient. The vines are patient. The people, sitting on their rooftops with their fish and their hand-tools, watching the fog, are patient too.

It is not unpleasant.

It is not finished either.
