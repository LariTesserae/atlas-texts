# A Cold City of Moss and Stone

You arrive from above. The only way to arrive is from above, because the place does not have approaches at ground level — it has edges. The edge is a shelf of dark rock, recently bare, where the last snowmelt has not yet finished its work, and beyond that shelf the ground falls away into a basin that is not really a basin at all but a compression. A clenched thing. A fist of stone and growth, holding water the way a hand holds water: badly, temporarily, with the leaks always showing.

The light is the first thing that fails you. It is not absent, only diffused — the kind of light that has passed through a great thickness of cloud and arrived having forgotten what color it once was. A pale, bluish, nameless gray. The sun, if there is a sun today, is somewhere above the overcast as a brightening, a place where the ceiling is thinner. The shadows underfoot are soft, ambiguous. Things in the distance are hazier than things nearby, but the haziness does not fall off cleanly into depth; it pools and lingers, because the air is full of small water, and the small water has memory.

It is cold. Not winter-cold, not yet, but the cold of high places in their off-seasons — a temperature that has opinions. Breath would fog, if breath were here. The rock underfoot holds the chill, and the chill works upward through boot-leather and into the foot. Water in the pockets of stone has a meniscus that trembles, fat and slow, deciding whether to be liquid or to become the thin, brittle film of ice that will come tonight.

The rock is young. You can see it. The edges are sharp where they should be soft, the fractures clean where they should be rounded. Whatever broke this place open — a fall of mountain, a retreat of something that was once heavier and colder than snow — happened in the time of grandfathers. The stone is dark, fine-grained, with a wet sheen. It is not yet soil. It is becoming soil, here, in front of you, while you watch, on a timescale the size of decades. Where the rock is broken it is black inside. Where the rock is wet it is almost purple.

And on this rock, in this cold, in this light that is not light but its rumor, the plants have built a city.

---

It is not a city of streets. There is no plan you can read. But the density has the quality of construction, the way a coral reef has the quality of construction — accretion piled upon accretion, layer atop layer, until the surface forgets the surface it was laid against. The "buildings" are clusters of growth, mounds and columns and shelves, each one a slow negotiation between a plant and the wet rock it is eating. The "streets" are the seams of water that run between them, the dark threads where the stone shows through, slick and beaded. The "plazas" are flat ledges where a single species has won and held a square meter of real estate for what might be a decade. The "bridges" are the long stems that arch from one mound to the next, hung with mosses that drip. The "plumbing" is everything: the seep, the rill, the sheet of water that runs down a tilted face in a continuous skin, the puddle in the cup of a leaf, the drop that hangs at the tip of a frond for an hour before letting go.

If you stand at the edge and look down into it — and you have to stand at the edge, because there is no walking in, not at first — you see, in the compressed way of things seen through mist, a vertical world. The plants have not built upward so much as *inward*. They have stuffed the basin full. Where a normal forest makes a column of air between its floor and its ceiling, this place has made a solid: a green-black mass, layered, tiered, every cubic meter occupied.

The lowest tier is the crust. Lichens paint the rock in pale ochre and umber and the gray-white of a thing that has decided to be neither plant nor mineral and is, in the end, both. Where the lichens have held for a while, the mosses have followed, and where the mosses have held, the small ferns have come, and the rosette-plants with their thick, waxy leaves pressed flat against the wet stone. This tier is the city's skin against the cold, the part that touches the rock and does the slow work of making dirt.

The middle tier is the city's body. Shrubby growths, woody at the base, with leaves that are not green so much as the green of deep water — almost black, almost blue. Stems lean and arc, find one another, fuse. The branching is so dense that you cannot see into it; you can only see the surface, the outer hull of a complicated thing. The mass holds water in itself, in a thousand internal pockets, and the water moves slowly through it the way blood moves through a slow body, and the body of the city is cold, and so the water moves slowly indeed.

The upper tier — there is not much of an upper tier. That is the young thing about this place, the youth, the fresh-emerged quality. The tallest growths are not tall. They are the height of a man, or a man and a half, and they do not form a canopy so much as a stippled ceiling, a lace. Through the lace comes the dimness, filtered again, made even softer. The tallest stems are the youngest rock, in a way: the latest arrivals in a colonization that is still happening, still reaching, still pushing outward at the margins where the broken stone meets the wet air.

---

What you hear, when you listen — and you have to listen, because the place is a hush, and the hush is not empty, it is full of small things — is this.

Dripping. A lot of dripping, never in rhythm, never in unison, the staggered percussion of a thousand small reservoirs deciding to empty. A drop from a leaf-axil. A drop from the tip of a frond. A drop from a stone lip onto a stone floor. The drops make different sounds on different surfaces: a flat *plip* on a wet leaf, a softer *tok* on moss, a bright *tick* on stone. Together they are not a noise. They are a texture.

Wind, when it comes. The wind is not strong here — the basin holds it, the walls break it — but it gets in, and when it gets in it moves through the wet growth with a low, continuous note, the way wind moves through a reed bed, but lower, and slower, and without the music. More like a sigh. More like the place itself is breathing out.

Ice. Ice is a sound in this place, when it forms and when it leaves. There is a tick, a creak, a small report — the sound of water becoming solid and pushing at the things around it. The plants are flexible enough to take it, mostly. Sometimes a stem is not, and you hear the snap, faint and far away, the way a knuckle cracks in another room.

And under all of it, under the drips and the wind and the ice, there is a sound that is almost not a sound: the sound of the plants themselves, growing. The creak of a stem thickening. The soft grind of a root-tip pushing into a crack in the stone. The whisper of a leaf unfurling, which is not a whisper at all, in any instrument you can name, but is the only word for what it is. The city is alive in the way that a city is alive, which is to say, slowly, in continuous small construction, without anyone noticing the construction except by the result.

---

What you do not see, mostly, is animals.

There are some. There must be some. A cold wet place with this much food, even if the food is plants, even if the plants are tough and waxy and full of the secondary compounds that say *do not eat me* — there must be eaters. There is a thing like a spider, perhaps, that lives in the dry interior of a moss-clump. There is a small brown bird, maybe, that has come down from somewhere higher and is hopping along a ledge, head tilted, looking. There is a creeping thing, slow with the cold, that slides along a stone face and is gone before you can name it. There might be a beetle, in the wet wood of a fallen stem. There might be a worm, in the thin film of soil that is beginning, here and there, in a protected crack.

But the fauna is rare. The fauna is a punctuation, not a sentence. The city is the plants, and the plants are the city, and the city does not need watchers, and does not have them.

---

The wetness is the binding element, the thing that holds the city together and keeps it from being merely a pile of cold growth on a pile of cold rock. The water comes from above, as mist, as drizzle, as the slow rain of cloud forests and high places. The water comes from within, as condensation on every cold surface, as the breath of the basin itself, the evaporation of yesterday's rain falling again tonight as dew. The water moves through the mass of plants in a thousand directions at once: down, along, up by capillary, sideways by the patient work of root pressure and surface tension. The city is, in this sense, a sponge. It holds the water, and the water holds the cold, and the cold holds the plants, and the plants hold the water. The system is closed, in feeling, even if it is not in fact. You could spend an hour here and not see a drop fall from the sky. You would still be wet, the whole time, with the wetness of the place itself, the wetness that does not come down but is *here*, has always been here, will be here tomorrow.

---

There is a particular stone near the center of the basin — you find it, eventually, by following the dark seam of a rill — that is older than the rest. It is more rounded, more water-worn, as if it had been here before the last breaking, before the last exposure. On this stone the growth is thickest. The plants have had the longest time on it, and the layering is most complete: lichens, mosses, a low herb of some kind, and at last, at the top, a small woody shrub with dark leaves, the only straight vertical thing in sight, rising about a meter above the mass. It looks, from a distance, like a tree. It is not a tree. It is the city's tallest building, its only spire, a thing that took decades to reach this height and will take decades more to reach the next. It is damp. The whole of it is damp, all the way through. Water beads on its leaves and slides down and falls and becomes part of the rill and joins the larger rill and goes somewhere you cannot see, somewhere beneath the mass, into the dark.

Standing near this stone, in the dimness, in the hush of drips, you feel the place as a place. Not as a forest, not as a meadow, not as a ruin. As a settlement. As a thing that has been built, in slow time, by things that do not build but only grow, and that has, in the growing, achieved the density of intention without ever intending. The plants have made themselves a city, here, in this cold basin, on this young stone, in this dim wet light, and the city is almost silent, and the city is almost empty of everything except itself, and the city is, in its slow and dumb way, full.
