# The Underbelly

You arrive by way of a crack in the floor of a dry riverbed, and the world falls away.

It is not one cavern. It is a country of caverns — a thousand-foot cathedral of basalt, a low labyrinth of drowned chambers, a long sloping valley floored in wet stone where a river runs in the dark. The ceiling, where you can find it, hangs at thirty feet, sometimes ten, sometimes eighty; the floor is rubble and sand and the slow black water of a stream that has been cutting its way through the rock since before the mountain had a name. The air is cool against the skin, holds the smell of wet iron and something sweeter, milkier, the way stone smells when it has been breathing for a long time in company with living things. It is not cold. It is the temperature of a closed hand.

Light, here, is a rumor. It comes only from the fungus — pale plaques of it, near-white, near-lilac, climbing the walls in thin scrolls and lacework, casting a glow so dim you cannot see by it but only *feel* it, the way the inside of an eyelid glows when you press a thumb against it. The river, where it pools, gathers a hint of that glow and holds it, so the water is the brightest thing in the valley — a dim path, winding, the only line in the dark you can follow with your eyes.

The river is not still.

Neither is anything else.

---

The sound is what hits you first. It is not one sound. It is a *weather*.

Above you, in a column of moving air, there are the bats. They fill the ceiling in a thick, breathing pelt, hanging by their hooked thumbs in clusters so dense the rock beneath them is invisible — and they are not sleeping. They are *talking*. A million high whistles, a million papery rustles, the wet click of jaws opening and closing on nothing, the deeper farting sound of a body adjusting its grip, the sudden percussive *chuff* of one settling beside another. And beneath all of that, woven through it like a cable, is the sound of wings. Not the occasional flap of an individual bat taking off. The *continuous* sound of an enormous population in constant, low-grade motion — bats shifting position, bats dropping from the cluster to drink, bats returning, bats colliding, bats passing each other in the column above the river on their way to and from somewhere deeper in. It sounds like rain falling on silk. It sounds like a forest on fire, very far away. It is, in the places where the column is thickest, loud enough that you feel it in your teeth, and after a few minutes of it you stop trying to speak because no one would hear you anyway.

This is the constant. Around it the rest of the sound arranges itself.

The river offers its own voice, lower and steadier — the patient conversation of water moving over stones it has known for ten thousand years, with the occasional bright clatter when a pebble tumbles into a deeper channel. From the ceiling, condensation gathers on the stalactites and falls. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* Not in rhythm. In a slow random music that goes on forever and never resolves. From somewhere in the wall, a wet hissing — not steam, but the breath of something exhaling through a crack in the rock, again and again, the same way a sleeper breathes. From below your feet, in the wet gravel at the river's edge, a chittering. The beetles. They are the size of your thumb, pale as ivory, with one long antenna that they tap against the stones before them as they walk — *tk tk tk tk tk* — and there are so many of them, and the stones are so layered with them, that the sound is not individual beetles but a field, a texture, like static, like rain on a tin roof in another room.

You cannot hear yourself think. You stop trying to think with words.

---

The fauna is what you keep finding, because it keeps finding you.

The bats first, because they are the most. They are not the bats of an evening sky — small, darting, separate creatures. These are the size of starlings, with wings broader than your spread hand, and they hang in drifts that move as one animal, the way a school of fish moves. They have no eyes, or such small eyes it does not seem to matter. Their fur is the color of milk, and their wing membranes, when they pass between you and the river, are translucent — you can see the dim glow of the water through them, and the slow dark shapes of their bones, like the sticks of an umbrella. They drink on the wing, lowering their jaw to the river's surface as they skim, and the sound of a million soft mouths touching the water is part of the weather. They do not, as far as you can tell, ever fully sleep. Some hang. Some fly. Some seem to do both at once, hanging from one foot and grooming with the other, suspended in a slow turn.

Then, lower, the beetles. They cover every surface that is not water or bare wet stone. They move in layers. There is a layer that walks. Beneath the walking layer, there is a layer that does not walk, that the walking layer walks on. The lower layer is made of the dead of the upper layer, and of shed shells, and of droppings, and of small pale grubs that twist in it. The beetles eat the dead. The grubs eat what the beetles leave. The grubs are eaten by something you cannot see, but you can hear — a soft wet crunching, just below audibility, somewhere in the mat. The mat, in places, is a foot deep. It is springy. Your foot sinks into it when you step, and the sound of the sinking is a *shhhh* of displaced beetles, and the smell that rises is the milk-iron smell of the place, only stronger.

In the river, the fish.

They are pale, and they have legs.

This is the strangeness of the place, the first thing you will say to yourself, *they have legs.* Not legs like a salamander's. Legs like a frog's, but wrong — too many of them, and set at the wrong angles, and used for walking along the bottom as much as for swimming. They are the color of the stones. They are not large — the biggest you see is the length of your forearm — but they are many. They move in the slow parts of the stream in loose groups, noses tipping up, mouths opening and closing on small floating things, on beetles that fall in, on the white threads of fungus that trail in from the banks. Their eyes, if they have eyes, are not visible. They navigate by some other sense — they swirl away from you before you see them, and you feel them more than you see them, as small pressures in the water against your ankles when you wade.

You do not wade far.

In the deeper pools, where the river slows and the ceiling is higher, there is something else. You see it only as a shape — a long shape, much longer than the fish, moving under the surface with a heavy, slow deliberation. You see the dim glow of the fungus on its back as it passes, and the wake it leaves, and you see the fish scatter to either side of it and then close again behind it, like water behind a boat. It does not surface. It does not need to. It breathes. You can hear it breathe — that wet hissing, not from the wall after all but from the water, from the place where the long shape passes. It is not hostile. It is not interested. It is simply there, the way the river is there, the way the bats are there, a fact of the place that has been a fact for longer than you can imagine.

---

The walls of the valley are not walls. They are surfaces. Surfaces covered in the fungus, which is the closest thing the place has to a plant and is not a plant — it is a film, a crust, a slow sprawl of pale threads that climb and spread and digest the stone where they pass. Where the threads have been a long time, the stone is pocked and softened, eaten into shallow bowls and branching channels that hold small pools of water, and in those pools there are smaller things again — white threads you can barely see, trembling in the water, and pale dots that may be eggs or may be a stage of something else. The fungus is the only fixed citizen. Everything else is in motion.

The mineral world, beneath the living one, is basalt — black, dense, fractured into columns by the slow cooling of a flow that happened before the river found its way in. Where the river has cut deepest, the columns are exposed, six-sided pillars marching into the dark like the trunks of a petrified forest, each one beaded with moisture, each one wearing its coat of fungus like a sleeve. In the high places, where the ceiling rises to its eighty feet, the stalactites come down — slow fingers of calcium, white as bone, dripping. Some of them have grown into the stalagmites below and become columns themselves, load-bearing pillars for a cathedral no one built. They are old. They are *very* old. The place is in balance. It has been in balance. The river cuts as fast as the stone reforms. The bats eat the beetles; the beetles eat the dead; the grubs eat the leavings; the long thing in the deep pool eats the fish; the fish eat the beetles that fall in; the fungus eats the stone. Nothing accumulates. Nothing starves. The dark is a closed economy.

---

The temperature is the temperature of a hand that has been closed a long time. It does not change between night and day because there is no night or day. It does not change between seasons because there are no seasons, not here. The river brings in cool air from somewhere higher in the mountain, and the bats bring in warm air with the heat of their bodies — millions of small bodies, each one a furnace — and the two mix somewhere near the ceiling and become the weather of the valley, which is the only weather, and which is always the same.

The damp is on you within a minute of being here. It is on the stone, in the air, in the sound. Condensation forms on your skin, on your clothes, on every surface. Your breath makes a small cloud that does not dissipate but hangs, dimly lit by the fungus, a pale ghost mouth that opens and closes above you.

You will lose track of time. There is no sun, no shadow, no change. The river runs. The bats shift. The beetles chitter. The long thing breathes. The fungus grows the width of a hair in an hour, which is to say it does not appear to grow at all. The stalactites fall, one drop in a span you cannot measure. A fish walks past your foot. A beetle climbs your boot. A bat brushes your hair with a wing, so softly you almost do not feel it, only the displacement of air, the faintest whisper of the membrane against your ear, and then it is past, and it is one of a million, and the column of them continues, and the sound continues, and the dark continues, and you stand in the middle of a place that was here before you were born and will be here after, in balance, in its own state, indifferent, deafening, alive, and full of the only kind of life this world has ever known.
