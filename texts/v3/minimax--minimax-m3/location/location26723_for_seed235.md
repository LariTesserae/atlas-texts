The valley sits where no valley should be: at an altitude where the air ought to be thin and bitter and burned clean by distance. Instead it presses down like a wet palm. Every breath is a swallowed mouthful of something almost too warm to be vapor, almost too thick to be air. The cliffs that ring it are the wrong color for the height — a deep rust-red instead of the white or slate you'd expect, and they weep. A continuous beading of condensation rolls down their faces in slow threads, gathering, sliding, never quite falling, the rocks themselves sweating in a heat they have no business holding this far up.

There is a sky. It is not the right sky. It is a low pewter lid, and the sun, when you can find it, is always in the same place — enormous, low, orange, balanced on the rim of the valley as though it has just risen or is just about to set, and has been doing neither for so long that the word *almost* has lost meaning here. Sometimes there are two of them. Sometimes none. It is hard to commit to the count.

The city is built into the valley's floor and up the lower cliffs and into the mist above and never finishes, because the mist never finishes. It is dense — a stacked, leaning, tier-upon-tier kind of city, made of stone piled without mortar, of palm-trunk beams, of woven vine, of mud brick baked in a sun that does not move. The buildings are narrow, shoulder-wide, leaning into each other for support, their bridges crossing at every height like the rigging of a ship run aground and overgrown. There is no metal anywhere. There are no wheels. There are no devices of any kind — nothing that hums, nothing that clicks, nothing that requires more than a hand and a plant and a rock. But it is urban in the way that a hive is urban, in the way that a reef is urban: every inch built upon, every inch above built into, the structures pressing close enough to nearly touch, and touching where they can.

The vegetation is lush and wrong. Ferns uncurl between the stones of the staircases. Orchids hang from the eaves of the tallest tiered houses, fat with moisture, their roots threading into the mortar. Vines thicker than a man's thigh braid themselves around pillars and across windows. The green is so saturated it is almost black in the shadows and almost white where the light finds it. The plants drink constantly — the air is wet enough to drink — and they drip constantly in turn, a perpetual slow rain from a thousand eaves. Nothing wilts. Nothing grows. The same frond has been uncurling for as long as anyone might have been watching.

There is water on every surface. It condenses on the leaves and on the stones and on the backs of things that should be moving but are not. It stands as droplets on railings, on the rims of clay pots, on the still shoulders of the figures below. Where droplets sit close together they sometimes, almost imperceptibly, lean toward each other and merge — but they do not fall. The water from the cliffs does not reach the ground. The water from the jugs does not reach the floor. It hangs in the air as ribbons and globes and thin curved sheets, catching what light there is, holding shapes no water in a living world ever holds. The air itself is a wet wool, a permanent mist, and you can see ten feet into it or a hundred, but never to the edge. The city goes on into the mist. You walk and you walk and you find the same fountain, the same stair, the same child.

The sound is the city. The sound is everything.

It is deafening. It is the loudest thing you have ever heard, and it presses against the chest cavity and lives there. A thousand drums, a thousand voices, flutes cutting through, bells of beaten stone, clay, hollow wood — children shouting, women keening a long sustained note that is not quite a wail and not quite a song, men chanting low. There is laughter in it. There is something in it that might once have been joy. It is so loud the mist has shape from it. The air is thicker for it.

And it has never changed. Not once. Not in any moment you could point to and say: *here, it shifted.* The drummer's hand is held above the drumhead, fingers still flexed, and the strike has never come. The voices are caught — a million throats sustaining a single held note that does not waver, does not fade, does not grow. It has been this way so long the sound has become a kind of stone. You can almost see it. You can feel it against your teeth.

The people are everywhere. They fill the city in layers — on the bridges, in the windows, on the steps, in the streets below, on the rooftops above. They are of every age and size. They wear cloth of indigo, ochre, madder, white. Their skin has color. Their eyes are open. Their mouths are shaped around the sound they are making. But they do not move.

A woman on a balcony is pouring water from a clay jug and the water has not reached the ground; it hangs in a frozen arc from lip to floor, a clear ribbon you could almost step through, held in a shape no water in the falling world ever holds. A child is reaching for a fruit that hangs from a vine in a window above, his fingers three inches from it, three inches and held there since before anyone can remember. A man on a stair is mid-step, one foot raised, his balance caught forever at the top of the climb.

They are beautiful. They are terrible. They are a city of the held moment, frozen at the height of its own joy, and the joy has worn grooves in the air.

No animal. No bird, no insect, no lizard, no rat. Nothing that moves by anything but leaf and root and the slow creep of vine. The city is silent of fauna, and the silence inside the sound is itself a kind of noise — a hollow, the space where the crickets would be, where the dogs would bark, where the pigeons would coo on the eaves.

The cliffs fold into themselves in places. You walk along a path along a path along a path and find yourself walking back the way you came, from the other direction, in a valley that is both huge and claustrophobic at once. The buildings repeat. The same drummer on the same corner. The same child reaching. The same orange sun hanging at the same impossible angle. Water runs uphill in some places — you can see it, a thin line of moisture creeping patiently against the rock face, gaining height. Geometry is a suggestion.

It is hot. It is wet. It is dense. It is lush. It is loud. It is still. It has been all of these things for so long that the difference between *is* and *was* has been worn away, the way the name of the place has been worn away by its own inhabitants saying it. The city is a scream that has lasted so long it has forgotten it was a scream.

It is the loudest silence you have ever heard.
