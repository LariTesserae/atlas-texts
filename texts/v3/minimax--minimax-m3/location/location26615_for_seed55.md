# The Sunken Masonry

It is dark here, and cold, and the cold is not the kind that bites but the kind that holds—the kind that has had so long to settle into the water that it has become a property of the water itself, indistinguishable from pressure, from stillness, from age.

The floor of this place is built.

There is no natural substrate beneath what you might call the ground. Every surface that the eye could find, given eyes here, was set in place by hand. Basalt hammer on granite block. Rope of braided kelp-stalk around rollers of petrified wood. Copper chisel struck by a stone maul, over and over, generation after generation, to cut the corner that would support the corner that would support the long tier above it. The constructors had no machines. They had time, and hands, and the patience of their own slow metabolisms. What they made endured not because it was clever but because there were so many of them, and so much time, and they did not stop.

Then they stopped, or the water rose, or the sun went out, or all three together, and what they made has had a very long time to become something else without becoming anything different.

The masonry has settled into its final shape. That is what you are looking across: a continental field of it, a low vastness of worked stone that has worn itself down over the millennia into the rounded, shouldered, near-flattened forms that stone wants to take when given long enough. The buttresses are long mounds now, only their inward curve betraying that they once held anything back. The terraces step down in gentle undulations, like ripples held in rock. The columns that remain stand thick and low, without capitals, without distinction between shaft and base—cylinders worn into slight ovoids, leaning by fractions of degrees into currents that have been pushing at them since before they were old.

Between the stones, and over them, the growth.

The vegetation is lush and dark-adapted and dense. It does not look like anything that ever grew on land. Long ropes of it, pale and fibrous, hang from the tops of the stone shoulders and sway in gyres that turn once every several minutes. They are not rooted in anything; they are rooted in everything—in the calcium leached from the mortar, in the slow upwelling of minerals from the stone's own slow dissolution, in the dim chemistry that happens when water sits patient against worked surfaces for long enough. The plants have no leaves, or rather their leaves are the ropes themselves, and they have no flowers, and they have no need of either.

Where a channel runs between two long mounds of former-buttress, the ropes braid together into a dense mat that floats just above the stone, undulating. Light does not reach here, or barely does—a faint green-gold wash from somewhere far above that gives the uppermost growths a slight translucence. The deeper ropes are the color of old pearls, of bone, of milk held against a fire. They brush against one another with a sound so soft it is felt more than heard: a long, distributed whisper, like rain heard through stone.

There are no animals. The web of consumption that once must have existed—the grazers, the filterers, the hunters, whatever they were—collapsed, or migrated, or was never there. What remains is a closed circuit of stone and plant and dark water. The plants take what the stone gives and give what the stone takes, and the water carries both down, gently, in its slow circulations.

The water itself is the dominant fact. It is not raining, exactly—it has finished raining, perhaps, or has been raining for so long that the distinction has lost meaning. The whole column of water, from the stone floor to wherever the surface is (if there is a surface; you cannot see it from down here), is the medium, is the weather, is the climate. It moves in slow bodies. It carries cold from the far side of the world and warmth from the other far side, and these meet here, in this vast submerged plain of worked granite, and mix without mixing, and pass on.

In places where the currents converge, the rope-plants lean all in one direction, in a slow hush. In places where the currents part, they stand more upright, more independent, and the spaces between them widen into dark corridors that you could follow, if you could see them, for a very long time. They go on. The place is vast. The place has weather of its own, slow storms of cold water sliding across warm, but the storms are centuries long and no one is here to notice them pass.

Sound is mostly the water. The deep hum of it, below hearing. The occasional groan of a stone block settling a fraction further into its final position—a sound that travels for miles and is heard by no ear, but registers on the rope-plants as a tremor they sway around and forget. Sometimes a current shifts and a long braid of growth slips against a worn corner of granite and produces a single note, low and clear, that rings once and dies. There might be one such note in an hour. There might be three. They do not come together into anything. They are not music. They are what remains when stone and plant and water have been left long enough alone.

Above, if you could rise for a day, you would find the surface far above the stone, and dim. Below the surface the light thins out into a long twilight that finally fails. The plants grow up into this twilight as far as they can. The tallest of them, in the deeper channels, may brush the underside of some invisible ceiling of light, where the last of the green-gold dissolves into the dark blue of the rest.

Nothing here is new. Nothing here is going to happen quickly. The stone has worn down as far as it can wear without becoming something other than stone, and it has become that now: a low, settled, vast field of worked forms at the bottom of an ocean, in the dark, in the cold, where the hand of every constructor who ever lived is held in the slow patience of the water and the slow patience of the plants that have replaced them and that will, in their own time, become the only builders this place will ever know again.
