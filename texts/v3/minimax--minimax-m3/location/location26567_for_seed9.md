# The Green Hangar

The first thing you notice is the drip.

Not the sound itself — it's nearly inaudible, a metronome of condensation ticking somewhere above and to the left — but the *fact* of it. The drip is constant. The whole ceiling of this place is weeping, and the place is small enough that you can stand in the middle of it and feel the humidity curdle against your skin like the air inside a greenhouse after a rain.

It is a hangar. You learn this by the bones of the thing: the ribbed vault of the ceiling, the long rectangular footprint, the riveted seams where iron plates join in overlapping rows like the scales of some armored beast. But the bones have been swallowed. The walls are not walls anymore. They are a vertical garden pressed up against the iron, climbing it, drinking it, dissolving the slow dissolve of tendril and rootlet and the patient excretion of acids that turn any substrate into soil if you give them enough years. The rivets weep rust. The rust feeds ferns. The ferns have grown to the size of small trees, and the small trees have grown to the ceiling.

You stand in a kind of cathedral nave, then, except the columns are not marble but the trunks of fig trees rooted in the ballast gravel of the original floor, and the stained glass is the canopy of leaves a hundred feet above, and the light that comes through is the most curious green-gold you have ever seen — filtered through ten thousand leaves, then through the condensation runnels on the iron roof, then through the steam that rises from the warm pools at your feet.

*Pools.* Yes. The floor, in the original scheme, was poured concrete, three inches thick over packed earth. The earth has been thinking about that concrete for a long time. It has buckled it. It has pried it up in slabs the size of dining tables, and the slabs have tipped onto their edges like the pages of a half-open book, and the spaces between them have filled with water — rainwater caught, dew distilled, the runoff of the weeping ceiling gathered in low places — and the water is warm. Warmer than the air. The earth is breathing heat up through it from somewhere below, and the warm water has colonized the broken floor entirely, so that to walk you pick your way across the tilted slabs and the standing water between them reflects the canopy in smeared green coin-shapes.

The figs are fruiting. You can see the dark purple drops hanging heavy in the middle distance, and the smell of them is overripe, almost wine-like, where they've fallen into the water and begun to ferment. There is a thick and constant buzz from somewhere — a hornet, perhaps, or a small colony of them, busy in their papery hanging city under the eastern eave. They are the loudest thing. Everything else is the drip, and the occasional heavy *plop* of a fig surrendering to gravity, and the slow continuous hiss of water running somewhere behind the green wall — some drainage system, some gutter, still half-functional, still doing the work it was built to do.

You are alone, or nearly. Once, halfway up the hangar, something moved — a shape in the branches, too deliberate to be wind, too small to be a person. A possum, perhaps, or something that has forgotten what it was. It watched you for a long moment with eyes that may or may not have caught the light, and then it was gone, and the leaves didn't even shake.

The planted geometry is still here if you know how to read it. You can see where the original work bays were — the long stripes of floor where the concrete is least buckled, where the gravel ballast shows through most cleanly. You can see the tracks of cranes that must have rolled here, once: iron rails, mostly buried, but their tops still glint dull between the roots. The hangar door at the far end is closed, or rusted shut, or buried under a hill of compost that has built itself against it from the inside. A buttress of root and leaf has reached up around the doorframe and seems to be slowly, patiently, prying it open.

And the drip goes on. The drip goes on. The whole warm green dim cathedral drips and hisses and forgets, very slowly, that it was ever anything but a forest. The ferns do not know they are growing in an iron shell. The figs do not know they are fruiting over a concrete floor. The possum in the branches does not know it is inside. And the warm water, running its slow chemistry under everything, will, in a hundred years, have eaten the iron entirely, and the place will be a sinkhole, and then a pond, and then a meadow.

But for now, for this afternoon, it is a hangar. And a forest. And a greenhouse. And a cathedral. And it smells like figs and wet iron and the green breath of ten thousand leaves.
