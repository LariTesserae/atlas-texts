The place announces itself first as sound — or rather, as the absence of wind where you would expect it. You're standing in a cleft along a high ridgeline, and the air around you has stopped moving. Beyond the cleft's mouth, it moves. Inside the pocket, it has settled, and the silence it makes is not silence but a very quiet arrangement.

The rock is black. Not the black of coal, not the black of shadow, but the black of glass — obsidian or something that behaves like obsidian, though it is heavier in the hand than obsidian should be, and colder, and it does not chip the way obsidian chips but instead fractures along planes that seem to have been chosen in advance. The surfaces catch the light almost as a refusal. You see a faint sheen along edges, a darker pool in the planes. The erosion here is young. The rock still wears the memory of its making, the way a new thing retains the fingerprints of its creation. Every edge is sharp. Nothing has yet been softened.

It is cold. Not the cold of ice — the cold of altitude, of thin air, of metal left out overnight in late autumn at the top of the world. You feel it first in your breathing. The air is dry enough to taste, and the wetness here is not rain but something else entirely.

It comes from the rock. You see it before you understand it: a fine mist on every surface, droplets that hold their shape without quite falling, a sheen that is neither water nor air but somewhere between. As you watch, the droplets grow. They slide a few centimeters down a glassy face and stop. They merge with others. They grow heavy. Then, rarely, one detaches, and the sound of it, in the quiet, is enormous — a tiny chime that the whole pocket takes a moment to absorb.

The place is built. You see this in the geometry, though you cannot always say where the building stops and the rock begins. The cleft is not entirely natural. Its walls are too regular in certain lights. Its floor is too level in certain directions. A stair descends into it from the cleft's upper lip, and the stair is not carved — it is *grown*, or formed, or arrived at by a process you don't have a name for. The steps are the same black glass as the surrounding mountain, but their edges are slightly too precise, slightly too uniform. They wear the rock's color but not its roughness. Beneath them, a corridor extends inward. From the corridor, a small room opens.

The room is layered. In a space no larger than a generous closet, there are walls within walls, surfaces that reveal themselves only as you move, infrastructure stacked in a way that suggests time more than space. You cannot see most of it. There is a faint warmth near one inner wall, which is strange in a place this cold. There is a hum — very low, at the floor of hearing, the kind of sound you feel in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. There are channels in the floor and along the lower walls, and the water moves through them in slow rivulets that freeze at the edges in delicate crystals, melt, and run again. The patterns repeat but never quite repeat. There is something patient about the water, and something purposeful, as if it is being read by the walls it passes over.

The infrastructure is dense, though the place is small. There are junctions in the rock you would have called solid. There are seams that are not cracks but junctions. There are places where the black glass has been coaxed into forms that hold something — a vial, a membrane, a soft-glowing thread — without any visible mechanism for how the coaxing was done. Whatever the systems are doing here, they are doing it in materials, not in machines. The walls are the machines. The water is part of the machines. The cold is part of the machines.

You see, then, the rare thing alive. It is small — pressed against an inner wall, no larger than a thumbnail, perhaps smaller. It is not a plant. It has the texture of the rock it clings to, but it shifts when you move, and it has, you realize, the suggestion of a face — or many faces — or no face at all, but a region of its surface where the eye keeps returning. It does not move toward you or away. It simply exists, in its place, the way lichen might exist on a stone, except that this is not lichen, and the stone is not a stone, and you cannot tell whether it is living or whether it is a thing the rock has grown and the rock has chosen to keep. It breathes, if it breathes, very slowly. You watch it for a long time and are not sure whether it watched you back.

The place is active. You feel it in the water moving, in the hum that never quite stops, in the faint warmth that shifts and re-settles, in the small creature's slow respiration, in the crystals forming and un-forming at the edges of the channels. You feel it in the way the light enters — and it does enter, though not from above; it enters from somewhere in the rock itself, a sourceless pale luminescence that makes the black glass legible without ever quite illuminating it. The place is running. It is running on what, you cannot say. It has been running, perhaps, for a long time. It will run, perhaps, after you leave.

You stay longer than you meant to. The cold is not unpleasant, exactly, but it asks things of you — steadier breathing, slower movement, a kind of attention that is not available to you in warmer places. The quiet is not empty. It is full — full of the water, of the hum, of whatever the small creature is doing, of whatever the place is doing to the water, and to the rock, and to itself. You begin to understand that you have not so much visited the place as been accommodated by it. Your presence has been noted. It has been folded into the running of the systems without disrupting them. You are, briefly, a small variable in a much larger quiet process.

You leave the way you came. The stair rises. The cleft narrows. At its mouth, the wind returns, and the noise of it after the pocket's quiet is almost violent. You step out onto the ridge and the world opens — bare, mineral, sharp-edged, glittering where meltwater has traced the rock in thin bright lines, going on for miles in every direction, all of it the same black glass, all of it young, all of it cold. There is nothing else for a long way.

Behind you, the pocket settles back into its processes. You cannot be entirely sure, walking away, whether the place you visited is a place you were really in, or whether it is a fold in the mountain — a small infrastructure the world has not yet noticed, running its quiet business in the rock, the way a heart runs its quiet business in a chest.
