# The Vermilion Floe

You are standing on the Vermilion Floe, though no one ever *stands* here long. The name is what the cartographers of other regions called it when they mapped it from above — a patch of brightness, a smear of red-brown against the silver-white of neighboring continents. From a height, it looks like rust on snow. From inside, it looks like nowhere else.

---

The altitude is the first thing. Whatever brought this plateau up did it recently, geologically speaking — the ridges are still knife-edged, the talus still loose, the valleys still raw. There has been no time for weather to soften anything. A million years of uplift, perhaps, against the billion-year patience of older ranges, and the difference is in every silhouette. The mountains here cut the sky with edges that have not yet learned to round. They are pale stone, sometimes almost white, sometimes shot through with veins of a darker mineral that the low sun turns the color of old blood. This is where the name comes from. When the wind strips the moss off the southern faces, the rock beneath is the same red, the same red as the lichen, the same red as the rust that climbs every iron-bearing stone.

The air is thin and bright. Light has weight here, almost — a real pressure against the closed eyelid, a reason that everything reflective glares and everything matte still seems to glow. The sun, when it shows, is a white disc you could shade with a thumb. The sky is the deep indigo of high places, and at noon it washes out toward a flat, colorless brilliance that makes distance hard to judge. Snow blindness is a real danger. So is the opposite: a kind of dazzle that comes not from snow but from the carapaces of the things that move below.

Because this place is damp, in its way. Not the damp of rainforests or coasts, but the damp of meltwater and condensation, of fog that rolls in off the glacier-fed valleys and stays for days, of dew that freezes and thaws and freezes again on every surface. The cold is mostly dry, but where moisture finds a hollow it pools, and pools here become ice-beaded tarns, and ice-beaded tarns become meadows of moss as soon as the season permits. There is a constant, slow percussion of dripping from every overhang. The sound of the Floe, beneath everything else, is water falling into water.

---

And the vegetation. *Lush* is a strange word for a cold place, but here it fits because *packed* fits. There is no bare ground anywhere. Every square meter is held by something alive. The dominant cover is moss — not the soft cushiony moss of gentler climates but a tougher, wired kind, a moss that grows in dense turfs the color of copper and tarnished silver, a moss that hums faintly when you press your hand to it, as though there are small machines running inside. Between the mosses, lichens: the red and orange ones, the pale green ones, the black crusty ones that look like spilled ink. Cushion plants huddle in the lee of stones, swelling into tight round mounds the size of melons, blooming when they can in flowers so white they are nearly blue. Grasses — there are grasses, though they are short and tough, growing in clonal mats that ripple when the wind moves across them.

Where the moisture gathers, the vegetation thickens into something almost tropical in its density. The boggy valleys are jungles of sedge and saxifrage, the streambanks walled with a kind of dwarf willow that creeps along the ground rather than rising. Everything is layered. Everything is in the way. To walk ten meters off the established paths is to disappear into the green, and the green does not always give you back.

---

And the fauna. This is the thing no map can prepare you for.

The Floe is *alive* with them. They are not animals in the sense you might be used to. They are not plants. They are not built. They are grown, and they *operate*. From a distance — when the fog permits a distance — they look like herds of slow machinery grazing the slopes: long low shapes, segmented, articulated, with the gleam of metal along their flanks and the soft, patient motion of something that has been doing its work since before there were words for it. Up close they are worse and better. Up close you can see the seams of their carapaces are not seams but joints of chitin and copper-mineral, the moving parts of their limbs are tendons threaded through shaped stone, their mouths are grinding chambers that hum with a low, sustained note as they work.

They do not seem to be alive in the way a cow is alive. They seem to be alive in the way a river or a forest is alive — processes at scale, sustained by the digestion of rock and water and the slow oxidation of the iron-bearing stone of these young mountains. They eat the mountain. They eat the moss. They eat each other's dead. They excrete, in long pale ribbons, the refined minerals they have pulled from the earth, and where the ribbons fall the soil changes, and the next generation of moss comes up a different color, and the process continues. They are, in some fundamental sense, the industry of this place. There are no factories because they are the factories. There are no engines because they are the engines. There is no construction because nothing here is *made*; everything here is *grown into being* and *grown out of being* in the slow turning of their generations.

They come in sizes. The smallest are the size of your hand and move in shimmering sheets across the rocks, clicking as they go — little processing creatures, refining trace metals, polishing the surface of the stone. The middling ones are the size of dogs and travel in herds of dozens, lowing to each other in deep harmonics that you feel in your sternum before you hear with your ears. The great ones — you see them rarely, only on the open slopes where the light falls clean — the great ones are the size of houses and move with a ponderous slowness that suggests geological time, and when they pass the ground trembles faintly for an hour afterward.

---

The sound, then. The sound is everywhere. The small ones click. The middling ones sing, in overlapping calls that are not quite harmonics and not quite rhythm but something between, a sound that knits itself into a sustained drone that you stop noticing until you step outside its range and realize how loud it was. The great ones, when they vocalize, produce a note so low it is more felt than heard, a vibration that loosens stones and sets the surface water shivering in concentric rings. Beneath all of this, the constant drip and murmur of meltwater. And beneath even that, sometimes, the distant grinding of the Floe's own interior processes — the slow tectonic complaint of a young geology still settling into itself, audible only at night, only when the wind drops, only when you press your ear to the cold red stone.

---

It is a strange place. There is no other word. The light is too bright and the cold too sharp and the air too thin and the *life* too dense and too metallic and too patient. Nothing here is hurried. The moss takes a century to spread a hand's width. The great fauna take a season to cross a valley. The mountains take a million years to round. And yet the place is loud, and bright, and saturated, and alive in a way that makes older landscapes feel like waiting rooms. The Vermilion Floe does not wait. It does not move quickly either. It simply *runs*, in its slow way, with the same steady hum the small creatures make as they work the stone — a sound like a planet clearing its throat, like a thing beginning, like a process so large that a human lifetime is only one note in one of its middling creatures' long harmonic call.
