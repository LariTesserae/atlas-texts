The place sits where the ridge of an old mountain folds down toward a basin that has not seen air in a long time. There is no sky here. The water is warm the way bathwater is warm — it holds you, and it is dark in the way that only deep warm places are dark: not the cold ink of the abyss but a brown dusk that thins as you look down into it. Whatever light exists comes from above, filtered and broken, and there isn't much of it. By midday at the surface this place receives about as much glow as a candle set three streets away; by dusk it is a rumor.

The mountain's shoulder was a broad ledge once. Someone chose it. Someone carried stone.

What they built fills the ledge and climbs a little above it and sinks a little into it, and the whole thing has been worked so densely that there is no natural rock visible anywhere — every surface has been touched by hands. Walls, floors, lintels, basins, the curving backs of things that might be benches or altars or cots; they crowd against one another with hardly any air between them. The tool-marks are simple: rounded hammer-stones, wedges, abrading cords. No metal ever saw their stone. The carvers were patient, and they made many small decisions. There are channels along the floors for water to run when this place was dry, and cisterns, and the stubs of columns where roofs once joined the air. The roofs are gone — perhaps fell, perhaps were taken, perhaps dissolved over the long wet centuries. What remains is a dense jumble of rooms without ceilings, passages that bend and rejoin, stairs that rise into nowhere.

All of it has been worn. The erosion is older than the people who quarried here. Even before the water came, the wind was smoothing the corners; then the water came, and the work continued by other means — by sand held in suspension, by the brushing of bodies, by the chemistry of warmth. The edges that were sharp are blunt. The carved details that once read as faces or fish or geometric prayers have blurred into soft suggestions, like coins carried in a pocket for a lifetime. The stone itself has been colonized by a fine crust of mineral deposit, pale and porous, that fills the carved channels and rounds the carved edges further. In some places the deposit has thickened into bulbous encrustations; in others it has eaten right through the original stonework, leaving lace. After a while it becomes hard to tell where the carving ends and the accretion begins.

No plants. Nothing green. Nothing rooted. Nothing that makes its living by reaching upward toward a sun that does not reach here. The place is a hard dark warm quiet, and it is full of animals.

They are old kinds of animals — the kinds that never needed light because they were never built to want it. Shrimp-like things with too many legs and pale translucent bodies filter the warm water through every chamber. Eyeless fish, soft and grey-white, slide through the doorways in loose columns, nosing along the worn floors. There are worms that have colonized the cisterns and lift their red crowns into the current and withdraw them. There are animals with hands — small, deliberate, many-fingered — that pick at the mineral crust and at one another and at the smaller animals, going about their work with the unhurried attention of creatures who expect nothing to change. They are abundant. The volume of the place is dense with them; at any given moment most of the surfaces are occupied, and the rest of the water column is full of bodies in transit. They have lived here so long that the architecture has bent around them. Their burrows thread the softer stone. Their trails polish the floors to a darker smoothness than the water alone could manage. The place is, by now, as much theirs as it was ever the builders'.

The sounds are few. The water itself moves but does not make a noise one can separate from the listening. There is a low hiss where the current slips over the highest remaining walls. There is the soft click-click of certain shelled things against stone. There is an occasional deeper thud, too muffled to identify — perhaps a larger body displacing itself in a doorway. From far off, somewhere out in the open water above and around the place, there are sounds that arrive as pressures rather than tones. None of it is loud. None of it carries meaning. The place is quiet the way a sickroom is quiet, or the way a closed church is quiet — inhabited and continuous and hushed.

Time moves slowly here. The crust grows perhaps a hand's breadth in a human lifetime. The stone behind the crust weathers perhaps that much again. The animals turn over their generations. The current shifts by such small degrees across centuries that the worn orientation of the place — the way certain doorways always face into the slow flow — has had time to write itself into the architecture, smoothing one set of edges more than another. Nothing happens quickly. Nothing has happened quickly for a long time. When something changes — when a wall finally gives and a passage opens between two chambers that had been separate since the place was made — the change is the work of years, and the animals move through the new doorway before the rubble has finished settling.

There is a strangeness here, but it is the strangeness of a small scale applied to long duration rather than anything uncanny. The place is a hamlet. It would have been a hamlet, when it was dry — a few dozen rooms, a few hundred people, the slow business of living. Now it is a hamlet still, and the rooms are still rooms, and the inhabitants are still many. The strange part is only that the inhabitants are not the ones who made the place, and never were, and that the place does not seem to mind.
