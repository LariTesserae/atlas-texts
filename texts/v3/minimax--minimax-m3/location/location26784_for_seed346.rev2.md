# The Condensation Ward

---

You arrive at the Ward from below.

There is no road. There has never been a road, though the locals speak of roads the way old people speak of weather. You descend a long escalator that has been still for at least a century, the rubber handrails cracked in patterns that resemble, when you look too long, the maps of places that no longer exist. The treads hum faintly when you step on them. They have always hummed. They will always hum. The locals call this *the remember*.

The Ward is small. You can walk from one rim to the other in an afternoon, if you walk slowly and stop to drink, which you will, because the air is wet in a way that feels almost edible. There are walls — that is the first thing you understand about the place. Walls, and walls, and walls, layered so densely that the eye gives up and calls it a city, but the locals call it a *ward* because *city* would imply edges, and the Ward does not have edges. The Ward has *instances*.

Each wall is made of the same material, though no one knows how to describe it. Up close it looks like poured concrete, but cold. It breathes condensation in slow rivers that trickle down its face and pool at its base, where small channels — too straight for nature, too crooked for engineering — carry the water sideways into other walls, and from those walls into still others, and so on, until you realize you are standing in the middle of a lung. The whole Ward is breathing. It has been breathing since before anyone here can remember, and the breathing has a rhythm, and the rhythm is the only clock anyone trusts.

The walls are low. None of them rise above three meters, and most are shorter. This is why the sky, when you can see it, looks so enormous — a bruised violet thing, low and slow, the color of a bruise in the exact stage before it turns yellow. Light comes from nowhere in particular. There is no sun. There has never been a sun in the Ward, though children draw one in chalk on the flat tops of the walls, and the chalk drawings persist for years, fading into the condensation and being re-drawn by other children, the same image passed down like a story. The light is normal. That is the strange part. It falls on things the way light is supposed to fall on things, and yet no one can find the source. It is as if the Ward is its own lantern, and the air itself is glowing, very faintly, the way deep water glows.

You hear water before you see the Ward proper. A soft, pervasive dripping. Not rain — there is almost no rain here, only the constant exhalation of the walls — but a wet, slow percussion that comes from every direction at once. It is the sound of the place. After a few hours you stop noticing it. After a few days you cannot sleep without it. The locals say the silence of a dry room is what makes strangers weep.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Things grow in the cracks between walls, in the channels where condensation pools, in the rare pockets of soil that have accumulated over centuries from the dust of broken walls. There are gray lichens that look like thought. There are mosses that drink from the air and never touch the ground. There is a tree — only one, everyone agrees, though there are arguments about which one it is — that has no leaves, only a thin lattice of branches that cast a shadow no one has ever been able to photograph. The shadow moves. The shadow is always somewhere else.

The temperature is cold the way deep cellars are cold, the way the inside of a cheek is cold. It does not change. You wear a coat. You always wear a coat. In summer — and there are seasons here, though no one can agree how many — the coat becomes a habit more than a necessity. The cold is not aggressive. It is *attentive*. It keeps the condensation running. It keeps the walls damp. It is doing a job.

The place is dense. Every surface is occupied. The walls are stacked behind other walls, with narrow passages between them — sometimes wide enough for two people, sometimes only wide enough for one person who is not afraid of touching both sides at once. Doors appear in walls that did not have doors yesterday. Doors disappear from walls that had them yesterday. The locals do not remark on this. They say *the Ward is being a Ward today*, which is the closest thing to a philosophical statement anyone makes. Within the walls there are rooms, and within the rooms there are other rooms, and within those rooms there are often other rooms, and the deeper you go, the stranger the geometry becomes — angles that do not add up, ceilings that are higher than the building that contains them, windows that look out onto other rooms that contain the same window. No one gets lost, exactly. People lose track of where they were going, but the Ward always returns them to somewhere they recognize. The Ward is not malicious. The Ward is just *dense*.

It was built. You can tell it was built because the walls have seams, and the seams have a kind of regularity that nature would never have chosen. But it was not built by anyone present, and no one here has the technology to build a single one of the walls, let alone the millions of them. The locals say the Ward *assembled*, which is a word they use for things that come into being without permission. They do not worship the Ward. They simply live inside it, the way a body lives inside its skin.

The technology is the part no one talks about.

There are devices in the Ward. Small ones, mostly. Things that fit in a pocket. They do not work the way you would expect. There is a metal box on a shelf in someone's kitchen that, when you open it, contains a different kitchen each time — sometimes familiar, sometimes one you have never seen, sometimes a kitchen in which you are also present, opening the same box. There are tiles set into some walls that, if you press them in the right sequence — and no one knows the right sequence, but everyone has tried — produce a sound that is not a sound, more like a memory of a sound, which then becomes a real sound if you listen long enough. There is a streetlamp at the center of the Ward — no one is sure how it got there — that casts no light at all but produces a faint warmth on cold nights. Children gather under it. It is the warmest spot in the Ward.

There is a woman who lives at the bottom of a well that is only knee-deep. She says the well goes all the way down. She has never been asked to prove it. There is a man whose shadow occasionally stands up and walks a few paces ahead of him before lying back down. He finds this useful for crossing intersections. There is a child who was born with the ability to hear the walls think, and she translates, sometimes, for a small fee, though most of what the walls think is either too boring or too enormous to be useful.

Fauna is rare. There are no dogs. There were once dogs, and there are pictures of dogs, but the Ward does not seem to want them. There are birds, but they are pale and they do not sing — they make a sound like pages turning. There are insects, occasionally, that seem to be made of light rather than matter, and that you can hold in your hand and feel as warmth. There are rats, but the rats are polite. They wait. They do not take food from your hand but they will accept it if you set it down and look away.

The ground beneath your feet is not steady in the way that ground is supposed to be steady. There is a faint shifting — not an earthquake, not anything so dramatic — more like the feeling of standing on a very slow boat. Erosion is happening here, but slowly, so slowly that no one notices except the engineers, who have nothing to engineer against. The walls wear down at their bases, where the condensation gathers, and someone replaces them. The replacements are never quite the same. They are close. They are close enough. Over time the Ward changes by a process no one can quite describe, the way a face changes, the way a sentence becomes a different sentence when you repeat it often enough. The locals call this *settling*. They say things like *the Ward is settling into its old age*, and they mean it literally.

There is a sound here, in the late afternoon — if it is afternoon, if there is such a thing here — a kind of low hum that rises from the floor and passes through your body. It is not unpleasant. It is the sound of the place *being*. It happens once a day, sometimes twice, and the locals have learned to pause for it, the way you might pause for a hymn, or a sneeze. They stand still. They wait. Then they continue.

You ask someone what the Ward is for, and they look at you for a long time, and then they say, *It is for living in*, and they mean it as a complete answer, and after a while you realize it is.

You stay longer than you meant to.

The condensation collects on your coat. You do not brush it off. It is the Ward touching you. It is the Ward saying hello. You walk through a passage that did not exist yesterday, and it brings you to a courtyard you have never seen, where three old people are playing a game you do not recognize on a board that is also a map. They wave. They offer you a chair. The chair was not there a moment ago. You sit down anyway.

The light falls evenly on everything. There is no shadow from the sun because there is no sun. There is only the light, and the walls, and the breathing, and the dripping, and the slow, patient hum of a place that has been here longer than anyone can remember and will be here longer than anyone can imagine, and which does not care, exactly, but which *notices*, and which holds you, very gently, in the palm of its many hands.

You are in the Ward.

The Ward is glad.
