A wall, fifteen meters high, weeping. That is the first thing. Water slides down its face in sheets so thin they look painted, and where the sheets meet they form rivers a hand's breadth wide that tumble into the channel below. The wall is warm. You can tell before you touch it. The warmth leaks through the air at its base as a humid exhalation, and in the early morning, when the air is still coolest, the water steams faintly on the way down. By afternoon the steam is gone and the wall just drips, endlessly, into the channel at its foot. The wall is not stone. It is a dense laminate, a stacked-and-fused thing whose ingredients would take a chemist a week to enumerate, some of them mineral, some of them not. The grain you can see in its face — that subtle vertical striping, almost the lines in a slab of cured meat, almost growth rings — is the signature of the way it was laid down, in slow layers, by machines that no longer run. The machines stopped, or left, a long time ago. The wall kept. The wall has been here, more or less in this shape, for nine centuries.

The wall is one wall of many. Inside it, in the deep layer where the laminate is oldest, there are pockets where small pale things live, each about the length of a finger. They are very still. They are not the intelligence of the city. They are the wall's.

This is a low, warm, wet place. The elevation is below the old sea-level datum, although the sea is a thousand kilometers away and not relevant. The ground is saturated. Water tables sit near the surface; you can mop a plaza in the morning and watch the tiles glisten by noon. Condensation forms on every upward-facing surface as the day warms. The city's breath, at dawn, is a visible fog a hundred meters thick in the deepest cuts, thinning to a faint haze above the highest spires. The light that comes through is a normal light — the sun is somewhere up there, doing its work, neither more nor less than expected. It is the wet that makes the warm feel warmer.

The city is the size of a small continent. Three thousand kilometers east to west, twelve hundred north to south, packed to saturation. Beyond its outermost walls there is — something else. Other things. They are not relevant to the interior. Inside the walls, for as far as the eye can measure and far past where the eye can measure, the city is full. Density of three on a scale of three. Every cubic meter is occupied: by structure, by water, by fauna, by air that has been breathed, by heat, by sound. The city is not crowded. Crowded implies more than the space can hold. The city is exactly as full as it intends to be. There is no slack.

The sound is the first thing any new creature in the city learns to navigate by, because the sound is constant. The city lives at 2.7 on a scale of three. A person, standing in one of the lower avenues, hears layered: the white-noise hiss of water moving through a million channels at once; a low rhythmic pulse coming up through the floor from the deep infrastructure, a thing of pressure cycles and slow osmotic pumps, the city's heartbeat; the high irregular chatter and shriek of fauna in the upper structures; and over all of it a structural moan, the city itself settling, expanding in the warm, contracting in the brief cool of night, breathing. If you stand still — which is rarely safe, but if you — the sound resolves into components. If you move, it becomes a wall. A person in this city is loud, because to be heard over the city a person must be loud. The people speak in low registers. They listen with their whole bodies, the way a person in a dark room listens.

The fauna are abundant. They are not, in any usual sense, wild. They are city fauna, evolved in the city, fed by the city, killed by the city and eaten by other city fauna and fed back into the system. There is no plant life. There has not been plant life here for a long time. The base of the food chain is not sunlight and chlorophyll. It is the city's own output: shed materials from the walls; the paste that the slow biochemical maintenance of the infrastructure exudes; the bodies of dead fauna; the runoff of every channel; the warmth itself, which some creatures convert. The city is a forest that has been replaced by a forest's opposite — a packed, wet, warm, animal system, in which the autotrophs are the walls and the heterotrophs are everything else.

The largest fauna are the lurkers. They are pale. They are slow. Roughly the size of a transit car, they move, when they move, by rippling the broad flat surfaces of their bodies against the floors of the upper avenues. They feed on the biofilm that the infrastructure sweats out in the warm hours, a thin slick that, if the lurkers did not consume it, would in a few decades choke every channel. They are the city's sanitation. They do not know it. Up close they have mouths; the mouths are not used for much. They are a kind of scar.

The most numerous fauna are the channel-worms. They are everywhere there is water. Pink, segmented, between a finger's length and a person's length, filter feeders, the reason the city's water, although never clear, is not the color it would be without them. They pulse in the channels in vast numbers, an undulant motion, and the sound of them — if you put your head near a channel, which is a thing you do not often do because the smell is its own kind of punishment — is a wet continuous sigh. They are eaten by the gullet-fish, which are not fish, long and grey and soft-boned, the main mid-level predator. The gullet-fish are eaten by the slashers, which are large and red and fast and which, when hungry, come up out of the deep channels into the avenues and are dangerous to anything that moves. People are eaten by slashers sometimes.

The loudest fauna are the spillers. They are small, the size of a forearm, and they live in the upper structures, in the band where the air is thinnest and the wind, when there is wind, can be felt. They are not birds. They have four wings and a long stiff body and they scream — constantly, even when feeding, even when mating, even when asleep — and the sound of a roost of them at dusk is a particular kind of torture. They feed on the small flying things that the city's air-handling systems generate, around the particulates carried in the condensing water of the warm upper atmosphere, an ecology assembled from the city's own weather.

The people are real, although the term has drifted, and the things called people here would not be the things called people elsewhere, and the question of whether they are people is settled only by consensus. They are one fauna among many. They live in the middle altitudes, in the band where the air is breathable, the water is not too thick with worms, the lurkers cannot easily reach, and the slashers are rare. They have engineered themselves, across the centuries since the machines stopped leaving, to fit. Their skin is a kind of city material. Their eyes are protected. They are warm, and wet, and loud, and they know the city, and the city tolerates them, and they tolerate the city, and the relationship has the long settled mutually parasitic look of an erosion-1.5 equilibrium. There are perhaps a hundred thousand of them in the whole vast city, which is not many, and which is enough.

The tech is beyond the modern by a margin that cannot be crossed by description. The walls are not stone. The lights are not electric. The pumps are not mechanical. The city's deep systems operate on a kind of matter-manipulation that would take a treatise to even begin. The lights work by something the city itself produces, a bioluminescent output from the deep infrastructure, the same processes that produce the biofilm the lurkers eat. The pumps work by osmotic differentials maintained across membranes the size of city blocks. The walls are self-repairing, but slowly, and only in the wettest conditions, and the people understand this and live in the wet parts because the wet parts fix themselves. The parts of the city that have dried out are dead. The dead parts are still standing. The people avoid them. The fauna do not go there because there is nothing to feed on. The dead parts are, in the interior, the closest thing to open space. There is not much of them.

The light, where it is allowed to be direct, is a normal light, sun-warm, neither more nor less. But inside the city it is filtered, by fog, by structure, by the water in the air. The light that reaches the lower avenues is a diffuse warm faintly green-tinged thing, the color of a forest at noon, except there is no forest. The light that reaches the upper spires is brighter, and whiter, and at sunset the upper city turns the color of a heated piece of metal, which is a kind of beauty, and the spillers scream louder in the sunset because something in the light bothers them.

The turbulence is everywhere and is the city's true medium. A channel that was clear yesterday is clogged with worms today and will be cleared by the gullet-fish by tomorrow. A wall that was weeping in the morning is sweating in the afternoon. A spire that was upright last month is leaning this month, and a new one is being extruded, very slowly, from a different part of the same block, the city re-growing itself in a direction no one planned. The people move. The fauna move. The water moves. The warmth moves. Nothing is fixed except the walls, and the walls are fixed in the way that a coastline is fixed: the line is there, but the substance shifts. The city is a thing of process, not of object. The city is what the processes are doing, at any given moment, in this packed and warm and wet and loud and turbulent space, below a normal sun, on a low plain, the size of a continent, in balance.
