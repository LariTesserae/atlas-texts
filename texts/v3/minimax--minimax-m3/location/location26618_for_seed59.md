The place sits in a basin between two forested ridges, and the basin is what matters. Three sides of rising ground, a fourth where the valley opens briefly toward a river you can hear but not see. The settlement is named, though names don't carry well through the wet air; people here speak of it as the Hanging, or simply as *inside*. From outside, the boundary is hard to spot. A ridge path winds between mossed boulders and a half-fallen tree marks the threshold in a way no signpost would: a single plank spiked into its flank, the wood already bearded with lichen. Cross it and you step out of one forest into another, except that the second one is respiring.

The first thing is the moisture. It is not raining when you arrive, but it is raining one ridge over, and the front drifts through the basin in slow pulses. By the time you reach the first of the houses, the underside of your sleeve is dark. The moss that clads the path is not a layer on the stones; it is the stones' new skin, and it drinks from beneath. The trees here are not trees in the way a tree is a tree in the lowlands. They are community projects. Their trunks are columns, low-branching, often several stems fusing above the ground into a single dome; their roots are buttresses, buttressed in turn by stones that people have stacked, generation on generation, against the patient march of root and water. Where the roots collar the air, ferns have nested in the elbows, and from the ferns dangle the lank threads of moss that brush your shoulder as you pass.

The houses are built into this. That is the right verb: *built into*, not built on. A longhouse begins as a gap between two neighbor figs, the space between roofed in with a steep slope of split palm and palm-fiber thatch. The walls are living wood trained flat, woven through with bamboo and rattan, the gaps packed with a kind of daub the women make from clay-and-rot. Each year, when the rains come in earnest, the living walls throw out new shoots, and the household is partly a gardening problem. You have to lasso the new leaders downward, weave them into the lattice, or cut them for kindling. People get stung for this; the wasps have made condominium arrangements in the upper eaves, and they are not evicted. The houses are not houses so much as tense agreements between a family and a forest, brokered with a machete and a length of bark-rope.

There is no cleared space. The notion of a street is a courtesy. The paths are simply where the moss has been stepped on enough that ferns have died back, leaving a green-brown ribbon you can follow. In the dry months this would be a problem; in the wet months it is helpful, because the sound of your foot on wet moss and the smell of it both tell you where you are. The central gathering is a flat shelf of rock the people call the plinth, though it is not plinth-shaped. It is the only place where the canopy opens, and the light that reaches it is the gray-gold of mountain overcast, diffuse, almost sourceless. The rain pools in shallow hands in the stone; nobody bails it. They drink from it. The children are learning to swim in puddles an inch deep, and they call out numbers to each other in a vocabulary that does not match the numbers of anything I can see.

The fauna is the first thing any newcomer notices, and the last thing any newcomer can describe. There is a bird whose call is two notes, always two notes, the second a little slurred, and you hear it from every direction at once, all day, and it is not one bird but hundreds, sitting in the upper canopy and passing the second note back and forth like a rumor. There are insects in clouds so dense around certain trees that the trees seem to smoke; standing within them, you can feel them on your eyelids. There are frogs the size of your thumbnail, the size of your little finger, the size of your thumb, each with a different frequency of chirp, and the night is a chord they are forever tuning. There are larger things too — something that crashes in the understory at intervals, breaking branches, and the people do not look up. They know what it is.

The settlement has work to do. That is why the energy is what it is. Every beam is a branch that was, until recently, still a branch. Every roof is thatch that has been cut, dressed, and hauled up in the last six weeks because the last roof is letting through the rain somewhere, and the time to fix a roof is the moment it starts to leak. The women who work the clay dig it from a pit in the northern ridge, and the men who work the wood cut where the foreman marks, and the children run ropes of vine-fiber between the houses for reasons of their own — to dry herbs, to hang clothes, to climb because climbing is what children do, and the plants reward them. In the gaps between the longhouses, the people have strung lines of bark-paper lanterns that are not lit, because there is no night dark enough to need them. There is the overcast. There is the moss-light. There is, in the late afternoon, a brief trembling warmth when the sun finds a hole in the cloud, and the whole basin exhales a smell of warm fern and wet fur, and the lanterns swell in the heat, and nobody lights them anyway.

The noise is layered. The lowest register is the water: the constant patter that is not a single rain but the sum of a thousand small rains, each leaf and stone contributing its drop. Above that the frogs, a continuous hum with peaks. Above that the birds, the two-noted one and the others, the catbirds, the barbet with its single repeated note like a hammer on a wooden bell. Above that the insects, a hiss that varies with the light. Above that the people: someone hammering a peg into a beam above your head, someone else arguing in a language whose vowels go on for whole seconds, a child singing something about a fish, a dog that has been asleep for hours and chooses now to test the acoustics of the plinth. The sound does not stop. The sound does not have a quiet version. If you climb to the highest ridge and put your ear to the wind, you can still hear it, a low rumble, the village's metabolism, the place being itself.

Everywhere the water is performing. It beads on every horizontal surface, gathers into droplets, lets go, gathers again. The leaves are pitched; the stones are cupped; the moss is a sponge the size of a continent. The roofs are canted at angles that took generations to refine, and still they shed only most of the water. The eaves drip into gutters of split bamboo that carry the runoff to barrels at the corners of each house, and the barrels are fuller than they should be, and the people are watching them, and someone will move them before morning. The standing water is full of small lives. The mosquito larvae are food for something; the small fish are food for something; the frogs are food for the larger things; the larger things are food for the larger things. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is sitting still.

The land is old. The boulders at the path's edge are not boulders; they are the cores of mountains that have otherwise been walked away from. The roots have been trained here since before the people were tracking time. The settlement is not so much built on the forest as it is one of the forest's ways of being inhabited, and the people are the forest's accountants, keeping the books in machete strokes and roof replacements. If you walked away, the place would continue. The vines would knit across the plinth. The moss would climb the beams. The frogs would inherit the floor. In fifty years the longhouses would be heaps, and the heaps would be ridges, and the ridges would be the next forest.

At the back of the basin, on a low rise, is a single tree larger than the others. The people have brought it flowers for as long as anyone can remember, and the flowers stay there until they rot, and the tree goes on. I am told this is because the tree is the largest tree. I am told this is also why flowers are brought to it. The tree is not sacred. The tree is the tree. The flowers are the flowers. The bowl is the bowl. The rain is the rain. Everything here is exactly what it is, and none of it stops, and the place is not strange, and you can live your whole life in it, and you will wake each morning to the same two-noted bird and the same small sound of water finding a new path down a leaf, and you will not think to call it anything at all.
