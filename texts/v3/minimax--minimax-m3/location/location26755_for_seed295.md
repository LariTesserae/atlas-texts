# The Humming Shelf

You have to feel it before you see it.

The sound arrives first, traveling up through the stone under your feet, then through the air, a layered thrum that seems less heard than inhabited. Ten thousand wings, ten thousand throats, ten thousand small bodies in continuous motion — not screaming, exactly, but *sustaining*. A held note of life so dense it has texture. It presses against your eardrums the way water pressure presses against a diver, and after a few minutes you stop trying to parse individual sources and simply stand inside it, breathing when the rhythm lets you.

You are standing on a shelf of stone that was not always a shelf.

A long time ago — long enough that the lichen has had seasons to grow over the joinery of it, long enough that the moss has settled into the chisel marks like felt into a hat — this place was carved. Worked. Built. The whole valley was built, and built again, and again, by creatures no longer present, using a craft that has been overtaken so thoroughly by the work it produced that the seam between the two has dissolved. The retaining walls are no longer recognizable as walls. The gutters cut into the floor of the upper terrace have become streambeds. The drainage channels are creeks. The plazas have become meadows, and the meadows have become forests, and the forests are full of birds, and the birds are deafening.

The valley opens south, but the opening is choked. Lush is not a strong enough word for the vegetation here. The undergrowth is layered: a groundcover of broad-leaved creepers in browns and deep greens, knee-high, waxy; above that a tangle of shrubs with thick, dark, ridged bark and flowers you can smell from a hundred yards — something resinous, something almost clove, something that catches in the back of the throat and makes the air feel heavier than it is; above that, the canopy, which is a country of its own. The trees are not what you expect. They are wide-crowned, low-branching, with leaves the size of dinner plates and bark that is furred with moss and lichen in vertical stripes of cream and charcoal. Their roots are exposed where the soil has thinned over the carved stone, and the roots have grown into the channels they were meant to drain, drinking from infrastructure built for a different purpose.

There is water, but it is not obvious. The moisture here is in the air more than on the ground. A fine mist climbs the valley in the mornings — you can see it now, the lower slopes still gauzy with it, the upper rim already bright where the light has not yet reached. The carved channels in the stone floors are damp rather than wet. The leaves glisten. When a creature moves through the canopy above, water falls in a brief scatter, and the sound of it is lost immediately in the bird-noise, but you can feel it on your arms. A condensation world. A damp-bright world, where the rain comes in drops too small to be called rain and too frequent to be called anything else.

It is warm. The warmth has weight. It rises from the stone, which has been holding the sun since dawn, and falls from the air above, which has not yet decided to release its day’s moisture. Your shirt clings. The moss is pulpy underfoot. The air smells of rot and growth in equal proportion, the sweet fermentation of things becoming other things.

You cannot see far.

The darkness is the strange part. The light is poor. The valley runs east-west, and the sun, when it clears the eastern rim, only reaches the floor for a few hours before it has passed behind the western wall. The rest of the time the light is a green-gold filtered thing, broken into coins and shafts by the canopy, falling on carvings you can barely make out. The carved faces on the retaining walls are obscured with growth. The reliefs of animals, of processes, of assemblies of figures whose meaning has gone out of the world — all of it is partly eaten, partly overgrown, and you can see them only as suggestions. A hand. A flank. A circle of figures whose heads are open into the sky.

This was a place of gathering. You can feel that, even without being able to read it. The acoustics alone tell you. The valley floor is bowl-shaped — not naturally, you can see the carved terracing that exaggerates it — and it holds sound the way a cupped hand holds water. Whoever made this wanted to be heard, or wanted to hear themselves be heard, by many bodies at once. The birds have inherited the bowl. They use it. They fill it.

They fill it so completely that you forget, for stretches, that the sound is made by individual animals. It becomes a medium. A weather. The pigeons — there are pigeons, big ones, slate-blue, with throat-pouches that inflate as they coo — operate in loose columns through the canopy, and their wings make a continuous low percussion under the higher calls of the smaller birds. The smaller birds are in flocks of dozens, then hundreds, lifting off in detonations of wing-beat when something disturbs the lower branches, then settling again in a slow rain of dropped leaves. There are swifts in the upper air when the mist clears. The swifts scream. Everything screams, in its own register.

The carved stone steps — you find them under the moss as you climb — are wide, and worn in the center to a polish, and damp. They switch back up the eastern wall, and as you climb the sound of the birds drops a little, becomes more articulate, and you can pick out a single alarm call repeated from three directions. Something has noted you. Something has passed the word.

At the top of the eastern terrace, the light returns. The canopy thins. There is a flat expanse of carved pavement, cracked now, with grasses growing through the seams in thick tufts, and a low wall around it that is also a seat, also a dam, also a feeder for a small channel that runs off to the south and is lost in the undergrowth. From here you can see the valley as a whole. The bowl. The dark forest filling it. The opposite rim, distant and uncertain, where the carved towers — you see them now, for the first time, low and broken and furred with growth, more suggestion than structure — keep their final watch over the gathering floor.

Nothing moves that is not feathered, or furred, or scaled. Nothing hums. Nothing glows. There is no mechanism visible. The world is what it has become, and what it has become is patient: stone softened by moss, channels drunk dry by roots, terraces held in place by root systems that were never designed for engineering and have re-engineered the earth around themselves. The light will shift. The mist will clear. The birds will perform their continuous unconsented orchestra. And the place will continue, as it has for a very long time, to be exactly what it is: a thing made, and unmade, and made again, by the creatures that came to it, and by the creatures that stayed.
