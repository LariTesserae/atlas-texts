The bowl.

Imagine a place that fits inside a half-kilometer. A roughly circular depression in a high ridge, walled on every side by dark stone that rises two hundred meters, more in places, less in others. The walls are sheer but not polished — they are weathered into facets, ledges, vertical seams, the kind of surface that a climber would not trust and a geologist would love. The stone is basalt or something like basalt: dense, fine-grained, almost black, with a faint reddish cast where the sun, on its rare visits, has oxidized the surface. The bowl sits high. The air at the rim is thin and cold, and the wind that comes over the edge has a knife in it. Down inside, the wind dies. The bowl holds its own weather, which is to say it holds its heat.

The sun here is a small thing. Either the star is distant, or the sky is permanently hazed with a high dust that never settles, or both. Whatever the cause, the light that makes it over the walls is a thin coppery wash, never bright, never warm-looking, more like the light at the bottom of a deep well at the end of the day. The sun, when it is well-placed, sends a single oblique shaft down into the bowl for maybe an hour, a slanting blade of pale gold that moves slowly across the floor and is gone. The rest of the time the bowl is in dusk. Not night. Dusk. A kind of dim red-brown gloom that has been there so long the stone itself seems to be the source of it, the way a kiln seems to glow after the fire is banked.

There is no water. There has been no water here longer than anything still standing can remember. The stone is dry past dry — desiccated mineral, every trace of moisture boiled out by the deep geological heat that still rises through the floor of the bowl in slow plumes. Touch the stone and you would flinch. The floor is hot, not uniformly but in patches, in a slow pattern of warmer and cooler that drifts over years, the way weather drifts over a continent, only slower. The hottest patches are the deepest red, and they breathe. If you put your hand near one, you would feel the air above it push faintly outward, a thermal exhalation, almost imperceptible, continuous.

The bowl is packed.

Every horizontal surface, every ledge, every slope that is not vertical, is covered in a living carpet of dark shapes. They are the fauna. They are the only life here. They are not plants, not animals, not fungi, not any category a field guide would offer. They are something older than those categories, or something that branched off before the categories hardened, or something that arrived here from a different provenance entirely. The point is they are alive, they are biological, they are not technological, and they are everywhere.

Their bodies are small. Most are fist-sized. Some are the size of a clenched hand. A few are larger — the size of a sleeping child, the size of a curled dog — and these are rare and slow and tend to settle over the hottest cracks. Their color is the color of the bowl: black, deep red-brown, the dull cherry of cooling lava, the bruise-purple of old blood. Their surfaces are not smooth. They are textured, ridged, faceted, sometimes leathery, sometimes crystalline, sometimes both at once, as if the inside of each one is trying to become the outside. Some of them have what you might call limbs, if you were being generous — stubby extensions used for leverage, for pushing against neighbors, for the slow work of moving. Some of them have no such extensions at all, and simply flow, or ooze, or are pushed by the ones behind them. They have no faces. They have no eyes that you can see. They have orifices, on their undersides or at their leading edges, that may be mouths.

The density is the first thing you would notice, if you came down into the bowl. There is no stone visible beneath them. The carpet is one creature thick in most places, but it is a thick that has no gaps. They are shoulder to shoulder, edge to edge, the smaller ones fitting into the spaces between the larger ones, a perfect packing that has been refined over longer than you can imagine. The mass of them moves. Slowly. In tides. A drift toward the warmer patches. A drift away from where a large one has settled. A slow circulation that has the patience of a season and the persistence of geology. There is no panic. There is no hurry. The creatures do not know hurry. They know warmth, and dark, and the bodies of the others against them, and the slow business of feeding.

It is quiet in the bowl. Quiet the way a sealed room is quiet, the way a held breath is quiet. The sound that does exist is felt more than heard. There is a subsonic hum, below the threshold, that you sense in your chest and in the soles of your feet. It is the sound of many small bodies pressing against each other, a soft continuous papery whisper, like the turning of many pages in a library with no one reading. When a larger creature shifts, there is a heavier sound, a muffled grinding, the sound of stone against stone but softer, wetter. When a small one is caught beneath a larger one, there is a small sound, very small, that might be a distress call or might be the stone cooling. There is no song. There is no chirp. There is no call and response. The creatures do not speak to each other in any way you would recognize as language. They may speak in another way entirely, in chemistry, in heat, in pressure, in something we have no word for. The bowl is full of that speech. The bowl is full of the sound of many living things not making sound.

Scattered through the carpet are the structures.

They are few. They are partial. They are old. A low cairn near the center of the bowl, its fitted stones still holding their shape but its top barely above the carpet, slowly being consumed. A line of upright slabs at the eastern edge, five or six of them, weathered almost to shapelessness, leaning at the same angle, as if they fell at the same moment, long ago, and have not been righted. A flat platform of fitted basalt in the southwest, perhaps six paces across, with a shallow depression in the center that catches nothing because nothing falls here. A fragment of wall, half-buried in the moving dark, with a single corner you could stand in if you were small enough and patient enough. The creatures flow around these things, over them, sometimes into the gaps of them, without acknowledging them. The structures have become part of the bowl the way a boulder is part of a hillside. They are not maintained. They are not visited. They are not decorated. They are placed, and the placed-ness of them is what makes them structures rather than rocks.

No tool marks are visible on any of them. No carving. No writing. No image. They are not ornamented. They are positioned. The line is a line. The cairn is a cairn. The platform is a platform. The wall has a corner. These are the gestures of someone, or something, that knew how to place stones in meaningful arrangements and then stopped, or died, or left, or became what is in the bowl now, or never was here at all and the stones placed themselves. There is no way to know. The placed-ness is the only evidence that there was ever a placer.

There is no device in the bowl. Nothing moves except the creatures, the heat, the slow drift of the light, the occasional fall of a stone from the rim. Nothing projects. Nothing records. Nothing signals. The technology of the bowl, if it can be called that, is the cairn, and the cairn is older than the creatures, or the creatures are older than the cairn, and there is no way to know which.

The light is dim. The walls of the bowl cut most of the sky. What sky is visible is a small ragged circle, and through that circle the light comes down filtered, reddened, weakened. The dusk inside the bowl is lit only by the sky-glow and by the faint thermal radiance of the heated stone and, in the deepest dusk, by a dim red glow from the undersides of the larger creatures when they shift over the hottest cracks. The carpet of creatures does not respond to the changing light. The shaft of sun that crosses the floor each day passes over them without effect. They continue their slow drift. They are creatures of the dusk, and the dusk is permanent.

The ecology of the bowl is closed. The creatures feed on each other and on the heat that rises through the stone. The smallest ones feed on the shed matter of the larger ones — a flake of old carapace, a dead husk, the leavings of a feeding. The medium ones feed on the smallest ones, slowly, taking one every few days, never in a rush, the way a cow grazes. The largest ones feed on the medium ones when the opportunity arises, and on the heat itself, positioning themselves over the deepest thermal plumes, absorbing the heat through their undersides, growing very slowly over years, becoming almost geological in their patience. The economy is closed. The matter circulates. Nothing leaves. Nothing enters, except the slow infall of dust from the sky, which is negligible, and the rare fall of a stone from the rim, which is ignored. The bowl is a closed system, a hot dim packed quiet world unto itself, and it has been in balance for longer than the structures can attest.

The bowl is active. The carpet is in constant slow motion, the tides shifting, the larger ones settling and rising over years, the smaller ones flowing around. Heat moves through the floor in slow waves, and the creatures track the waves, drifting toward warmth. The structures shift slightly in the carpet as the carpet shifts around them — a slab that was upright last season may lean further this season, will eventually fall, will be covered, will become part of the floor. The walls of the bowl weather. A stone falls from the rim every few years, lands with a crash that is the loudest sound in the bowl, sends the creatures nearest it into a brief ripple of motion that subsides within minutes, and is then slowly covered, slowly absorbed into the carpet, slowly forgotten. The sky above changes — the haze thickens and thins, the light dims and brightens over long cycles, the distant sun moves through its seasons. The bowl is in motion. The bowl is patient. The bowl is in its own state, and has been for a very long time.

There is one more thing.

In the deepest part of the bowl, where the heat is strongest, where the largest creatures lie, there is a place the creatures do not go. A circle, perhaps ten paces across, of bare stone. The stone inside the circle is hot past hot — it glows a faint cherry red, even in the dusk, even through the shimmer. The creatures flow around it with a wide berth, the way water flows around a stone in a stream, the way a school of fish gives a rock its distance. They do not touch it. They do not cross it. They give it perhaps two body-widths of clearance on every side, and the space inside that clearance is empty of them, a ring of bare stone around the glowing circle, a moat of heat.

Inside the circle, nothing moves. The stone is hot and bare. There is no ash. There is no marking. There is no structure, no cairn, no slab, no platform. The circle is older than the structures, or the structures are older than the circle, and there is no way to know. The air above the circle is slightly different — clearer, perhaps, less distorted by the heat-shimmer, or more distorted, the shimmer compressed into a still column that rises straight up out of the bowl. If you stood at the edge of the circle, you would feel the heat on your face like an open oven door. If you stepped into it, you would not step out, or you would step out different, or you would not step out at all. The creatures know this. They have always known this. The circle is not theirs. The circle is part of the bowl, but the bowl is not the master of the circle. The circle is older, or deeper, or other. The creatures work around it the way a tide works around a rock — they have shaped their entire slow circulation to accommodate it, the way a river accommodates the boulder in its bed. Remove the circle and the bowl would not be the bowl. The circle is the thing the bowl is built around, even though the bowl is not built.

That is the place. A small high bowl, hot and dim, packed with quiet dark life, scattered with the weathered remains of something that once placed stones, containing a circle of deeper heat that nothing will cross. The sound of many living things not speaking. The light of a sun that is far away. The patience of stone, and the patience of the things on the stone, and the patience of the heat under both.
