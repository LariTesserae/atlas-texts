The plateau runs to every horizon. It has been high for longer than any shape now on it would help you remember — high when it first lifted, high through the slow grinding of the air against its skin, high still. The air is thin and cold and very clean. Light comes down unsoftened. Stone, where it stands bare, is the color of bone or old plaster, marked with long pale-rust stains where some mineral has bled down over the ages. In places the rock is glassy — fused, long ago, by some heat that is no longer here — and in the low angle of the high sun it throws a dry white glare back into your eyes. The shadows are short at noon and very long and very blue at the edges of the day, and the sun, when it is high, is a hard white disc that does not warm the way you expect a sun to warm. The sky is paler than deep blue — a luminous white-violet — and the stars, when they come out, do not twinkle, because the air is too thin and too steady to make them dance.

The erosion is not fast. Nothing here is fast. But it has been working for a long time, and the plateau is no longer a plateau in any simple sense. It is a plateau that has been taught, over deep time, the shape of wind. There are long shallow valleys where the prevailing wind has scoured the rock into parallel grooves, like the grain of an old board. There are basins where the stone has been scooped out into round dry depressions the way a river might pool. There are tors — standing columns of harder rock — left behind as the softer material around them was carried off, grain by grain, into the bright air. The edges are not sharp. The shapes are rounded. This is a place that has been sanded, slowly, with very fine paper, for longer than anything alive here would care to count.

The growth is the strange thing. It is abundant in the sense that, where it occurs, it occurs thickly — a dense, mounded, slow accumulation that in certain light looks almost like a wet moss or a thicket of small pale fingers, but it is not wet and it is not leafy. It is, as best can be said, a kind of mineral-fungal mat that builds standing structures out of windblown grit and the trace chemistries of the air. The substrate is dust, dissolved stone, the faint volatile compounds the thin atmosphere carries. The structures feed, very slowly, on light, and they accrete. Some are low and carpet-like, a few centimeters of dense pale growth that ripples slightly in the constant wind. Some are taller, knuckled columns a meter or two high, branching at the top into fans of fine brittle blades that click faintly when the air moves through them. They are not green. They are the color of the rock, or a faint ochre, or, in the oldest stands, a dull lavender that might be a stain from within or might be a trick of the light. They gather most thickly in the lee of the larger tors and along the floors of the shallow valleys, where the wind drops its load of dust.

In a few places the growth is so dense it forms what can only be called forests, in the sense that you could not see through them, though the tallest structures would not reach your chest. A person, if there were a person, would have to push through. The sound of pushing would be a soft dry crunch and a faint high singing, as the brittle blades flexed and let go.

The air is quiet. Sound does not travel well in so thin an atmosphere — voices would fall flat, and there is almost nothing here to make a loud noise. The wind is the loudest thing, and the wind is not a roar. It is a long low hiss along the rock, and a dry whistle where it funnels through a stand of growth, and the growths themselves, when they bend, give back a tiny chorus of small glassy notes. It is the kind of sound you have to stop breathing to hear. Far off, sometimes, there is a low groan — the rock cooling, the rock warming, a joint settling somewhere in the floor of the plateau. The groan travels a long way in the thin air and then is gone.

Almost nothing moves. The fauna is thin and rare. There is a small flat pale crawler that lives in the mats and feeds on the dust trapped there, and moves only when the light is at a certain angle, which is most of the day. There is something larger that you might see once in a long walk — a long pale shape that sits in the lee of a tor and looks, until the wind moves it, like a fold of the rock. What it does, or what it eats, is not obvious. The growth itself is not obviously alive. It does not move. It does not flower. It is not clear it is aware of being a thing at all. But it is there, and in a sense it is more there than the rock: the rock is being slowly unmade by the air, and the growth is being slowly made.

There is no water. There has perhaps never been water, not in any form that would pool, or run, or fall from the sky. The chemistry here is the chemistry of the dry places — silicas, sulfates, the long slow reactions of stone with air. If you tasted the dust it would not taste of much. If you held a stone to your ear you would not hear the sea. The growth is not a green growth. It is not a photosynthesis as anyone from a wetter world would recognize. It is a thing that uses light to drive a slow building, but the building is of a different order — more like a crystal than a plant, more like a fungus than a crystal, and it has been here, in one form or another, for as long as the plateau has been a plateau, and it will be here when the plateau has been ground down to something else.

The dynamic is real, but it is the dynamic of the place. The wind moves the dust, and the dust is what the growth eats, and the growth holds the dust in place when the wind is strong, and so the basins where the growth is thickest are slowly deepening into the rock, and the bare ridges between them are slowly being sharpened, and the whole plateau is, over centuries, becoming a corrugated thing, a long set of parallel furrows with their own weather in them — slightly less wind, slightly more dust, slightly more life. The tors are being undercut. Eventually one will fall. When it falls it will make a sound that travels a long way in the thin air and will be the loudest sound in a long time, and a pale dust will rise from the impact and drift for days in the lee of things, and the growth will take a few centuries to recolonize the fresh face. It does not hurry.

You could walk here for a long time and not see anything change, and that is the point. The change is the place. The change is what the place is, at the speed of the place. If you came back in a thousand years you would see, from the right vantage, that the long furrows had deepened by a hand's breadth, and a new tor had fallen, and a new pale grove had risen in the lee of it, and that would be enough. The light would still be coming down hard. The wind would still be hissing along the stone. The brittle blades would still be giving back their tiny glassy notes to no one in particular. The plateau would still be high, and the air would still be thin, and the dust would still be moving, and the growth would still be doing the slow patient work of turning the dust into a thing that was not quite stone and not quite anything else, and the place would still, in its own long way, be in balance, and the silence would still be the loudest thing in it.
