# The Basin of the Six Cairns

You arrive at the rim on a morning when the air is already warm, and you stop, because the place is larger than you expected. Below you, the basin opens — a long oval of pale ground, perhaps four miles across, ringed on every side by weathered ridges that have been here longer than the grasses have. The ridges are the color of rust dried over by sun. The basin floor is lighter, almost bone-white in places, with seams of darker mineral that catch the sun and throw it back. The sky above is a deep, thin blue, the blue that only comes at altitude. The sun, even early, is white and close. The light is the first thing you really feel here, before you feel temperature, before you feel ground.

The ground is settled. It is not a violent landscape, has not been for a long time. The rocks have arranged themselves into rounded shoulders, broken shelves, low domes. There are cracked polygons of dried mud in some stretches, but the cracks have been there so long that grass and lichen have crept into them and made them permanent. The whole basin has the quality of something that has finished being made and is now merely being maintained — by weather, by the animals, by the slow movement of water that arrives and leaves and arrives.

Water arrives as condensation more often than as rain. Most mornings, before the sun crests the eastern ridge, a thin fog forms in the basin and climbs the inner slopes. By the time the light is fully on the ground the fog is gone, but the rocks weep for another hour, and there are dark streaks running down their faces like old stains that reappear. There are pools in the lee of outcrops — most are just shallow saucers that hold a finger's depth, but some, near the center of the basin, are deeper and more permanent, and you can see the stone at the bottom, green-black with algae, and small pale shapes moving in it. The animals know every pool. So do the burrowers, who come out only at night and only to drink.

The vegetation is sparse but stubborn. The dominant plant is a silver grass that grows in tussocks, knee-high in the protected folds, waist-high at the bases of the eastern cliffs, almost absent in the exposed center. Where the grass clumps, there is nearly always something beneath it — a low thorny mat, or a small woody shrub with pale bark that flowers once a year in a single white bloom, almost glowing in the dusk. The lichen is everywhere on the rock: orange in the sun, black in the shade, a kind of slow territorial marking. A few of the cairns have moss growing at their bases, but only on the north sides, only where the sun does not reach, and the moss is a different green from anything else in the basin — darker, with a faint bluish cast, like something that has learned to live without light.

The fauna is more than you would expect for a place this empty. Small herds of six-legged grazers move through the basin in loose formations, browsing the tussocks at dawn and again at dusk, then lying down in the warmer hollows and around the cairns for the middle of the day. They are tall, narrow animals, almost crane-like in the line of their necks, with small heads and eyes set wide for the kind of vigilance that open country requires. They make no sound you can hear. When they move, the grass speaks; otherwise they are silent, and at a distance you only know them as a slow shifting of silver. There are burrowers whose holes are neat and vertical, going straight down into ground that should be too hard for burrowing. You see the holes in the hundreds, and at night, if you sit still long enough, you see the dark heads appear and consider you, then withdraw. There are flyers — bats, but not quite — with very long tail-fans that trail in the air behind them like ribbons, and whose wings beat at a frequency just under hearing, so you feel them pass rather than hear them. And there are predators. There is at least one predator. You almost never see it. You know it is there because of the silence that follows it — a localized, almost formal silence, as if the air has been told to be quiet. It is a low, wide-shouldered thing, the color of the cracked ground, that hunts in the noon heat when the herds have folded themselves into the warm hollows. You catch it sometimes, at the edge of vision, and then it is gone.

The cairns.

There are six of them. You can see five from where you stand at the rim. They are dark, smooth, taller than a person, roughly cylindrical but with surfaces that are not carved — they are the way the material is. The material is the trouble. It is not quite stone. It is heavier than stone should be for its size, and lighter than metal should be for its weight. It does not weather. The cairns have been here longer than the basin has been a basin, by the look of them, and they look exactly as they must have looked when the basin was still raw. They radiate a faint warmth even when they are in full shade. Around each one, the grass grows slightly differently — taller, a darker silver, in a rough circle — and the animals graze right up to the edge of that circle but no further. The cairns do not burn. They do not invite. The herds lie down near them in the cool hours but keep a body-length of distance, as if the cairns have a perimeter that is not visual but something else.

At twilight, when the sun is low and the light is going copper, you can feel the cairns in your teeth and your sternum. The sky above each one is slightly off — a thumbprint of darker blue, a small local dimness, as if the cairn is drinking the light. Compasses behave strangely near them. Shadows fall in slightly wrong directions, not dramatically, but enough that you catch yourself recalibrating. A minute spent standing at the base of a cairn feels longer than a minute spent elsewhere in the basin. Not longer in the sense of boredom — longer in the sense that you have more of it, that time is being held out to you like something poured slowly.

What the cairns are, in the terms that the place allows, is a network. They are in communication, or in resonance, with each other in a way that is not electromagnetic and not anything else you have a name for. They are doing something to the basin. The basin is warmer than it should be at this elevation, and the cairns are the reason. The cairns hold warmth in the ground beneath them, and the ground holds it, and the warmth spreads. The herds follow the warmth in their slow seasonal circuit, touching every cairn in turn, and the cairns hold them in a kind of orbit. The whole ecosystem is, without quite saying so, organized around the cairns. The cairns are the oldest thing in the basin. The basin grew around them. The animals learned the cairns. The cairns did not learn the animals. The cairns do not change. The cairns are not growing old.

The sound of the basin is small. The wind is heavy and slow because the air is warm; it does not whistle, it pushes. You hear your own footsteps, and the dry click of grass stems when something moves through them. You hear, if you listen for it, a low pulse that is not a pulse — something more like a long held note that rises and falls below the threshold of hearing, so you feel it in the soft tissue of your chest rather than hear it with your ears. Sometimes, late at night, the cairns themselves emit a faint tonal hum, almost in tune with itself, the way a wine glass sounds when you run a wet finger around its rim. It comes and goes. You do not get used to it. You get more used to it.

The activity is steady. Fog forms, fog lifts. Condensation gathers, evaporates, gathers again. The herds move on their long slow rotation. The predators wait in the cracked ground and consider the herds. The burrowers come up, drink, withdraw. The flyers pass overhead at a frequency you almost cannot track. The cairns hum at their own long pace. The seasons here are not seasons of snow but seasons of light — a wetter season when more condensation comes, a drier season when less does — and the basin responds to these by thinning or thickening its grass, by which pools last the year, by how far the herds range. Nothing is dramatic. Nothing is urgent. The basin is in balance, and has been in balance for a long time, and the balance is held by the cairns, and the cairns do not appear to be holding it deliberately. They are just there. The place has arranged itself around them the way a stream arranges itself around a stone.

The strangeness is not in any single thing. The strangeness is in the rules. The basin follows rules that are not the rules you arrived with. The cairns do not move but the shadows around them do. The animals are silent and the predators are silent and the cairns are almost silent and the whole place is held in a kind of listening, as if it is waiting for something that has not yet come, or has come and gone, or is coming in a way that cannot be heard. The light is bright but the silence is brighter. The cairns are warm but they do not invite. The place is balanced but the balance is held in place by something that physics does not have a name for. You can live here for a long time and not understand it. You can leave and not be sure you were there. You can come back and find it almost the same, with the cairns in the same positions, the herds on the same routes, the fog arriving at almost the same hour. Almost. As if the basin is being played again from the same score, with very small variations, by something that is not quite playing it.

It is a place. It has been a place. It will continue to be a place. You stand at the rim with the warm wind pushing at you and the sun white in your eyes and the six dark cairns below you, and you feel that you have been here before, even if you have not. The place is waiting for you to decide what to do with it. The place will wait. The place is very good at waiting.
