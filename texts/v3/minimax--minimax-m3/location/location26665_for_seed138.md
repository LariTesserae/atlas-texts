# The Cistern Quarter

The heat sits on the stones like something with weight. Even in the small hours of the morning — and the morning here is always small, a brief smudge of gray between longer hours of dark — the air is thick enough to chew. The whole district knows this, has arranged itself around this knowledge. Everything that matters is underground.

You come down the algae-slip steps between two black walls sweating with condensation. The walls are basalt, or something darker than basalt, something the quarriers stopped naming because the word never came out the same twice. The steps have been worn by feet and then by water and then by feet again, so that the center of each tread is now a shallow bowl, and these bowls always hold a film of water no matter how dry the day. The buildings on either side are low, shoulder-height at most, because nothing tall was ever built here. What was built down here first, the cisterns and the pump-houses, dictated the rest.

The cisterns are the size of courtyards. Their caps are stone drums the color of oxblood, set flush with the cobbles, and on each drum a number has been chiseled — XLII, XIX, VII — and around the number a ring of breathing-holes where the air goes in and out, in and out, a slow respiration that never stops. Some say the cisterns breathe in summer and exhale in winter. Others say the opposite. Nobody who lives here has any interest in the question, because the breathing is part of the sound, and the sound is part of the place, and the place is the place.

The sound. It is the first thing that catches you when you step off the last stair, and it does not let you go. The great pumps are working — they are always working — eight of them, in their engine house at the south end of the quarter, each the size of a small house, each driven by a steel beam walking up and down like a slow horse. The beams are twenty feet long. They have been walking for as long as anyone remembers. They are the size of bridges and the patience of rivers. The sound they make is a deep, even, unhurried *thock-thock-thock* that you feel in the soles of your feet before you hear it, and that you feel in your back teeth after you have been here an hour, and that you stop feeling only when you leave, and then you miss it. The pumps are fed by the lagoon to the north, where the water stands green and warm and is home to creatures nobody names at table.

Between the cisterns run the streets, which are not so much streets as channels. In winter they fill to the curb with slow brown water, and the people move along raised stone strips laid down for that purpose. In summer — and it is almost always summer — the water is a hand's depth at the center of the channel and ankle depth at the crossings, and the people wade. They wade in their work boots, in their wooden pattens, in their bare feet. Children wade up to their hips and do not seem to find it remarkable. A man sells roasted peppers from a brazier that stands on a triangle of brickwork in the middle of one channel, and the smoke from the brazier mixes with the mist from the cistern vents and the steam from the ground, and the smell is the smell of the place: green, sulfurous, sweet, faintly metallic.

The buildings are stone, low, and crowded together as if for company. Their roofs are flat, and the flat roofs are also the floor of the next building, in many cases, because the district grew up rather than out, up onto itself in a slow heap. Windows are narrow and dark. Doors are thick. Walls are thick. Everything is for keeping the heat out, or the dark in, or both. The construction is layered the way a reef is layered: a building of the 1870s inside a building of the 1920s inside a building of an era nobody dates anymore. Pipes run on the outside of walls and disappear into them and come out again. The pipes are sweating. Everything is sweating.

The light comes from the lanterns. They are gas, or something that smells like gas, and they hang on stanchions at every crossing, and they are never put out, because the light in the quarter is always this orange, always this dim, always wet-looking, because the air itself is wet, and the orange of the flame is refracted through the water in the air and softened and spread, so that the whole quarter glows the way a lantern glows: warm at the center, fading at the edges, and not quite reaching the corners. In the corners there are small dark places where the cats live, and the cats are many, and the cats are large, and they sit in the unlit places and watch the rest of the district with the patience of fish.

The fauna is not only cats. There are eels in the channels, sometimes, when the pumps are running hard and the back-pressure pushes them up out of the lagoon and into the streets. The eels are eaten when they appear. There are swallows, in season, and they nest in the breathing-holes of the cisterns, and they go in and out, in and out, with the rhythm of the pumps. There are beetles, enormous and lacquered, that move across the stone in the evenings. There is a frog, or a small population of frogs, that lives in the engine house and has, over generations, become the same color as the iron. The frogs are not heard unless you put your ear to the housing of a pump. The sound inside the housing is the sound of a wet place that is also a machine. The frogs are part of it.

Vegetation is sparse and willful. A fig tree has taken root in the gutter of Rue XIV and has cracked the curbstone to do it; it bears fruit that is small and bitter and eaten only by children and by the old man who keeps the pump-house keys. Mosses grow on the north faces of walls. A single oleander stands in a cracked pot outside the door of the woman who reads the cistern gauges, and it is covered in pink flowers, and the flowers are always falling, and the flowers are always being swept up by someone and thrown into the channel, where they float for a while and then sink.

Above the quarter, the rest of the city goes about its business, but the quarter is a place that has decided, in some way that is older than anyone living, to belong to the water and to the machines that move the water and to the small lives that have grown up around both. The pumps will go on. The cisterns will breathe. The channels will hold their thin warm water. The orange light will be steady. The cats will be in the corners. If you stand on a flat roof at the small hour of the morning and look up, you will see the square of sky above the quarter, and the sky will be lighter than the quarter, and you will understand that the quarter is a place that has made its own weather, and the weather is this: warmth, wetness, the long patient sound of iron walking, the smell of pepper and sulfur, and the knowledge, felt in the body, that the water is always near and the water is always moving.
