# The Wettrot Reach

The land sits low in a bowl the size of a small country, pressed down beneath a sky that never quite decides whether to be light or dark. It is cool here, always cool, the way a cellar is cool, the way the inside of a mouth is cool. The air has weight. It tastes of wet stone and fur.

The bowl is young. You can tell by the shapes — the rocks are sharp in places, torn-looking, as though something had only just finished making them and hadn't gotten around to softening the edges. Ridges rise along the perimeter in ragged spines, tilted and cracked. Toward the center the ground is flatter, but not flat. It rolls in long low swells like the back of something sleeping, and between the swells the water collects.

The water is the first thing you understand about the place. It is not everywhere. It is not a flood. But it is present in patches, in sheets that never dry but never spread, in shallow ribbons that curve between the rises and hold the sky in them. The rain comes and goes in soft passes, and where it falls it darkens the stone for hours afterward, leaving the rest of the bowl in a state of permanent-damp, like the walls of a cave that has forgotten what a dry season is. Condensation beads on every upward-facing surface. If you kneel and put your ear to the ground you can hear it moving under you, down in the gravel, in thin trickles that don't know where they're going.

The vegetation is thin and stubborn. Grasses — if they are grasses — grow in clumps spaced far apart, more hair than carpet, the color of old rope. They lean away from the center of the bowl as though something there breathes outward. There are no trees. There is no canopy. The tallest thing green in the Reach is about waist-high and you could walk from one clump to the next in twenty paces. Between the clumps the ground shows itself, gray and rust and the dull black of wet mineral, and you can see how the water has been finding its way for a long time.

The animals are not between the clumps.

The animals are the Reach. The animals are why the Reach exists. You cannot see a horizon that is not also moving. The ground shimmers with them, the way heat shimmers above a road, except the shimmer has legs. It is the color of the ground, mostly. Long and low things, the size of dogs or smaller, in coats that match the wet stone. They lie across the swells in their thousands. They lie across the flats in their tens of thousands. They are so numerous that the bowl seems to have been built to contain them, or perhaps they came to fit whatever shape the bowl offered; the difference, here, is hard to settle.

They are quiet most of the time. Not silent — the bowl is never silent — but quiet in the way that a held breath is quiet, the way a great number of bodies breathing in the same place produces a low continuous sound that is less a sound than a condition. If you stand still long enough, your own breathing starts to synchronize with it, and you have to remind yourself to breathe on your own schedule.

When they move — and they move often, in slow waves that pass across the bowl like wind through a field — the sound changes. It becomes a scrape and click, a small thunder of claws on wet rock, a whispering of fur against fur. When something disturbs them, a section rises at once, a thousand heads up at once, a thousand pairs of eyes catching what little light the sky offers, and the sound becomes a sharp chatter, a clattering, a sound like a tree full of startled birds if the tree were made of small mammals. Then they settle again. Then the bowl is quiet again.

The light is poor. The sky is a low thing, pale, the color of old milk, and the light that comes through it is thin and sour and casts no real shadows. In the morning, if there is a morning, the eastern ridges are not edged in gold but in a slightly less gray gray, and that is the only way you know the day has changed. By afternoon the light is failing. By what anyone else would call evening the bowl is in a kind of permanent twilight, and the eyes of the animals begin to show as small wet points scattered across the ground, as numerous as the stars but lower and closer and watching.

There is no sound of water falling. The rain, when it comes, comes sideways in fine needles, and you hear it as a hiss on the stone, a sound like sand thrown at a wall, that lasts for an hour or a day and then stops. The trickles underground are louder than the rain. Sometimes a deeper sound comes from the bowl's center — not a roar, more a settling, a long slow exhalation, the sound of a great weight adjusting itself. The animals do not react to it. They know what it is.

The place is unbuilt. No one has ever laid a stone on another stone here with intent. There are no paths, because the animals do not need paths and nothing else has needed to move through. The only marks on the ground that are not water or claw are the deep grooves worn into the stone along certain ridgelines, where the smaller animals commute in single file every day between their sleeping swells and the central water. The grooves shine. They are the only human-smooth things in the bowl, though no human made them.

Sometimes, on the stillest days, a smell rises from the center of the Reach. It is not unpleasant. It is the smell of warm fur and wet mineral and something underneath both of those, something older, something that smells like the inside of the earth when you have dug very far down. The animals turn their heads toward it when it comes. The grasses stop their leaning. The water in the shallow ribbons trembles. Then it passes, and everything resumes, and the bowl continues as it has always continued, damp and cool and sharp-edged and full to the brim with small lives pressed together, waiting for nothing, changing toward nothing, a place that has decided what it is and will not be argued out of it.
