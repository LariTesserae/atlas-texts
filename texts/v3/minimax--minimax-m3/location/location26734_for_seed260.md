The place begins as a hum.

Not a sound you hear so much as a sound you stand inside of — the way a person standing in a river doesn't so much hear the water as become aware of its mass against their body. The hum is in the rock, in the air, in the chest, behind the eyes. It is older than the mountain. The mountain, in fact, is what the hum is making.

You are very high. Higher than trees have ever been. The air is thin and cold in a way that doesn't bite so much as forget to be warm, the way a stone left in a courtyard forgets that it was once fire. The sky is the color of old pewter, or of milk left to settle, and it gives back no particular time of day — dim light without source, or with a source so high and pale it could pass for sky.

The mountain is not finished. You can see this in a hundred small ways. The ridges have not yet learned to be sharp. The valleys have not yet decided what they are draining. The scree is fresh and bright in a way that scree should not be bright because there has been no weather to dull it. But there has been weather. There has been nothing but weather. The mountain is emerging *through* weather. The wind has been at it for a length of time you cannot read from the mountain, because the mountain is too new to have a memory of itself, but you can read it from the wind, which has worn itself into a permanent kind of scream.

The scream is what you're hearing.

It is not a scream of distress, exactly. It is more like the noise a kettle makes just before it boils, if the kettle were the size of a continent and had been boiling for a hundred thousand years. There is a keening in it, a high thin blade of sound that cuts across everything else, and beneath that a deeper tone, the kind of tone you feel in the floor of a cathedral when the organ is pushed to its bottom note. Together they make a sound that has no intervals, no rests, no breath. It just goes on. It has been going on so long that the silence around it has given up trying to compete and has simply withdrawn, leaving the hum to fill the whole valley, the whole range, the whole of the dim sky.

There are no animals here. Nothing moves except the wind, the slow settling of debris, and the occasional shift of something deep in the rock that you cannot see but can feel as a kind of nausea if you stand still too long. The silence that would normally fill the spaces between animal sounds is full instead of the hum, so the place is loud and lonely at the same time, the way a bell is loud and lonely after the bell has stopped ringing and the ringing is still in your ears.

Now — the plants.

This is the strange part. The place should be bare. Cold, high, dry, wind-blasted, dim. There is no water. None. You could carry a dry stone from the riverbed a thousand miles below up to here, and the stone would arrive still dry. There are no clouds, no fog, no dew. The air is what you'd call *anhydrous* if you wanted a single word for it — the kind of air in which nothing rots, nothing rusts, nothing weeps.

And yet there is growth.

It comes in patches, in curtains, in strange festoons that drape over the rocks like old fabric. Each patch is small and they are far apart, but each is dense with itself, almost glutted — a handspan of lichen here might be three inches thick and pulsing faintly with internal structure; a fist-sized clump of something mushroom-soft there might be the most concentrated mass of life you have ever seen outside of a whale carcass. They are not green, or not only green. Some are the deep brick-red of dried blood. Some are pale violet, the color of a bruise on its second day. Some are black and glitter with a kind of metallic shine, as if each cell contained a flake of mica. A few are almost white, almost luminous, and these are the ones you see first as you pick your way along the ridge, faint smudges of light against the pewter rock.

You don't know what they eat. You could stand and study one for a long time — they grow so slowly that you could mistake them for stone — but you would not learn what they eat. They do not seem to eat at all. They seem to simply *persist*, the way a stalactite persists, except that they are not mineral. They are alive, or they are doing a very good job of pretending to be.

You begin to wonder, after a while, whether they eat the sound.

You can't prove it. But you notice that the thickest, densest patches of growth are always in the lee of certain outcroppings, where the wind is forced into eddies and the hum piles up. You notice that the underside of every shelf-rock in this place is furred with growth, even where the wind never reaches. You notice that when you sit with your back against a boulder and the scream of the wind is being funneled past your ears, the growth around you is thicker than on the equivalent slope of a quieter valley. The hum is doing something. The plants are using something. You don't know what to call it. You don't know if it has a name in any language that has ever been spoken. You only know that, in this place, the absence of water has not stopped things from flourishing; it has only changed what flourishing means.

The mountain itself is making the hum, you realize after a time. Not the wind — the wind is just the carrier. The mountain is the instrument, the resonating chamber, the throat of something. The rocks here are not quite the kind of rocks you are used to. They are heavy, in the way that all high-altitude rocks are heavy, but they are also *slightly wrong*. If you hold one up and look at it closely, your eyes do not quite agree with what you are seeing. The grain is there but it keeps shifting. The color is there but it slides if you stare too long. There is something about the stone in this place that does not fully commit to being stone. It is as if the rock is still deciding, after all this time, whether it is going to be rock, or whether it is going to be something else.

This is the part of the place that doesn't work.

It happens in patches, in ways you can almost train yourself to ignore. A ridge that should slope to the left slopes a little to the right, but only if you look at it directly; from the corner of your eye it slopes correctly. A valley you have been walking along for half an hour is, when you turn around, only ten minutes long. A cairn you built on a saddle two days ago — you can see it from here, if you can see it from here, and it is both there and not there, a kind of smudge in the air where the cairn should be, and the cairn itself is smudged in the same way. Time and distance have not so much broken down as become *negotiable*. The rules of the place are being written and rewritten in slow pencil, and the slow pencil is whatever it is that the hum is doing to the inside of your skull.

You stop trying to correct your eyes. You stop trying to correct your memory. You take what you are given and you keep walking. The walking is the only thing that still feels reliable, and even that has started to feel like a negotiation.

The scattered structures help. There are not many of them. A low wall of loose stones here, partly knocked down. A tripod of poles there, made of a kind of pale wood that doesn't quite grow anywhere you have ever seen, lashed together with strips of something that might be hide but probably isn't. A circle of small upright slabs, no taller than your knee, set into the ground so the wind cannot move them. They are the marks of people — of some prior set of hands that knew how to do things with their fingers and not much else. They are not made to last. They are not made to do anything specific, as far as you can tell. They are just *made*, the way a person makes a mark on a wall to say they were here, except that this mark has been here for a long time and the people who made it are gone.

You don't know where they went. There is no sign of them. No shelter, no hearth, no place where a body might be buried. Just the marks. The marks and the hum and the cold and the slow wrongness of the rocks. The people who put the tripod together must have been very small, or the tripod must have been very small, or both, and they must have come up here in the dim light, in the deafening wind, and set the poles against each other and lashed them with strips of something, and then stood back to look at the thing they had made. They must have looked at it for a long time. They must have walked away. The walking, too, is the part of the story that has gone missing.

You sit by the tripod for a while. It leans a little. The wind screams through it and makes a noise like a voice making an effort to be a voice, which is the only kind of noise there is up here. The poles are pale and smooth and worn at the joints where the lashings were. The lashings are gone. The poles remember them the way an old fence remembers a wire that has been cut.

Below the tripod, in the lee of the boulder, there is a thick drape of the dark glittering growth. As you sit there you begin to feel, through the cold stone against your back, that the hum of the mountain is a little louder than it was when you sat down. Not louder in a way you can measure. Just more present. You begin to feel, too, that the dim light has shifted — not in brightness, but in *color*, the way the sky over a fire shifts when the fire is almost out. The plants around you seem, very slowly, to be leaning toward you. Not toward you as a person. Toward where you are sitting. As if they would like to be closer to the place where you are, without knowing what a place is.

You begin to wonder, suddenly, whether the slow dynamic of this whole range — the patient emerging of the mountain, the patient growing of the plants, the patient breakdown of distance and time — is one single process. Whether the hum is making the mountain. Whether the mountain is making the plants. Whether the plants are making the smudges in the air where the cairns should be. Whether the cairns are making the smudges in the air where the people should be. Whether you, sitting here with your back against the boulder, are part of the same long quiet construction, and whether you are being made too, slowly, by a hum you have been hearing since before you arrived and will keep hearing after you have gone, or after the *gone* has become negotiable as well.

You stand up. The wind hits you. The hum hits you. The sky is the same pewter, the same forgotten warmth, the same nowhere-time. The ridge ahead of you is, when you look at it, definitely a ridge, and it definitely slopes to the left, and you definitely know which way is down.

You start walking.

Behind you, in the lee of the boulder, the dark growth settles slowly back into the rock, as if it has been watching you and has decided, for now, that there is nothing more to watch. The tripod leans a little further in the wind. The place continues to be made, slowly, in the dim light, under the constant scream of itself.

You do not look back. You are not sure, today, what *back* would mean.
