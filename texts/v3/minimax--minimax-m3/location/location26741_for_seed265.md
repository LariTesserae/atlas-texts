The place came up out of itself recently.

That is how it feels to walk it, though *walk* is a generous word for what can be done here — the surface has the consistency of something remembered rather than something trodden. Knife-ridged folds of sediment stand up where they should be lying flat, because whatever had been holding them down (a sea, a layer of older world, a weight of time) let go only recently, geologically speaking. The edges haven't been worn. They cut. So: a regional valley, ten miles across, maybe more, where the floor has only just learned to be a floor. Sharp little canyons that aren't canyons yet — drainage that hasn't run long enough to round itself. Cracked plates of mud tilted vertical like pages in an open book. A pale substrate underfoot that gives when you stand on it, not sand, not quite clay, more like the inside of something that had been closed for a long time. It is warm. The warmth is everywhere, even in the shadows — especially in the shadows, which are the majority.

Because there is no light here. Not *less than usual*. None. The sky, where it can be glimpsed between the standing plates and the pipes and the tall skeletal things, is not dark in the way night is dark, with stars or moon or the suggestion of distance. It is dark in the way a closed mouth is dark. Whatever happened to the sky in this place, it isn't sky anymore. A black ceiling a few hundred feet up, sometimes lower, sometimes pressing close enough that you can hear it breathing — a slow wet sound, almost subsonic. The darkness is warm because the air is warm. The warmth is wet because the air is wet. The wetness is constant because the ground itself is wet, and the ground is wet because water rises through every crack in the young surface, through the tilted pages, through the spaces around the pipes, and there is nowhere lower for it to go. It condenses on every metal surface as soon as the surface is colder than the air, which is always, because the air is warm and wet and the metal is whatever temperature metal is. Pipes weep at their joints. Towers drip from invisible heights. The ground itself seems to sweat. Where the water collects, it doesn't quite behave. It sits in puddles that look flat from one angle and concave from another, and when you walk past, sometimes the puddle drifts after you for a few steps before losing interest. The fauna has learned not to drink from these. Certain kinds drink anyway and are wrong afterward.

The fauna is the thing you cannot avoid. *Abundant* is too small a word. The air is full of it. The water is full of it. The pipes are full of it, inside, if *inside* is the right word. The standing plates of sediment are bored through with tunnels, and the tunnels are full of it. Most of it is small and most of it is quiet, and the quietness is part of the place — not silence, because there are sounds: a constant soft rustle, the slow drip and the slow rise, a low electrical hiss from the towers, the click and whir of small things moving in the pipes. But quiet enough that your own breathing, if you breathe, is the loudest thing in the room you happen to be standing in. So the fauna has to be seen or inferred, and in the dark it is mostly inferred, and what can be inferred is that you are never alone.

What can be *seen*: things that produce their own dim signals. Lantern-flies that are not flies, six-winged, that hang in the still air with their abdomens pulsing a faint blue-cyan, so that the not-sky has, in certain stretches, a low constellation of living points that drifts and rearranges itself on no schedule you can read. Long pale worms in the standing water that emit, from segments along their length, a greenish intermittent light, like beads on a wire that have forgotten their rhythm. A kind of frog, perhaps a frog, that sits on the rims of the pipes and shines a steady red from its throat — red that does not fall into shadows the way red should. It spills, finds its way around corners, makes the pipes look as if they contain warm water when they are empty. And the things you cannot see but know are there: the rustlings in the substrate, the things that move across your path as a displacement of the wet air, a faint wrong feeling across the back of the hand. A thing, larger, that lives in one of the unfilled canals and breathes; you can hear it breathing if you stand near the rim. It is patient. It will be there tomorrow. It has probably been there for a long time.

The industrial.

When this place came up out of itself, it brought something with it, or something was already threaded through the lower layers and came up with the layers, or it has been growing since, threaded into the wet air by the place's own slow metabolism. Pipes. Many pipes. Some as thick as a man lying down, some the width of a finger. They run in straight lines where straight lines are possible and curve where the standing plates force them to curve, and they are everywhere — at every height, in the ground, along the ground, suspended between things, climbing the towers. The substance of them is hard to identify. Not iron, not quite. They have a color that depends on how wet they are. They hum, faintly, at the edge of hearing. At the joints, where two pipes meet, there are fittings of unclear purpose: a wheel that doesn't turn, a glassy disc you can see through but cannot see into, a hexagonal nut that appears to be welded but can be unscrewed by hand, and when unscrewed reveals the same pipe continuing, inside, with no discontinuity, no interior, only the next length of pipe pretending to be inside itself.

Towers. Skeletal towers of pipe and angle, going up into the not-sky, some of them a thousand feet tall and you can only tell by the slow way they vanish. They carry nothing visible. Some of them carry sounds — a slow heartbeat from inside, as if something tall and patient were suspended in each one. Conveyors, just the frames, the belts gone or never there, suspended between towers like rigging on a ship that lost its sails. The frames move sometimes, very slowly, when there is enough water to grease them — a faint groan of metal that hasn't been oiled and won't be. A few buildings. Scant. A low structure that might be a pump house, but the pump inside has been replaced by something you can only see the back of, and the front of it is whatever direction you are looking from. A tank, the size of a room, full of a liquid that is not water and is not anything else you can name; it is dark inside the tank, and you can see the surface of it from outside because the wall is not behaving as a wall. A chimney, or a vent, that exhales a slow warm breath every few minutes, smelling of metal and ozone and something sweet you almost remember. All of this is industrial. All of it is doing something. None of it is making what industrial things are meant to make. It is producing, continuously, the conditions of the place: the wetness, the warmth, the faint hum, the fauna's abundance, the wrongness of the geometry. It is a machine for making this place, and this place is the only place it knows how to make.

The weirdness.

Stand still for long enough and you notice that the distance to the nearest tower is uncertain. Not *you forgot where you started* uncertain. The tower is forty feet away and also two hundred feet away. The path between you and it is forty feet long and also two hundred feet long. If you walk toward it, you arrive when you arrive, and the distance on the way back is different. Stand still longer and you notice that the tower is not quite the same tower. Not in any way you can point to. The angles are right. The pipes are right. The hum is right. But you would not swear, on anything you cared about, that it is the same object. Look at a puddle. The puddle looks back. Not metaphorically. Something in the water arranges itself into a configuration that is paying attention to you, and it is not the reflection of anything above it, because there is nothing above it that would produce this. After a while it loses interest. The not-sky breathes. It expands and contracts on a slow cycle, maybe hourly, maybe daily — hard to tell down here where the light doesn't move and the fauna is the only clock. When it contracts, the pressure increases and your ears pop and the standing water rises slightly in the cracks. When it expands, you can breathe more easily and the pipes exhale a faint mist and the things in the water settle.

The fauna, some of it, is not quite fauna. The lantern-flies have, on closer inspection, too many wings. The number is not fixed. Sometimes six, sometimes seven, sometimes five and a half — the half a stub. The frogs with the red throats have a second throat, lower, that does not produce sound. The worms in the water are very long, longer than the water is deep, longer than any worm should be in any geometry that respects itself, and they pass through each other sometimes without seeming to notice. There are things that you might call buildings and things that you might call animals and things that you might call machines, and the categories do not hold. A thing like a tower has, halfway up, a horizontal opening that breathes, and out of the opening comes a soft grey animal the size of a cat that climbs the rest of the way and sits at the top. The top is the same temperature as the bottom. The animal looks down at you with patience. The tower, the animal, the breath — they are one thing, or three, or one thing pretending to be three, and which reading is correct depends on what phase of the cycle it is.

Vegetation, such as it is.

A few pale filaments clinging to the undersides of the standing plates where the water runs slowest, in the dark, in the warm, in the wet. Things that are not quite plants — no leaves, no green, no chlorophyll's red either — but that have a root and a tip and grow toward the warmest wet surface. They are sparse. They are slow. They will, given enough of the place's long slow time, cover the plates, and then the place will not be this place anymore, or it will be the same place with a different skin. That is the dynamic of it — too slow to see, too slow to feel, but real. The not-sky contracts and expands. The water rises and falls by inches over timescales that are not hours and not years but something between. The sparse filaments grow a fraction of a fraction. The sharp edges of the young sediment weather by a thickness you could not measure, smoothed by the wet air, softened by the abundance of small things that pass through them on the way to somewhere else. The place is changing. It is changing right now. You are standing in it and it is changing, but the change is in increments that belong to the place and not to you, and you will be gone, or different, before the change is large enough to be the change of anything at all.

The sound, mostly quiet.

The quiet is not silence. The quiet is the resting state of a machine that is always running, and the running makes a low sound that has become the floor of perception. Underneath it: drips, slow drips, slow rises. The hum of pipes. The whisper of the not-sky. The click of small things in tunnels. The occasional groan of a conveyor frame. The breathing of the larger thing in the canal. The breathing of the not-sky. The breathing of you. Sometimes, rarely, a sound that is louder — a low tone from a tower that lasts for about a minute and is felt more than heard, and during it the lantern-flies go out and the frogs close their throats and the worms dim, and the not-sky contracts a little harder, and the standing water in the cracks rises about an inch. Then it passes and the lights come back, dimmer, and the fauna resumes.

This happens once an hour, or once a day, or once a year. You cannot tell. The slow dynamic of the place means the changes are too gradual to catch, and the clock is fauna that does not keep time, and the not-sky does not keep time either. Down here, in the wet warm dark, time is something the surface has and the depths do not, and the place has not yet decided which of those it is.
