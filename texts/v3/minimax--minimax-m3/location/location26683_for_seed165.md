# The Col Above the Cloudline

The basin sits where the wind first learns to scream. Five miles across, irregular, half-circled by the broken teeth of ridges that have been grinding themselves down for longer than anything living can remember. The air is thin enough to taste. It tastes of copper and of nothing.

## The Ground

The stone is a deep blue-grey, almost charcoal when the light is low, and it is never not low. It has the texture of something that was once liquid and then remembered it shouldn't be. Long flutes run down the standing rocks, scored by centuries of the same wind, the same grit, the same punishment, and the flutes are so regular they look carved, but they are not carved. They are the patient handwriting of weather on a substance that does not want to be weather.

Where the flutes widen into shallow basins, there is gravel. The gravel moves when you are not watching. This is not metaphor. A stone you set down in the evening will be three feet to the west by morning, as if the slope exhaled in its sleep. Nothing is steep here — the floor of the basin is nearly level — and yet everything drifts westward in small, slow betrayals. No one has ever watched it happen. The displacement is too gradual. You only know because you marked the stone, and the mark is not where you left it.

There are eight standing stones in the basin. Or there have been eight for as long as the oldest lichen records. They are waist-high, blunt-topped, set in no pattern that any geometry can parse. Two of them, when you stand between them at certain hours, make a sound in your inner ear that is not the wind. Some travelers describe it as a low chord. Some as a pressure. Some weep without knowing why and walk away and do not come back.

## The Water

It is damp here in a way that defies the cold. The air holds almost no warmth and yet it is never quite dry. Condensation beads on every vertical surface an hour before dawn, and in the lee of the larger rocks there are thin films of ice that form and do not melt, only sublimate, becoming again the air. Pools appear in the hollows in late summer — small, black, reflecting nothing — and they are gone by midmorning, though no one sees them leave. The stones around these pools are rimmed with a pale mineral crust, like the tide-line of an invisible sea.

When it rains, it rains horizontally. The drops arrive already broken, atomized into a mist that stings any exposed skin. The mist does not fall so much as *pass through*. After such a rain, the standing stones are darker for days, as if they had been drinking.

There is a stream. It enters the basin through a cleft in the western wall — a tongue of clear water sliding over dark rock — and it leaves through a fissure at the eastern end that is too narrow for a person to follow. Where it crosses the basin floor, it does not behave as a stream should. It runs in three parallel ribbons separated by dry gravel, and the ribbons do not merge. They never merge. They have had, by all evidence, geological ages to find each other and they have chosen not to.

## The Flora

What grows here grows in the cracks. A creeping saxifrage, its rosettes no larger than a thumbnail, its flowers the pale, bruised lavender of things that have not seen pollinators in a long time. A lichen, ochre and crustose, that paints the lee sides of the larger rocks in shapes that resemble, when you are tired, the outlines of buildings that never were. A moss, dark green almost to black, that appears only in the deepest crevices and only on the side of the crevice that faces away from the prevailing wind, as if it has strong opinions about shelter.

There are perhaps forty individual plants in the entire basin. They are not clustered. They are scattered across the rock with the deliberate spacing of things that cannot afford to share.

## The Sound

This is the loudest quiet place you have ever stood in.

The wind is the first thing, and it does not stop. It comes over the western ridge and it pours into the basin like water into a bowl, and it cannot escape upward — there is a higher rim — so it eddies and rebounds and arrives at your ears from four directions at once. It sings in the flutes of the standing stones, and each flute has its own pitch. The bass notes come from the deep cuts at the base of the central monolith. The treble is from the high scarps of the western wall. The wind is not one sound but a chord, and the chord never resolves.

Beneath that, lower, lower than the wind, there is another sound. It is felt in the soles of the feet before it is heard. A subsonic tremor, as if the basin itself were a struck drum that has never finished ringing. When the wind gusts hard, the tremor swells, and the gravel in the shallows rearranges itself in soft, whispering avalanches that are not caused by gravity but by standing waves in the air. Some travelers, lying down to sleep on the flatter stones, have reported that the sound follows them into dreams and that in the dreams the basin is larger, much larger, and the standing stones are not stones at all.

There are birds. Rarely. A snowfinch, blown up from below, will pass through in a streak of white. A lammergeier, if you are very fortunate and very unlucky, will hang in the wind above the eastern col, turning, turning, its shadow passing across the basin floor like the hand of a slow clock. The birds do not land. The basin is not for landing.

## The Light

It is dim here, and not because of cloud. The sun, when it crests the eastern ridge, gives a watery illumination that seems to pass through the air rather than touch it. Shadows are too sharp, almost black, as if the light is being decided rather than diffused. In the hour before sunset — which is also the hour before the temperature drops below what is bearable — the basin fills with a color that has no name in any language the visitors speak. It is between lavender and ash. It makes the standing stones look like they are about to speak, and then the light dies and they are just stones again.

The moon, when it is up, does something wrong. Its light lands on the floor of the basin in patches that are brighter than they should be, and the patches do not move as the moon moves. They hold their positions through the night. In the morning they are gone.

## The Scattered Marks

There is a low wall on the southern lip of the basin. Three courses of unmortared stone, perhaps four meters long, built with a care that the rest of the basin does not invite. It does not enclose anything. It faces east. Beside it, half-buried in gravel, a piece of worked bone — antler, probably — with notches cut into it in groups of three. The notches mean nothing. Or they mean everything, and the difference cannot be resolved from inside the basin.

A little further on, a cairn that has fallen open. Inside, a folded textile so old it has become mineral, a knife blade of greenish copper, and a small clay lamp that still, impossibly, holds a wick. No one has lit it. No one will.

There is a single upright slab at the basin's western edge, taller than the standing stones, and on its face there is a symbol. It is not a letter. It is a single closed curve, like an eye, or a loop, or a zero. The people who come here — and they do come, sometimes, from the valleys far below, with great effort and no small fear — say that if you look at the symbol long enough, you can see the basin the way it was before the basin. They do not say what that means. They go home.

## The Equilibrium

Nothing here is in the process of becoming anything else. The stones do not fall faster or slower. The stream does not widen its bed. The lichen does not creep. The wind does not strengthen. The tremor does not deepen. The light is always the same shade of inadequate. The air is always the same temperature of reluctant. The cup of the basin holds what it holds, has held it, will hold it. The reality of the place is the same reality it has always been, and this is not reassuring. This is the kind of permanence that wears a face.

When you leave — and you will leave, everyone leaves — you will find, halfway down the slope, that you have forgotten the shape of the basin. You will know it was there. You will remember the wind. But the arrangement of the standing stones, the path of the stream, the location of the wall — these will have left you, gently, as if they were never yours to keep.
