The place is a basin — a broad, irregular depression in the earth with curving walls of pale stone rising ten, twenty, sometimes thirty meters above its floor. It is open-topped. The sky above is wide and visible, and the sun pours in directly for most of the day. The light inside is hard and unmediated, the kind of light that gives everything a sharp, exposed quality — no diffusion, no softening, no half-tones. Stone and leaf alike look almost drawn.

The geology is young. The stone hasn't been here long. In places the slope looks placed — clean fracture-lines, dry surfaces, the pale, almost chalky grey of rock that hasn't yet been worked by weather. In other areas the stone is iron-stained ochre, warmer in tone. There is no developed soil. Where the floor is visible, it is stone; where it isn't, the plants have buried it.

The vegetation is total. Every surface that can hold a root holds a root, and most surfaces that can hold more than one hold many — layered, climbing, climbing each other. The plants are the architecture here. Where the basin's walls rise steep, climbing growth hangs from ledges in curtains and spills down toward the floor. Where the floor is flat, the plants have built it up into a dense, knee-deep mat of living stems on top of dead stems on top of stone. There are no trees. The basin is too open, the light too direct; what grows here grows horizontally, in thickets and skeins and dense mounds. The growth reaches a certain density and stops. It does not progress further; it does not retreat. The place has been colonized fully and the colonization is complete and frozen.

Up close, in the zones where the sun falls directly, the plants have an order to them that's faintly off. From a distance the basin looks like any overgrown pit — tangled, chaotic, green. Closer in, the patterns repeat. A spiral here. A grid there. Leaves that arrange themselves in rows too even for chance. The growth forms a slow geometry, as if something set it down once and it has stayed that way. The patterns show most clearly in full sun; in the shadowed perimeter, where the walls curve, the plants are looser and darker and don't seem to care.

Water is present but selective. Rain falls into the basin when it falls — the open top catches it. Some runs off; some collects. There is a standing pool in the lowest part of the depression, permanent, fed as much by condensation as by direct rain. The air is humid enough that everything is wet most of the time. Stone drips from its undersides. Leaves drip from their tips. The pool is warm and still and doesn't go away, even in the drier spells when the leaf edges crisp slightly. There is always moisture somewhere — on a leaf, on a wall, in the soil-fake of accumulated plant matter, in the air itself.

The heat is constant and close. The walls hold the warmth. The transpiration of all the plants keeps the humidity high. There is no cooling — no breeze reaches the floor; the shadow is not deep enough to drop the temperature meaningfully. Things that live here live small, live low. Insects, small ones, that hum in the warm air and aren't often seen. Lizards, quick, low, vanishing into the foliage at the slightest movement. Occasionally a bird, briefly, passing overhead — but birds don't stay. The place is too hot for them.

The structures are few. In one part of the basin, half-swallowed by the plant mat, there is a low terrace — old worked stone, in steps, that descends a meter or two into the foliage and ends. The steps lead nowhere. In another part, there is a wall, low, that has been pushed out of true by roots growing beneath it. And near the basin's center, rising out of the thicket like a finger, there is a column. Dark stone, different from the surrounding geology, worked smooth. It has no carving. It has no visible purpose. It is just placed there.

The sound is mostly quiet. The basin muffles. The walls stop most wind from reaching the floor; what wind there is passes over the top. The sounds that exist: the slow drip of water from leaf to leaf, from stone to pool. The hum of insects. The small, occasional rustle of a lizard in dead stems. Mostly — quiet. A hushed, warm, bright place.

The light is what gives the basin its character. It is a noon light, an open-sky light. It falls straight down. It makes hard shadows under every overhang and inside every leaf-clump. The basin divides into two main zones: the directly-lit areas where the strange ordered plant patterns show most clearly, and the shadow perimeter where the walls curve and the plants grow wilder and looser. The transition between the two is sharp.

The strangeness of the place is not dramatic. It looks, at first, almost natural — like any hot, damp, overgrown sink. But the column. The geometric plants. The terrace that descends to nothing. The basin has corners that don't quite fit the original shape of a depression. It is the kind of place that, on first seeing, you might mistake for any abandoned pit of vegetation, and then, looking longer, you might not be sure. The strangeness doesn't accumulate into anything specific. It just sits there, like a question that hasn't been asked.

The place doesn't change. The plants are at the density they have always been at; the pool is at the level it is; the column stands as it stands; the shadows fall in the same places at the same hours; the same drops gather on the same leaves. There is no growth to wait for, no erosion to watch, no weather to anticipate. The basin is what it is, completely, and the parameters that hold it will continue to hold it. It is the same tomorrow. It was the same yesterday. The sun moves; the shadows move with it. Nothing else does.
