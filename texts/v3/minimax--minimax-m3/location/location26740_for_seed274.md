# The Weeping Cellar

The place is not large. You can walk across it in perhaps ten minutes if you pick your way carefully, though few people do pick their way carefully here. The path curves with the shape of the basin. The basin is the place.

It is a low, shallow bowl pressed into warm stone — sandstone, perhaps, or something older that has forgotten what to call itself. The rock is the color of weak tea, the color of bone left out in summer, the color of things that have been wet for a long time. Water moves through it constantly, not as a stream but as a presence. It weeps from the upper lip of the bowl in thin veils and beads. It condenses on every surface in fine droplets that never quite fall. The air itself tastes of it.

The stone is packed. There is no soil, no leaf, no root. No moss, no lichen, no dark creeping thing. The plants never came here, or they left long ago, and the minerals have since closed over the absence like skin over a healed wound. What remains is a world of stone and water and the slow chemistry between them.

But the stone is not bare. It is built into.

---

The walls of the basin rise perhaps twice a man's height at their tallest, sloping inward like the interior of a cupped hand. And almost every surface is occupied. Alcoves carved at irregular heights. Ledges worn or chiseled into the rock. Shallow shelves. Recessed shelves. A honeycomb of small chambers that go back only a body's length, some of them deeper, one or two that descend out of sight into the warm dark.

The work is hand-work. You can see the chisel-marks if you come close, the slight unevenness where a tool slipped or a worker tired. The tool-marks are shallow and patient. No metal rang here when these were made — or only the simplest metal, copper, bronze at most. Stone-on-stone, and the dust carried out in baskets, and the dust then perhaps used for something else. There are no ores visible. There is no evidence of smelting, only of grinding and cutting.

The chambers are filled. Not with living things. With objects.

---

In one alcove, a stack of flat stones, each one carved with a single curving line, set so the lines together form a larger curve that begins at the bottom and spirals up through the stack and ends at the top — though whether it is meant to be read as a path or a sound or a season or a kind of water, no one knows anymore, or no one alive remembers. The stones are stained dark where water has run over them for years.

In another, a low shelf holds rows of small clay vessels, unglazed, each one stopped with a fitted stone. Inside each vessel — by the weight of them, by the occasional dull glint when the dim light catches — is water. There are perhaps three hundred of them, though the count varies because some have cracked and been replaced and some have been left to leak. A slow, patient collection. The water in them is never quite the same; it weeps from different parts of the wall in different seasons, and the vessels are rotated by hand.

In another alcove, slightly higher, a single slab of stone has been worked into a long shallow trough that runs the whole length of the niche. The trough is full. The water in it is perfectly still and very clear, and at its bottom lie a number of small dark shapes — seeds, perhaps, or pebbles, or teeth, or carved chips. They have been there so long that they have taken on the color of the trough itself. You cannot tell if they are being kept or dissolved.

There are many such arrangements. The whole interior of the basin is given over to them. Alcoves within alcoves. Ledges below ledges. A series of stepped basins cut into the floor that catch the water as it gathers and pass it on from one to another through small overflow channels, each basin holding it a little longer before it spills. The water moves. It is always moving. But it does not go anywhere.

---

The light is poor. There is no opening to the sky above; the basin is roofed by a natural span of stone, cracked and weathered but still holding. Light comes in from a single low passage on one side, which is itself half-blocked by a fall of stones that no one has bothered to clear, and from a narrower gap on the other side that admits only a thin pale shaft. The light falls in two places, and it does not move. There is no sun visible from inside, and the dimness is steady and cool — though the air is warm, and the stone is warm to the touch, and the water is warm.

Because of this, the eye adjusts. After a while the alcoves are not dark but only quieter than the lit places, and you can see that the objects within them are not arranged by chance but by long, deliberate habit. Some of them, on close inspection, are not objects at all. A dark patch on a wall turns out to be a pattern of mineral stain that has been deliberately encouraged, fed, kept from spreading to other walls. A pale streak in the stone turns out to be the imprint of a hand held against the rock while the rock was still soft, sometime, sometime.

---

The place is quiet. The only sound is water. Water dripping from lip to lip of the stepped basins. Water finding its way through unseen channels in the stone. The occasional heavier sound of a vessel cracking somewhere in the dark and being left until morning. A low, persistent hiss from somewhere deeper where water meets heated stone, and the air there is faintly warmer. The air moves very little. It has had a long time to settle.

There is no one here.

Or — there is rarely anyone here. There are signs of occasional presence: a smoothed place where a knee has been set, repeatedly, in front of one of the larger arrangements; a basket left in a corner, old, the reeds gone soft; a stone tool on a ledge that has been placed rather than dropped. Whoever comes here comes alone, or in a very few number, and does not stay long, and does not speak. The marks they leave are gentle. They do not move the stones.

---

The whole of the basin feels old. Not ruined — not collapsed or abandoned in the sense of forgetting. Old in the way that a settled thing is old, the way a tide-pool is old, the way a stain of iron on a wall is old. It has been here long enough that the weathering has come to a stop, or come to a balance. The water does not eat the stone faster than the stone can weep it. The vessels do not crack faster than they can be replaced. The light does not fail.

There is a sense that the place continues whether or not it is observed. That the vessels will fill. That the stepped basins will overflow. That the hand-prints in the stone will not fade further. That this will go on.

You stand inside the basin. The water runs past your feet in the thin sheet between two of the lower basins. It is warm. It does not hurry. The alcoves look out at you from the walls, each one holding its small arrangement in its dim air, and there is no answer you are required to give them.
