A walled hollow, somewhere far up. The basin is perhaps a third of a mile across, and the rim sits a long way above — basalt and stranger volcanic glass, weathered into blunt fingers and slumped shelves by what cannot strictly be called rain. A mist comes and goes. Whether it is a true mist, or vapor, or some kind of slow exhalation from the plants themselves, no one living here has ever settled the question, and no one bothers to. The light that reaches the floor of the hollow is greenish and dim, filtered through a thick drift of canopy that has grown, over many years, until it nearly bridges the opening like a lid. You do not arrive here; you descend. The trail is a goat-track that ends in stone stairs cut by hands that did not use metal. The stairs are older than the tracks in them.

There is no water here. Not as water.

What wells up through the cracked floor of the old caldera is a thin, warm seep — iron-bright, tasting of salts and sulfur and a faint sweet rot, carrying copper and stranger metals in suspension. The lowest plants drink it directly. Their roots lie on the surface like spilled yarn. Above them, the next tier of plants drinks them. And above that, the next. The whole hollow is a stacked economy of moisture that no one would call water but that functions, in its way, like water. It moves. It is drunk. It is breathed out again. The basin smells of pennies, of wet fur, of cut melon left too long in the sun, and beneath all of it, faintly, of ozone, as if a storm is always happening somewhere just out of sight.

The vegetation is heavy with itself. Towering stalks of something one might generously call bamboo but that has knuckles of black wax and exudes a slow amber syrup when scored. Cordons of vine as thick as an arm, scabbed with lichen the color of a bruise, swinging from branch to branch in slow, sluggish arcs that do not stop even when there is no wind — pulled by something other than wind. Broad, fleshy rosettes, each as wide as a table, their leaves the green of a vein, glossy, weeping at the tips a clear bead that is not water either but does the work of water. Pillar-fungi, pallid, taller than a person, ringed in shelves like the pages of a book no one is reading. The whole forest floor is a wet patchwork: a thing like a pitcher-plant the size of a barrel; another like coral, brittle and orange, that flinches when you step near it; a carpet of something red and furred that hums faintly underfoot, as if the ground itself is unwell.

It is loud. It is always loud.

From every direction, the chitter of cuticle on cuticle. Things moving through things. The slither-and-tap of small bodies up the stalks. A rhythmic ticking, somewhere up in the canopy — not cricket, not clock — a beating of folded wings against folded wings. The slow hiss of vents along the basin wall where the warm seep escapes in pale threads of vapor. The deeper, occasional groan of the basalt itself settling, the caldera remembering that it is a wound. At night — and it does get darker, though never fully black, because the seep glows faintly where it pools, and the fungi keep their own small lights, foxfire and amber — the calls begin. A long, bell-toned hoot from somewhere in the upper canopy. A thin, almost metallic trill, answered by another, then another, then a small chorus of them, then nothing, then the trill again from somewhere else as if the whole forest is talking to itself and is not quite listening.

The fauna is everywhere and almost nowhere. A density of *presence* rather than a density of *body*. A column of dark, jointed things half the size of a thumb, moving in a single thick rope along a vine — paused now to drink the syrup off a scarred stalk — resuming in a wave, a thousand legs in a small arithmetic. Long, pale worms as thick as a wrist, levering themselves between the basalt cracks by slow peristalsis. Flat, many-winged things that never quite land, that roost only on the undersides of leaves and hang there in clustered commas, fanning. And larger — there are larger things. You sense them more than see them. A slow displacement in the undergrowth. A weight that bows the vine. A careful, deliberate crunch, somewhere in the rosette garden, that goes on too long to be accidental. The hunters, the grazers, the things that eat the things that eat the things. The hollow is a stomach with views.

The structures are few and small. Against the far wall, a low stone enclosure, roofed with lashed stalks, open on one side to the seep. Beside it, two clay vessels, hand-coiled, fired in some local fumarole, one cracked and mended with a smear of resin. A line of dressed stones set into the ground in a long curve, like the beginning of a wall that no one ever finished. A pole, leaning, hung with strips of cured fungus-skin and a few bleached shards of carapace — wind-chimes, possibly, though they do not chime so much as click, irregularly, in the warmth. Whoever lives here — and someone does, in ones and twos, never in numbers that would count as a village — lives lightly. They cut with chipped stone. They carry in woven baskets of the cordage-vine. They eat the syrup, the pale flesh of the pillar-fungi, the small jointed things they pull from the stalks in careful handfuls and bite the heads off. They do not farm. They tend, the way one tends a wound.

The air moves constantly. Hot air finding the upper canopy, cool air falling back. The whole hollow is breathing, in and out, in and out, a slow lung that smells of pennies and melon and ozone and the very faint, dry, mineral reek of a sky no one has seen clearly in a long time.

It is a place of churn. Things grow here at a pace you can watch — the rosettes unfurling in the heat, the vines visibly thickening by the hour, the fungi throwing out new shelves overnight. Other things die in the same interval: a stalk comes down soft in the morning, already being eaten; a column of the jointed things crosses a place where a sister column crossed yesterday and finds it dark and dry and gone. There is no season here, or every season is happening at once. A flower opens in the same breath as a body is finished. Nothing is fixed. Everything is metabolizing. The hollow is not a ruin and not a garden. It is something in between that has been happening for a long time and will keep happening for a long time, in its own strange, dim, loud, briny, furiously balanced way — a small green wound in the side of a mountain, breathing.
