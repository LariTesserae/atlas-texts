A basin, mostly. The mountains around it have been worn down so far that they are only suggestions of mountains — long gray ridges that catch the dim light like worn teeth. Inside the basin the ground is a kind of wet slate, near-flat, ancient beyond any era that has a name. It has been here, in roughly this shape, since before shape was finished. You can feel that the erosion is over. The work has been done. The land has settled into its final form, and the final form is this: a wide cold floor, ringed by low peaks, breathing mist.

The plants are everywhere and they are sparse. Each one is enormous — taller than anything built, wider than any road — but there are only a handful of them, scattered across the slate floor like monuments someone forgot, or like the trees of a forest after the forest has been almost entirely removed. They are the architecture. They are the only architecture. They are not really plants. They are plants the way coral is a plant, the way a cathedral is a rock, the way an engine is a kind of animal. They stand and they work and they grow and they are cold to the touch.

The wetness comes from everywhere. From the ground, which sweats a thin film of dark water that never quite becomes a puddle. From the plants themselves, which bead constantly with condensate, so that in the dim light each trunk looks as if it were sweating, or weeping, or sweating from weeping. From the air, which is a mist that has not decided to become rain and probably never will. From the rocks along the ridges, which weep in long vertical stains. From the creatures, which breathe out visible vapor and leave wet footprints that hiss faintly as they set.

The cold is not only temperature. The cold is a substance. It has weight. It sits on the back of the neck. It is a sound, in the way that a constant tone is a sound. It is gray, the way cold is gray.

The plants are doing something. They are not merely standing. Somewhere inside them, deep in the fibrous dark of their trunks, there are chambers where something is being made, or unmade, or kept. They breathe in the mist and exhale a heavier mist. Their roots go down into the wet slate and come up tasting of iron and copper. They have pulses, slow ones. They groan. Each one hums at its own frequency and the frequencies do not quite harmonize, so that there is a slow shifting chord of low sound running through the valley at all times. This is what loud means here: a city heard from a great height, or the inside of a body, or a forest in a wind that never stops. The valley is loud the way an old house is loud — not with any one noise, but with the sum of its keeping-on.

Things live here, and they live in numbers. Most of them are cold-blooded and move slowly, or warm-blooded in a sort of stubbornness against the cold. They are strange and they are many. There are small dark ones in the shadow of the plants, going about small dark business in the wet cracks. There are large pale ones in the open, browsing on whatever the plants exude from their lower bark, which is sometimes a sap and sometimes a kind of fine dark powder that smells of ozone. There are flyers — birds, or things built like birds and flown by something else — that wheel through the mist at a height no one can quite focus on and that never seem to land. There are worms in the slate that are longer than memory and that hum faintly when you stand over them. There is at least one thing so large and so patient that it can only be inferred: a slow dark shape moving behind one ridge and emerging, much later, behind another.

The light is dim. The sky is not overcast exactly; it is more that the sky has become a thick medium, something you could almost wade through. If there is a sun, it is a rumor of brightness somewhere far to the south, the way a fire is a rumor when you hear it from the next valley. The plants catch this dim light and do something to it. They hold it. They bend it. They let it drip from their leaves in slow luminous drops that are too heavy to be rain and too liquid to be light. At certain hours the valley glows with its own light, faintly, the way deep water glows — as if the plants are leaking the day back out at the end of it.

The ground is in motion. This is the strange part, or one of them. The valley has been eroded to its final form, but the final form is not stable. Parts of the slate floor rise. Parts of it sink. There are slow waves moving through the rock, as if the bedrock were a lake under a long invisible tide. The plants sway in sympathy, though there is no wind. The creatures pause and lift their heads and go on. Sometimes a section of the floor becomes briefly translucent, and you can see, dimly, something beneath — pipes, or roots, or veins, or a darkness that is also a machinery — and then it is stone again. The valley is being unmade and remade at the same time, gently, all the time.

There is no village here. There is no road, no fence, no marker, no wall, no ruin that suggests a wall. The plants are the closest thing to buildings, and they were not built; they grew, or they were always there, or they are still arriving. The machines that hum inside them are not built either. They are biological, or geological, or the distinction has stopped meaning anything in this basin. There is industry here. There is production. There are engines. But it is the industry of an old world that has long since stopped advertising itself, and the factory is the valley itself, and the product is whatever the mist becomes when it has been processed for a very long time inside the dark trunks of enormous plants.

The weirdness is everywhere and it is not loud about it. The plants cast shadows that fall in the wrong direction, or in several directions at once, or that are the wrong color. The mist sometimes organizes itself into columns that have nothing to do with the wind and that drift sideways across the basin for no reason. Sound arrives from a distance that has not yet happened, or from a direction that does not exist on the compass. The cold is not always cold; sometimes it is a pressure, a feeling behind the eyes, a color that has no name, a taste of metal in the back of the throat. The creatures behave according to laws that are mostly the laws of biology and occasionally not. Once, somewhere in the basin, a plant is taller on the inside than on the outside, and the inside is warm, and contains a room. Once, the slate floor remembers being a sky, briefly, and the things on the floor remember being birds. These things pass. No one comments. The valley continues.

There is a kind of weather here that is not weather. Storms pass through that are made mostly of sound. Rain falls upward in thin silver lines and evaporates halfway up. The temperature changes in vertical bands, so the air at your knees is colder than the air at your chest, and you can feel the boundary between them like a soft horizontal line across your body. The mist thins and thickens according to a rhythm that has nothing to do with the day, a slow breathing that may be the valley's or may be the plants' or may be yours, in sympathy, by the time you have been here long enough.

This is a place that has reached its old age and has not been allowed to rest. It is wet and cold and dim and loud and full of strange slow life and strange fast machinery. It is the floor of something. It is the inside of something. It has been here longer than most things, and it will be here longer than most things. It does not need to be understood to continue. It continues.
