# The Drowned Yard

I am under water. I have been under water for as long as I can remember, and the water is the world.

It is cool — not the cold of polar seas, not the warmth of a tropical shelf, but the working temperature of an engineered liquid. Sixty degrees, maybe less. The kind of cool that wakes the skin without numbing it. I feel it through my suit, against my face, along the backs of my hands when I reach out to brace against a wall. It moves. It is never still.

The light comes from above. There is a surface somewhere — far above — and the day is in it, and the day falls down through the water in pale columns that shift and bend with the surface chop. I can't see the surface itself; I can only see the light it sends. It reaches me filtered, softened, a green-blue daylight that turns everything the color of a copper bowl. The brightness is enough to see by. It is enough to work by. It is the light of a normal afternoon, slowed and cooled and put under a lens.

Around me, in every direction, the geometry is wrong for nature.

There is a wall. It rises past me on my left, going up into the bright zone and down past me into dimness. It is concrete — poured in panels, the seams between them visible as faint horizontal lines, the surface still carrying the ghost-pattern of the formwork boards it was cast against. The corners are sharp. The edges have not been rounded by weather because there has not been weather in the usual sense — only water, only this. Where a piece of facing has broken away, the steel reinforcement is visible, bright and rust-orange at the cut, fresh. This wall was built. It was built recently. It is large enough that I cannot see its end in any direction; it just goes.

To my right, a hundred meters away through clear water, another wall. Parallel. Between them is a channel of open water, and in that channel the current runs fast — pushed, I think, by some pump somewhere I cannot see, because the water here has direction and force. I drift in it. I let it carry me. It hums against the steel of my suit.

I am in a yard.

The yard is vast. The walls of the yard are the size of city blocks, and they are spaced the size of city blocks apart, and there are dozens of them I can see, and dozens more I can sense in the haze beyond. This is not a metaphor. I have stood on a deck — I have been to the upper works, I have seen from above — and the layout from above is a grid. A grid drawn with a very large ruler, on a very large sheet. The grid extends until the haze eats it. The haze is not fog. It is just distance. The scale is such that I lose sight of walls not because they end but because the water between us becomes too thick to see through.

The space between the walls is mostly empty.

This is what strikes me most, when I come down here fresh from a shift and my eyes are still adjusting: how much nothing there is. The structures are enormous and they are spaced far apart, and the water between them is the dominant volume. The water is the medium. The structures are obstacles in it. I move through the water the way a bird moves through the air between the columns of a ruined temple. The columns are the punctuation. The air is the sentence.

I can see, sometimes, that I am not alone in the water.

Fish. Schools of them, dark and silver, turning as one body past a distant wall. They have made their home in the structure — in the nooks where pipes meet plates, in the gaps between beams, in the lee of any obstacle that breaks the current and offers still water. They are abundant. I see them everywhere. They do not fear me, exactly, but they keep their distance; they part around me and re-form behind me, the way a stream parts around a stone.

Larger things move at the edge of sight. I have seen, twice, a shape pass slowly behind a far wall — long, dark, deliberate. I could not tell you what it was. It was not a fish. It had the unhurried pace of something that knows it has no enemies here.

There is growth on the surfaces, but it is sparse. A fuzz of something green-brown on the concrete where the light reaches, hair-fine, waving in the current. On the steel, especially the steel down-current from the warm plumes, a different growth — pale, branching, more like a sponge or a soft coral than any plant I would recognize. It does not cover. It colonizes. It begins. It is the first chapter of biology on a substrate that has only just been put down.

The sound is constant and it is loud.

It is the sound of the place working.

The pumps are the bass note — a deep rhythmic thrum I feel in the cage of my ribs before I hear it through my ears. They run somewhere I cannot see, always running, probably never having stopped. They push water through the structure, water through the pipes, water through the channels between the walls. They are the heart of this place.

Above that, the higher whine of turbines. Generators somewhere. The electrical plant of the yard, still producing, still humming, the sound carrying through the steel frame the way a tuning fork's note carries through a table. I can localize it by ear. I know which direction the plant is in by the way the sound sits in that side of my head.

And then the water itself — the water's own sound, the constant sibilance of flow past edges, over weirs, through grates. It is a hiss and a gurgle and sometimes a clatter where a loose piece of hardware has been left to rattle in the current, somewhere.

I hear my own breath in my regulator, of course. I hear the bubbles I make. They rise past me in silver ropes, climbing toward the surface I cannot see, and I watch them go.

Some of the lights are still working.

I see them as clusters of pale yellow cones cast into the water from fixtures mounted along the walls — industrial lamps in their waterproof housings, still lit, still sending their beams into the volume. The beams do not travel far in this medium; they fade and diffuse within a few dozen meters, so that each lamp illuminates a small bubble of yellow around itself, and beyond that bubble the green-blue daylight resumes. The lamps mark the working points. The places where someone is meant to be doing something. The intersections of the grid. I know where I am by which lamp-cone I am nearest.

The pipes are everywhere, once I learn to see them. Running between walls. Crossing channels on trusses. Climbing the surfaces in bundled groups, sheathed in waterproofed insulation, marked with stenciled codes I cannot read at this distance. They carry things — fluids, gases, I do not know what. They go somewhere. They come from somewhere. They are part of the working.

The cables are the same. Strung along the walls, fixed at intervals with brackets, dropping down into the water where they must and rising back up. They are the nerves of the structure, carrying signal and power to places I will probably never go.

It is active. I can see it, even in stillness. Sediment drifts in the current in long veils. Warm water plumes — I have learned to see them by the way they bend the light, the way they shimmer, the slight haze that marks the boundary between cool ambient and machine-heated discharge — rise from grates set in the lower walls and climb, slowly, toward the upper works. The fish follow the plumes. Something is being processed somewhere, and the by-product is heat, and the heat makes its own weather inside the larger weather of the yard.

I am in the middle of the volume. Below the upper works. Above the foundations. The middle is where the current runs and the fish school and the machines hum and the light comes down filtered green and the walls go off in every direction until the haze eats them.

It is a place. It is the place. It is vast and it is empty and it is loud and it is working and I am in it and the water is all around me and the water is the world.

I do not need to go up today. The work is here.
