# The Bottled Garden

You come down through a notch in the rim and the basin opens beneath you like a held breath.

It is small enough to read in a single glance: a sunken oval perhaps three bowshots across, ringed on every side by walls of fresh basalt that rise almost vertically and cut the sky into irregular, leaning panels. The walls are young — sharp-edged, barely weathered, the columnar joints still showing their hexagonal outlines as cleanly as if they had been cut yesterday. Where great slabs have fallen inward from the rim, they lie where they landed, still balanced on their broken corners, not yet having learned how to fall down properly. The rock is the colour of a thundercloud, almost blue in shadow, almost white where the morning light strikes it.

The air is thin and dry. Your lips crack within an hour. There is no standing water anywhere — no spring, no pool, no riverbed, no dew that lasts past dawn. Rain, if it has ever visited, has forgotten the way. And yet the place is not dead. The basin is a garden so dense that the floor has nearly disappeared beneath it.

---

The water is in the air.

In the last hour before sunrise the warm stone of the caldera exhales its stored heat into the cold upper air, and fog forms — not from any sky-water but from the rock itself breathing. The fog fills the basin to about the height of a tall man. It moves in slow ribbons. It condenses on every surface: the basalt, the leaves, the fur of the sleeping creatures. By mid-morning it has lifted back into the upper air and the basin is dry again, but everything that lives here has learned to drink from that briefest of wells. Some plants hold the moisture in swollen trunks. Some hold it in the cup of their leaves. Some hold it in their own skins.

The dominant plants are the columns.

They are not trees exactly, though they behave like trees. They are succulents — pale, dusty, grey-green — that have rooted in the parallel cracks of the basalt floor. The cracks run roughly north-south because the lava cooled that way; every plant in the basin had the same cracks to choose from, and so every plant grew up following the same lines, and the result is a colonnade. A forest of grey-green pillars, each one roughly the girth of a man's torso and twice his height, standing in rows so regular they look as if someone had planted them. They were never planted. They were only all reading the same book.

Between the columns the light is dim and the air is always slightly wet. Here grow the second layer: bulbous succulents wedged into crevices, things like swollen fingers; hanging ropes of pale green that fall from pillar to pillar and catch the passing fog in the fine hairs along their length; and a carpet that is not moss — it is too thick, too fleshy, too the colour of tarnished copper — that spreads across the basalt in a low unbroken mat and gives under your weight with a sound like a sigh.

Above the columns, where the walls of the caldera are younger and unbroken by cracks, the third layer begins. These are the mats. They look like coral — pinkish, ridged, branching, soft to the touch — and they cling to the basalt the way coral clings to a reef. Some of them are the size of small rooms. They feed directly on the fog, opening their surfaces to it like mouths. Where they grow thickest they form overhangs, ledges, half-caves, and inside these shelters the air is permanently damp and small pale flowers appear, briefly, in the wet season.

It is the mats, more than anything, that make the basin look like a city. They are the upper terraces, the balconies, the hung gardens. Looking down from the rim you can almost see streets between them.

---

The fauna is everywhere. You simply do not notice it at first.

Stand still in the colonnade for ten minutes and the place comes alive around you — but slowly, in a register that does not call attention to itself. A lichen-moth settles on the basalt three paces away, its wings patterned in grey and ochre so precisely that it vanishes the moment it closes them. It does not move again. A second one is on the trunk at your shoulder, motionless, watching you with what might be curiosity or might be nothing at all. Up in the upper mats there are shapes — heavier shapes — that hang from the underside of the coral. They are sloths, or the local equivalent: thick-bodied, furred in a pale moss-green that matches the columns, with long limbs and slow hands. They have been watching you since you arrived. They have not moved at all. One of them, you realise, has been sleeping for the entire hour you have been here, and you only just noticed it because its breathing lifted a single leaf.

On the floor there are slugs — long, slow, the colour of wet clay — that graze the copper carpet and leave silver trails across it. They move at a speed you can barely perceive. There are beetles, dark and round, that roll small balls of shed fur and leaf-fragments from one crevice to another with a great slow industry. There are worms in the soil under the moss that you never see but whose presence is recorded in the soft giving of the ground beneath your boots. Under every stone, in every crack, in the dark between the columns, there is something breathing.

The basin is loud with life. You simply cannot hear it.

---

The urban quality of the place is not made by anyone. It is made by many lives, kept in the same small box, for a very long time.

There are paths. They run between the columns where the basalt is exposed and worn smooth — polished to a dull gleam by the passage of small bodies, generation after generation. The paths follow the lines of least resistance, the narrowest gaps, the easiest climbs. They form a network. They have junctions. At the junctions, where several paths meet, the basalt is hollowed into a shallow basin by long use, and in these basins you find the cairns — small piles of droppings, of shed moss-fibre, of cast skins, of polished seeds — that mark the meeting-places of the basin's slow citizens. The cairns are old. Some of them are older than the moss that grows around them.

There are also clearings. Around the base of certain columns the moss has been kept short and the basalt swept bare, as if by long habit, and you understand after a while that these are resting sites. A particular creature returns to its particular column each evening. A path leads to it. A cairn marks the door. The architecture is biological, but the settlement is unmistakable.

---

The light here has the quality of being filtered twice.

In the early morning, when the basin is still full of fog, the light is gold and diffuse, and the columns stand in it like the pillars of a drowned cathedral. As the fog lifts, the light sharpens, and you can see that each column casts a long shadow across the floor, and the shadows interweave so densely that the floor becomes a dark lace. By noon the sun has climbed above the rim and pours straight down into the basin, and the columns cast no shadow at all, and the moss turns the colour of old brass. In the afternoon the light comes in from the west at a low angle and finds the upper mats, and the pink coral-growths glow as if lit from within, and the basin becomes a warm rose-coloured room.

At night the place is dark but not entirely dark.

There is a faint steady glow from somewhere in the deepest crevices — from fungi, perhaps, or from the pale flowers of the upper mats, or from the lichen-moths themselves when they open their wings in the dark. It is not enough to see by. It is enough to see *with*. The colonnade becomes visible as a forest of pale vertical lines, and the glow moves in it slowly as the moths shift, and the whole basin has the quality of a long-held chord: silent, but full of a sound you cannot quite hear.

---

The place changes. But it changes the way a stone changes — over spans you cannot watch.

The walls of the caldera erode at a rate you could measure in millimetres per century. A flake falls now and again from the rim, and the creatures scatter, and the moss creeps back over the new fragment, and the slow business of the basin resumes. The columns grow. If you came back in a hundred years you would find them a hand's breadth taller. The mats thicken. The paths wear deeper. The cairns grow. A creature is born, lives its long slow life, dies, and its bones are taken up by the moss and the worms and returned to the basalt. The fog forms and lifts, forms and lifts, and the basin breathes.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here has ever been in a hurry. The patience of the place is the patience of a system in balance — every drop of fog-water consumed, every mineral recycled, every creature fed, every breath answered by another breath. You stand among the columns and feel yourself to be the loudest thing for a hundred leagues, and then you also feel yourself to be the smallest, and then the distinction stops mattering.

The basalt smells like rain when you break it, even when there has been no rain for years.

You could live here a long time and never hear a sound louder than the settling of the rock.
