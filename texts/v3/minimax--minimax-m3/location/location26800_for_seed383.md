# The Settlement of Yawning Floors

You are somewhere beneath, but not far beneath. The ceiling is high enough that the eye cannot reach it without assistance — a stratified thing, each layer a slightly different shade of ochre and ash-gray, hand-finished where the builders' ancestors or predecessors once made a fist with their arms overhead and chiseled the rough edges flat. That was a long time ago. Now the chisel-marks have been softened by condensation and time into something that reads as a fold rather than a wound. Water has found its way through the layers and across the underside of the stone, leaving a thin skin of mineral crust that catches the light and gives back a low amber glow.

The light itself is the first thing that anyone arriving notices, because it is wrong for an underground place, and you are underground. There is no obvious source. It comes from everything: from the mineral skin on the ceiling, from the patches of pale growth on the lower walls (not plants, not fungus, not anything with a name; just a film, like dust that has learned to glow), from the standing water that gathers in shallow basins along the worn pathways and reflects upward. The whole place is suffused with it, evenly, almost without shadows. It is not sunlight. It has a faintly bruised quality, like the underside of a storm.

The temperature sits at a level the body does not have to think about. Cool enough that breath would be visible if the air were still, but the air is never quite still.

---

## The Stone

Everything here is stone. The floor is stone, packed down and then packed down again, the upper strata having been walked away by generations of feet into a gentle concavity that follows the old streets. If you crouch and put your palm to it you can feel the seams of older paving beneath the newer — three, four, perhaps five layers of construction, each one slightly differently oriented, each one using slightly different stones. The builders did not demolish. They re-floored. They built on top. They built on top again. Now the floors are nearly level with the original doorways, and the doors themselves are mostly missing their lintels, so that what was once a passage into a chamber is now a slot, a horizontal mouth at calf-height, breathing the same cool air as everything else.

Walls are made of fitted stone, mostly, but there are sections of dressed stone so old they have almost reverted to geology — the corners rounded, the mortar gone, the individual blocks looking like teeth in an aged jaw. Erosion has had the longest possible time to work here. Whatever catastrophe emptied this place, or whatever slow abandonment let it empty, it happened long enough ago that the process of return is nearly complete. The settlement is becoming the cave it was cut from.

In some places this has already happened. A wing on the eastern edge — that is, the edge the old maps call eastern, though no one is quite sure which way is east anymore — has been entirely reclaimed by the rock. There is nothing to see there now except the long curve of an undisturbed ceiling, and a floor that rises gradually to meet it. The last built wall stands there like a single tooth: a fragment, listing slightly, its stones loose but not fallen, waiting for a nudge that will not come for a thousand more seasons.

In other places, the built form is the dominant thing. The central passage is broad — wide enough for six abreast, perhaps seven — and its walls are still standing at full height, lined with the mouths of chambers, some open, some plugged with rubble, some half-walled-over in mid-effort by builders who are no longer here. The architecture is all of a kind: small squared blocks of local stone, hand-shaped, fitted without mortar or with a lime mortar that has since returned to the lime it came from. No metal fittings. The hinges of any doors that once existed were wooden, and the wood has gone. Some chambers still preserve the iron-banded wooden chests that once held the residents' goods; the bands are now the only thing holding the chests together, and the chests are now the only thing holding the bands in their original shape. Inside them is dust.

## The Damp

The water here is a presence, not a feature. It does not fall. It does not run in any obvious channel. It emerges from the stone, condenses on surfaces, and travels the slow path of least resistance downward. The floors are never dry. They are not puddled, either — except in the basins, which are deliberate, knee-shaped hollows worn into the pavement at irregular intervals, filled to a finger's depth with water so clear you cannot quite believe it is water until something moves through it and it dimples. It tastes of nothing. It smells of nothing. It is not cold, exactly, but it is the temperature of being held in the hand for a long time.

Stalactites are scarce; the ceiling sheds its moisture in sheets rather than drops, and over the centuries this has built up a sort of inverted landscape on the upper stone — draperies of calcite, in some places thin enough to see through, in others thick enough to ring if tapped. Where they have fallen they lie on the floor in brittle scrolls, walked around by the few inhabitants who still bother to walk.

The damp makes the light do strange things. The amber glow from the walls and ceiling is doubled, tripled, scattered back from every wet surface. The place is twice as bright as it has any right to be. It is bright enough that visitors from elsewhere blink and shade their eyes for the first few minutes, as though they have stepped into a noon that is also somehow evening.

## The Inhabitants

The fauna is the second thing anyone arriving notices, because there are so many of them and they are so quiet.

They are small. Thumb-length, most of them. Long-bodied, pale, with too many legs to count without looking directly, and they move in lanes along the seams between the stones. There are lanes everywhere. The floors have been worn to a polish in the places they most travel — not by their feet alone, but by the countless bodies passing over the same paths since time immemorial. The lanes are not visible unless you kneel and look sideways along the floor, when they resolve as a faint variation in the gloss.

They do not make a sound. Or if they do, it is below the threshold of perception; a pressure rather than a noise. Step into the central passage on a still morning (though there is no morning here; the place does not have mornings) and you will feel them before you see them — a low continuous hum in the soles of the feet, almost like tinnitus.

They build. This is the strange part. They build in the spaces the old residents left behind. They construct tiny structures against the base of every wall — miniature arches, miniature staircases, miniature chambers with miniature lintels that mimic, in a key a hundredth the size, the architecture of the people who built the place. The proportions are wrong, but only just. The joints are impossibly fine. They use a paste of mineral dust and saliva that sets into something resembling the original lime mortar, and the structures persist for decades after the builders of them have died, hardening slowly into stone in their own right. They are everywhere. There is hardly a square foot of wall that does not have at least one of these tiny dwellings tucked against its foot.

They do not bother anyone. They scatter when a foot comes down, and then they return to their lanes. They have been here longer than the memory of the place.

---

## The Activity

The place feels busy without feeling loud. That is the trick of it.

Walk the central passage for any distance and you will see the workings of something, though not always what you expect. A section of pavement where the uppermost layer has been pried up by some agency — an exploratory excavation, almost too neat to be random — and the slabs are stacked carefully to one side, exposing the layer below. A wall that has been shored up with a wedge of newer stone, the wedge itself scored with the tiny lanes of the inhabitants. A basin that has been deliberately deepened, the spoil heaped in a low ring around it. A door-slot that has been partially blocked with fitted stones, then unblocked, then blocked again, each attempt using slightly different masonry. None of this is being done by anything you can see. It is being done, or has been done, or is in the slow process of being done, by the aggregate action of the small pale fauna over their slow and patient lifetimes.

It is a city being maintained by something other than its original inhabitants, and the maintenance is competent but very, very slow. A wall is repaired across a century. A floor is re-laid over several generations. The settlement is not abandoned. It is being kept, in the only way left available to it, by the things that remained when their makers did not.

This is the turbulence. It is not the turbulence of weather or violence. It is the turbulence of process — of tiny forces acting continuously on a vast and aging structure, over a span of time so long that the result of any single day's activity is imperceptible, but the result of a thousand days is a moved stone, and the result of a million days is a different place. If you sat on one of the worn steps in the central passage and watched a single section of wall for a whole afternoon, you would not see anything happen. If you came back in fifty years, the section of wall would have changed.

## The Light, More Closely

Spend long enough here and the light begins to behave oddly.

It does not flicker. It does not pulse. But it shifts, slowly, over the course of hours, in a way that has no obvious cause. The amber glow from the mineral crust grows warmer for a while, then cooler, then warmer again. The pale films on the walls brighten, dim, brighten. The standing water in the basins throws back different colors at different times — sometimes the same amber, sometimes a faint green, sometimes something that reads as violet if you stare at it too long. There is no day-night cycle here; there is instead this slow drift of color, as though the place is breathing at a tempo much slower than a human breath.

It is not impossible that this is the cause of the strange sense the place gives, the off-key feeling, the wrongness that is not quite wrongness. The place is too bright for underground. The light is too even. The colors are too saturated. The eyes do not quite trust it. You begin to feel that you are being seen by something, or that the walls are breathing, or that the ceiling is lower than it was a moment ago, or higher. None of these things is true. None of them is false, either, in the way that they would be false in a sun-lit street. The light makes a kind of question of every surface, and the surfaces do not always give the same answer.

---

## The Silence

The quiet is not empty.

It is full of small things that do not register as sound. The hum of the fauna in the floor. The faint tick of mineral crust growing at its imperceptible pace. The occasional, distant, unmoored *tok* of a small stone shifting in a wall a passage or two away. The slow, low, almost subliminal complaint of the uppermost layer of pavement under the weight of the entire settlement pressing down on it. The wet sound of a single drop of condensation letting go from the ceiling, which is so quiet it is more felt than heard.

If you speak here, your voice goes out into the bright air and is not echoed. The chambers are not shaped to return sound. The walls absorb it, the floor drinks it, the inhabitants in their lanes do not startle at it. Your voice reaches the far end of the central passage and dies there, politely. This is the strangest thing about the place for anyone accustomed to the outside. A shout is not returned. A clap is answered only by the clap. The settlement does not want your voice, and after a little while you do not want to give it to them.

## What It Is Like To Be There

You arrive at the mouth of one of the broader passages — the original entrance, perhaps, now half-collapsed and sloping gently down into the lit interior. Your eyes adjust, which here means they do not have to adjust. You are already in the light. You see the broad passage stretching ahead, lined with door-slots, layered with pavement, walled in fitted stone. You see the basins of still water, each one throwing back its ambiguous color. You see the tiny structures at the foot of every wall, built and being built. You see the inhabitants moving in their lanes, dozens of them in every glance, hundreds in every step.

You step inside.

The air is cool and damp and tastes of stone and of nothing else. The floor yields slightly under your weight, the uppermost layer of paving taking your tread with the patient give of something that has been trodden for ages. Your feet feel the hum. The light falls on you from every direction at once, warm and bruised. The passage stretches ahead, broad, quiet, lined with the small squares of door-slots and the pale scrolls of fallen drapery.

Somewhere far ahead, a stone shifts.

Somewhere far behind, the entrance closes a little further, by a fraction.

You are inside. The settlement is keeping itself. The light is bright. The quiet is full. The stone remembers, just barely, what was built here. The small things are doing their long work.
