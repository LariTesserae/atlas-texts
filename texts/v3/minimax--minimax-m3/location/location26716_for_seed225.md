# The Stone Cup

You come upon it gradually. The land has been tilting for some time, a slow descent through scrub and pale grass, and then the ground simply stops — not at a cliff's edge but at a rim, curved, a low lip of worn limestone that you can stand on with your heels hanging over nothing. Below you, the earth opens into a bowl.

The bowl is not large. You could, if you stood at one side and shouted, hear your own voice reach the other before the echo died — perhaps two hundred paces across, perhaps a hundred and fifty. The walls are smooth in the way that only very long water can make stone smooth, and they curve inward as they descend, a shape that feels familiar in some part of you before you can name it: a hand cupped, a vessel, the inside of a cup turned up. The floor is flat and pale, scattered with darker patches where water has stood or still stands now in thin, still tongues.

The light is wrong for a place underground, and that is the first thing you have to make your peace with. The sky is right there — a perfect circle of it, directly above, so blue it looks lacquered. The sun must be high, because the light comes almost straight down, a thick white column that fills the bowl and makes the stone walls glow faintly cream and bone. The shadows are short and sharp and lie flat against the floor. Squinting up at the rim where you stand, you can see grasses silhouetted against the brightness, every blade distinct. Down in the bowl, you are not in shade at all. You are simply in a deep room that happens to have no ceiling.

It is warm. Not hot — warm, the way a closed room is warm in late afternoon, the way stone is warm when the day has been long. The air moves very little. What little there is seems to come from above, slipping down the curved walls in slow invisible sheets. Sometimes you feel a different kind of movement: a softer, damper exhalation from somewhere lower in the rock, as if the bowl itself is breathing.

The walls are damp. You can see it in the way the light clings to them, in the way the limestone darkens in long vertical streaks that you trace with your eye and find no source for. But you listen, and after a while, you hear it — a drip, somewhere out of sight, with the patient rhythm of a thing that has been happening longer than anything you can think of. Drip. Drip. The interval is irregular in a way that feels almost intentional, almost counted. Where the water lands, the stone is black, and small green things have taken hold there — a moss, a fern no bigger than a finger, a single trailing vine that has somehow climbed twenty feet from a crack in the floor and now hangs in the air with no visible purpose.

The plants are few. They are stubborn. They grow only where the water has marked a path, in the dark vertical stains on the walls, in the shallow pools that never quite dry. The rest of the stone is bare — pale, faintly veined, warm to the touch where the sun has been on it and cool in the shadow of the curve. The vegetation has the look of a thing that arrived one seed at a time over many seasons and will not be hurried further.

The animals are another matter.

There are birds, first. You notice them because they are loud in a quiet place, or at least conspicuous — pale things, smaller than a fist, with quick wings and a song that goes up and up and then stops as if the note has been cut with scissors. They nest in holes you cannot see, in the upper walls where the curve begins to close. They fly across the column of light and for a moment they are silhouettes, and then they are gone again into the stone. Their droppings whiten the rim and the upper ledges in long pale smears.

Lower, on the warm flat floor, there are lizards. They are the color of the stone — gray, faintly pink, faintly gold — and they are so still that you do not see them until you have been standing a long time. Then, as your eyes adjust to the slow, you begin to see them everywhere: a head lifted, a tail curved, a small throat pulsing. They are watching you with the same patience the water has. They are not afraid. They have, perhaps, never had reason to be.

In the damp patches there are other things. Insects with long bodies that skate on the surface of the shallow pools without breaking it. A pale, soft creature, slug-like, that leaves a silver trail across the stone and then withdraws into a crack. Something — you cannot quite see it — that makes a small dry rustle among the ferns and then is silent.

And once, far above, something wide and dark passes across the circle of sky. You do not get a good look. It could be a bird of prey. It could be a leaf. It could be the shadow of a cloud. It crosses and is gone, and the bowl is bright again.

The sound, apart from the birds, is mostly absence. The drip. A faint wind at the rim, which sometimes brings the smell of dry grass and warm earth down into the cup. The very soft hiss of water running in a channel too small to see, somewhere along one wall, that you only notice when you stop trying to hear it. When a bird sings, the song hangs in the curved air for a long time after the bird has flown. When a stone falls — and small stones fall here, sometimes, from the rim, sometimes pushed by a small gray foot — the sound it makes on the floor is a clear bright click that travels to every wall and returns to you from several directions at once, and you can no longer tell where the stone is, only where it was.

You stand there a long time. The light does not seem to move, but it must; the shadows on the floor shift by inches over the hours, and the column of brightness drifts slowly from one side of the bowl to the other. The air stays warm. The drips keep their slow count. The birds fly their circuits. A lizard has not moved in the time you have been watching it, and you understand, watching it, that you are the only thing here in a hurry.

There is no path down. There is no ladder, no worn steps, no rope. The rim is smooth, and the bowl is for the things that have always been in it, or for the things that fall.

It is not a place that was made. It is a place that is being made, still, very slowly, by the patient argument between water and stone that began before you were born and will not be finished soon. It has been here longer than anything human. It is, in its way, the most ordinary place in the world — water found a weakness in the rock, the rock gave way a little, and over a great long time the giving became this. A cup. A room. A held breath of air, open to the sky, full of slow light and the quiet industry of small things that have made a home of the inside of a wound.

You stand at the rim a while longer. A bird crosses the light. The drip counts. The lizards watch. The warm stone breathes, faintly, against your heels.
