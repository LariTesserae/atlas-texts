# The Hollow Reach

There is a place beneath the old stone where the world has been settling into itself for so long that everything has forgotten how to be dramatic. The ceiling hangs far overhead — high enough that the dim light never quite decides what shape it is, only that it is there, gathered in slow pools along the undersides of things. The floor has been worn patient and smooth over an age no one counts anymore. Gentle rises, shallow valleys, the long exhaling curve of a basin that was once, perhaps, something more vertical and is now simply itself. You could walk for hours and never meet a sharp edge. The stone remembers nothing of impact. Everything that could fall has already fallen, and what is left is what remains.

The light is a kind of green-grey, the color of things that glow without conviction. It comes from the ceiling mostly — from growths that cling there in sheets and fronds, breathing out a low phosphorescence that is not quite enough to see by but is never quite not there. There are patches where it gathers warmer, where a certain kind of clustered growth pulses faintly in slow rhythm with something below it, and the rock beneath these patches has been stained in long amber trails by centuries of patient dripping of light onto mineral. The walls are not really walls. They are distances — long curves away into dimness where the eye gives up before the geography does. Somewhere very far off in one direction the ceiling lowers and the light dims further and the sound changes pitch and that is one of the edges. The other edges are similarly disposed. The Reach is bounded, but only in the way a held breath is bounded.

The vegetation is not what you would call green. There is green in it, but the green is buried under silver, under grey, under a kind of dark plum color that has no name in any surface language. It grows thick where it grows at all — and it grows thickly, in dense carpets and layered curtains and slow upright columns that may once have been trees and are now something between tree and mineral. The columns are the oldest things. They rise from the floor in clusters, their surfaces crusted with growth upon growth upon growth, each layer a different color of dim glow, and they do not move but they are alive in the way that patient things are alive. They breathe. You can see it if you stand still long enough — the faintest expansion and contraction along their trunks, so slow it is almost geological, so slow that the dust of spores settles on their shoulders between breaths.

Beneath the columns, between them, spreading across the open floor where the columns thin out, is a continuous low growth. It looks like moss from a distance. It is not moss. It is a thousand kinds of thing, layered, and each layer drinks from the air rather than from any ground. The air down here is heavy. It is heavy with the breath of everything alive in it — exhaled and exhaled and rebreathed until the moisture is a saturated warm-cool blanket that hangs at chest height and never quite falls. The lushness of the Reach lives on this. There is no water here, no river, no pool, no falling rain — but there is breath, and the breath is wet enough, and the plants have learned to live on it. They spread across the stone in low humps and ridges, curling over themselves in slow spirals, sending up pale fruiting bodies that release their spores in a constant silent drift downward and sideways. The spores are everywhere. They are in the air you breathe. They are the dim light's medium. They catch the glow and make the air itself faintly visible as a thing, as a presence, as a slow current you can watch move through the columns when the larger creatures pass.

The creatures are why it is loud.

The sound of the Reach is not a sound you can step out of. It begins at the edge of hearing and never stops. There is the constant mid-range roar of the smaller flyers — not birds, nothing with feathers, but membrane-winged things the size of your hand that swarm in columns up toward the ceiling and back down again in continuous loops, their wings a dry clattering that, multiplied by the millions of them in any given column, becomes a sound like a hard rain heard from inside a drum. There is the deeper sound, beneath that, of the ground-walkers — six-legged and four-legged and things that have too many legs to count and do not seem to care — moving across the moss-carpet in a slow continuous migration that has no destination, only direction. Their feet, or their bellies, or whatever parts of them touch the stone, make a sound like thick cloth being pulled across wood. Continuous. Across the whole Reach at once. There is the high piping and trilling of the small ground-dwellers, the things that live in the moss and sing to each other from hump to hump in frequencies that overlap into a kind of shimmering static. There is, occasionally, the singular low call of something very large moving somewhere in the dimness, and when that call comes, the whole chorus pauses for a half-breath to let it through, and then resumes.

It is deafening. It is the loudest quiet you have ever been inside of, because none of it is sharp or alarming — it is the sound of a place being thoroughly alive, and the volume of it is the volume of the living, and there is no way to subtract yourself from it. You do not get used to it. You simply join it. After a while you stop trying to speak, because anything you say will be absorbed into the texture of the sound and lost, and you start instead to move with it, to let your walking find the rhythm of the ground-walkers' passage, to let your breathing find the rhythm of the columns' slow respiration.

The city is part of all of this, and you do not immediately see it as a city. It has been here so long that the line between settlement and terrain has softened into suggestion.

The buildings are mostly of compacted earth and old root. They are low, mostly — they rise from the moss-carpet like the moss itself has decided to take a more permanent shape. Walls a couple of hands thick, made of soil that has been pressed and re-pressed by generations of hands until it is harder than the stone beneath it but still warm, still faintly breathing. Roofs are layers of the same material, curved over in shallow domes, and on top of every roof the moss has come back and resumed its work, so that from a little distance the buildings look like larger humps in the carpet, like the moss has simply chosen to rise a little higher in these places. There are doorways — openings without doors, just shaped absences in the walls, framed by root-wood that has been forced to grow around the edges in smooth curves. The frames are alive. They grow tighter over decades. When a doorway gets too narrow, a new one is opened beside it, and the old one fills in with wall. Some of the older buildings are nearly solid now — just rounded mounds with a single small opening still being used. Some are completely sealed, the moss having reclaimed them entirely, and they are treated as ancestors. People sit on them.

The settlement is spread across the floor of the Reach in a loose scatter that follows no plan except the availability of space between the columns. The columns are respected. They are not cut down. They are not even leaned against. People build around them, and where a column rises through what would have been a doorway or a roof, the roof or the doorway accommodates the column and curves around it, and the column continues upward through the building into the dim air above, and the moss grows on the column all the way up through the ceiling of the room. The buildings and the columns are not separate things. They are one continuous fabric, root-tied beneath the surface in a network that no one has ever fully mapped, because no one has ever fully needed to.

There are pathways. They are not paved. They are simply the places where the moss has been walked down to bare earth, packed by traffic, worn to a kind of soft red-brown surface that is warmer underfoot than the moss around it. The pathways curve between the columns, between the buildings, sometimes disappearing entirely into a stretch of moss that everyone knows is a path anyway. At certain intersections the pathways widen into clearings — places where the moss has been deliberately kept down and the bare earth shows, and there are low rings of root-wood set into the ground where people sit. These are the gathering places. No one speaks loudly enough to be heard over the chorus of the Reach, so gatherings are not about speech. People come and sit and feel each other's presence through the ground, through the low vibration of bodies close together, and sometimes through touch — hands on hands, hands on shoulders, a kind of slow continuous contact that runs through a gathering like a current. Decisions are made this way, or rather they are felt into being, and when the feeling is complete, the gathering disperses.

There are no devices. There are no metal things. There is no glass. There is nothing that was not alive when it was made and is not returning to being alive now. The root-wood is cut with stone, and the stone is found, and the cutting is done slowly, and the cut root is set into the wall and resumes growing and eventually becomes indistinguishable from the wall around it. The tools are kept in the buildings. They are passed down. They are not precious, exactly, but they are known — each one has a name and a history of hands, and when a tool finally fails, when the stone edge wears through and the wooden handle is taken back by growth, its name is spoken once and then let go.

The dimness is not a problem. The Reach-dwellers do not see as surface beings see. They see more by vibration than by light — the sense of the ground beneath them, the sense of the air moving around them, the sense of the sound itself, which is not just heard but felt, pressed against the skin, entered into the chest. In the louder moments of the chorus, they navigate by the gaps in the sound, by the places where the roar parts enough to let a body pass. In the quieter moments — and there are quieter moments, between the large calls, when the whole chorus takes its half-breath pause — they see the dim shapes of the columns and the buildings as faint differences in the green-grey of the air, and that is enough. They have lived here so long that the dimness is not a restriction. It is a condition, like temperature, like pressure. It is simply what the air is.

The Reach is not empty between the buildings. The moss-carpet is full of the small ground-dwellers — the piping things, the things with too many legs, the low burrowing things that move beneath the moss in tunnels you can feel as faint tremors underfoot if you stand still. There are larger things too. There is a thing that lives in the columns — a slow dark shape that moves up and down the trunks in a continuous circuit, eating the growths, and its passage leaves a bright trail behind it that fades over hours. There is a thing that walks the open floor in long looping paths, low and broad, that the pathways sometimes follow without anyone knowing why. There are the membrane-winged things, of course, in their millions, their columns rising and falling through the dim air like rivers of clatter. And there are things that no one talks about, that live in the further reaches where the light is too dim even for the moss-glow, and that occasionally wander closer in the time of the large calls, and are seen only as moving absences in the dimness, and are felt only as a sudden drop in the temperature of the air around them.

Time in the Reach is slow. A person grows from small to grown across what might be twenty years of surface time, and an old person is someone whose growth-rings have thickened into ridges, and the ridges are counted, and the count is remembered. The moss advances across a clearing at a pace you can watch if you set a marker, and the advance is steady, and the marker is overgrown in a year and lost in a decade. The columns breathe their slow breath. The buildings settle into the earth by fractions of a finger-width each century. The pathways shift, slowly, as old routes fill in and new ones are worn. Nothing in the Reach is fast. Everything is in the middle of a long becoming, and the becoming is so gradual that it looks, from inside it, like staying the same.

The Reach-dwellers know they are at the bottom of something. Above them, far above, there is stone, and above the stone there is more stone, and above that there is a world they have never seen. They have no myths about it. They have no interest in it. The ceiling is just the ceiling, and the air is just the air, and the dim light is just the dim light, and the sound is just the sound of being here. They were always here. The buildings were always here, in some form. The columns were always here. The chorus was always here. It has been like this for so long that the question of how it began has folded into the place itself and become part of its texture, like a name spoken so often it has become the breath that speaks it.

When you stand in the Reach, after the first few minutes of trying to make your small surface-senses work, you stop trying. You let the sound take you. You let the dimness be the dimness. You let the slow breath of the columns become your breath. You sit on the warm earth at one of the clearings, and one of the dwellers comes and sits beside you, and a hand finds your hand, and the contact is not a greeting exactly but a kind of recognition that you are here, that you are also part of the chorus now, that the Reach has accepted another presence and will continue its long slow respiration around you as it always has, as it always will.

The light shifts, almost imperceptibly, through its slow greens and greys and faint ambers. The chorus swells, and pauses, and swells again. The moss breathes. Somewhere in the further dimness, something very large lets out a low call, and the whole Reach pauses, and the pause is a held breath, and then the breathing resumes.
