# The Hollow

It is a wound in the earth, roughly circular, the kind of feature that looks like it was made rather than grown. The rim is sharp — pale tuff and fresh basalt, the edges still clean, the stone still tasting of the dust it came from. Rain has barely had time to round the corners. A few scrubby roots have begun to grip the upper lip, but nothing has softened it yet. The hollow is small enough that if you stand at one edge and shout, the voice comes back from the other in under a second. Cathedral scale. A room the world forgot to roof.

The bottom of the hollow is flat, and that is also where the city is.

---

The walls go straight down. Ladders and stairs have been carved into them — narrow, switchbacking, barely wide enough for two. The lowest tier sits in permanent shade; the upper tiers catch a slanting wash of sun for an hour in the morning and an hour in the late afternoon, and that is the whole of the day. The rest is dim. The light that survives the descent is the color of weak tea, and by the time it reaches the floor it has gone olive, then grey, then nothing. People who live here learn to read the gradients of almost-dark.

Everything is wet. Steam rises from grates in the floor; condensation beads on every horizontal surface and slides down. The walls sweat. When it rains above, the downpour pours over the rim in sheets and the upper terraces become waterfalls for ten minutes, then the air goes back to its slow, soaked quiet. Pools collect in the low places — green-black, scummed with algae that gives off a pale, sour light. The reek of warm sulfur threads through everything. The heat is geological. It rises from somewhere deep and has nowhere to go, and the hollow has become a kind of slow cooker. Even in the small hours of the night the stones are warm to the touch.

---

The vegetation has taken the offer. Ferns push out of every crack in the wall and unfurl into the dim air. Moss runs in continuous sheets across the rock, across the pipes, across the unused walks. There are things that look like cabbages and things that look like lilies and things that look like nothing at all — strap-leaved, pale, ephemeral, flowering once and collapsing into a fragrant wet heap. A kind of tall stalk rises from the central platform, broad bladed, leaning toward the small bright slot of sky overhead with the patient idiot insistence of a plant that has never seen the sun. The vines are the worst. They drape across balconies, fill stairwells, soften the hard edges of the buildings until some of the structures are more leaf than wall.

The buildings are layered. They are layered in the way a city is layered when it has run out of land and started to build upon itself — platforms cantilevered out over the void, bridges lashed between, walkways sprouting from walkways. Many are old enough that the green has eaten them; many are new and raw, planks still smelling of sap, bolted to the stone without grace. They are packed. There is no margin. A person walking the upper terrace can reach out and touch the wall of the next structure without extending the arm. The air between is a working corridor, a pipe run, a clothesline, a place to set a child. Smoke comes from a dozen chimneys at once and finds no room to disperse; it hangs.

The infrastructure is industrial, but it is slow industrial. There is a condenser at the bottom of the hollow — a great squat cylinder of riveted iron, sweating, fed by a thousand small pipes that descend the walls — and it does its work without hurry. Somewhere a flywheel turns at a sedate pace. Somewhere a pump clucks. The sound of the place is the sound of a small steam engine idling for a long time: hiss, tick, hiss, the occasional deep complaint of a metal joint expanding in the heat. Nothing screams. Nothing demands. The machines here are patient, and so is the place that hosts them.

What they are for is hard to say. The pipes go up and they go down. The condenser exhales a thin plume of vapor that joins the wet air. The flywheel is attached to nothing one can see. The whole apparatus has the air of something that was built by people who no longer remember what it was for and have decided that stopping it would be worse than not knowing. It is kept going. It is warm. It is something to do.

---

Light comes from three sources. The first is the slot of sky, which is more suggestion than illumination. The second is the furnace glow from beneath the iron grating of the central platform — a deep red, the color of banked coals, occasionally lighter when a stoker opens a door. The third is the algae in the pools, which gives off a greenish luminescence that is enough to see a face by, if the face is close. The combined effect is a place that is never dark and never bright. It is the light of a heavy evening at the bottom of a well.

There are not many animals. A few frogs in the warmer pools, their calls a low burble that becomes part of the ambient noise. Insects — beetles that click against the walls, pale moths that blunder into the furnace light and stop existing. A lizard of some kind, three-toed, that hunts the moths and darts away from feet. Something that might be a bird nests at the rim, where there is sun, and never comes down. The settlement's residents are the largest moving things in the hollow, and they move slowly, drawing their hands along the wet walls as they go, knowing the stairs by feel.

---

The strangeness of the place is not in any single thing but in the way the things assemble. The walls are too vertical, as if they were cut. The stairs switch back at angles that do not quite match, as if drawn by different hands who never consulted. The buildings overhang in ways that seem to defy the load. The pipes run in straight lines and then stop in mid-air, their open ends weeping into troughs that lead nowhere useful. The proportions are off in a way the eye registers before the mind does — a doorway that is taller than it should be, a window that is somehow wide and thin at the same time, a balcony that extends further than the building below it could possibly support. The place feels like a sentence in a language one almost knows. It feels like a memory that has been slightly edited.

The sound is mostly quiet. The drips, the hiss, the tick, the burble of frogs, the slow tread of feet on wet stone, the occasional low murmur of voices in a room one is not in. The silence is not empty. It is occupied. It is the silence of a place that is listening, or of a place that has been listening for a long time and has begun to listen back.

---

Time moves here the way water moves down a wall — in any direction at once, and not quickly. The moss creeps across a pipe over a season. The buildings settle a thumb's width in a year. The iron rusts. The ferns uncurl. The condensers turn. A child is born in a room the size of a closet and grows up in the green half-light and learns to climb the ladders before she learns to read. The shapes of the hollow change, but they change the way a face changes over a long life — so that one cannot say when, exactly, it stopped being the thing it was.

It is hot. It is damp. It is dim. It is packed. It is strange. It is patient.

It is still there.
