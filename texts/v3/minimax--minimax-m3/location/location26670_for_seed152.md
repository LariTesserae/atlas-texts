# The Pocket at the Hollow's Heart

The land here is still arriving.

You can feel it when you stand on it — that slight give, not of softness but of unreadiness. The ground is dark. Wet-iron colored. It smells of minerals that haven't yet had time to oxidize into the patient brown of older earth. New ground, alluvial or volcanic or something in between, still breathing its own chemistry into the air. It has not decided what it is. It is becoming, slowly, beneath everything else.

## The Settlement

A handful of structures stand in this hollow. Not many. Spaced apart with the careful hesitation of things that have not yet learned whether they will last. Between them, the original ground continues — the wet clay, the low ferns, the small slow trickles of water finding their paths — as if the buildings have been set down inside the green rather than carved out of it.

The structures are made of things that were alive not long ago. Cane pressed into walls. Palm leaves stripped into thatch and layered in overlapping rows. Mud gathered by the handful and smoothed by palm against palm. No nails. No fired brick. No metal anywhere. The buildings look like something the forest itself might have woven — except for the will behind them. The will is the only industrial thing here, the only process that is not biological. And even it is patient.

Vines have already found the cane walls and are climbing them with slow purpose. Moss fills the gaps between thatch leaves. On three of the roofs, ferns have taken root in the accumulated dust and the occasional bird-dropping, and they nod in any breath of wind, dripping water from their curled tips all day into the puddles that gather in the clay below. The settlement is being absorbed into the larger green. Or the green is being absorbed into the settlement. The two processes are not opposed. They are the same process, happening from both directions.

## Water

Damp, but not soaked. The air holds moisture the way a held breath holds it — not committed to falling yet. Mornings are misty, and the mist never quite finishes leaving. Sometimes, in the warm afternoons, a brief rain passes through, and it passes through the roofs as if they were not there, and the clay below accepts it without complaint. The puddles form and persist. Within hours they have inhabitants — small frogs first, then insects hovering above the surface, then in a day or two the silver fish appear, no bigger than a finger, as if they came up through the wet ground itself, which is not impossible.

Each structure has its own small puddle-world at its base. Each puddle-world has its own weather, its own fauna, its own slow dramas. They are like rooms inside a room.

## Light

Dim, and not in the way of shadows. In the way of filtering. The original trees — the ones that were here before any hand pressed any cane — have grown up around and over the hollow, and their canopy cups the place like a hand. What sun reaches the ground reaches it through many layers. It arrives green, uncertain, slanting. Mornings are grey-pearl. Midday is a soft jade. Afternoons, when the angle is right, long warm shafts of gold slip through the gaps between leaves, and the settlement moves along those shafts the way any settlement moves along its light — because the light tells you where the openings are, where the path between the buildings is, where it is safe to walk.

## Temperature

Warm the way warm is meant to be. Not pressing. Not insistent. It sits. It lets you move. Sweat gathers at the small of the back and dries before it becomes uncomfortable. The kind of warmth that is almost company.

## Sound

Nearly silent. But the silence is inhabited. It is the silence of a place that has many small living things in it, none of which is loud.

The cane leaves, when they move, make a sound like pages turning in a book no one is reading. The drip from the fern tips is individual — you can hear each one. The frogs enter the puddles with a soft *plop* that is more felt than heard. Somewhere, at the edge of hearing, a bird is calling — a long, low note, like a flute heard through deep water, repeated at intervals that are not quite regular. Insects hum, but the hum is felt in the chest rather than caught by the ear. The clay settles. The cane walls creak very softly as the moisture in them shifts through the day.

There is no human sound. Or almost none. Sometimes, at the very edge, a voice, or a footfall on wet ground. But the place does not need it. The place is already full of small voices that are saying what needs saying.

## Fauna

Abundant, but small-scale. The world here is the size of a fingertip.

Beetles the color of old bronze move along the cane stems in patient lines. Lizards no longer than a hand bask on the mud walls and flick their tongues at passing gnats. Frogs, in three or four varieties, call from the puddles in voices that fit inside a single breath. Birds no bigger than a fist move through the canopy above — you see them as movement more often than as shape, leaves that are not leaves.

In the moss at the base of every wall, things move that you cannot see but know are there because the moss shifts in small waves. Shrews. Something like a squirrel but with a longer face and no tail, that runs along the cane and pauses to look down at you with both hands held to its chest. Fish, briefly, in the puddles, glinting silver, gone. Insects hatching and dying in their thousands, the air for a moment above any puddle full of a slow rising and falling of small wings.

Nothing large comes here. Or rarely. This is not a place for large things. It is a place for things that can fit inside a hand.

## Between the Structures

Walking from one building to another is not walking on a path. It is walking through the larger green, which has decided, very gently, to let you pass. The vegetation closes behind you but does not press. The puddles are skirting around, not deep enough to stop you. The clay holds your footprint and the next rain will soften it, and the rain after that will erase it, and the small creatures will use the depression for a home for a few days until the next footprint comes through.

There are no marked routes. There are habitual ones. The cane is bent at certain heights where hands have passed. The moss on certain stones is thinner. The fish in certain puddles are tamer.

## The Whole Thing

The place feels like a single breathing organism. The vegetation is taking the settlement. The fauna is using it. The clay is settling under every rain. The cane is slowly darkening as it seasons. The mud walls are slowly being colonized by lichens at their bases and small ferns along their tops. In fifty years it will be partly ruin. In a hundred years it will be mostly ruin. In two hundred it will be difficult to tell that anything was ever here at all, except that the cane will have grown back into itself, and the fish will live in deeper pools, and the bird will still call from the canopy in its long, underwater note.

Right now, though, it is what it is. A pocket. A hollow within a hollow. A small arriving place inside a larger one that has already arrived. Warm and damp and dim and full of small things and almost silent and still becoming itself.

The ground beneath your feet has not yet decided what it is. That is the whole feeling of the place: that nothing here has quite committed to being what it will become. The ferns are deciding whether to take the roof. The vines are deciding whether to take the wall. The clay is deciding whether to become soil. The fish are deciding whether the puddle is permanent enough to spawn in.

You are standing in the middle of a place that is still asking itself questions.

The answer, when it comes, will be green.
