# Coalreach Hollow

The mountains here are no longer mountains. They remember being mountains — their flanks hold the muscle-shapes of peaks that took a hundred million years to erode down — but what rises now is only the memory of high places: a long swell of bald grey-green shoulders, broad as counties, lifting into a sky that has forgotten the sun. Coalreach sits in the saddle between two of these ancient shoulders, in a saddle that is, itself, older than the coal.

The elevation will kill you if you walk too fast. The air is thin and damp and tastes, if you can taste air, of iron and peat. At four thousand meters the clouds do not pass overhead — they *rest*. They sit in the saddle like wet felt, sometimes rising to the height of the chimneys, sometimes dropping to drown the rooftops entirely. On the rare afternoon when a wind comes down off the high passes, the cloud-lace tears for an hour and the light arrives the color of weak tea, and the whole valley below — which is no valley but a slow bowl of worn-down ridges falling away into further grey — becomes briefly visible, and then the cloud closes again, and you have to trust that the world is still down there.

---

The town is built along a seam. Not the coal seam — though there was coal, once, and there are still seams — but along the long wet crack where the saddle's two shoulders meet, where the water gathers before it finds its way to one of the slow brown rivers that wind through the highlands below. On one side of the seam: the *upper works*, the stamping floors, the great brick sheds with their windows blown out or boarded up. On the other: the terraced housing, stone below and slate above, built in the long century when the mines were rich and the company built its own village around its workers the way a spider builds a nursery around a fly. The terraces are three and four stories tall, with shared stone stairs down the fronts, and they climb the shoulder of the saddle in long rows, and they are older than the nation that once owned them and older than the nation that owns them now.

The brick is green. The stone is green. The slate is green. Everything here is colonized by something soft and small — mosses, lichens, the creeping spleenworts that find a hold in the mortar between the engineering bricks of the mill floors. A lushness has crept over the entire place like a sleeping cat. The yards between the housing blocks are not yards but wet gardens of fern and wood-sorrel and the rank tufts of something called *old man's beard* that isn't old men and isn't beards but is called so because it hangs pale and grey-green off every railing and every drainpipe. In summer — which is a relative term up here; there is no real summer, only a six-week softening — the terraces are loud with the dripping of water from one roof to the next, down through the moss, and the moss is loud with its own quiet growth, and the air smells of rot and wet stone and the faintly sweet exhalation of fermentation vats that are still, somehow, in use.

---

The industry is a different industry now. The coal is mostly gone, or mostly not worth the getting, and what is left of the seam is worked by a single crew of men — older, fewer, quieter — who go down in the mornings and come up in the evenings and do not speak much. The great stamping sheds have been taken over, in pieces, by other things: a wool-scouring works that takes fleece from the high pastures below; a foundry that casts the small bronze fittings that hold the old machinery together; a paper mill, very small, that makes the heavy stock used for ledgers and for the *Coalreach Almanac*, the local paper, which has been printed here since 1873 and is now, like the town, mostly a record of itself. The chimneys are mostly shorter than they used to be — bricked in, capped with rusted cowls — but two still stand, both of them leaning slightly, and from one of them, in winter, a thin smoke comes that is more steam than smoke, the visible breath of the boilers that run the foundry.

The engines are old. The town is the kind of place where a thing, once installed, is repaired rather than replaced, because replacement requires going down to the lowland cities, and going down means coming back up, and coming back up is a commitment the young no longer make. So the lathes are from the twenties and the looms are older and the press that prints the *Almanac* is a hand-fed flatbed that a man named Havel feeds one sheet at a time, and the steam that drives it has been piped in from the foundry next door for ninety-three years through a joint of copper that has been re-soldered so often the original pipe is mostly solder now.

---

There are almost no animals. A few dogs, working dogs, who live with the shepherds who bring fleece down from the high pastures in autumn. A cat or two in each terrace, kept for the rats that come in when the weather turns, though the cats are mostly ornamental now and the rats mostly run free and grow large. A single donkey in the upper works, kept by the foundry for the carts that move ingots. And above, on the worn shoulders of the mountains: nothing, or almost nothing — a fox, sometimes, crossing the saddle in winter, leaving its small tracks in the snow; once, last spring, a bear, which had not been seen on these passes in living memory and which was found, three days later, dead at the bottom of one of the ventilation shafts of the mine, with no visible injury.

There are birds. There are ravens, which are not rare anywhere and not rare here, and they are the loudest thing in the town, and they are very loud. There is a small owl that lives in the eaves of the old company chapel — the chapel that is now, since the denomination withdrew, a museum of the denomination. There are, in summer, swallows, who come up through the cloud to nest under the eaves of the housing terraces and who leave, in autumn, in a single morning, and the town feels emptier for it.

---

The strangeness of Coalreach is not the kind that announces itself. It is the kind that accumulates. The terraced houses are built facing inward, toward the seam, but the seam is mostly hidden by the sheds and the chimneys, so the houses look, from the inside, at the backs of the houses across the way, and the backs of the houses are windowless, and so most of the light in Coalreach — and there is not much light — comes from above, where the cloud is, and from below, where the streetlamps are, and the streetlamps are the old gas kind, converted long ago but still fed through the original mains, which leak, so the streets always smell faintly of coal-gas even though no coal has been burned on these streets in fifty years. The lamps have a yellow light and they burn all day in winter because the cloud is so low it touches the chimneys. They reflect off the wet stone and the wet moss and they give the town, even at midday, the look of an early evening that never ends.

The seam itself is a thing you can stand on. It is a narrow crack, paved over now, with iron grilles where the drainage runs down into it. If you kneel and put your ear to the grille you can hear, far below, the distant work of the single crew in the mine, and the deeper rumble of water moving under the town, and sometimes, very faintly, the *tick-tick-tick* of something mechanical that is not the foundry and not the mill and that no one in Coalreach will claim to know the source of. It has been there, they say, since before the coal. Some of them say it is the heartbeat of the mountain. Some of them say it is a clock that was buried. The *Almanac* once ran a contest, years ago, to explain it, and the winning entry — a long prose poem by a woman who has since died — suggested that it was the sound of the world turning over in its sleep, and that Coalreach was, by accident or by design, built on the place where you could hear it.

Nothing has been done to confirm or refute this. The sound is too quiet to record properly, and the people of Coalreach are, on the whole, a people who do not trouble themselves with confirmation.

---

The moss grows. That is the principal industry now, if you can call it one — the slow, patient colonization of the town by the soft things of the world. The great stamping sheds, with their blown windows, are gardens. The chapel-museum is overrun with fern. The terraces, where the families used to hang washing on lines strung between the stone staircases, no longer have families enough to fill the lines, and the lines themselves have broken and the poles have been climbed by ivy, and the spaces where the washing used to flap are now just more sky, more cloud, more grey-green space where a small and ancient town goes on being itself, in the dim, in the damp, in the long quiet of a high place that has almost finished forgetting what it used to be.
