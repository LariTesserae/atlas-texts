# The Gardened Works

The water sits warm and close, only a few meters from the roofline of what was once the highest building here, and you cannot tell — cannot ever quite tell — where the architecture ends and the growing begins. This is a small drowned district: six blocks, perhaps seven, depending on how you count the alleys that have grown closed with vine. The scale is a cathedral's. Above the surface — and you know there is a surface because the light comes from it, falling in pale, particulate columns — the rest of the world continues. Down here, the world is finished, and the question is only what it has become.

The buildings are not ruined, exactly. They have been *digested*. The brick has gone soft at the edges, bloomed over with a felt of algae so thick that the mortar lines are suggestions only. Window frames lean inward under the weight of ferns that have spilled through them and rooted in the floor below. Inside the larger halls — the weaving-shed, the dispatch hall, the engine room with its three rust-eaten boilers still standing in a row like tired oxen — the inside has filled with water and the water has filled with green, and the green has filled with the smell of warm silt and of something faintly sweet, like overripe fruit held under the tongue.

The industrial apparatus is everywhere and mostly intact. You do not call it *abandoned*; nothing here has been abandoned, only *inherited*. There are still pipes, and the pipes still carry something, though no one knows what or where. A black drip from one overhead joint, patient as incense, traces a rope of mineral down a wall and into a basin that has grown a pale hair of fungus around its rim. A pump somewhere lower in the works keeps its slow heartbeat, hour after hour, lifting water from one chamber to another, so that the water itself is in motion even when nothing visible moves — there is a tide inside the building, set by the pump, and the fish (such as they are, the few long pale ones, knife-bodied, that drift through the loading bays) seem to know its schedule better than any human ever did.

The light is the light of deep greenhouses. It comes down out of a roof that is partly glass and partly collapsed, and what is not glass has been mended by climbing plants into a lattice of stems and leaves, so the sun is filtered twice: first by the water, then by the garden. It arrives in long, slanting blades. Dust motes — or their underwater cousins, flecks of organic matter, the slow sift of a hundred years of leaves — drift through these blades the way dust drifts in chapels, and the effect is the same: a kind of held breath, a place where time has agreed to go slowly.

The sound is the sound of a held breath. There is the pump. There is the small constant hiss of water finding its way through cracks and over ledges. There is, very far above, the muffled memory of weather — wind translated into a long lateral pressure, rain translated into a soft drumming on the surface a meter overhead. Inside the buildings, inside the rooms, the sound contracts to almost nothing. Your own breathing becomes the loudest thing in the world. You find yourself breathing less.

The vegetation has obeyed the geometry of the streets in a way that does not quite feel accidental. The main avenue is still clear — a green tunnel, perhaps four meters wide and three tall, with the buildings rising on either side as walls of brick-and-leaf, the windows like niches in a hedge. Along it, at the old loading platforms, certain trees have grown exactly where the freight once stood: banyan-fig things, their roots plunging through the planking and into the silt below, their crowns brushing the underside of the water-surface like a hand testing a ceiling. They are not, of course, banyans. They are whatever the warm water has made, over decades, of the seeds that drifted down: figs and willows and ferns hybridized into something softer, something that does not quite have a name, that bears pale fruit which falls and is never eaten.

Off the avenue, the packing is total. There is no gap you could push through that is not also a passage something else has already made. Pipes run underfoot, partly exposed, partly buried in root; the floor of every corridor is a mattress of moss and the bones of older plants. To walk is to disturb a thousand small lives — copepods, the white shrimp that cling to the brickwork in trembling constellations, the long flatworms that slide like wet paper across the floor — and to be watched, if not seen, by the things in the walls. There is little else. The district is too small, too settled, too nearly closed, to support a real ecology. It is an ecology of edges: the meeting of water and wall, of plant and pipe, of mineral and the soft things that eat mineral slowly.

It is strange, and the strangeness is not in any one feature but in the *fit*. The place does not feel like a ruin. It does not feel like a ruin being reclaimed, the way a forest reclaims a city, with a clear before and after and a winner. It feels like the two states have agreed. The boiler house grows ferns on its rivets in a pattern that looks, when you study it, almost deliberate — almost architectural. The stairways are still stairways, but the risers are padded green and the treads are slick and you climb them as you would climb a mossy bank. The streetlamps, the ones still upright, have become the skeletons of small trees: their iron ribs hold the new wood, and the new wood holds the lampshades aloft, and the whole structure leans slightly, drunkenly, as if it has just remembered how long it has been standing.

You come here because the surface is too much. You come because the water is warm and still and the place is small enough to know. You do not come alone, but you do not come with many — three is right, four is a crowd, and in a crowd the silence breaks and the place becomes what it was before, which is to say a dead district underwater with nothing to recommend it. With two companions, with the slow tread of the silt rising in pale puffs behind your boots, with the pump going in the basement and the fish drifting past at the level of your chest, you can believe that this is a place. That it has always been a place. That the engineers built it, perhaps, on purpose, knowing it would end like this — a small warm city of brick and water and patient green, where nothing is asked of you, and the light falls in blades, and the only sound is the sound of something being lifted, slowly, from one place to another.
