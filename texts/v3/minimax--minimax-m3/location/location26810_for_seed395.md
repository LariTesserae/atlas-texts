A vast basin, warm and low. The kind of place you could walk across for weeks and never see a horizon break — just the slow rise of mountains at the edges, days away, where the light gathers into something sharper.

The floor of the basin is raw. The geology here is young — younger than the things that live on it. Wind has been at work, but only recently. Ridges still hold their first edges; the dust has only begun to settle into the pockets it will eventually fill. Where the dust is thin, the substrate shows itself: pale stone in places, rust-red in others, salt-white in the deeper parts where something watery once stood and left its residue behind. The basin remembers water the way a bone remembers the marrow — as a shape, not a substance. What remains is mineral crust, cracked into plates that flex softly when the wind passes over them.

Onto this floor, the vegetation has climbed. It is lush in the sense that nothing else is — there is no bare ground anywhere the plants have chosen to root. They are thick-stemmed, columnar, water-storing things, dense as a forest but spaced like a city. Some rise twenty feet from the dust; some lie low and rosette against the warm stone. The spines are everywhere. Between the columns, the floor is a woven mat of smaller succulents — paddled, segmented, blue-green or pale grey depending on the mineral content of the dust they sit in. From a distance, the canopy has the look of a heavy green fog settled over the basin, broken only by the pale threads of paths that have been walked into it over generations. The lushness is not the lushness of a rainforest. It is the lushness of things that have arranged themselves with great deliberation to avoid losing what they have. Every stem, every waxy cuticle, every downward-pointing spine: an argument against evaporation.

Settlements sit inside this vegetation the way fish sit inside a reef — they cannot be separated from it. The walls are packed dust, slightly moistened, compressed by hand and by foot, dried to the color of the basin itself. Roofs are stalks, lashed with fiber, sloped to shed the rare downpour that comes once in a season if it comes at all. The construction is organized: streets, courtyards, terraces, watch-points. Without tools beyond the body and what the ground provides, the architecture has the slow patience of woven cloth — every wall was made by someone standing in front of it for weeks, building it up in layers the way the dust itself was laid down. The cities are dense. People live close; the spines push them together.

The fauna is abundant in the way that small things can be abundant when nothing eats them faster than they replace themselves. Lizards in the hundreds, basking on the warm walls at morning, vanishing into cracks by noon. Burrowers under the dust that surface only at the cool hours. Beetles that navigate the columns like rivers. A few larger forms — predators, patient and quiet — that move between the trunks with the slow deliberation of things that know their prey by sound and silhouette. They are not numerous. They do not need to be. The prey is plentiful and slow.

The sounds are few. A spine tapping against a column when the wind is right. The faint click of a beetle turning over. The almost-not-there pad of a predator's foot. Inside the settlements, the sounds of bodies working: a hand smoothing a wall, a basket being woven, a child asking a question and being answered. From the basin floor itself, when the wind has stopped and the air has settled into its heat, there is a quiet so complete that the inner ear starts inventing tones to fill it. The dust absorbs what little sound the world makes. Even the predator-prey exchange, when it happens, is conducted mostly in silence — a rustle, a pause, a settling of dust where one creature used to be.

The dynamics are continuous and largely unseen. Dust moves from the edges inward. The columns grow a fraction each year, flower in sudden brief pulses, are pollinated by the creatures that live among them. The settlements expand where the soil has built up enough to hold a wall; they retreat where a salt-crust has cracked and exposed something the foundations cannot sit on. The mountains at the basin's rim are still being eroded — sharp peaks shedding their first talus, the first generation of stones beginning to travel. The whole place is in its youth: not chaotic, not tender, but unfinished in the way a young forest is unfinished — everything present, nothing yet worn into its final shape. The quiet is part of that. A landscape this young has not yet learned to be loud.
