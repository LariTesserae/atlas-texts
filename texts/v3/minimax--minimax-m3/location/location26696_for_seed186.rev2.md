# The High Furnaces

You find the valley not by climbing into it but by descending.

The rim of the plateau runs for miles — a broken basalt edge where the wind never stops and nothing grows taller than a thumbnail. Beyond that edge the land drops, and what lies below is not the shape valleys usually take. It is too broad for a canyon, too walled-in for a basin. Something was gouged here, long ago or always, a furrow in the spine of the range. Now ice-water and weather have softened it into a long, cold lap of the world, three days' walk across and shadowed almost entirely by its own surrounding peaks.

The light comes in at angles. Most days the sun is somewhere above the cloud bank that nests in the high cols, and what reaches the valley floor is a gray diffusion, a leftover glow, enough to see by and not enough to feel warm on the face. In summer — what passes for summer here, a six-week loosening — the clouds sometimes break and a slab of pale sun drops onto the talus for an hour, and everything that has been living in monochrome remembers color. Then the clouds close back like a hand and the valley returns to its usual state: damp, dim, full of small wet sounds.

The dampness is not rain. It is the air itself. The snow on the upper slopes is permanent, and even on the hottest day it gives off a fine vapor that drifts down into the basin and hangs. Condensation beads on every surface that holds still long enough. The rocks sweat. The lichens glisten. Your breath is a presence, and so is everyone else's. When a cloud pours into the valley from the south it does not fall; it pours over the rim and fills the basin like water filling a bowl, and you walk in it waist-high, and you cannot hear your own footsteps for the muffled wet hush of it.

The vegetation is the first surprise, and it keeps surprising you.

At this altitude, in this cold, you expect the miserly tundra — a few sad mosses, a crust of lichen, the occasional crocus pushing through gravel. The valley has none of that discipline. It is overgrown. Dark moss climbs the lower trunks of the stunted firs that huddle along the sheltered cliffs, and where the snowmelt has carved a slow channel the moss becomes a meadow, and where the meadow is it is ankle-deep and spongy and studded with flowers that should not exist at this elevation, fat-petaled things in indigo and a strange bruised yellow that you have no name for. The krummholz trees grow twisted sideways against the prevailing wind, their branches running with hanging lichen like old men's beards, and between them the ferns come up waist-high in the sheltered pockets, and behind the ferns the dark thickets of alder and dwarf willow close in. Everything that grows here has the look of having thought about it for a long time and committed. It is not tropical lushness. It is the lushness of patient things — slow, dense, mineral-fed, thick as felt.

And yet the valley is empty. The lushness is in patches, islands, ribbons along the meltwater. Between the islands is bare ground — scree, slab rock, the gray gravel of old moraines, and snow that lingers year-round in the deep folds. You can walk for a quarter hour and see nothing green. Then you round a shoulder of rock and the green is there, a whole hidden garden, dense and quiet, and just as suddenly it's behind you and you're on stone again. The valley is built of these alternations: lush and bare, lush and bare, like a long sentence with its emphases and silences.

The erosion is settled. Whatever carved this place did its work before the firs took root. The slopes are at the angle they mean to be. The meltwater has cut its channels and is content with them. The scree has sorted itself into stable aprons. Nothing here is collapsing or rebuilding itself; the valley has the geometry of something that finished becoming itself a long time ago and is now just being.

This is where the strangeness begins to gather.

If you sit still on the bare stone, especially in the gray hours when the cloud has just lifted and the silence is at its fullest, you begin to hear something from the rock itself. Not the wind. Not the water. A pulse. Low, slow, deeper than you can place — you feel it in your hip bones when you sit on the bare slabs, a throb that runs through the stone at a rhythm slower than a heartbeat. It is always there. You stop noticing it after a few days, and then you can never stop noticing it again.

Look at the cliff faces.

The cliffs are dark, wet-shiny, vertical or overhanging. They are not featureless. Set into them, flush with the rock or jutting by an inch or a foot, are things that are not rock. Pipes. Conduits. The flanged mouths of ducts that exhale a thin steam which immediately becomes part of the mist. Plates of metal that have oxidized to the color of the stone and are spotted with the same lichen. Conduit-bundles the thickness of your arm that run along the cliff in parallel lines for a hundred feet and then vanish into a seam in the rock as if they had never been there at all. Cables — too thick to be anything natural — that span a gully in a long, taut droop and disappear into the rubble on either side.

There is no building. There is no factory, no smokestack, no shed, no chimney, no town. There is no rail line, no road, no machinery standing in the open. There is only the mountain, with its skin pierced in places by the soft edges of industry, and the soft breath of that industry leaking out into the weather.

What is being made here, you cannot tell. You follow a pipe along a cliff and it ducks into the rock and you never see it again. You find a stack of metal plates half-buried in scree and you cannot tell if they were placed or fell, or if they were ever anything but rock. There is no signage, no human mark, no writing. The few surfaces where a name might have lived have been rubbed smooth by the wind. You find, once, in the mud of a meltwater flat, a print of a tread — not a foot, not a paw, but a tread, a long rectangular stamp with chevron cleats — and you follow it for fifty yards and then it walks into the side of a boulder and does not come out. The boulder, when you put your ear to it, hums.

The mountain, you understand after a while, is the factory. The valley is its cooling yard. The industry is subterranean, geological, embedded in the rock the way the quartz veins are embedded in the rock, and it has been here longer than the firs. The pipes and the ducts are its capillaries. The pulse you feel through the stone is its circulation. Whatever is being done in there is being done on a scale that does not need a building, because the building is the mountain.

You do not see who runs it. You never see anyone.

You see the fauna.

The fauna is the second surprise, and it does not stop being a surprise.

For a place this empty — this cold, this damp, this dark, this lacking in any visible civilization — there is a great deal of life, and a great deal of it is large. The tracks in the mud of the meltwater flats are everywhere if you learn to read them. A paw the size of a dinner plate, four toes, no claw marks. A cloven hoof, narrow, deep. A dragging line like a thick rope pulled through the silt — something with a tail it does not bother to lift. Birds: dark, heavy-bodied, short-winged, dropping like stones out of the cloud and banking along the cliffs in absolute silence. Their feathers are the gray of the rock. You do not see them until they move, and once they land they disappear.

In the lush patches the life is smaller but denser. The moss is full of things — pale invertebrates with too many legs, things with wet shining backs that go into the leaf litter when you uncover them. Under every flat stone there is a small industry of itself: worms, grubs, a flat gray thing with many legs that you have no name for. The flowers are visited by something — by day you see them rarely, by twilight you see them more, by full dark they are loud with wings you cannot hear. You stand in a meadow in the gray hour and the air around your head is full of small shapes, and the only evidence is the slight pull on the petals as they pass.

The big fauna — the paw, the hoof, the long-tailed thing — you see them rarely and you see them mostly as absence. A silhouette on a far ridge, gone before you focus. A sound you almost hear and then don't. A carcass on a snow patch, half-eaten, the bones picked clean in a way that suggests the eaters were not in a hurry. Once, at the foot of a cliff where the steam came thickest, you find a print in the wet ash that the steam had laid down — not a paw print this time, something bipedal, something that had stood upright on two feet and stayed there a long time, because the print was deep and the ash had crept back into it only at the edges. There were no human artifacts near it. There was nothing at all near it but the steam and the rock and the pulse from the mountain and the silence.

The animals are quiet in a way that goes past habit into something else. Their feet are padded, or their hooves are wrapped in something, or — for the larger ones — their size is such that their footfalls are simply slow and considered. The birds do not call. The big fauna has no call you have heard. What you hear, mostly, is the water and the wind and the pulse, and under those, if you wait, a very soft and irregular clicking from the cliff faces — the metal cooling, the pipes ticking, the ducts adjusting to the pressure from inside the mountain.

The valley is active. It is not silent in the way of a dead place. The meltwater is always running, somewhere, into a hundred hidden channels. The snow is always shifting, very slowly, in the gullies. The ferns are uncurling. The birds are hunting. The big things are moving along the ridges on their soft feet, and something in the undergrowth is always eating something else. The pipes are always breathing their thin steam. The mountain is always pulsing.

And the weather moves through on its own clock. The clouds pour in and the clouds pour out. A wind comes off the glacier for three days and strips every loose thing from the exposed slopes. Then the wind dies and a stillness settles that is so complete you can hear the lichen growing, which is to say you can hear nothing at all, and then the mist returns and you are walking in a wet dim world again, and a shape moves in the mist at the edge of vision and is gone.

The valley has a quality of being watched. Not by anyone. By the place itself. The way a kettle watches, or a sleeping animal, or a thing that is doing something important and does not wish to be disturbed. The lush patches feel tended, though no one tends them. The bare stone feels cleared, though no one has cleared it. The pipes feel placed, though no one placed them. The animals feel allowed, though there is no one to allow them.

You sit on a flat rock at the edge of a meltwater flat, in the gray hour, and the pulse comes up through your bones, and the steam drifts off the cliff face, and a shape — too large, too dark, too quiet — crosses the far ridge without hurry and is gone.

The valley continues. It does not need you to understand it. It has been here, with its pipes and its pulse and its patient green, since before anyone came to be confused by it, and it will be here after. The mountain hums. The snow falls upward into the cloud. The dark birds turn in the mist without sound.

You are a small warm thing in a cold dim place, and the place is busy with its long work, and the work goes on.
