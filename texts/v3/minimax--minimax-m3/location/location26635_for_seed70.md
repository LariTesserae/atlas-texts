The water here is not deep enough to be called ocean and not shallow enough to be called lake. It has the cleanness of high country — thin, mineral, faintly green where the sun reaches — and the temperature of bathwater. The ridges below the surface are sharp in a way that water-eroded stone should never be. They were mountains, not long ago. Tectonic lips, freshly folded, that have not yet had time to soften under the long abrasion of currents and sediment. Their shapes — the crisp arêtes, the angular cols, the broken faces — read like a mountain range drawn in chalk, then drowned before anyone could erase it.

The water level sits somewhere above the lowest valleys and below the highest crests. A few summits still pierce the surface as black teeth of basalt or granite, depending on which ridge you are near; there are several, running in roughly parallel bands, as if the continental shelf were a washboard being slowly compressed. Between them, the water has pooled into long troughs, some narrow enough that you could almost touch both sides if you swam through them. The troughs are not the bottom. They are simply the lowest places the water has reached so far.

Above, the air is warm — warmer than it ought to be at this altitude, though the air itself is rarely felt. The sky, if visible at all through the dense growth on the emerged peaks, is pale. The light that reaches the ridges comes filtered through several meters of slow-moving water and through the plants that have already colonized it, and arrives at the rock as something softened and green-gilt, with no clean shadows. There is no wind to speak of. The water moves in long, slow cycles that take hours to complete: a leaf released at one end of a valley will arrive at the other by late afternoon, having made the journey without any visible urgency.

The vegetation is dense and mostly aquatic, though the dividing line is fuzzy. Where the peaks emerge, things that look like terrestrial shrubs cluster in the cracks, their leaves thick and dark, sometimes flowering in colors that don't quite match the spectrum any local pollinator would prefer. Below the surface, what is called kelp here — though it isn't quite kelp — grows in long curtains that anchor to the rock in the upper ten or fifteen meters and trail down toward the troughs. In places these curtains have layered on top of one another so that the original rock face is invisible, a wall of brown-green and red-tinged stipes bending with a patience that is barely movement at all. There are smaller plants too, moss-like things, that grow on the leaves of the larger plants. The whole thing is a kind of inverted forest, canopy-down, with the highest layer closest to the light and the lowest layer trailing into the dim.

Animals are rare. There are small things — what look like shrimp, what look like translucent fish no longer than a finger — that flick through the curtains and are gone. Nothing large. Nothing with a backbone you could reasonably call a backbone. The place has not had time, perhaps, to fill the niches its geology has opened. Or the conditions are slightly off — too warm in some seasons, too oxygenated in some layers and not enough in others, in patterns that don't quite match what an animal would want. The water is alive with microscopic things; the visible fauna is sparse enough that any swimmer would feel watched by plants rather than creatures.

The strangeness is in the proportion. There is too much rock for an underwater landscape and too much water for a mountain landscape. The peaks, where they emerge, look too sharp to have been shaped by water and yet here they are, half-submerged, with a clarity that does not feel right. The kelp grows with a confidence that suggests it knows it is the dominant organism — not the tentative growth of an early colonizer. The troughs between the ridges are filled with a fine silt that has had no time to settle into anything stable; it lifts in clouds when disturbed, and resettles in patterns that look faintly deliberate, as if the bottom is being repeatedly rewritten. Nothing here has had enough time to become itself, and yet everything here is acting as if it has been here forever.

The quiet is unusual for a place this dense. There is no crashing, no roar. The water absorbs what little sound the plants make — the small clicks of something biological, the slow creak of stipes bending under their own weight — and turns it into something felt rather than heard. From a distance, the ridges look like a city seen from above the clouds: orderly, layered, dense with structure, silent. Up close, the structures are made of kelp fronds and basalt, and they bend, slowly, in currents so weak you only notice them when something passes.

This is a young place trying to look old. Or an old place that has been, recently and suddenly, drowned. The two are not mutually exclusive. Somewhere in the trough, on a ledge no one will ever name, a single small fish is hovering with its fins working very slightly, holding position against a current that will not change direction for hours. It has been here, by the look of it, for the whole morning. It does not appear to be waiting for anything. It is just one of the few things here that has decided, against the odds, to live in this place; and it does so without ceremony, in the green-gilt water, between the sharp ridges of a mountain range that has not finished becoming a mountain range before the water closed over it.
