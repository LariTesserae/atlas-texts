The settlement has no name anyone uses aloud. It is called *the Below* by those who live above, and *here* by those who live in it, and that is enough.

You arrive through a crevasse that opens in a hillside and goes down at an angle steep enough to require handholds cut into the stone — fresh cuts, the chisel-marks pale and unweathered, the dust still smelling of something between mineral and chalk. The crevasse widens after a hundred paces, and then the city is simply there, pressed against you from every direction at once.

It is carved mostly, and built partly. The stone is a pale, banded limestone in long horizontal stripes — cream and a dull, almost-blue gray — and the city follows those stripes the way a river follows a valley floor. The chambers and corridors were excavated along the natural bedding planes, so the ceilings curve in long, low arcs and the walls have a kind of layered architecture already in them before the chisels arrived. At the working edges of the city the layering is still visible as raw geology: a band of coquina full of shell-fragments just exposed for the first time, a seam of darker marl that has not yet been cut away, the rough faces of stone that has only just been freed from the surrounding rock and is still holding its original moisture in the center of each block.

The place is packed. There is almost no room between presences. Houses share walls. A family's sleeping alcove opens onto the back wall of someone else's kitchen. Stairs are narrow and steep and not all of them agree on which direction is up. There are plazas, but they are plazas only because someone stopped carving for a moment and three streets happened to meet there; they are not very large and they have benches carved into them and the benches are usually occupied. The whole of the city is a single organism of joined rooms and joined tunnels and the occasional vertical shaft, and the sound of a hammer from one chamber reaches the next through the stone.

It is cool in a constant, even way — not the deep chill of a true cave, not the warmth of a hearth, but the temperature of stone that has never known the sun. The stone gives back a little of what it receives. Bodies warm the rooms they sleep in; the corridors keep the mean temperature of the rock. People wear layered wool. In the unlit chambers a thin fog will sometimes form and lie across the floor; in the lit ones, the air is clearer but always a little damp on the skin.

Water is everywhere in the small way. It seeps. From the ceiling of any long tunnel you can hear it — a tick, a tick, a longer drip, and then a quietness again — and the floors are slightly concave where generations of feet and water together have worn the stone. There are cisterns cut into low places and they fill on a slow schedule no one tracks because it is not in anyone's power to do anything about it. In the deepest chambers, where the air doesn't move much, there are shallow pools of clear water on the floor that never seem to either grow or shrink; their surfaces are mirror-flat except when someone walks past, and then the disturbance takes a long time to settle. Above the ceiling, somewhere up in the uncarved rock, there must be a great deal of water because the dripping never stops in any season, but down here it arrives as patience, not as rain.

There are light wells. A city this size, this deep, this packed, requires them and has them — shafts cut up through the stone from the plaza-levels, narrow at the top and widening slightly partway up to catch more sky. The shafts are vertical and the daylight comes down them as a column, brighter at the top and dimmer at the bottom, and the bottom of each shaft is the brightest place in the city. Around the shafts the floors are kept clear of buildings because the light is needed and because the dust of construction rises into them and is then carried up and out by the slow chimney-draft. There are seven of these main shafts and many smaller ones. Around the rim of each one, on the upper ledges, there are wooden frames and pulleys, and baskets come down and go up all day — quarried stone from the working face, water from the cisterns, refuse, the occasional person. The light that reaches the floor is enough to see by but not enough to do fine work; the working faces are dim and the chisels ring off the stone and the chips fall into darkness and the light arrives there later, when it is carried down.

Vegetation is sparse and lives only in negotiation with the light wells. In the bright circles beneath the shafts there is sometimes a thin skin of moss on the stone where the drips fall consistently; in some of these places people have set out shallow clay bowls and the moss grows in them too, in tight green pads. There is no grass here. There is no tree. There are lichens on the upper walls of the older tunnels and a kind of pale fern that grows only in one chamber near a shaft that catches morning light, and that one chamber is treated as a kind of shared garden and a kind of shared miracle, and people sit in it. The air smells like wet stone and the faint green vegetable smell of growing things in a small space.

The construction is layered in the way that all cities of any age are layered, except that here you can read the layering on the walls. The older chambers have smooth-finished walls, sometimes plastered with a lime wash that has gone soft with age; the plaster is pale yellow and is repainted in places and not repainted in others, and the difference reads as a kind of dating. Middle-period walls have chisel-marks at a regular spacing, the strokes of a practiced hand. The newest walls — the ones that go off into the dark at the working face — are rough, almost raw, and the chisel-marks are uneven and the stone is dusty and pale where it has just been freed. Each style of wall sits beside the others. A grandmother can stand in her kitchen and put her hand on a wall her grandfather carved and through a doorway is a chamber being carved now by her grandchild, and the three of them are visible in the stone at the same time.

Tools are simple. Bronze picks, mostly. Some iron, but iron is expensive and reserved for edges that need to hold. Chisels of bronze and bone. Wooden wedges soaked in water and driven into natural cracks to split the stone along its grain. Abrasive sand for finishing. Rope made from plant fiber gathered above and brought down. Wooden scaffolding, reused many times, lashed together with cord and weighted against the floor. There are no machines. The work is the work of bodies, of muscle and patience, and a chamber the size of a family's home takes a long time — long enough that the people who began it may not be the people who finish it, but the room is begun and finished in the same visual style, and the seam between the two campaigns of work is invisible in the wall.

The light is normal in the sense that nothing is wrong with the light; it is a day's worth of light, distributed unevenly. The shafts are bright and around them the city is lit; further from them it dims, and at the working faces it is only lamp-light, and the lamps are small clay bowls of oil with a wick of plant fiber and they give a yellow, wavering light that doesn't travel far. At dusk above, the shafts go from white to gray to blue, and the lamps are lit one by one in the deeper chambers, and the city becomes a constellation of small yellow points in a much larger dark. At dawn the lamps are blown out and the shafts brighten again and the constellation dissolves.

There are very few animals. There is no livestock — there is no pasture, no fodder, and the smell of an animal would not be bearable in the packed air of the residential chambers. There are pigeons in the upper parts of the shafts, where they roost on the wooden pulley-frames and come down sometimes into the light wells; people tolerate them for what they contribute to the diet and for the news of weather they seem to carry. There are rats. There are insects that no one thinks about. There is a small brown lizard that lives in the warm places near hearths and eats insects and is thought of as lucky. That is most of the fauna.

Sound is mostly the absence of sound. There is no wind, except what people make by walking. There is the tick of water I mentioned. There is, at the working face, the distant ring of bronze on stone, and sometimes a voice calling through the dark to someone who is out of sight. There are voices in the residential chambers, low because the walls are thin and people hear each other anyway, so they speak at the volume they would speak in the same room. There is the occasional crying of a child and the occasional laughter. There is, from time to time, a long groan from the rock itself, the slow movement of the earth settling against the chambers that have been cut into it, and when that sound happens people pause in what they are doing and wait for it to pass.

The activity of the place is steady rather than dramatic. Water seeps. The stone is being worked. The lamps are lit and extinguished. The pulley-baskets go up and down. Children are born in the residential chambers and old people die there and the dead are carried up the shafts in the night, in bundles, and laid out on the hillside above. The city is not in a hurry. The work continues in the dark at the pace of bronze on stone and the pace of breath, and the city grows by a chamber or two in the time most cities would build a block. The working face is the youngest part of the city and is also the most alive, and if you stand there in the half-light you can hear the breathing of the people at work and the breathing of the stone being slowly opened, and the two sounds are difficult to tell apart.
