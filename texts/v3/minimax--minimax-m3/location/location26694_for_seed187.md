# The Respiring Cathedral

Two hundred years ago, a paper mill stood here. The smokestack is still erect, though no one laid a single brick to keep it so. The brickwork has been wholly subsumed — colonized, augmented, woven into — by a single organism that has no name because the taxonomists who once cared about such things ran out of roadmaps and specimen jars somewhere around the third generation of its growth. The stack rises a hundred and forty feet. It is the only vertical thing for a mile in any direction. What surrounds it is a grove.

The grove fits inside a bowl of low hills, roughly the footprint of a market square, and this is all the growth there is. Beyond the rim the world is pasture, then fen, then the long rust of the coastal flats. The grove is not a wood. It is a single creature's body, and it knows where its edges are.

---

You enter through a gap in the perimeter where the outer wall of the plant has folded back against itself like a lip. The wall is eighteen feet thick and breathed through by a thousand small openings — not holes, exactly, but interruptions in the weave, each one a different diameter, each one exhaling a faintly sweet, mineral dampness that you can taste on the back of your teeth. The light comes in sideways through these openings and across the floor of the inner space in long, slow, almost-still shafts, because the air inside is not still: it is being drawn, continuously, toward the stack.

The floor of the cathedral is springy. It is the upper surface of the plant's rootplate, which has consumed the old mill floor and the cellars beneath it, and has mounded itself up into a working surface that is roughly flat but not quite — there are swales, there are places where the surface balloons upward into fleshy pillows, and in one corner a basin where the plant has made a small round lake of itself, cupped and held, three inches deep, perpetually refilled by condensation from the breathing walls. The water is brown and smells of iron and old sugar.

Above you, the roof is a vault of woven living stems, so dense that the daylight outside becomes a green twilight inside, and the shadow of every leaf is the same shadow. The vault is not sound-absorbent. It is the opposite. Every leaf is a small tympanum, and the organism is listening with its whole ceiling, and the sound of the place is the sound of itself listening.

The loudness is not mechanical. It is the sound of the plant transpiring at scale. There is a continuous exhale from every pore of the inner space — a soft, sibilant, many-throated breath, the kind of sound a forest makes in a high wind, except there is no wind and the sound does not stop. It is the sound of a large lung. Layered into it, deeper, is the working noise of the organ: a low, rhythmic, hydraulic pulse, somewhat faster than a resting human heart, that you feel in the soles of your feet before you hear it, and that you can hear for a long time after you leave. Layered into that, intermittently, is the high, dry, papery chatter of the leaves themselves adjusting to the light — not a wind-chatter, because the air does not move on its own, but the organism is constantly reorienting its surfaces to track the sun, and this produces a slow, continuous, papery shuffling, like a deck of cards being shuffled by a patient and enormous hand.

The fauna is not absent. It is concentrated.

In the roof, a permanent residency of swift analogues — forked, pale-bellied, with the long curved wings of things that have eaten only the small insects that live exclusively in this cathedral — wheel and shriek in tight, looping circuits around the inside of the dome. There are perhaps three hundred of them. Their voices are the upper register of the place's sound: a high, bright, tearing screech that runs in waves around the circumference as one group passes another. They nest in the pockets of the wall, in cavities the plant has made for them or permitted, and the floor beneath any given nest is white with their droppings, which the plant has not cleaned up and seems to be using.

On the floor, and in the lake, and on the pillows, a busy guild of small invertebrates — long-bodied, rust-colored, with many quick legs — moves in deliberate, purpose-looking columns. They are not ants. They are something else, the same something-else as the birds, the same something-else as everything in here: a lineage that has been inside this organism so long that it has become the organism's circulatory system and the organism has become its world. They carry, in their mandibles and on their backs, small fragments of plant tissue from one part of the cathedral to another, and they do this constantly, and the plant tolerates them and seems to be waiting for them. There is a faint, dry, clicking sound from their columns, like beads dropped on a wooden floor, and it is everywhere.

A single larger animal lives here. It is the size of a small dog, with a long, soft, almost boneless body, and it moves through the cathedral by means of the tunnels the plant has threaded through the wall — tunnels just wide enough for it, ribbed and lined with a darker, wetter tissue, and you can hear it moving through them sometimes, a soft, wet, peristaltic sound, like someone slowly squeezing a wet towel. It is asleep most of the time. It surfaces occasionally, in the evening, to drink from the lake. The plant does not seem to mind it. They are very old acquaintances.

There is no wind outside; the air is still and cool and smells of cut grass and approaching weather. The plant does not extend beyond the bowl of the hills. Its roots are inside the bowl, deep and old, and beyond the rim the soil is just soil, and the grass is just grass, and the world is the same world it has been. The organism has not colonized outward. It does not seem to want to. It is using all of itself for what it is doing here, and what it is doing here is, as far as anyone can tell, breathing.

The brick of the old stack, where it is exposed at the top and along the upper third, is still brick. The plant has not climbed it. The chimney is hollow, and the hollow goes all the way up, and out of the top of the chimney, in cool weather, a thin, nearly transparent plume of vapor rises vertically into the sky and disperses at about three hundred feet. From a distance, the stack looks like a working chimney. It is not. It is a lung.

The machinery is gone. The boilers, the vats, the great iron rollers, the steam pipes — all of it has been digested, or buried in the floor, or incorporated into the wall as internal armature. You can still feel the shapes of the boilers underfoot if you know where to step: long, cylindrical mounds, firm, slightly warmer than the surrounding floor. The plant has grown around them and is using them, in some way that has not been explained, to keep its hydraulic pulse regular. The great cast-iron flywheel of the mill's original steam engine stands upright in the lake, half-submerged, draped in a thick pelt of dark, wet root, and the plant has begun, very slowly, to rotate it. It turns one full revolution every eleven minutes. You can hear it turning. It is the deepest sound in the place, beneath the breath and the clicks and the shrieks, a low, slow, metallic groan, like a ship in a calm sea, and it does not stop.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The organism is old. The flywheel is older. The hills are the oldest thing, and they are not in a hurry either. A human standing in the cathedral for any length of time begins to synchronize — breath to pulse, pulse to flywheel, flywheel to the slow change of the light through the wall — and after an hour or so the feeling of being inside a thing gives way to the feeling of being inside a process, and after that, if you stay, to the feeling of being inside a sentence that has been running for a very long time and will not, you understand, ever reach its period.

The light through the openings in the wall moves, but the light through the openings in the wall moving is the only clock. The plant does not change. The water does not rise. The flywheel does not speed up. The swift analogues do not migrate. The big boneless animal sleeps. The hills, outside, do not erode. The chimney breathes. The brick holds.

It is, in the way of very old living things, completely uninterested in you. You may come and go. It will be here.
