You arrive at the edge of it on a flat rail, and there is no edge.

What the maps call the city's boundary is, in fact, where the dense built fabric thins to a denser built fabric, and then that thins again, and again, until the only thing that thins is your ability to perceive where one structure ends and another begins. From the rail, looking out, you see a single slab of construction extending to the curve of the world. There are no skylines. There are no breaks. There is only the texture — dark stone, pale ceramic, ribbons of something metallic that catches the warm light, lattices of glassy material that hold a faint inner glow. From orbit it would look like a skin. From the rail it looks like a verdict.

The continental shelf is low and flat, and the city sits on it the way moss sits on a stone, the way a rash sits on skin. Geologically speaking, this is a young continent, but the city is not. The city is old. The city is older than the language the locals speak, which is itself older than most languages. It has settled into itself, into its weight, into its wet.

The wet. You cannot escape the wet. The air is warm and the air is heavy and the air is full of water that has not yet decided what form it wants to be in. It clings to your skin. It pools in the seams of your clothes. It films every surface in the city with a thin trembling layer that rings when struck and runs down vertical walls in long silver traces, gathering at the bottom in slow black channels that wind between the foundations like veins. The moisture is not unpleasant. It is just present. It is the medium through which everything else happens, the way air is the medium for birds, the way heat is the medium for this place.

The materials of the city are not quite the materials of any geology you studied. There is stone — dark, dense, fine-grained, almost black — but it is laid in courses so perfect that you suspect it was not laid so much as instructed to be there. There is ceramic — pale, cool, faintly translucent at the edges — and it has the look of something that was once liquid and chose to stop being liquid. There is metal — but the metal is wrong: it folds without creasing, it ripples in slow waves when you watch it, it casts shadows that fall in directions the light source did not authorize. And there is a fifth material, the one nobody can name, that runs through all the others like a thread, that you see mostly as a faint sheen on otherwise plain surfaces, as a thin line of color that shifts when you move your head. The fifth material is in everything. The fifth material may be the city.

No plants. Not one. The closest thing to growth is the orange-black mineral crust that creeps along the older foundations, slow chemical hunger spreading in fractal patterns, eating stone and metal with equal indifference. There are lichens of a sort, but they are not lichens — they are surface phenomena, reactions between the materials and the wet air and the long patient pressure of use. They look like rust and they are not rust. They look like moss and they are not moss. They are the city's version of lichen, which is to say they are the city's version of forgetting itself.

The fauna, though. The fauna is what keeps you from going mad in the first weeks, because the fauna is wrong in ways that are specific and therefore knowable. There are pigeons with three eyes arranged in a triangle on their heads, plumage that looks like beaten copper, voices pitched two octaves below what a pigeon should manage. They roost on the upper terraces in long murmuring ranks and they watch you with an attention that feels almost administrative. There are rats — silver rats, ghost rats — that live inside the walls, eating whatever it is that passes for wiring, their whiskers faintly luminous in the gaps. There are insects with carapaces like obsidian that click against the floors in long syncopated rhythms, and these rhythms are not random, and you will eventually realize they are not random. There is something larger in the upper structures that you only ever hear — a slow breathing, a shifting weight, the creak of something vast moving through spaces you cannot see. None of these creatures are biological in the way your training prepared you for. They have all been rewritten by the density, by the wet warmth, by the strange diet, by something in the air itself that mutates anything that lives in it for more than a few generations.

The sound. The sound is the thing that breaks most arrivals, in the first days. It is not one sound. It is not ten sounds. It is a single sustained roar, a chord built from frequencies that span the entire audible spectrum and most of the inaudible ones. There are sub-frequencies that you feel in your sternum, in your pelvis, in the soles of your feet. There are high pitches that make your back teeth ache, that make your vision swim at the edges. There are percussive elements — hammers, drills, the rhythmic impact of something massive being moved through a narrow space. There are voices, but they are not voices, they are the aggregate of voices, the way the sea is the aggregate of drops. There are tones that come from machines and tones that come from the structures themselves and tones that come from things you cannot identify. The sound has no off-switch. The sound has no quiet hour. The sound has been continuous for so long that silence, in this city, is a kind of rumor.

The tech is — and this is the part that the briefings did not prepare you for — the tech is not quite tech. The walls move. You watch a wall for a minute and you see it: a slow ripple, like a muscle under skin, like something considering whether to be still. The lights in the upper levels do not behave as light should — they cast shadows that fall the wrong way, they illuminate surfaces that are not in their path, they bend around corners they should be blocked by. There are machines that appear to be made of folded space, that occupy less room than they should, that hum at pitches just below hearing. The whole place feels like it is operating on slightly different rules, as if the engineers who built it had access to a physics that was not included in your training, or as if your training is a low-resolution sketch of something much finer.

The dynamic is constant. Nothing here is finished. Even the most weathered of the structures, the ones stained dark with age and slick with their perpetual moisture, are still moving — still reconfiguring, still adding, still taking away. Scaffolding climbs every wall. Demolition crews work in parallel with construction crews on the same building, the same wall, the same column. A section of terrace might be dismantled and rebuilt three times in a week. The crowds move through the narrow channels between structures in rivers that never slow, never thin, never stop, even at what your body insists is the middle of the night. The city does not sleep because the city does not know how to sleep. The city is an anthill and it has been an anthill for so long that being an anthill has become its resting state.

The light is the only ordinary thing, and that is what unsettles you most of all. A warm sun climbs a warm sky. The shadows fall in roughly the right direction. The clouds are thin and infrequent. The day looks like a day should look. But the shadows here are busy — interrupted by things rising and falling in front of them, by scaffolding, by transit, by the slow ripple of the moving walls. And the sun, when you finally catch it between structures, is a yellow coin that seems slightly too close, slightly too warm, slightly too yellow. The sky above the city is the color of old milk, and it does not change color across the day in quite the way a sky should.

The strangeness is not the strangeness of alien things. It is the strangeness of familiar things turned up slightly. The angles of the buildings do not quite add up to right angles. The shadows are a fraction too long. The fauna looks at you with too many eyes. The walls move when you are not looking directly at them.
