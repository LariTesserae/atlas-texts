# The Upper Drainage

High. So high that the air tastes thin and metallic on the back of the tongue, the way copper pennies taste when you were a child and put them in your mouth for reasons you can't reconstruct. The light here isn't dimmed, but it has been *diluted* — spread thinner across more atmosphere than seems necessary, arriving in a wash of pale, slightly bluish white that gives no object a shadow long enough to mean anything. It is the light of an interior, oddly: a hangar light, a cathedral light, a light that has been engineered somewhere upstream and arrives exhausted but still serviceable.

The elevation has gone to work on the moisture. Water is everywhere and in every form but is rarely seen as water. It clings to the underside of every surface as condensation. It beads on stone and runs in sheets across broad rock faces, joining into a thin film that moves at the pace of a slow walker and pools in the countless shallow basins pocked across the high platform. The basins are everywhere, like the surface of a thumbprint pressed enormous and flat, and most of them hold a half-inch of water that is black with the sky's reflection. When you walk — if you walk — your footfall is almost always a soft *slap*, the sound of disturbing a thin skin of water over stone, and the only sound louder is the breath in your own chest working to get enough.

The stone is old in a way that doesn't read as age. It reads as *agreement*. The forms it has settled into are the forms it intends to keep. Basins, ledges, shallow gutters, broad sills, low rim-curves where water has agreed to run a particular path for so long that the stone has stopped pretending to be anything else. The erosion is in balance: water removes a molecule, lichen replaces it, frost cracks a flake, moss colonizes the crack, the crack becomes a groove, the groove becomes a channel, the channel becomes a place. Nothing dramatic. Nothing is being unmade. The landscape is post-process, post-history, in the long middle of being itself.

And the moss. The moss is the landscape.

It cannot be called vegetation in any ordinary sense, because there is no longer any clear distinction between moss and stone. It has packed itself across every vertical surface, every overhang, every half-sheltered face, and where the rock is too steep for it, the moss has simply climbed to the lip of the steepness and leaned over, hanging in pale green curtains that move a little in the wind, which is constant, which is a fact here rather than an event. The curtains are not decorative. They are the infrastructure. Behind them, water moves down the rock face in capillary films, and the moss ferries it from one ledge to the next, and the ledges collect it in the basins, and the basins overflow into the gutters, and the gutters discharge over the next lip. The whole upper world is a waterworks built of plants, and the plants are built of the stone they are dismantling into peat and humus an inch at a time.

You can stand in it and feel the density. There is no view. There is no distance. Looking outward from any point is looking into a wall of green and grey within thirty feet, and the wall has texture upon texture upon texture — the fat puckered cushions of sphagnum, the trailing strands of some nameless feathery moss like greenish hair, the broad liverwort thalli pressed flat and dark as old silver, the crustose lichens in their powdery oranges and dust greys. When you look at it your eye cannot rest. There is always a smaller thing inside the thing you are looking at. A beetle inside a moss-cushion inside a tuft inside a curtain. The place is *packed*. There is no room. Every square centimeter of surface has been claimed, occupied, negotiated over, built upon, eaten, and rebuilt.

The fauna is here. You do not see most of it, because the fauna is small and the fauna is silent. The sound of the place, at any given moment, is almost nothing: the thin hiss of the wind moving through the moss curtains, the occasional soft *plip* of a drop falling from a saturated overhang into a basin below, the very distant clatter of something with too many legs running through leaf litter somewhere you cannot locate. Sometimes, very rarely, a low groan from the throat of a fumarole or a snowfield, the sound the whole plateau makes when it breathes in. You stand in it and hear your own pulse and feel, with mild alarm, that you are the loudest thing.

But the fauna is here. Scurrying in the moss are creatures like pale sowbugs but with too many segments. Tiny hexapods the color of wet bark. A spider, sometimes, fat and dun-colored, hanging upside down in a web the size of a hand. A bird, once an hour perhaps, that you will see only as a movement at the corner of your vision — a low fast smear of dark color between the green — and then gone. Larger things move in the deeper channels and the boggy saddles: something with weight enough to make the moss mattresses under your feet bounce subtly when it passes, something you will never see and which is, you come to understand, the reason the small things scurry. Predation is present but discreet, conducted in the manner of the place, which is the manner of everything here: slowly, quietly, by saturation rather than violence.

And the strangeness.

The strangeness is this: the machines.

There is no building. There is no road. There is no wall, no chimney, no rail, no wire, no evidence in any conventional sense of manufacture. But if you crouch and lift the trailing moss at the base of a particular outcrop — and you will crouch eventually, because to be here and not crouch is to be only half arrived — you will see, embedded in the rock and the peat, a thing made of metal. It is not a tool in any shape you recognize. It has the geometry of a heat sink. It has the geometry of a flange. It has the color of iron that has been wet for a very long time, and the wetness, you realize, is the only thing keeping it from being stone itself. If you put your hand on it, it is cold in a particular way — colder than the rock around it, as if it is still, decades or centuries or longer after its making, slowly coming to the temperature of the place. It hums. Not audibly. You feel it in the bones of your wrist. A tiny, constant, low vibration, like a tuning fork struck long ago and never quite allowed to stop.

These machines are everywhere. Not in the moss-curtain zones, where the water is too active and the chemistry too aggressive, but on the broad sills, on the dry-ish ridges between the channels, half-buried in lichen and moss and the felted peat of ten thousand growing seasons. They are in groups of three or four, or in long irregular lines, or sometimes — and this is the part you will think about afterward, lying in your own bed far below the elevation line, unable to sleep — sometimes in patterns that are very nearly circular, with a hollow in the center that has been kept clear of moss by some persistent emission of heat or vibration you cannot locate the source of. Inside the hollow, the stone is bare, and dry, and faintly warm. Sit there. Sit there long enough and you will feel the hum in your sit bones, and you will begin to feel that the hum is not random, that it has intervals, that it is, in fact, a kind of patience, or a kind of waiting, or a kind of labor being done at a scale your body cannot participate in.

No one comes to service them. No one is coming. They are doing whatever they are doing on their own, slowly, in the wet and the cool and the silence, and the moss is climbing over them and they are sinking into the stone and the stone is being lifted by the moss and the machines do not mind, the moss does not mind, the water does not mind, and you, after a while, sitting in one of the dry warm hollows with the hum in your pelvis and the thin light in your eyes, do not mind either.

The clouds come down to the level of the plateau most afternoons. They do not arrive; they are already there, and you rise into them, or they were always around you and you only now noticed. Inside a cloud, the place becomes its purest version: no horizon, no distant rock, no scale at all, only the moss, the water, the wet stone under your feet, the slow chime of drops, the hum, and the pale diffused glow of a sun you cannot locate but which is doing, somewhere above the white, the work of a sun.

This is the upper drainage. This is where the water goes before it goes lower. The machines hum. The moss grows. The small things run. The water climbs down through a hundred thousand capillary decisions, and the decisions are the moss, and the moss is the place, and the place is waiting for no one and producing no one and ending nowhere, and the only question it has ever asked of you is whether you are willing to sit still long enough to notice that the hum has a key, and that the key is, against all reason, beautiful.
