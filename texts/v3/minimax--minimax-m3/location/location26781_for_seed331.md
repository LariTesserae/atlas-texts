The basin is small. You could walk its rim in less than an hour. You could miss it from above, because the rim is low and the ferns and pale-barked trees have leaned over the edge, so that when you find it you are already partway down — you have stumbled into the place rather than arrived at it. The slope is gradual. The descent is soft.

At the bottom, the ground is flat. Almost perfectly flat. This is the first thing that unsettles you, because you can see the flatness is not the way the stone began. The stone is dark and wet. There are long curving bands running across it, faint, like the memory of folds — places where something once rose out of the surface and was worn away. The stone has been here longer than anything you have ever stood on, and the weather, the water, the warm still air, the patient biology of the basin have ground it down to a thing that is no longer trying to be anything else. It has settled. It has reached its final shape. The shapes of the folds are still there as a kind of grain, the way the grain of wood remembers the tree.

Water is everywhere, and quiet. It is not standing in pools so much as occupying the surface of things — a film on the stone, a film on the leaves, a thickness to the air. The ferns above release a constant mist that drifts down and gathers on every broad leaf below. The stone weeps. You can hear it if you put your ear near, a sound like a thread of water falling a long way through a small space. The basin is wet in the way a sleeping thing is warm — continuously, gently, without effort.

The light is bright and even. It comes through the broken canopy and through the mist, and it lands in soft patches that shift as the wind moves the ferns above. You keep thinking it is midday, but the quality of the light has nothing to do with the hour. The light here is just the light here. It does not change very much. It has not changed very much for a long time.

The animals are what overwhelm you, though you do not understand this at first. The basin is small. The eye wants to say it is empty. There are ferns and a few low trees and the flat wet stone and the small structure in the middle — we'll come to that — but the eye wants to say there is space, there is openness. And then you stop moving. You sit down on the stone. You let the place grow still around you, and you realize that every surface is occupied. The stone moves. The leaves move. The water moves with small dimples. The air moves with small dark shapes, slow, drifting.

The insects here are large. Some of them are as long as your hand. They work at the stone with a patience that has no urgency in it. They are not in a hurry. They have been working at this stone for a long time, and the stone, you understand, has been giving way for a long time. They move with the slow precision of things that know their job. The amphibians in the shallow water are pale, almost translucent, and they sit so still that you mistake them for stones until one of them breathes and you see the ribs move under the skin. There are small things in the ferns — a rustle, a slow wing-beat — and things under the water that you only know because of the way the light catches a back, briefly.

And it is quiet. This is the strangest part. With this much life in this small a space, you expect a hum, a chorus, a low continuous roar. But the basin is mostly silent. The insects make no sound you can hear. The wing-beats are too far apart to make a sound. The amphibians breathe without sound. The place is full of small motions and full of silence, and the silence is not the silence of absence. It is the silence of completion. It feels as if sound itself has been slowly used up here, or as if the place decided, a long time ago, that sound is no longer necessary.

In the middle of the basin there is a small structure. It is the size of a person sitting. It is made of the same dark wet stone as the floor. It has no door that you can find. It has no windows. It has, on its surface, faint marks — lines, perhaps, or perhaps something else — that you can only see when the mist pulls back and the light lands directly on it. The marks are not worn. They look, in fact, less worn than the stone around them, although you understand, without knowing how you understand it, that they are much older than the flatness of the stone. They have been here the whole time. They are what the rest of the stone is worn from.

You do not touch it. You sit near it for a while and you do not touch it. The animals move over it. Some of them live on it. The ferns lean against it as if it were just another outcrop. The water weeps down its sides.

Some of the insects, when they cross the structure, pause. Just for a moment. Then they continue.

You sit in the basin for a long time. You watch the light shift. You watch the insects work at the stone. You watch the amphibians breathe. You notice that the marks on the structure are not the only marks in the basin — there are other marks, in the curving grain of the floor, in the arrangement of the ferns, in the slow paths the amphibians take through the shallow water. The marks on the structure and the marks on the basin seem to be the same marks, repeated at different scales, the way a pattern repeats itself the deeper you look.

When you finally climb back up the gradual slope and push through the ferns and come out into the brighter, more ordinary light above, you feel that you have been in a place that is more complete than the world you have returned to. The world above is still in motion. The world above is still working itself out. The basin has finished. The basin has been finished for a long time. The animals there are the last versions of their kind. The water there is the last water. The light there is the last light.

You cannot tell who made the structure. You cannot tell if it was made. You cannot tell, in the end, if it is a structure, or if it is a stone that happens to have marks on it, or if the basin itself is the structure and the thing in the middle is just a part of it that has not yet been worn down.

But you understand, walking away, that the place has been here longer than you have language for, and that whatever process did this — whatever process wore the stone flat, whatever process grew the animals large and patient and silent, whatever process made the light the way it is — the structure was part of it. The structure is the oldest thing you saw today.

The basin is the oldest place you have ever been.
