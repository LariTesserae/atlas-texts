# The Hollow Settlement

You are inside something. That is the first thing to notice — that this place is *inside* something. Not on top of a thing, not beside a thing, not under a thing in the casual sense of earth and sky. Inside the way a seed is inside its shell, the way a thought is inside a skull. There are walls that curve in toward you, and they have been curving in toward you for longer than the walls know how to stop.

The place is a hollow. A pocket in the body of something much larger — you can infer this from the fact that the ceiling does not match itself anywhere, that the floor has the dim suggestion of once having been one continuous surface before it settled into the soft pits and rises it now wears. You are in a chamber no more than a few hundred paces across, and yet it does not feel small, because most of what is here is not. Most of what is here is *between*.

## The Plants

The plants are everywhere. There is no other word. The walls are plants. The floor is plants. The dim suggestions of construction are plants. They are not green — there is nothing for green here, no light has reached this place in such a long time that the color has gone out of the word "green" entirely — but they are plants. You can tell by the way they have organized themselves around an old intention. They remember, in their slow way, that something was trying to be built here once.

They are dry things. Fibrous, papery, layered like the skins of an onion that never ripened. They are pale: the color of old paper, of dust, of bone left in a closed room. Some of them are furred with a fine grey powder that moves when air moves. Some of them have grown into shapes that suggest they once leaned against a wall and never quite unlearned the leaning. Their surfaces are matte. When you (if you could) brushed a fingertip across them, they would rasp.

They are built of no water. You can tell because nothing here rots. Nothing here softens. Nothing decays in the way that wet things decay. Instead, the plants here simply *age* — they thicken, they harden, they take on the slow geological composure of things that have been doing exactly the same thing for an amount of time you do not have a word for. A fallen leaf here does not become humus. It becomes a slightly more leathery leaf. Then a brittle one. Then a powder. Then a stain. Then a memory in the stratigraphy of the floor.

## The Built

Once — long ago, but not so long ago that the layers have forgotten each other — there was a settlement here. You can read it the way you read the rings of a tree cut across the grain. There were floors, and on the floors there were floors, and on those floors there were floors. Someone laid a foundation. Someone raised a wall. Someone added a beam. Someone built over the beam. Someone added a room over the wall. Someone tore out the room and built two smaller rooms. Someone filled in the smaller rooms and built a single larger one over the top, and then someone planted something in the new larger room — or rather, the room itself became the planter, the room and the thing planted in it having agreed, somewhere along the way, to stop being distinguishable.

The tools that made this place are in the palette too — small ones. Hand-size. Edge-tools, scraping-tools, perhaps tools for working the dry plant fibers themselves into rigid form. There is no metal here, or very little, or what metal there was has long since been taken or fallen away. The earliest layers were probably shaped with stone; the later layers were shaped with metal that didn't last; the latest layers — and these are the ones you can still almost see — were shaped with something in between, a dull cooper-color alloy that has gone brown and is flaking off the walls in scrolls.

This is what is left of the settlement. Not the settlement as it was. The settlement as the plants have made it. A city of dry paper walls, brittle floors, leaning lintels, doors that have grown shut. A village-sized hive of compartments, some still recognizable as rooms, most now simply *thickenings* — places where the layering grew dense and never thinned again. A settlement that was, and that is, and that continues to be at a pace so slow that to watch it is to watch stone.

## The Sound

It is loud here.

This is the strange part. The strange part is that a place this old, this dry, this dark, this empty, should be so loud. But it is.

The sound is not animal sound. There are no animals here — none have ever been here, you suspect, or if they were, they were not the kind that stayed. There are no footsteps, no wings, no small rustling of furred bodies moving through undergrowth. There is only the sound of the plants, and the sound of the plants is considerable.

It is a creaking. A deep, ongoing creaking, like the timbers of a ship at sea, except the ship is not going anywhere and the sea is not moving. It is the sound of dry fibers bearing weight. It is the sound of layered walls settling against each other by fractions of fractions of fractions of a millimeter per hour. It is the sound of the air itself moving through the chamber — and the air does move, in slow currents, because the chamber is not sealed against the great body it sits inside, and somewhere outside this pocket, temperatures shift, and the shifts propagate inward as sighs — and as the air moves, it pushes against the dry plant surfaces, and the dry plant surfaces push back, and the small frictions of these encounters accumulate into a kind of choir. There is no melody. There is only the constant sound of paper being slowly crumpled by a giant patient hand.

Sometimes — not often, but sometimes — a section of wall detaches itself and falls. This is louder. This is the loudest thing that happens here. It is a soft, catastrophic *fwump*, followed by the smaller sounds of pieces of the fallen wall continuing to break apart on the floor for several minutes afterward. Then the creaking resumes. Then, after a long while, another wall detaches itself, somewhere else, and the cycle continues.

You could, if you listened long enough, learn the rhythm of it. You could predict which wall would fall next, if you knew the geometry, and if you had enough time. The place rewards patience. It rewards very little else.

## The Light

There is no light here. Or almost no light. There is the dark, which is the dominant note — a dark so complete that your eyes, given long enough, would stop trying to invent shapes for it. There is the dark, and then there is, occasionally, a slightly less dark — a faint suggestion of warmth in one corner, or a faint suggestion of cold in another, that your eyes might learn to read as orientation but never as illumination.

The plants do not need light. They have not needed light for an age. They do not photosynthesize. They do something else — or perhaps they do nothing at all, and what they are doing is simply continuing, the way a wound continues to be a scar. They grew when there was something to grow toward. They grew when there were hands to lay them, or seed them, or coax them. They grew when there was an intention. Now there is no intention, but the growing has not stopped, because the plants do not know that. The plants are not the kind of thing that knows things. They simply continue to add layer to layer to layer, the way that sedimentation continues at the bottom of a sea that has forgotten it is a sea.

## The Air

The air is cool. Not cold — cold would imply a temperature gradient, would imply an outside that was colder than an inside. The air here is simply *cool*, in the way that the inside of a clay jar is cool, in the way that a stone that has been in shadow all day is cool. It is the temperature of a place that has not been touched by direct energy in a very long time. It has the slightly papery smell of dry organic matter that has not yet committed to being dust. It has, beneath that, the mineral smell of the larger body that surrounds this pocket — a smell of iron, perhaps, or of limestone, or of something that has no name on the surface of any world you have ever been to.

If you breathe it in, it tastes old. If you breathe it out, your breath does not visibly disturb it.

## The Rules

There are rules here. They are not laws that have been declared. They are laws that have been worn in by repetition, the way a groove is worn into a threshold.

Nothing falls fast. The slow falls, the fast does not arrive. The walls detach at their own pace. The creaking continues at its own pace. The plants add their layers at their own pace. You could wait here for what would feel like a very long time and see almost nothing happen. You could wait here for what would actually be a very long time and see almost nothing happen. The place is not in a hurry. The place was not in a hurry before you arrived. The place will not be in a hurry after you leave.

Nothing is alive here in the way that you mean by alive. The plants are alive in the way that a coral reef is alive, in the way that a forest is alive — that is, they are alive in aggregate, in the slow way that masses of small living things can be alive without any of them individually being aware of it. There is no mind here. There is no watcher. There is no malice, no welcome, no curiosity. There is only the continuing of the place.

Nothing has been forgotten, but nothing is being remembered either. The settlement is not a ruin. A ruin is a place that has been abandoned. A ruin implies a before and an after, a fall from a prior grace. This place has no before and after. It has only a *during*. It has been during for a long time. It will continue to be during for longer than you have patience for, and then it will continue to be during for longer than that, and the walls will keep falling and the creaking will keep going and the air will keep its cool mineral patience and the layers will keep adding themselves.

## The Ending

This is a place that has reached its final form. Not because it has stopped changing — it has not stopped changing — but because the changes it is making now are no longer the kind that produce new shapes. The settlement has been absorbed into the plants. The plants have been absorbed into the chamber. The chamber has been absorbed into the larger body. The larger body is itself, perhaps, being absorbed into something larger still, a thing whose name would mean nothing to you and whose shape your imagination could not hold.

You are in a hollow at the heart of something. You are in the quiet, loud, dark, dry, ancient, plant-filled, empty, full place where a small city once was built by small tools and then went on being built, by other small things, long after the small hands had stopped.

You are in a place that has settled into itself.

It is cool here. It is loud here. It is dark.

You can stay as long as you like. The place will not notice. The place will not mind. The place will creak on, and add its layers, and wait for the next wall to fall, and then wait for the wall after that, and the one after that, for a very long time.
