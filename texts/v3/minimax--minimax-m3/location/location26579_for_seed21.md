# The Steaming Saddle

The place is small enough to cross on foot in an afternoon, though no one crosses it on foot anymore. It sits between two ridgelines of dark basalt, a saddle-shaped depression about a kilometer across, opened into the sky like a cupped palm. The rock everywhere is sharp and recent. Less than two centuries ago, geologists would have said, the whole bowl pushed itself up out of the plateau; the edges of the rim are still fluted with fresh fracture planes that catch the light like wet obsidian, and the higher outcrops have not yet had time to round themselves. You can tell where the uplift stopped because that is where the lichen begins.

It is hot, here, in a way that has nothing to do with the sun. The air temperature at noon is a steady thirty-two degrees; in the wet season it climbs past thirty-six and stays there. The altitude — three thousand two hundred meters, give or take — is high enough that breathing costs a little more than it should, low enough that the heat is real and not theatrical. What cooks the saddle from below is something else.

## Water, Water Everywhere

Steam comes up through a dozen places, not as columns but as slow ground-hugging breaths. The bowl is mostly dry-looking — there is no standing lake, no obvious river — but the rock itself is weeping. Every surface that faces the sky is beaded with moisture that never quite dries. Condensation runs down the basalt in silver threads, pools briefly in the hollows of the fresh fractures, and vanishes into crevasses too narrow to see into. When you crouch and put your ear to the warm stone you can hear the water moving beneath you, a soft percussive whisper, the sound of a kettle that has been on for centuries.

Along the southern lip, where the rim is lowest, a hot spring has built itself a small apron of orange and cream mineral crust. The water there is almost too hot to hold a hand in — fifty-eight, sixty degrees — and runs a meter and a half before it disappears back under a shelf of cooled travertine. The mineral apron is one of the few things in the saddle that has had time to grow old; it is the color of a sunset that has been left out in the rain.

Twice a day, in the late afternoon, a rain comes through that has no name in the local weather reports because it isn't rain, exactly. It is the cloud on the western ridge deciding to fall. It lasts between six and eleven minutes. After it passes, every surface steams gently for an hour, and the air becomes so thick with humidity that sound travels differently, that your own footsteps come back to you a half-second late from the basalt walls.

## The Green That Bites

Vegetation is sparse, but the plants that are here are not timid. They arrive in clusters around the thermal vents and along the seams where water is most reliable, and they are aggressive about it. A stand of tree ferns has colonized the western shoulder of the bowl, their trunks black with moisture, their fronds unfurling in spirals so mathematically clean they look hand-drawn. Among them, a few stunted podocarps have taken hold — old-line conifers, slow-growing, their bark furred with epiphytic moss so thick it has become the bark.

Closer to the thermal features, the plant life is stranger. There are succulents here that should not exist at this altitude, fat-leaved things with waxy cuticles, drinking from the steam directly through their skin. There are orchids, pale and almost translucent, rooted into cracks that look too small to hold anything. The mosses on the cooler stones have a color that is not quite green — somewhere between grey and lavender — and they advance across the basalt in slow visible pulses, year by year, as if they are not plants at all but something else wearing a plant costume.

Nothing is tall. The tallest of the podocarps is maybe nine meters. Most of the saddle is open stone, dark, fissured, breathing.

## The Geometry in the Middle

In the center of the bowl, on a low platform of rock that has been — and there is no other word for it — *flattened*, there are structures. Three of them. They do not look like buildings. They look like buildings that have been compressed, or folded, or that are trying very hard not to be buildings.

The largest is a six-sided prism standing about four meters tall. From some angles it has the appearance of a basalt column; from other angles you can see that it is not basalt at all, that its surface is too uniform, that the grey-black of it has a depth to it like looking down a well. There is no door. There is no window. There is, on one face, a shallow depression that has collected rainwater and a single bright-orange mineral precipitate around its rim, and if you stand near it long enough you can feel a faint warmth coming off the surface that has nothing to do with the springs.

The two smaller structures are lower, more horizontal, set into the rock at angles that don't quite repeat. One of them hums, very softly, at a frequency just below the threshold of hearing — most people over forty cannot hear it; most people under forty can, and they tend not to linger near it. The other appears to be doing nothing at all, except that the moss has been unable to colonize a two-meter radius around it, despite twenty years of being there.

No one lives at the structures. No one maintains them. They were here when the basin was surveyed in 1987; they were here when the basalt was first dated at twenty thousand years younger than expected; they were here, presumably, before either of those facts. The three geometries are not on any of the maps that the geological survey has produced. They are on the maps that the meteorological agency has produced, but only as fixed reference points — *unmoved by storm or season* — because they are useful for that.

## What Moves

The fauna is sparse, and almost all of it is small. A high-altitude lizard, dark grey, basks on the warm rocks near the hot spring and vanishes into cracks when disturbed. There are insects — long-legged crane flies that seem too large for the air they are in, beetles with shells that reflect the orange of the mineral crust. In the fern grove there are birds: a single species, dull green, almost never seen, whose call is a two-note descending whistle that carries across the basin and stops, abruptly, as if cut off. There are no mammals. There are no snakes. Anything larger than a cat that has tried to make a living in the saddle has, over the decades, not stayed.

Once, in the late 1990s, a team of researchers set up motion-sensitive cameras near the prism for a season. They retrieved, three months later, eleven thousand photographs of the basalt, the ferns, the steam, the rain, and the night sky. In seven of them, taken at intervals between three and four in the morning, there was a faint smudge near the top of the structure — out of focus, the wrong shape to be a moth, the wrong color to be a bird — that was present in the frame and absent in the next frame, less than a second later, with no visible point of arrival or departure. The cameras were taken down the following year and the data was, as far as can be determined, never published.

## The Quiet That Isn't

For most of the day the saddle is the quietest place you have ever been in. The wind, which is constant on the ridge above, breaks over the rim and drops into the bowl as a slow circular current that you cannot feel but can sometimes see, in the way it carries the steam. The silence has texture. It has the sound of water running under stone. It has, very faintly, the hum of the second structure, which you may or may not be able to hear. It has, when the rain comes, the sound of ten thousand small impacts on basalt, which is louder than it should be, and then a long, dripping aftermath that lasts an hour.

At night the temperature does not drop as much as you would expect. The thermal mass of the rock holds the heat. The air is dense with moisture. Stars are visible but soft-edged, their light scattered by the steam that continues to rise even after dark. The geometry in the middle of the bowl, if you happen to be looking at it from the rim at the right moment, will be faintly visible — a darker patch against the dark, with sometimes, just sometimes, a thin line of light at one of its edges, as if something inside is checking the time.

You could walk through this place and see only rock and water and ferns. You could walk through it and feel that you had been somewhere that was older than it looked and stranger than it was old. Both are true. The saddle does not insist on either.
