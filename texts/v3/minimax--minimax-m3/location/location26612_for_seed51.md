# The Long Valley

The valley is older than the hills that hold it, though the hills do not know this, and the valley does not say.

It runs roughly north, about eleven miles from where the bedrock folds down into the peat, to where the river that is not a river pours itself into a basin that has no bottom anyone has found. The floor is almost level — a long, slow sinking of land that has been settling, with dignity, for a very long while. Peat and silty clay over fractured shale. Where the shale outcrops, water comes up through it in a hundred small weeping seams, and the sound of those seams — never one, always a hundred, never stopping — is what you hear first when you enter.

The light is dim. Not dark — you can see the distance. But the sky is the color of wet slate and stays that way most days, sometimes for weeks. Clouds sit low, often down into the crowns of the trees. In the afternoons, when the air is warmer than the ground, fog forms and lies in long bands along the contours, and a person standing at the right place can look up and see the fog moving against them as if it were a slow river running through the air. There is no sun in the ordinary sense. There is, instead, a soft pervasive glow, a little brighter than overcast, a little cooler than moonlight, and it comes from no direction you can point to. The shadows under things are not as dark as they should be. A knife left in the grass keeps its edge longer than it should.

## What grows here

The vegetation is dense in the way a library is dense — packed with things, layered, every cubic foot accounted for. Sphagnum mosses, red and amber and a green so dark it is almost black. Sedges in clumps tall as a man's waist. Alders along the drainage lines, their roots knobbed and ankle-twisting. In the higher, drier pockets, rowan and birch, leaning, never straight. Ferns — vast ferns — whose fronds uncurl in slow spirals that you can watch if you have a moment to waste. There is a kind of reed with hollow stems that rings like metal when wind passes through it, and there is so much of this reed that on a still day the whole valley hums at a low A, or sometimes a low E, depending on the season, depending on who is listening.

Almost nothing moves that has a backbone. A hare, sometimes, in the dusk. A snipe that you hear but never see. An otter, perhaps, in the deeper channels, but you will never be sure you saw it. The plant life crowds in to fill the space the animals do not take. There are lichens on the stones that grow in patterns which, if you look at them long enough, are not patterns. There is a small pale flower, almost white, that opens only when no one is looking at it, and closes — visibly, with a small motion — if you turn your head back toward it. People who have come here to study this have written papers about it. The papers are, on close reading, slightly different from each other in ways that are hard to account for.

## The sound

The sound is the first thing, and it does not stop.

Water, mostly. The weeping seams. The slow pourings through the peat. The bigger channels that sound like someone dragging a heavy cloth across a stone floor. But under the water-sound, or woven through it, there is another sound: a long, low, almost-musical tone that rises and falls but never quite stops. It is not the wind in the reeds. It is not a bell. It has harmonics. If you stand at the basin where the river-that-is-not-a-river goes down, you can feel it in the soles of your boots — not quite vibration, not quite sound, but something your inner ear is doing with the information. Some people who have stood there for a long time have reported that they began to hear their own thoughts in a voice that was not theirs. Some of those people did not come back the same. Most did. The ones who did not come back the same often said afterward that they had understood something they could not now un-understand, and that it had been a kindness, and that they were not unhappy, but that they had lost the ability to want to leave.

## The technology

There is no building in this valley. There is no wall, no post, no path that has been cut by anything that could be called a tool. The place is, by every measure, untouched. It is also, by every measure, full of a technology so far beyond what is currently understood that the word *technology* does not really fit. The word is used anyway, because the alternative is to invent a new word, and that would require explaining what the old one means now.

The basin where the river goes down is not a hole. It is an arrangement. The water enters it and does not come out, and the level of the water at the rim does not change, and yet there is always water entering, and the basin never fills. The arrangement is made of stone that is not quite stone — it has the weight and the coolness of stone, but if you look at it with a good enough eye, the surface has a structure, a kind of grain, that is finer than any grain geology has a name for. The grain does not repeat. It is also, in some lights, faintly moving. Not flowing. Not growing. More the way a thought moves across a face.

There are places in the valley where the soil gives back, if you press a hand into it, a faint warmth. Not geothermal warmth — there is no heat source the surveys have ever found. More like the warmth of something that has been held for a long time by something that is alive. The plants root very deeply here, far deeper than the same species grows anywhere else, and the roots, when traced, do something they are not supposed to do. They join. They form, in the dark below the peat, a single continuous network, with no gaps, no failures, no plant that is not, at its base, the same plant as every other plant in the valley. This has been verified. It has not been explained. The biologist who first described it refused, after his second visit, to call it a plant. He would only say *the green*.

## The breaking-down

The valley does not obey all the rules.

It is cooler than the surroundings — by about three degrees, year-round, even in the height of summer. The weatherman's instruments do not agree about why. The barometric pressure is steady at a value that is not, technically, possible at this altitude. Light bends slightly as it crosses the basin. The stars, on the rare nights when the cloud breaks, do not appear in quite the configuration that astronomy says they should. A compass needle held above the basin does not point north. It points, gently, toward the center of the long valley, as if north were a suggestion.

Time, also, is a suggestion. A watch that is wound here and taken elsewhere will keep correct time for about a day, and then begin to drift. A watch brought in from elsewhere will, within an hour, begin to run slow, and will continue to run slow for as long as it remains. People who have stayed for more than a few days report a feeling that the day is longer than it was, but only by a little, and only at the edges. A person who has stayed for a year and come back will say, if asked, that the year was a year, and will mean it, and will be unable to say more.

There are moments — not rare, but not frequent — when the valley does something that can only be described as *remembering*. A shape will appear in the mist that is not quite an animal and not quite a person and not quite a fold of the land. It will stay for a few seconds and then be gone. The reeds will ring at a slightly higher pitch for a while after. The peat will give back a warmth for an hour. And the long low tone under everything will rise, just barely, into a region of the audible that is uncomfortable for the teeth.

## The rule of the place

There is one rule. It is not spoken, because nothing here speaks. But it is felt, the way weather is felt, and it is this:

The valley is not a place to take from. You may come. You may look. You may sit for a while. You may put your hand into the peat and feel the faint warmth come up against your palm. You may stand at the basin and listen to the long tone. You may, if you are very quiet, hear the green think.

But you must not attempt to alter it. You must not take a sample that is not offered. You must not mark it. You must not bring a tool that is meant to change what it touches. It will know. It has always known. It was old when the hills were young, and the hills are not young, and the valley will be here, in its own state, in its own long settling, in its cool dim wet, when whatever brought you here is no longer something that anyone remembers.

The sound of water in the seams does not stop.

The light does not brighten.

The tone continues.
