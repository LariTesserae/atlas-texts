# The Tall Dark

To reach this place you would climb for days. You would start in some warmer country — a place where water lies openly on the ground and the light comes from a sun you can look at — and you would climb, by trail and then by scramble and then by the routes only certain animals know, until the air thins and the color of the sky changes from blue to something like iron. Somewhere up there the rain stops looking like weather and starts looking like architecture: long curtains of it hanging from the clouds to the ridges, sometimes touching, sometimes not, the whole sky a gray consortium of low ceilings. When you come at last over the last rise, the place opens.

It is a plateau. Or it is not a plateau — it is the upper surface of something younger, something that has not yet decided what it is. The stone is sharp. Ridges cut the ground like the backs of knives, in long parallel lines pointing roughly northeast, as if some force had dragged itself across the world not long ago and left its track. Between the ridges the ground is loose: shattered slabs, fresh scree, the kind of rock that breaks clean under your heel and the broken edge is still pale, unweathered, because it hasn't been there long. There are no rounded shapes. Even the boulders that have rolled down to rest are still mostly themselves — sharp-cornered, recently broken. The place has the feeling of a country that just now remembered it was allowed to exist.

And then you notice the towers.

The towers are why you are here, if you are one of the people who come here. Otherwise you are here because you were born here, in which case the towers are simply what the world is.

A tower is not, exactly, a tree. It is also not, exactly, not a tree. Its skin is bark, but the bark is thick as hide and warm to the touch even in the cold, and the grain of it runs in spirals that catch the dim light like the lines of a hand. At the base a tower is wider than a house; at the height of a house the trunk is already the width of a doorway; higher still it tapers but does not vanish, and you lose it in the cloud, and on a clear day you can sometimes see that it goes up farther than the eye accepts. A big tower is a small village in cross-section. A big tower is older than the people who live in it, and no one knows how old that is. They grow, but slowly. They grow upward and they grow inward, hollowing themselves as they age, making chambers — some of which the people use and some of which the people only enter when they have to.

Between the towers is a country of almost nothing. You can walk a full day and not pass one. The distances between them are the distances of small towns — measured in hours, not paces. If you shout from one tower you will not hear the echo of another unless the wind is very strange. The ground between is lichen and bare stone and, in the wetter folds, a low red moss that grows in mats you can peel up like a skin. It is cold here, always cold, but it is not dry — water is everywhere in the form of mist, of condensation, of the slow weep of ice that runs down the tower trunks in the afternoons and freezes again at night. After a rain the rocks are slick for hours. Sometimes the rain is rain. Sometimes it is freezing rain, and the towers are sheathed in clear ice that catches the rare good light and the whole forest becomes a room full of chandeliers. Sometimes it is snow, which the wind puts down in long drifts against the eastern faces of the ridges and takes back the next day.

You are very high here. The air is thin enough to feel as if it has weight, or as if you have less. The wind does not so much blow as conduct itself, with intent, down the long lines of the ridges, picking up a voice somewhere to the southwest and carrying it northeast for a hundred miles. It is never quiet. Even on the stillest day there is a kind of held breath, a pressure in the ears, and on any other day there is the long singing of the towers themselves, which is the most constant sound of the place. The wood of a tower is not stiff; it flexes a little in the wind, and at certain heights the hollows inside it act as a kind of organ pipe. A big tower in a strong wind produces a low note you can feel through the soles of your boots a hundred yards away. A forest of them, which is what this is, although it does not look like one, makes a music that goes on through the night.

The people who live here are few and they are widely scattered. They call themselves by a word that also means *those who listen*, or *those of the long wind*, depending on who you ask. They live mostly in the towers, not at the ground. They have learned to climb — not easily, not the way the climbers of warmer countries climb, but with the help of certain handholds the trees themselves grow for them, knobbed ridges that spiral the trunk in a regular pattern and that on a young tree are just bark-thickenings and on an old tree are staircases. They build in the wood, but they build lightly. A platform of lashed poles. A floor of split bark. A fire of twigs and dry dung laid in a stone ring at the inner edge of a chamber, the smoke finding its way out through a chimney the tree has made for itself. They use stone axes for the felling of dead wood only — no one fells a living tower, or only in certain rituals, and the consequences are said to be bad. They have knives of chipped stone and some of cold-hammered copper traded from far below. They weave ropes from the inner fibers of bark, soft and strong, and from those ropes they make nets, ladders, slings, the harnesses by which a woman can carry a child down the long trunk to the ground to gather water from a seep at the base.

At the ground there are almost no structures. There are the seeps — places where water emerges from between rocks and pools in basins that the people have widened with stone over many generations. There are the scree gardens, where they tend certain mosses and a small bitter lichen that they eat in winter. There are, here and there, in the lee of a ridge, the remnants of older camps: a stone circle, a few postholes, a scatter of broken bone. The wind has been filling these in. The wind is patient and the wind has time. Some of the circles you would not recognize as human work if you did not know what to look for. The wind and the moss are slowly taking them back.

There is fauna here, and there is a lot of it, though you might not see it for a long time because it lives mostly in the upper towers and the cloud. There are birds — large dark birds with long wings, the size of a child's arm each, that nest in colonies on the lee sides of the trunks and ride the wind all day, screaming at each other across the gaps. There are climbing things: a small six-legged grazer, slow and thick-furred, that eats the red moss and the bark-lichens and can hold motionless against the wind for so long that you will think it is a swelling of the wood until it moves. There are flyers smaller than your thumb that emerge by the thousands from certain chambers after a rain and make, briefly, a living fog of their own; their predators come up to meet them, and for an hour the air around a tower is full of the snap and zip of small kills, and then it is quiet again. On the ground there are few animals, but at night, if you sit still and don't make a light, you will see them: pale low things with too many legs, and a larger thing, dog-sized, that pads along the ridge tops in the dark and leaves the only tracks you can read in the morning.

The light is wrong here, or the light is only honest. In summer, which is short, there is a kind of dim brightness from a sun that never climbs high and that you can look at. In winter the sun does not come at all, or comes only as a brightening in the south at midday, and the light is the light of the moon and the stars and the slow mineral glow of certain lichens that grow on the underside of stones. In the long dark the towers are not invisible; they are suggestions, slightly darker shapes against the dark, and sometimes, after a particularly cold night, the trunks are sheathed in a faint ice that the morning's first pale light picks out along the spiral handholds, so that a tower becomes a dim sketch of itself drawn in white. People find their way home by following these lines.

You can be lost in this place easily. You can be lost in this place for a long time. The towers look the same from a distance. The ridges repeat themselves. The wind wipes out tracks in a day. People who die out here, if they are not brought back to a tower, are not always found. The wind fills the scree over them and the moss comes up and the lichen takes its time. The place absorbs its dead the way a slow river absorbs a leaf — without hurry, without ceremony. The people who live in the towers are not afraid of this, exactly, but they do not walk far from a tower at night, and they teach their children the names of the towers.

The names are the names. Each tower is a name. Each name is a story. A tower's name is sometimes the name of the person who first climbed it, or the name of the bird that nested there, or the name of the wind that broke one of its upper limbs in some remembered storm. A child is taught the name of the tower they were born in, and the names of the towers within a day's walk, and a few of the names of the towers further out, which they may someday visit if they are lucky. There are towers so far out that no one knows their names. The people sometimes speak of these as the *speaking towers*, because in a hard wind you can hear them from a great distance and you cannot tell which direction the sound has come from.

This is what is here. Stone, sharp and young. Water, in the form of vapor and slow ice. The towers, vast and patient and older than anyone. The wind, which is the loudest thing. The people, few and high up. The fauna, mostly above you, mostly out of sight, mostly heard. The dark, which is not absolute but is the background against which everything else is a small bright thing. The cold, which holds the place together.

If you stood at the base of one of the older towers in the long winter dark and put your hand against the bark, you would feel a slow pulse. Not a heartbeat — slower than that, more regular, more like breathing. The wood is warm. Something in the wood is alive in a way that is not quite the way you are alive, and it has been alive for a long time, and it will be alive after the people are gone, and after the names are forgotten, and after the wind has finished filling in the last of the stone circles at the ground.

You would not be able to see anything. The tower would be a darkness against the darkness. But you would feel the pulse, and you would hear, very far off, the long singing of the others.
