# The Intake

The chamber is the size of a small house, and it is full of screaming.

To call it a chamber is to be generous — it is more like a throat cut into wet stone, lined entirely in riveted steel and pale concrete so that not one square inch of the mountain's actual body remains visible. The construction is total. Even the floor is plated. Even the place where the ceiling meets the walls is sealed with a continuous bead of gunite that has begun, in some places, to weep a thin rust-colored sweat. You stand on metal. You breathe air that has been drawn through a metal grate. The mountain outside is irrelevant to the inside; the inside is the only thing that exists.

The sound is the first thing, and it is also the room. There is no distinction between the architecture and the noise — the noise *is* the architecture, the way a bell is its sound. Water comes down. Water comes down through a vertical pipe at the back of the room, a pipe you cannot see the top of because it disappears into the ceiling and the ceiling itself is shaking with what is passing through it, and the water that emerges is not falling so much as being *expressed*, forced out under a pressure that turns the falling column into a solid white rod that hits the receiving basin below with a crack that you do not so much hear as inhabit. You are inside the sound. Your sternum resonates. The rivets in the walls are vibrating; if you pressed a fingertip to one, you could feel the small individual shiver of it. The basin overflows. The overflow runs off through a curved trough at the floor and then falls again, somewhere just out of sight, into a deeper chamber, and that falling is a second voice, lower, a kind of wet bellowing that you feel in the arches of your feet. And underneath both voices, threaded through everything, the thin continuous hiss of condensation — water vapor in the cool air finding every surface and condensing on it, pooling on every bolt head, every flange, every inch of horizontal steel, every light fixture, until the whole room is filmed in a moving skin of moisture that gathers and rolls and falls as separate drops, and the drops have their own small impacts, and the impacts have their own small sounds, and there are so many of them that what you hear is a kind of white noise of falling, a constant fine rain of audio, and it does not stop, it has not stopped, it will not stop.

The light is low. Three caged bulbs hang from the ceiling on long stems, and two of them have hazed over with condensation inside their cages so that the light they throw is not light so much as a dim suggestion of light, an orange-tinted guess at illumination. The third bulb is out. The far wall is in shadow. The basin at the back throws up a fine mist that catches the light and turns it into something gauzy, and the mist moves in slow tongues across the room, drifting, and where the mist touches your face it is cold and tastes of iron and stone. The rivets down the seams of the walls glisten in rows, each one a small wet eye. The trough at the floor is dark with moving water. The white rod of the fall is the brightest thing in the room, and the room is not bright.

There is almost nothing else in the room. A maintenance walkway crosses above the basin on steel I-beams, with a handrail that is beaded with water. A pressure gauge is mounted on the wall nearest you, its face fogged, its needle steady, its little numerals illegible. A heavy valve wheel, painted blue, sits untouched on a standpipe, the paint worn from its rim where hands have gripped it. A wooden crate in the corner holds what may be spare parts; the wood is dark and swollen. There is a single steel door, shut, with a small wired-glass window through which you can see, faintly, the next room's lights, and water running down the glass from the condensation on the outside. These are the only presences. Everything else is space, and the space is full of sound and full of wet air and is otherwise empty.

The coolness is everywhere. It is the coolness of high places, of altitude, of air that has been drawn down through long shafts and tunnels from somewhere above the treeline, somewhere where the mountain is bare and the wind moves without obstruction. The metal under your hand is cold enough to hurt. The air on your face is cold enough to make you aware of your own breathing. The water, when it touches you, is cold, and the mist is cold, and the small wind that moves through the room — drawn by the falling water, by the pressure differentials, by the simple fact that there are openings somewhere — is cold. You are cold in the way that high places are cold, which is to say permanently, regardless of season, regardless of the weather outside the door.

There is no vegetation. There cannot be. There is no soil, no sun, no place where a root could grip or a leaf could unfold. The concrete is poured too thick. The steel is too smooth. The light is too dim and too brief. The sound would kill a seedling by vibration alone. The room is not hostile to plant life; it is simply indifferent to it, the way a pipe is indifferent to what flows through it. Plant life has no purchase here, and so plant life does not exist here, and the walls are clean of it, and the floor is clean of it, and the only living presences in the room are the bacteria that are surely setting up their colonies in the seam between the trough and the floor, and they are too small to count.

There are no animals. No birds. No insects. The wetness would draw them — the wetness always draws them — but the construction is airtight enough and the metal is too treacherous and the air is moving too quickly and the sound is too much. Nothing comes in. Nothing has come in for a long time. The room is sealed off from the world, and the world is sealed off from the room.

The mountain itself, around the chamber, is young. You can tell this by the way the construction meets the rock — where the steel plates do not reach, in the small access tunnels, the rock is fresh-cut, sharp-edged, a pale granite that fractures in clean planes and has not yet had time to weather or stain or soften. The chamber is new. The facility is new. Someone cut it into the mountain recently, in clean industrial blasts, and the water was turned on, and the water has been falling ever since, and the falling has already begun to do its slow work — the rust at the gunite beads, the haze on the bulbs, the swelling of the wooden crate, the slow oxidation of the pressure gauge's needle — but these are just the first signs. The room is new. The mountain is old. The room is built. The room is loud. The room is wet.

You stand on the metal floor with your hands at your sides and the column of water hammers the basin in front of you and the mist drifts across your shins and the sound is so loud that your own thoughts have to be louder than the sound to be heard, and most thoughts are not. You can shout, and the shout will be eaten and dissolved and returned to you as a meaningless vibration in the wet air. You can hear the mountain living around you, the water running through its hidden channels, the machinery of the intake pulling its weight of the world downward through the constructed throat of the room. There is a process somewhere above you, lifting water from a reservoir or a river or a glacier or a snowfield, and that process is sending it down to you, and you are receiving it, and you are passing it on, and the room is only ever the middle of a sentence. The water comes. The water goes. The sound is the sound of going.

Everything here is made. The room is made. The water is gathered. The sound is engineered. The cold is produced. The wetness is incidental — a byproduct, a leak, a fact of physics rather than intention, and yet it is the thing that makes the room feel alive, the thing that makes the metal breathe and the rivets glisten and the air taste of stone, and without it the room would be only a machine, and with it the room is a small wet cathedral to the process of moving water from where it is to where it is needed, and the only witnesses are you and the bacteria and the dim caged bulbs and the steady needle on the fogged gauge.

The pressure gauge's needle does not move. The water keeps falling. The mist keeps drifting. The mountain outside is high and bare and new and does not know the room is inside it.
