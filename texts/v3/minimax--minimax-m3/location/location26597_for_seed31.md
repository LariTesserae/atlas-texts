The valley was made recently. Not by any measure people remember — the rock remembers — but by the measure of stone, which is the only honest measure here. The ridges on either side still hold the shapes they had when they came up out of the ground. The fractures are sharp. The shoulders of the slopes are squared off, the kind of edges that catch on a sleeve and tear it. A million winters from now these ridges will be softened, rounded, patient. They are not patient yet. They are fresh, and they are still slightly too large for themselves, as if they had not had time to settle into their own outlines.

Water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. There are no streams to speak of. There is a channel along the floor of the valley, narrow and overfull, but mostly the water is in transit — moving as a thin skin over every available surface. It condenses out of the air, which is warm and heavy and holds it the way a cloth holds a dampness. The air meets the cold rock and gives up what it carries. The rock weeps. Every vertical face is beaded with moisture. The undersides of the eaves, the insides of doorways, the lower courses of every wall — all of them dark, all of them slowly giving back what they took.

The settlement follows the weeping. It is built on the drier ground between the seep-lines, where the slope flattens for a span, and it climbs up onto the outcrops where the bedrock shows through. The buildings are spaced, generously. Even in the closer parts of the valley you can see between them, past them, to the rock beyond. They are dressed stone on the lower courses, timber above — beams that have swollen in the wet until the joints are tight as bone. The roofs are heavy slate laid in overlapping courses, and from every roof comes a line of dripping, continuous, beaded, falling the same distance to the same dark stones beneath. The buildings are not tall. Most are a story and a half. The largest — a hall, raised on a fitted platform where the valley floor is at its driest — is two. Nothing here tries to be higher than the rock around it. The buildings are made from the rock around it.

There are paths between the buildings, paved with fitted stones that glisten. There are bridges — three of them, low-arched, stone — that cross the wetter channels. There is a wall that follows the inhabited side of the valley, rough and low, more a marker than a fortification. The construction is simple iron and simpler craft. Pegged timber. Hand-forged nails. Stones shaped against other stones until they sit right. Everything is plain. There is no carving. The walls do not speak. They are walls.

Vegetation grows where it can. In the pockets between rocks, where dust and leaf-fall have gathered into something that can hold a root. Ferns come up against the foundations of houses, broad and dark. Moss climbs a foot or two up the walls and stops, as if it has reached the level where the air is no longer quite wet enough. Small trees grow in the gaps between buildings — not in rows, not arranged, but singly, as if each one had to argue its case with the ground. Their bark is the color of wet iron. Their leaves are dense and do not move, because the air does not move. The grass is tussock grass, and grows in clumps the size of a hand or the size of a stool, never quite joining up. Between the tussocks the dark ground shows through.

The light here is the light of cloud cover. It is silver, flat, even. There is no sun that reaches the floor of the valley, or rarely — the ridges are high enough to hold weather against themselves, and the weather sits. Mist can be thicker or thinner. Some days the upper slopes are clear and the lower valley is in a slow, knee-deep fog that softens everything below the rooftops. Some days the fog fills the valley to the brim and only the tops of the ridges show. The light does not really change. It is the same light in the morning as in the afternoon, the same in one season as another. The seasons, such as they are, are marked mostly by the amount of water the air can carry.

There are no animals. This is the thing that takes a visitor the longest to settle into. There are no birds. The eaves do not rustle with nesting. The water on the paths is unmarked by tracks other than boots. The air is empty of wings. There are no insects in any obvious number — perhaps a few, in the stillest corners, but not enough to register. The settlement is not abandoned; there are people here, and there is the evidence of people, and there are paths that have been walked. But the rest of the valley's fauna is absent, or nearly so, and has been for as long as anyone asks about. No one here finds it strange. It is the way the valley is.

What sound there is, is the sound of water in slow transit. Drip from eaves onto stone. The thin patter of condensation running down a wall. The small, endless commentary of moisture moving across a quiet surface. There is no wind to push it faster. There is no thunder, because the weather is held by the ridges and does not break. Sometimes, very rarely, the larger hall creaks as the timber shifts in the wet, but the sound is so rare that when it comes, it is noted. Apart from that, the valley holds its peace. The same peace, in the same way, in the same volume, year after year.

The place is stable. It does not move toward anything. The rock does not wear down appreciably, in any human-scale time. The vegetation does not spread or retreat. The water does not rise or fall. The buildings are rebuilt as they need to be, in the same way, of the same materials, by the same methods. The population holds to a number, more or less. The valley is in equilibrium with itself. If you came back in a hundred years you would find it the same. In a thousand years, you would find it the same. The ridges would have softened a fraction. The paths would have been relaid. The tussocks would be in slightly different places. Otherwise, exactly as you left it. A young landscape that has already arrived at the place it means to be.
