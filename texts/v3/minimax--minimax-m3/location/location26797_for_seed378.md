I'll start with what's given and let the rest find itself as I go.

---

**The City Above the Dew-Line**

The air is wet the way air gets wet at altitude: not raining, not misting, but *saturated*, as though the sky itself has forgotten how to hold less than everything. Condensation beads on every surface that faces any direction. The metal railings of the upper gantries sweat. The stone steps of the amphitheater are dark with moisture that never quite becomes a stream. Drink from the air here, and you'd die of thirst anyway — it's all sulfur and mineral steam, the breath of something deep.

This is a high place. Not mountain-high; mesa-high. The kind of high where the horizon has been working on flattening itself for a long time, and you can see weather happening forty miles away, and weather is mostly what's happening. The city sits on a shelf of basalt and welded tuff, the kind of rock that was once lava and remembers being lava and has been keeping that memory in its crystals ever since.

---

**The Stone**

Black. Glossy in places where it's been polished by the boots and paws and the bellies of things that have crossed it. Pumice-grey and pockmarked where it hasn't. It weeps — literally weeps — a thin ferric trickle down every vertical face, leaving rust-orange stains that read almost like writing. The stone is hot to the touch on its upper surfaces where the sun has been at it, but a meter down into any crevice the heat intensifies rather than fading, a reminder that the shelf is a thin crust over something that is still cooking.

There are hot pools in the courtyards. There are hot pools in the cellars. There is a hot spring under the foundation of the eastern granary that the granary was, characteristically, built around rather than relocated. Steam rises from grates. The whole lower city has the soft, persistent haze of a Turkish bath.

The basalt takes the heat and gives it back at night, which is why the city is livable in the first place — the rocks bank the warmth the way a kiln banks warmth, and the wet air carries it. Nothing here is ever cold. Nothing here is ever dry.

---

**The Built**

It is a real city. Not a ruin, not a fable, not a tourist ruin — a working settlement of perhaps fifteen thousand souls, scaled up from a mining camp that grew up from a thermal spring that grew up from a fissure in the crust. The architecture is heavy and practical and layered in the way that settlements get when they've been continuously occupied for a long time without ever being demolished: new walls built against old walls, new roofs spanning what used to be courtyards, the bones of three or four different centuries showing through the plasterwork like strata.

The buildings are mostly dark stone, mortared with something volcanic that has gone pale with age. Roofs are flat and edged with low parapets — catchment surfaces, though for what I will come to. The streets are narrow in the lower town and widen into plazas as you climb, because the upper town is newer and the upper town had more time to think about itself. There is a civic plaza with a fountain. The fountain runs with hot water and smells of sulfur and the children play in it anyway.

Industrial infrastructure is everywhere, and everywhere it is *quiet*. I will come to that.

The streets are paved. The paving is irregular — setts of basalt and tuff, some of them fused into the bedrock beneath, some of them replaced a hundred times by different generations of civic pride. There are gutters along every street and the gutters run with thin warm water at all times, fed by a hundred seeps and condensate channels. In heavy weather — and weather here is heavy, the condensation tipping over into actual rain that falls briefly and hard and evaporates almost before it hits the ground — the gutters become little rivers and the plazas become shallow lakes and the city hums with the sound of water finding its way down.

---

**The Industry**

Geothermal. Of course geothermal. The whole settlement is a power plant wearing a city's clothing.

There are wellheads in fenced compounds at the edges of the inhabited zone. There are pipelines, painted in faded industrial colors, that carry steam down-slope to turbine halls. There are turbine halls — long, low, shed-roofed buildings with corrugated metal skins gone the color of old pewter — and beside each one, a cooling pond that steams perpetually, because the water coming out is hotter than the water going in, and there is no cold place to put it.

The turbines themselves are old and enormous and they do not move fast. This is not a place of spinning — the technology here is built around patience. Slow-spinning generators the size of small houses, turning at a rate you have to watch for several seconds to confirm. There are no screaming fans, no clattering valves. The whole industrial complex murmurs.

There is a refinery, or something that functions as one — a place where the dissolved minerals in the geothermal brine are precipitated out into great settling tanks. The tanks are open to the air. They are pale blue-green, sometimes orange, depending on what is precipitating that month. The mineral harvest is collected, dried in solar sheds, and shipped down the one road that leads off the shelf. The road is switchbacked and steep and you can see it for a long way, descending through nothing in particular, because —

---

**The Vegetation**

There isn't any.

This is the strange thing. This is the thing you keep noticing and then forgetting and then noticing again.

The shelf is mineral. The slopes below are mineral. The rocks are not even lichenous — the sulfur in the steam kills whatever might have tried. There are no trees, no grasses, no mosses. The gardens of the city are rock gardens in the Japanese sense, arrangements of stone chosen for shape and color, sometimes with a single dark pool for reflection. The parks are plazas with fountains.

And yet. And yet.

There is food. There must be food, there are fifteen thousand people. The food comes from below — from the lower country, from the river valley, from the agricultural terraces that begin at the foot of the long switchback road and extend, presumably, into a region where conditions are kinder to plants. Or it comes from *within*. There are fungal farms. There are vats. There are sealed buildings in the lower city that nobody talks about and the air around which smells of — something organic, something that is not quite the smell of any food I can name, something yeasty and dark and warm.

There are also the patterns.

---

**The Patterns**

On the walls of the refinery. On the floors of the turbine halls. On the basalt paving of the older streets, in places where no one walks.

Patterns. Not graffiti — too regular, too recursive. Not mold — they don't grow. Not corrosion — corrosion is random and these are not random. They are fractal. They are dendritic. They are branching shapes that look like rivers, or trees, or lung-tissue, or lightning, or the mycelial networks of fungi that don't exist here. They are dark against the stone, almost black, and they do not move and they do not spread and they do not, as far as anyone has been able to determine, *do* anything. They are just there. They have always been there. The city treats them the way cities treat old graffiti — as a feature of the walls.

No one knows what they are. The official position is mineral deposition. The unofficial position is more interesting. There are people who touch them and feel something and do not talk about what. There are people who have tried to lift them — chemical means, mechanical means — and have not been able to. The patterns go *into* the stone. They are in the grain of the basalt the way the grain of the basalt is in itself. They were there before the city.

---

**The Fauna**

This is the part that takes getting used to.

The city has animals. The animals are — present. They are everywhere, in the way that pigeons are everywhere in a city, except they are not pigeons. They are things. They have too many legs, or too few. They have the wrong number of eyes. They have skins in colors that don't occur in the natural world — the pale lavender of the thermal vents, the rust-orange of the brine, the pale near-translucent grey of the condensate. They move in ways that are not quite right. They are not frightening. They are just — *adjacent*. Like the patterns. Like the sulfur in the air. Like the heat.

They are accepted. They live in the eaves of the buildings and in the gaps between the basalt setts and in the warm condensate pools on the rooftops. They are fed, in some places — there are shallow ceramic dishes outside many of the doors, and the dishes contain something that is not quite food in any sense I can recognize, and the things eat it. They make no sound, or they make sounds at the very top of the range of human hearing, a faint thin glassy whistle that some people can hear and some can't.

There are children who keep them as pets. There are old women who feed them by the fountain in the civic plaza. There are cats — actual cats, the only thing here that looks like it belongs to the world I came from — and the cats and the things regard each other with mutual indifference, which is the most unsettling part, because cats regard almost nothing with indifference.

The things breed. They die. They leave tiny pale bones that dissolve in the rain.

---

**The Light**

Bright. The light here is bright in the way that high-desert light is bright, except that high-desert light is usually dry and this light is full of moisture and so the brightness has a softness to it, a haze, an opalescence. The sun is hot. The sun is very hot. Shadows are short and sharp at noon and lengthen into long blue-violet smears in the late afternoon. The wet air scatters the light so that every shadow has a colored fringe.

At dawn the whole shelf is the color of a bruise. At noon it is the color of a mirror. At dusk it is the color of a hot coal, because the basalt is giving back the day's heat, and the steam from the vents catches the last of the light, and the city looks, briefly, like it is on fire.

The light does interesting things to the patterns on the walls. They catch it. They hold it. They glow faintly after the sun is gone, a faint residual phosphorescence, for about an hour, every night, and then they are dark.

---

**The Sound**

Or rather, the absence of it.

I said the industry murmurs. I said the turbines turn slowly. I said the children play in the fountain. None of this is loud. The whole city runs on a kind of hush. The wet air absorbs sound. The slow machinery doesn't generate much. The animals don't cry out. The wind, when it comes, is soft and warm and continuous, not gusting, not howling. The rain, when it falls, falls straight down and is gone in minutes.

There is a sound. There is one sound, always, in the city. It is the sound of water. The fountains, the gutters, the seeps, the pools, the steam hissing softly out of every grate and vent — water is the city's voice, and it speaks low. People speak quietly to each other here, instinctively. Voices are pitched for the indoors. There is a custom of speaking softly in the open air, too, a kind of courtesy, or a kind of habit, or a kind of *deference to something*.

---

**The Air of Being Watched**

There is no word for it. The people here would not describe it that way. But the city has a quality — a *presence* — that I keep returning to. The patterns on the walls. The things in the eaves. The way the basalt holds its heat. The way the light lingers. The faint smell of the fungal farms. The quiet.

It feels like a place that is paying attention.

Not to me specifically. Not to anyone. To itself. To the long, slow, patient business of being here. It has been here for a long time. It will be here for a long time after. The patterns are patient. The turbines are patient. The things in the eaves are patient. The steam rises and condenses and falls and rises again. The brine precipitates its minerals. The rocks give back the heat of the sun. Nothing is in a hurry. Nothing has ever been in a hurry.

The fifteen thousand people live their lives in the middle of all this patience, and they are patient too, mostly. They work in the turbine halls and the refineries and the fungal farms. They walk in the rock gardens. They feed the things. They wash in the hot springs. They send their children down the long road to school in the valley, and the children come back for holidays, and the city takes them back without comment.

The patterns on the walls glow, briefly, in the dusk.

The water runs down the gutters, faintly.

The things watch from the eaves, with eyes that are not quite eyes, in colors that are not quite colors.

The city, above the dew-line, on its shelf of hot black stone, persists.
