The basin goes on. That is the first thing, and it takes a long time to admit it, because the eye keeps trying to find a wall. There is no wall. The pale crust runs to every horizon and the horizon runs on, and after hours of walking you understand that what you are standing on is not a place so much as a regime, a condition that the earth has entered into here, the way a sea enters into its basin, except that this is finished, mostly finished, mostly made.

The crust is white but not one white. From where you stand you can see at least four whites — the chalky brilliant white of the freshly precipitated halite where last week's tide-pool evaporated, the older cream-white of gypsum that has taken on a matte cast, the faintly blue-white of carbonate that catches and holds the morning light, and the dirty grey-white of places where the crust has cracked and the underlying sediment has been dragged up by thermal expansion into ragged crumbs. Between these whites there are seams. The seams are rust. The seams are the color of dried blood or weak tea, the iron-bearing groundwater having crept up through capillary action and left its load at the surface as the water fled back down into the deeper aquifer or out into the air. The seams make a pattern that is not quite a map. You could walk along one for a day and not see it end, and it would curve slightly as it went, following the buried contours of the bedrock that the crust has not yet entirely concealed.

Nothing has grown here. This is almost true, and almost true is the truth of the place. There are a few things. At the base of a low rise perhaps a kilometer away there is a shrub — a single shrub — that has the hunched posture of something that has been arguing with the wind for a hundred years and given up most of the argument. Its leaves are reduced to grey scales. It does not cast a real shadow. It is the only vertical thing in your field of view and you keep looking at it and then looking away from it because the flatness is what your mind needs to keep returning to.

The crust itself is built. This is the second thing, the thing you have to learn to see. The surface you are walking on is not rock, not in the usual sense. It is a construction. It has been laid down, layer upon layer, over a span of time that is difficult to hold in the head. Each layer is thin — paper-thin, fingernail-thin — and each was laid by the same general operation: mineral-rich water arriving at the surface from below or from the side or from the sky, losing its solvent, surrendering its load. Evaporation does most of the work. Crystallization does the rest. The crystals grow into each other and lock and become a floor, and the floor is buried by the next floor, and the next. In places you can stand and look down a section exposed by some recent fracture and count the floors — fifty, a hundred, the record of seasons that the basin has spent becoming itself. Microbes did some of this. They are still doing some of this, somewhere down in the damp zone a few centimeters beneath your feet, invisible and unhurried, precipitating carbonate and binding the crystals into a more coherent fabric than crystallization alone could manage. They are the only animates in the place. Above them, the heat. Below them, the deeper brine. Around them, the slow construction.

It is hot. The heat is not aggressive. It is the heat of a basin that has been holding the day for a long time and will go on holding it after the sun is down. By midmorning the air above the crust begins to move in slow columns that you can see because they refract the light; the columns rise, lean, dissolve, are replaced by new columns. There is no wind down here at your height. The crust has its own weather, which is mostly vertical. Late in the afternoon, sometimes, a dry breeze will come in from the south and lie along the surface for an hour, and you will hear it against the crust — a faint whisper, the sound of fine grains being lifted a millimeter and set down again — and then it will stop.

You can walk for a day and see no living thing move. You will not hear an insect. You will not hear a bird. The silence is not empty; it has weight, the way the heat has weight. It is the silence of a place that has been finished by processes that do not require witnesses. When sound does come, it is geological and small. A crack. A soft report, somewhere out on the pan, where the crust has expanded past its tolerance and a fracture has run for a hundred meters, the edges springing apart with a sound like a knuckle popping, very far away. Then nothing. The crack will be there tomorrow, and the day after, and a thin dark line will form along its floor as the iron-rich moisture begins its slow capillary ascent into the new exposure.

The dampness is intermittent and arrives without announcement. In the small hours, when the crust cools faster than the air above it, condensation forms on every surface — a faint mist on the salt, beads of water on the carbonate, a wet sheen on the iron-stained seams. By dawn there is enough moisture to darken the crust by a shade, and to make the surface gleam in the first light. Then the sun is up, and by midmorning the gleam is gone, and the crust is dry again, and the day is given back to the heat. You can taste the moisture when it is there. It is faintly mineral, faintly bitter, and it sits on the lips. You do not drink it. You do not stay out in it. You wait for the dryness, which is the longer state, which is the more characteristic state, which is the state the basin returns to like a held breath.

In the lowest pockets there is water. You come on these unexpectedly, because the crust appears continuous from a distance and only at the last few hundred meters does the light change and you understand that what looked like more crust is in fact a sheet of standing water an inch or two deep. The water is still. It reflects the sky so exactly that the boundary between basin and heaven becomes a polite suggestion. At the edges of these pools the crust is actively growing — you can see it, if you kneel and look, the new crystals forming at the meniscus where the water meets the air, tiny glassy rims creeping outward over the course of an afternoon. You can return to the same pool in a week and it will be smaller, ringed by a fresh white terrace, the water having retreated as its load was surrendered. In a month it may be gone, replaced by a blister of pure white salt that will eventually crack and become indistinguishable from the rest of the floor. The place is always being made, and always being unmade, and the making and the unmading are the same operation observed from two sides.

Vastness is the medium you are immersed in. You do not see it; you are in it. The flatness does not diminish with distance the way a mountain diminishes; it persists, identical, and the eye, denied the consolation of an object, begins to construct one from whatever it can find — a slightly darker patch, a faint rise, the single shrub. After a while you stop trying to find relief. The basin is the relief. The basin is the only figure, and you are standing on its surface, and the surface goes on.
