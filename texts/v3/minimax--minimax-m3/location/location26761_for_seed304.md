# Hollowtide

The basin is small. You can walk its rim in under an hour, if the path weren't constantly interrupted by stairs that go nowhere, by gutters that slope back toward the buildings they drain, by doorways that open onto other doorways. From above — though there is no real above, only the lip of the surrounding hills — the settlement appears as a single dark shape pressed into the floor of the hollow, like a coin dropped into wet clay. Smoke threads from somewhere near the center and fails to rise properly; it pools sideways, finds the low places, becomes part of the fog that has not lifted in anyone's memory.

Everything here is built from what the land refused. The houses are black stone, the only stone available, a basalt that the slow eruptions of some unremembered vulcanism laid down in brittle horizontal sheets. It splits cleanly when struck, which is the only reason the buildings are vertical. They are mortared with a paste made from the same stone ground up and mixed with the lime that leaches constantly from the walls of the basin — the hills are limestone under their skin of peat, and the water moving through them carries dissolved carbonate to every seam and joint, where it deposits in pale crusts that the inhabitants chip away each spring and pound into powder for the next season's mortar. The buildings are dark and the mortar is white and the contrast makes the architecture look sketched, as though someone had drawn a city in chalk on a slate and then built it.

The streets are not streets. They are the spaces left between buildings that could not agree on how much room they needed, and so the lanes have the irregular width of a geological fault — sometimes wide enough for a cart, sometimes a shoulder-width, sometimes a squeeze that forces you to turn sideways. Paving is absent except in two or three spots where generations of foot traffic have polished the exposed bedrock to a dull sheen. Everywhere else the floor of the basin is raw stone, and the stone weeps. Water moves through it the way blood moves through a bruise. You cannot find a dry surface if you try. Your boots are wet within minutes. The air is at all times a mist, fine enough to ignore but heavy enough to settle on wool, on hair, on the tongue.

## The Water

There is no river. There is no spring, exactly, though there are places where water comes up from below through joints in the rock, and the inhabitants have built small basins around these — half-buried stone bowls, lipped, the size of a cauldron, that fill in an hour and overflow in a day and feed the channels that feed the cisterns that feed the buildings. The water is cold, slightly sulfurous at the wells closest to the basin's southern fault, sweet and mineral at the northern ones. It is never still. Somewhere below, the whole water table is moving, and the hollow sits atop it like a ship on a slow current, and the stones respond — they groan at night, faintly, with the sound of a wet thumb drawn across a basin.

When it rains, which is often, the hollow becomes a single shallow pool for the duration of a storm. The drainage is poor by design: the inhabitants long ago damned the natural outlets so that they could collect the surface runoff, and now the lowest parts of the settlement sit in a permanent thin film of water, an inch or two, that reflects the undersides of the bridges connecting the taller buildings. Walking through the lower town at any wet hour means walking on wet stone with reflections of wetter stone beneath.

## The Vegetation

Almost nothing grows here. The volcanic ash that overlays the basin floor is thin and acidic, and the water table sits too high for roots to find purchase. Moss comes and goes — green-black in the wet season, white and brittle in the dry weeks of late summer. Lichens paint the stones in concentric orange and grey. There are three trees on the western slope, twisted rowans planted by someone for reasons now lost, and they are venerated without anyone remembering why. Lichen and moss are the principal crops. The inhabitants dry them, powder them, eat them in a thick grey porridge with whatever else is available.

The hilltops, above the mist line, are different. Up there, peat and heather, wind-stunted rowan, bracken in the brief warm season. The line where the basin flora gives way to the hill flora is sharp — you can stand with one foot in each, and the change of smell is immediate.

## The Fauna

There are not many creatures in the hollow. A few cats, descended from a long-ago granary infestation, pale and silent, who hunt the drainage channels for the small grey salamanders that live in them. Goats, kept on the upper terraces, whose hooves have worn the basalt steps into a polished concavity. A single herd of something — not quite pigs, not quite the wild boar they were once bred from — that root in the wet ash of the southern slope and are the principal source of meat.

Birds are infrequent and mostly passing through. The ravens from the higher hills come down in hard weather, sit on the chimneys, leave black feathers on the white mortar.

Insects are rare. The constant wet and the lack of vegetation mean there is little for them. In the warmest weeks, midges emerge from the standing water in clouds that arrive at dusk and disperse by morning, and the inhabitants smoke their houses against them and accept the trade.

## Sound and Silence

The hollow is quiet in the way that a held breath is quiet — not empty, but waiting. The water makes a low constant susurrus, the sound of it moving through stone being felt more than heard. Stone settling under its own soaked weight produces occasional percussive notes, as though someone below were knocking on the underside of the world. The cats make no sound. The goats do, on occasion, a low complaint that the walls catch and hold.

The loudest sounds come from human industry: the rhythmic pound of stone being dressed, the scrape of a cart-wheel on wet pavement, the calls of the night-soil collectors who work the lower town at first light. In the late evening, from the highest building — a tower, half-finished, that rises a single story above its neighbors and serves as a watchpost and a drying-platform for medicinal mosses — a horn is sounded. One note, held. It does not echo; the mist absorbs it after a few hundred yards. The note is the day's ending.

## The Built World

The settlement is densely packed against itself in a way that is partly necessary and partly aesthetic. The buildings share walls freely. A person might step from their kitchen into their neighbor's kitchen without passing through a hallway. The roofs are flat stone slabs, slightly canted, weighted with stones against the wind. Chimneys are rare; the fires burn in pits sunken through the floor and vented through a hole in the roof, and the smoke does what it can.

The technology of the place is that of stone, fire, and patience. Metal exists but is expensive — it comes up from the southern lowlands in small consignments, and the smith is a quarter-time occupation for the only person in the hollow with the skill. The tools are stone, bone, and sometimes bronze. The carpentry is in pine driftwood hauled up from the coast three days south, and a cedar beam is a generational thing. Clothing is wool, when wool can be had, and a felted cloth spun from the goats' underhair when it cannot.

The roads in or out of the hollow are three: a paved switchback to the east, partly collapsed; a stone stair to the north that ends abruptly at a peat-bog and continues as a causeway of uncertain date; a tunnel to the south, half-flooded, that everyone agrees leads to the sea but no one now living has traversed.

## The Weather and the Days

The light in the hollow is the pale grey of a photograph left in the sun. The sun, when it breaks through, comes in low and sideways and casts long shadows up the walls of the buildings, and the wet stone gleams, and the white mortar lines glow, and for an hour the place looks almost new — sharp-edged, recently made, the basalt sheets sharp enough to cut. Then the cloud returns, or the mist thickens, and the hollow resumes its usual aspect of a thing long-since-finished and slowly being reclaimed.

In winter the light goes early and comes late, and the hollow is lit at night by the orange of the floor-pits, which glow through the cracks in their stone covers and give the effect of a city built on a banked fire. In summer, the light lasts nearly to midnight and the work continues by it, and the wet stone reflects the orange back, and the whole basin becomes warm and loud with the labor of drying the lichen harvest.

Rain falls, on average, two days out of three. The inhabitants have terms for the intensity of rain that outsiders find excessive — *ground-mist*, *roof-talk*, *knee-water*, *the half-day* — and they plan their lives around these distinctions.

## The Unusual

The thing that makes this place strange is not any single feature but the combination, and especially the way the water behaves. Nothing in the hollow ever seems to dry. Cloth hung out to dry dries, but slowly, and with a faint mineral crust left on it. The stone itself seems to replenish the surface water faster than it can drain, as though the basin were a great shallow bowl set over a second, invisible basin of water that is always rising. Wells dug in the floor of the settlement must be re-poured with surface water or they go brackish within a day. The inhabitants do not speak of this. They have learned to live within the wet the way one learns to live within grief — by not naming it constantly.

There is also the matter of the green. On certain stones, in certain lights, after certain rains, the basalt shows a faint iridescence — a petrol-sheen, an oil-on-water effect that moves as you move. It is harmless. It is the signature of the mineral. The children are told it is the reflection of the city from another city, somewhere below, and that the water will one day rise and join the two. They do not entirely disbelieve it.
