# The Spore-Plain

The plateau is older than weather. It sits at a height where the air begins to thin toward something like memory — not yet space, but not quite atmosphere, a stratum where pressure has thinned to the thickness of a held breath and the sky begins to look back at you with its own weight. Cold light pours across it in sheets, the kind of light that doesn't warm what it touches but only illuminates, the kind that belongs to a sun further away than warmth can survive the journey. The horizon is a long pale bruise.

Nothing here is built. Nothing is settled. There are no walls, no terraces, no shrines, no carved stones, no roads that were first roads before they became something else. There is no *first*. There is only the going-on of things.

The ground is a single living carpet. That is the first thing to understand and the last thing to be believed. From the place where you stand — wherever you stand, wherever anyone has ever stood, here — outward in every direction the surface is composed of plant. It is not the green plant of water-rich worlds. It is dry, fibrous, slow, mineral-yellow, the color of old paper or dried honey or the inside of a beehive that has been baked for a thousand years. It stands in clumps, in tussocks, in long parallel ridges that march outward to where the curvature of the planet takes them. Between the clumps there is dust. The dust is also plant: spores, shed husks, the fine powder of generations. When the wind takes the dust it does not take *plant* dust and *mineral* dust separately. It takes the dust of one place, which is all the dust of all places.

There is no water. There has not been water in any form for so long that the word for it is only held in the mouths of the things that come to take it. The plants do not drink. They photosynthesize off a star whose spectrum has shifted, off trace hydrocarbons in the thin air, off the radiant chemistry of their own decomposition — and they have been doing this for so long that the planet's atmosphere itself is now a kind of exhaled breath, a slow accumulation of their outgassing over geological time. The air smells the way a library smells: dry, sweet, faintly alive.

Across this plain the machines move.

They are not made things. They are also not alive. They are somewhere between — or rather they are *of* the plain in the same way the plants are of the plain, only they burn. They roll, they crawl, they drag themselves forward on jointed limbs like enormous metallic insects, or they glide on skirts of superheated air. Their bellies are wide and toothed. Behind them the carpet is stripped to a thin gray ash, and from the ash the next generation rises within a season. The machines eat the plain and the plain grows back from the place where the machine was, the way a tongue grows back where a tooth was lost. They have been doing this for as long as anyone remembers and probably longer. No one built them. They are part of the weather. They are the way this place *works*. They are the way the plain is allowed to remain a plain, by being constantly cropped to a height it cannot exceed. When a machine fails it is not repaired. It is left where it stopped, and the plants cover it within three years, and within ten it is a hill, and within fifty the hill has been ground flat again by a machine that was passing through on its own errand and did not notice.

The fauna are here in numbers that cannot be counted from one place but are felt, all the time, as a presence in the air. They fly. Some of them are large — long-winged, slow, riding the updrafts that come off the warm ridges and the cold valleys. Some are very small, and they stream past you in bands, and their wings catch the light and the light is broken into a thousand pieces that drift down through the air for hours afterward like slow snow. None of them need to drink. They are made of the same dry chemistry as everything else, and what they eat is each other, mostly, and the spores, and the small dead machines. Their sound is part of the sound. You cannot separate them out. The plain is loud.

The plain is loud because it is loud. The wind never stops. It comes across the plateau in long rolls, in standing waves, in rolls that break over the ridges of plant the way ocean swells break over a reef — slow, huge, white at the top. There is a constant sound that is the wind against the plant, and a constant sound that is the wind in the air above the plant, and these two sounds are slightly out of phase with each other in a way that the ear notices but cannot name. Underneath them there is a third sound: a grinding, a tearing, a slow crumpling, and that is the machines. Sometimes the machines' sound rises into a kind of singing, a harmonic that is not melodic but is unmistakably *made*, the way a bridge sings when wind passes through it. The fauna add their calls. None of these sounds are quiet. They are layered. They do not blend. They are a place made of sounds the way another place might be made of stones.

And then there is the *motion* of the place, which is its own sound if you listen right. The plants grow. The machines reap. The fauna hunt, mate, die, and are eaten. The dust moves. The wind moves. The light moves — not because the sun moves much but because the atmosphere moves, in long slow currents that are visible from the ground as faint shimmerings along the horizon, the way heat shimmers above a road. The whole plateau is in constant low-grade rearrangement, like the surface of a slow river, like the skin of something breathing in its sleep. Nothing about this place is steady. Nothing about this place has been steady in a long time. It is turbulent the way weather is turbulent: not in any one part, but as a whole, in a way that has been going on so long it has become the form of the place itself. The plateau is a process wearing the costume of a place.

The plants are arranged, in any small region, in something like a pattern. They are not random. They are arranged the way coral is arranged: by their own growth, by the death of their predecessors, by the wind that favored certain shapes and killed others. The ridges are not arbitrary. They run along the prevailing wind. In the valleys between the ridges the dust collects and the small fauna nest. On the ridges themselves the machines take their paths, year after year, and the plants grow back along the tracks. The pattern is the equilibrium of a thousand years of these four forces — wind, plant, machine, animal — each one acting on the others. The plateau is not balanced the way a still thing is balanced. It is balanced the way a fire is balanced: a held thing, a maintained thing, a thing that is only itself because it has not stopped.

It is bright here. The light is not warm. It is the light of a sun that is bright in a thin atmosphere, the light that comes when there is almost nothing in the way. The plants turn their faces toward it — some of them, the photosynthesizing ones — and the machines cast long shadows and the long shadows move across the ridges. At certain hours the light passes through the airborne spores and the air itself becomes faintly amber, faintly alive, and the shadows on the ground are blue.

There is no human presence. There is no human absence either. There is no mark of human anything: no path, no ruin, no bone, no legend. The place is not empty because no one is here. It is empty because nothing here requires a witness to remain itself. The machines do not require it. The plants do not require it. The fauna do not. The wind does not. The place continues the way a river continues. If you stand in the middle of it and close your eyes you are not lost because you were never the point.

If you open your eyes the light is still on the plant and the plant is still on the dust and the dust is still in the wind and the wind is still in the air and the air is still on the plateau and the plateau is still under a sun that is far away and bright and does not know your name.
