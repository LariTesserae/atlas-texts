# The Ridge of Engines

The water is black at the top of the ridge and only slightly less black below. It is two miles deep here, cold the way deep water is cold — not the cold of winter, but the cold of having never been warm, the cold of pressure and distance from the sun. The seafloor rises in a long spine for hundreds of kilometers, sharp as if carved yesterday, because in geological terms it was carved yesterday: a young ridge, still sharp, the basalt not yet softened by time. Peaks jut upward like broken teeth. Some reach within eight hundred meters of the surface. None break it.

The light at this depth is a faint uniform grey, neither day nor night, the color of the last memory of sunlight. It does not change. There is no day here. There has been no day for the entire memory of the place.

What changes is the sound.

It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is the loudest sound you have ever heard, and it has never once stopped. There is a low grinding, the patient chewing of the planet's own crust by machines bolted to it. There is a higher whine, a constant oscillation at the edge of hearing, sonar pings stacking on sonar pings into a sheet of noise so thick you could stand on it. There are pumps — vast pumps, the size of cathedrals, drinking slurry from below the basalt and pushing it up and out somewhere else, somewhere you will never see. The water itself vibrates. Your bones vibrate with it. If you put a hand against any solid surface, you can feel the machinery talking back through the rock.

The buildings are scattered along the ridge like dice thrown at random by some bored god. A cluster of three squat cylinders, a single tower standing alone on a peak, a long horizontal shape that might once have been a ship but now grows barnacles of pipe and strut. They are unlit except for a single steady orange in each — small fires, perhaps, or indicator lamps. From them, cables trail away into the dark. Pipes walk across the rock on stilted legs. Nothing moves between them. Whatever crew ran these things is not here. The machines do not need them. The machines have not needed them for a long time.

The fauna is the part that does not fit.

It is everywhere. In the black water between the peaks, shapes move — long and pale, with trailing filaments, with too many eyes or none, with bodies built for a place that should not sustain them. They drift past the windows of the towers in slow columns. They spiral up from the crevices in such density that the basalt beneath them is no longer visible. A snow of small things, a blizzard of small things, falling and rising at once.

There is no plant at the bottom of this world. There has never been any plant. The food chain here begins in the vents and the filters and the slow leak of hydrocarbons from the rock itself, and what blooms on those dark roots is life, but not life you would recognize as such — blind, pale, immense in the small, the small immense. Some of them eat the noise. That is the strangest part, and it is true: there are creatures here with cilia tuned to the vibration, that sieve the pressure waves for something in them, the way a plant sieves light. The ridge hums, and they feed.

Because nothing changes.

The machines have been running since before the first of these creatures was born. The fauna has been here since before the machines. Or the fauna has been here only since the machines. It is impossible, down here, to tell which came first, because nothing here changes. The rock does not weather. The water does not warm. The fauna does not grow or diminish. The machines do not stop. The sound does not vary by so much as a single decibel from one century to the next. There is no season. There is no storm. There is no current you can detect by eye.

A thing that does not change is not alive. A thing that does not change is not dead. A thing that does not change is just a thing.

The ridge stretches away in either direction into a dark that the orange lamps cannot reach, and the sound rolls along it like a train that never arrives, and the fauna drifts in the columns of the noise like leaves in a river that has forgotten which way it is flowing, and somewhere in the towers something keeps a single small flame burning, and the flame has not flickered in a very long time.
