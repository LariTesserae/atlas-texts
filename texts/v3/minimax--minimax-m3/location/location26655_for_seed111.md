# The Cirque of the Sharp Wall

The place is a bowl.

It sits just below the summit ridge of a mountain range that is still, in geological terms, deciding whether to be a mountain. The walls around it are young — black basalt, dark-grey andesite, seams of obsidian that catch what little light arrives and return it as a thin shine. The edges have not yet been softened by weather. Every arête is a blade. Every ridge is a fresh fracture. The cirque itself was cut not by the patient work of centuries of ice but by a single event — a chunk of the upper mountain broke loose in some not-very-long-ago season and slid, taking the lip of an older bowl with it, and what remains is this: a hollow with no smoothing in it, sharp-walled, the size of a hamlet if you measured from one rim to the other, the size of a cathedral if you stood on its floor and looked up.

The elevation is such that the air has lost most of what it carried down in the valleys below. It is thin, and it is cool. Breath does not leave the lungs as a warm puff; it leaves as nothing visible. The cold is not a killing cold but a steady, low-temperature kind that sits in the joints of stone and never quite leaves. Snow arrives intermittently — sometimes as proper flurries that whiten the floor for a few hours and then are blown or sublimated away, sometimes as a single dark cloud passing overhead that drops a fingerful of crystals and moves on. Condensation gathers on the obsidian at dawn. A stream threads the lower part of the cirque and disappears into a crack.

There is almost no vegetation. What there is, is the patient kind — orange and grey lichens that have colonized the more stable faces, hair-thin mosses in the cracks where meltwater seeps, a few alpine grasses that have taken hold in the wind-sheltered pockets near the floor. Nothing that rises above the ankle. Nothing that suggests it knows what a tree is.

The light is poor. The peaks to the east shadow the cirque in the morning, the peaks to the west shadow it in the afternoon, and in between there is only what filters through the high cirrus that most days has come down to settle like a thin ceiling across the whole bowl. The light that arrives is blue-grey, or violet-grey, or, on the best days, the pale lemon of sun seen through ice. The shadows on the floor are not black — they are a deeper version of the light around them, and they merge into the rocks until the rocks are shadows and the shadows are rocks. The whole place operates on a low-contrast budget. To move through it is to walk through an argument between dark and darker.

The fauna is the place.

The walls are lined. Every ledge has something on it. Every crevice has something in it. The floor is not the floor; it is the space between the walls, and that space is taken. The creatures are dense. They are layered — the older ones on the higher ledges, the younger ones lower, the juveniles on the floor in clusters, the eggs and the small new things tucked into the deepest cracks where the cold is least. The population is in the tens of thousands. The population is overwhelming. The population is what the cirque is for.

What they are is not quite bird. They have wings but they do not use them for true flight; they launch from the walls and they glide, and they land, and they walk, and they walk more than they glide. Their feathers are dull — the brown-grey of the rock, the off-white of the droppings, the dark rust of the lichen. They are the color of the cirque. From a distance, they are not visible. From a distance, the cirque looks empty, and from up close it is full, and this is one of the strangenesses of the place.

The strangeness.

The creatures break the obsidian. They break it deliberately. They take a shard in their beaks and they carry it to a ledge where they have nested, and they set it down next to other shards. They make arrangements. The arrangements are not random. They are arcs and lines and, in some of the older nests, spirals that tighten toward a center. Some of the arrangements are large — a meter across, set in a low place where many ledges converge. Some are small — a single row of three shards on a high ledge where only one creature tends them. No one has watched one being made from start to finish. When they are made, the makers leave. The arrangements persist. New creatures come to old arrangements and tend them, replacing a shard that has been lost, adding one, taking one away. The arrangements accumulate. The arrangements are old.

There is no building here. There are no structures, no tools of the kind a hand would recognize, no woven things, no fired things. There is only the rock that is here and the rock that has been moved, and the difference between the two is small but it is there. The tech of the place is the beak and the claw and the long inheritance of knowing which stone to break and how to set it. The tech is old. The tech is patient.

The sound is loud. The sound is layered. There is the wind, when it comes — and it comes often, pouring over the rim and dropping into the bowl with a sound like a single held note. There are the calls, which are constant — a high, shrill, near-musical piping that does not come from one creature but from many, in waves, with rests between the waves, and in the rests one can hear the next wave preparing. There are the wing-beats, which are not wing-beats but the clatter of small bodies moving on stone, hopping, scraping, the soft pad of feet on lichen and the harder tap of beaks on rock. There is, somewhere up among the higher ledges, a deeper sound — a slow, resonant hum that may be the wind in a particular crack or may be the creatures themselves in some collective register. It is not clear. It is not clear what any of it is for.

The dynamic is slow. The mountain rises, millimeter by millimeter, as it has risen for the few hundred thousand years since it began. The cirque deepens, fractionally, as the glacier in the upper bowl — barely a glacier, more a permanent snow — slowly removes what the cirque has of its lip. The lichen grows at the speed of lichen. The creatures live thirty years each and are replaced by their own. The arrangements of shards accumulate a new shard every season or two. The stream cuts its channel a little deeper, a little wider, over decades.

And yet nothing is static in the cirque during a day. The calls rise and fall. The juveniles swarm the floor in a slow tide that washes from one wall to the other as the light changes. The wind shifts. The cloud cover varies. A new shard is added somewhere. An old creature falls from a high ledge and the population absorbs the loss within the hour. The cirque is busy and also it is slow. The cirque has many rhythms at once and the rhythms do not all agree.

To be in the cirque is to be in a place that is dense with something and to not be able to see what the something is. To be in it is to be in a dark, sharp, cold bowl full of sound, and to know that the sound is a kind of language, and to not know what is being said. To be in it is to suspect that the place was not always here and is still in some sense arriving. To be in it is to see, in a moment when the light lets you see, that the floor is patterned — patterned with the shadows of the creatures, with the white of their droppings, with the dark of their shards — and that the pattern has a kind of coherence that the eye can almost take in and the mind cannot.

The place is strange because it is so full of something and the something is not visible as a single thing. The place is strange because the creatures are working in rock, slowly, in patterns no one understands. The place is strange because it is new and it is full and it is dark and it is loud and it has been all of these for only a little while, geologically, and yet it feels older than that, and you cannot tell whether the feeling is correct or whether the cirque is doing something to your sense of time.

The cirque does not explain itself. It is here. It is sharp and dark and full of sound and slow to change and full of life and full of something that looks like work and not quite like anything you have a word for.

The cirque continues.
