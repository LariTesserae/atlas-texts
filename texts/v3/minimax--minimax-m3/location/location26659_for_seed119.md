# The Hall of the Long Damp

A room, mostly. A room that has been made entirely — no natural surface shows itself anywhere, and no surface goes unfinished. The floor is laid in hand-fired hexagonal tiles, each one pegged slightly off, so the pattern is almost-regular and your eye keeps slipping between them. The grout between them has gone soft and dark with the moisture that lives here, and in the northern third of the floor the joints have moss in them — sparse, pale, the kind that thrives on damp stone and patience. The walls are stacked slate and river-rock fitted dry then mortared with lime and pitch, and at every third course there is a band of bone: knuckles, vertebrae, the small polished skulls of things once eaten, inlaid flush with the masonry. The ceiling is planked cedar, pegged with bronze, every board numbered on its underside in a script no one living reads. Above the cedar you can hear weather. Below it the room holds itself.

It is long and low — thirty paces by twelve, with the roof vaulting inward at two points so that a tall person could almost touch the apex with a raised hand. Cathedral-scale but compressed, the way a held breath is compressed. The proportions are off in a way you can feel in your shoulders without being able to name. The corners pull the eye; you keep catching yourself looking toward them.

**Water** is everywhere here, and everywhere near the surface. The walls weep. There is always a bead of condensation traveling down them, finding the bone-bands and pooling briefly in the eye-sockets of the small skulls before continuing. The ceiling drips in an irregular rhythm that the frogs have learned to anticipate. When it rains above — and it rains often, you can hear it as a drumroll through the cedar — the drips become a steady patter and the floor pools spread until most of the southern half stands in a shallow sheet, an inch deep, mirror-still in the long pauses between drops. The air is so wet you taste it on your teeth.

**Vegetation** is sparse and has had to argue its way in. Moss in the grout. A single drooping fern rooted in a hairline crack between two stones near the second bone-band, leaning out over a niche that holds a clay figure faceless from years of fingers. Pale lichen on the ceiling beams, growing in patterns that almost resemble writing and then stop. A small pale fruiting body — fungus or nest, hard to say — emerging from the bone-band at the far end of the hall. Nothing that would shelter you. Nothing you would lean on.

The space is **empty**, and this is its first quality. Most of the volume is wet air. You could dance in it. You could lie down across the width and your head and feet would not touch the walls. The emptiness is the point of the room, not its failure. Whatever was meant to happen here needed room to happen in.

But the emptiness is loud and it is full of **fauna**, small and abundant, living in the margins. Plum-sized frogs, blue-gray, sit in every puddle and on every horizontal surface and call — their throats ballooning, their answers returning from the walls a half-beat later. Silverfish-things the length of a finger crawl along the bone-bands and faintly glow, a phosphorescent cool blue, enough to see a hand by on the darkest hours. Moths the color of wet ash powder the ceiling and fold themselves away when anything moves. Beetles with ridged backs click as they walk, a sound like pebbles dropped one at a time into a basin. Something with claws scrapes in the gaps between the cedar planks overhead — slow, patient, at home. Once in a long while a slow heavy flapping passes above the planks, and once in an even longer while a fish flips in one of the deeper pools, which no one has ever satisfactorily explained.

**Light** is dim. It enters through a slatted bronze grating set high in the east wall, so on a clear day the room fills with bars of pale gold that shift across the floor as the sun moves; on a cloudy day there is almost no light at all, and you navigate by the silverfish-glow and the dull gleam of bone. Candles, when lit, look embarrassed — their flames thin and uncertain, leaning away from some current only they can feel.

The **sound** never stops. Dripping, croaking, clicking, scraping, the muffled drumming of rain above, the rustle of moths taking off and settling, and underneath all of it a low rushing — water moving through hidden channels behind the walls, you can hear it pressing through the stonework — and under that, a hum you feel in the soles of your feet more than you hear, steady as a tuning fork.

It is **strange**, but quietly so, the way a sentence is strange when one word is wrong. The bone-bands are not ornamented, just placed. The clay figure has no face. The tiles are almost right. The frogs do not flee when you approach; they stop calling, watch you, and then resume. Something about the room makes you not want to stay long but not want to leave either. People who come here tend to come back. They leave small things in the niches — a bowl of water, a beetle on a leaf, a finger-bone wrapped in linen — and they sit, and they listen, and they go.

**Tech** is primitive and total. Every tile was hand-shaped and fired in a wood kiln. Every stone was quarried with bronze wedges and fitted with a chisel. The cedar was cut with bronze axes and pegged with hand-hammered nails. The mortar was mixed by kneeling people in wooden troughs. The bone was boiled, scraped, polished with river-sand, set into the wall while the mortar was still wet. You could replicate any of it, given materials and decades. You would not build the room the same way. Whoever built it knew things that aren't written down.

The room is **active** without being busy. The condensation is always traveling somewhere. The insects are always breeding and dying. The moss creeps a finger's width in a year. The fruiting body at the far end is bigger than it was last season, or smaller, no one is sure. The frogs call in waves that crest and subside. The light moves. The water moves. Something grows; something eats it. The room is in motion the way a tide is in motion — never still, never hurried.

It is **eroded** only in the sense that an old man is eroded — worn into its own final shape, which is also its first. The moss does not overgrow. The water does not flood, even in the heaviest rains. The frogs do not strip the insects to nothing. The bones do not decay further; they have decided what they are. The room is in balance with itself, and has been for a long time, and will be for a long time after, and the balance is not peaceful but it is settled, like the surface of a pond with fish turning slowly underneath.

It does not have a name that anyone uses to its face. People call it the Lower Hall, or the Antechamber, or just *the wet room*. It is not at the bottom of anything. It is simply where the water gathers, and the frogs, and the listening.
