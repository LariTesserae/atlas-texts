# The Hollow Garden

The first thing is the cold. Not the biting, animal cold of wind on skin, but a still, geological cold, the kind that lives in basements and deep cellars and the bellies of ships. It presses up from below and holds everything in a permanent, breathless chill, the temperature of stone that has never seen sun. Above, far above, the cold meets the light, and where they touch, things grow.

There is no sky. There is no horizon. There is only the bowl of the world curving away in every direction, vast enough that weather assembles itself inside it the way weather assembles inside a planet — with clouds, with pressure, with the slow turning of seasons no one will ever mark. The light comes from the ceiling of this bowl, which is a distant, fibrous canopy, a mat of woven roots and stems so dense and so high up that it filters the daylight above into something softer, greener, almost aquatic. Light enters in long shafts and pools on the forest floor like spilled milk, and between the pools, the dark holds.

The forest is not a forest. A forest implies trees standing among emptiness; here, the emptiness is what stands among the trees. Every surface is growth. The trunks of the great columns are themselves vines braided around something older, something mineral, something that might once have been architecture and is now only a rumor inside the wood. They rise eighty meters, a hundred, two hundred — the eye loses them in the upper green — and from each trunk, in spiral, in lattice, in net, a thousand other plants have woven themselves into living walls, living bridges, living floors. The floor itself is not floor. It is a slow tide of moss and liverwort and tiny white flowers, inches deep, that ripples when you step and closes behind you within a minute as if you had never been.

This is a vastness that has been overgrown. The shape of it — the curve, the bowl, the unimaginable span — suggests that something enormous held this space open and then withdrew, and the green came in after, tentative at first and then triumphant. There are still bones of that older order. A pillar of worked basalt rises through the moss and stops, broken, at thirty meters, its capital long since swallowed by a fig tree whose roots have pried the carved acanthus leaves apart like bread. A road of fitted flagstone runs in a long, straight arrow away into the dimness and ends, after a quarter mile, in a thicket of bamboo so dense the eye cannot follow it further. Bridges of stone and iron span nothing now, or span only more green, their parapets grown over with succulents that flower in pale, nauseous pink.

A people are here. Few of them. The scale of the place dwarfs them so completely that a settlement of two hundred feels the way a single ant feels on a cathedral floor. They have built where the growth gives them a foothold: on the basalt outcrops that jut from the moss like black teeth, on the wide backs of fallen trunks, on a terrace of fitted stone that was once, perhaps, a courtyard and is now a village. Their houses are low and rounded, made of wattle and daub and thatch, but the thatch is alive — they plant the roofs, they seed them with moss and sedum, and the houses look, after a few seasons, as if the forest has exhaled them. Smoke comes from a few of them; the smoke is thin and white and goes straight up into the still air and is caught and digested by the canopy far above. They grow things in pits lined with clay, where the soil is deep enough to hold a root. They hunt, but the hunting is mostly patience: snares set on runways through the bamboo, a spear thrown at a bird that lives nowhere else.

The animals are sparse, and they are strange. A pale deer, almost white, with legs too long for its body, browses the high moss and watches the village from two hundred meters with the mild, incurious gaze of something that has never been hunted to fear. There are foxes the color of ash. There are insects that hum at a pitch just below the edge of hearing, so that the sound is felt rather than heard, a vibration in the sternum. Once, in a generation, something larger passes through — a shape in the deep green, a movement felt through the floor before it is seen — and the village does not speak of it for weeks.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It condenses on every surface. In the morning, the moss exhales a mist so fine it looks like the air has thickened, and every leaf wears a pearl, and the long light-shafts become visible as columns of soft gold falling through fog. There are no streams, no rivers; the water moves through the living architecture the way blood moves through a body, capillary-slow, kept inside the cells of the plants, given back to the air at dusk. After rain — and rain does come, sudden and brief, drumming on the canopy so far above that it sounds like a memory of rain rather than rain itself — the forest floor floods an inch deep, and the flowers open all at once, and the smell is green and cold and sweet, and then the water withdraws into the green and the flowers close and the cold closes after.

The cold is the constant. It is the temperature of patience. It keeps the moss from drying, keeps the lichen pale, keeps the air in the lungs of the place slow-moving and heavy with moisture. It is not hostile; it is simply indifferent. It does not care what is built on top of it. It will outlast the basalt and the roads and the wattle houses and the people. It was here before the forest, and it will be here after.

Above, the canopy moves. Something vast turns in it, shifting the light-shafts across the floor in long, slow arcs, and for a moment the great columns are lit and the dimness between them deepens, and the village on its terrace is briefly, startlingly visible — the thin smoke, the small figures, the pale deer at the edge of sight — and then the light moves on and they are gone again into the green and the cold and the vast, breathing dim.

The place is not finished. It is barely begun. Erosion has not yet softened the broken stone; the moss has not yet buried the last carved leaf. In a thousand years the village will be a hummock. In ten thousand, a contour in the floor. In a hundred thousand, a fossil under the root-mat, and the forest will still be growing, and the cold will still be holding, and the light will still be falling in long, slow, golden shafts through a green so deep and so old that no one will remember it was ever anything else.

The village, for now, lights its small fires in the hollow of an old god's footprint and calls it home.
