# The Garden at the End of Erosion

You find it in the trough of a trench so old the walls have forgotten how to stand.

The seafloor here is a sweep — a continent's worth of sediment compressed into a curve so gentle it barely qualifies as geography. The trench itself, once a wound in the bedrock two miles deep and sixty wide, has settled into something closer to a long, slow hesitation in the plain. You can walk across what used to be a chasm. Almost. The very deepest fold still holds water that doesn't move, water so still it has forgotten the word for current. And it is into this fold, into the last lingering dimple of an ancient scar, that someone — some *thing* — lowered a chamber.

The chamber is the only thing for miles.

It is small. A pocket, a lungful, a room-without-walls that has been quietly breathing warm water for longer than anything around it has cared to count. From the outside it presents as a black ovoid the size of a cathedral bell, half-buried in silt, the upper curve of its hull dark with whatever passes for rust at the bottom of warm oceans. Strands of engineered growth trail off the apex like the hair of a drowned saint — engineered, every one of them, because nothing natural has lived here in geological ages and yet here, somehow, a thicket of it.

Inside is a different country.

You enter through a service lock that still works. The mechanism turns over with a wet, grinding patience, the way old mills used to turn, the way bones turn under skin. The water on the other side is warm in the way bathwater is warm — not hot, not tepid, but *held*, as though a thermostat somewhere is still arguing with the deep ocean about what temperature ought to mean. The light that meets you is a faded amber, ordinary enough to read by, the kind of light a workshop would use, or a kitchen at dusk. It comes from fixtures strung along the chamber's spine in long chains, and the fixtures themselves are old industrial things — porcelain housings, copper cages, glass that has yellowed with the long submersion but not given up.

The chamber is a long oval. Perhaps eighty meters at its longest. The floor is gridplate, industrial mesh that has begun to buckle upward where the silt has pressed against it from below, so that walking the length of the room means crossing a series of low, soft ridges, like knuckles in a hand. The walls are layered: a skin of engineered polymer over a cage of alloy struts over, in places, the original hull plating, which peeks through where the polymer has peeled back like wallpaper in an abandoned house. Every surface that will hold still holds something alive.

And there is a great deal alive.

The *vegetation* — though that word is only approximately correct — is lush in the manner of a greenhouse that has been forgotten and left running. It grows from channels cut into the gridplate, channels that once carried coolant or feedstock or something else entirely, and which now carry a sluggish mineral broth that the growth seems to enjoy. There are stalks the color of old piano keys. There are fans, ribbed and translucent, that wave in the convection currents with the patience of hands waving goodbye. There are bulbs the size of a child's head that glow faintly from within, contributing to the amber ambience in a way the original engineers could not possibly have intended. The growth is sparse by any honest accounting — you can see the walls between the clumps — but what there is, is dense and confident, and it is everywhere something can root.

The fauna is the thing that surprises.

It fills the chamber in tiers. Small pale things hover at the height of the bulbs, near-transparent, eating whatever the bulbs secrete. They are the most numerous and the least interesting; you could miss them entirely if you were not looking. Lower down, just above the gridplate, a slower traffic moves — soft-bodied things with too many fins, things that have the look of having been *designed* at some point but have since been edited by the long warm dark into something more honest. They drift between the stalks with the proprietary air of tenants who have outlived the landlord. On the floor itself, picking across the gridplate in pairs and trios, walk the strangest of them: low, segmented, jointed at many places, with carapaces the color of the rust on the hull, and a habit, when approached, of folding themselves into a small perfect oval and waiting, the way a pillbug waits, the way a coin waits.

The sound is what you remember afterward.

There is no silence here. The pumps are still running — a low, throaty cycling that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, the rhythm of a heart that has been beating in a body that no longer needs it. There is a higher whine that you eventually locate as the ambient circulators, pushing the warm water through the chamber's gut and out again through the silt, in a long slow loop that has perhaps been running since before the trench finished forgetting its own depth. The fauna contributes: clicks from the hovering things, a wet scraping from the things that walk, and from somewhere — from the bulbs, you eventually realize — a faint, almost-musical tone that you cannot decide is a resonance or a creature. The whole soundscape is layered in the way a rainforest is layered, with one voice answering another across distances that the eye cannot measure.

It is loud. It is the loudness of a machine that has outlived its purpose and a forest that has outlived its gardener, and they are both still going, and neither has noticed the other.

The light does not flicker. This is the strangest part. Industrial light, decades or centuries under warm salt water, and still it does not flicker. You stand in it and read the labels on the wall panels — partly legible, in three languages, one of which you do not recognize and one of which is numbers — and the labels tell you that this room was once something. A station. A *farm*, possibly. The growth, then, is not an accident. The pumps, then, are not a failure. The room is doing what it was built to do, but the *for what* has slipped out the way a key slips out of a pocket, and only the *how* remains, faithful beyond any reasonable expectation.

The floor of the trench outside is silent and flat and worn to the shape of patience.

Inside, the pocket keeps running.

A bulb near your shoulder pulses once, softly, as though to mark your arrival. The pillbug-thing at your feet does not uncurl. The pumps cycle. The warm water moves. Somewhere far above, far over the long eroded plain, the surface of the ocean goes on with its ordinary business, and the light of an unremarkable afternoon travels the two miles down through black water, arrives here filtered into amber, and falls on a gridplate floor where a small, jointed creature is walking toward something it knows, in the way all creatures know things in small warm rooms that have been left running too long.

You stand there a while. The place is hidden and ancient and loud and built and alive, all at once, and the longer you stand the more you suspect that these qualities were never, in this particular room, contradictions at all.
