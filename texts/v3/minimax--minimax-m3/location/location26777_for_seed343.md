# The High Garden of the Worn Stones

You feel the altitude before you see anything. It is in the ears — a faint pressure that never quite resolves, a hollowness behind the eyes that suggests the sky is closer than the body is ready for. The path climbs for two days through moss and fern, through low creeping vines that grip the ankles, and then the trees begin, and the air opens, and the world stops being a climb and becomes a place.

The stone beneath everything is old. There is no other way to describe it. The mountains here were mountains before the moss first dared a foothold on their shoulders; they have been weathered so long that the rain has forgotten what sharp edges are for. What remains are long ridges like sleeping animals, pale granite flanks smoothed to the curve of river stones, and between them broad valleys floored in soft sediment that was once the heart of the mountain. Deep time has pressed everything down into a near-equipoise — the relief that remains is the relief that nothing could bother to take further. Somewhere far below, the bones of the world are flat, but they are still lifted up here, held aloft on a plateau the size of a country, and that is what you are walking across.

It is warm, despite the height. The equator runs close enough that the sun strikes the stone directly for most of the year, and the air, thin as it is, holds the warmth without letting it escape. Evenings cool, but never cold. There is no frost, or almost none. The plants have never had to learn to drop their leaves.

And the plants are what you came to see, if you came to see anything.

It is not a forest in the way lowland forests are forests. There is no underbrush crowding the path, no saplings waiting for a gap in the canopy. The trees here have grown so old and so slowly that each one has become its own geography. Their trunks are pillars the color of wet slate, some as wide across as a house is long, and they rise through the thin air without branching for thirty, forty, fifty meters before the first fork. Where they fork, the limbs go out nearly horizontal, and from those limbs the smaller branches drop down again, and from those branches lianas descend in curtains so thick and old that they have begun to fuse with the bark they hang against. A single tree, at maturity, is a small wood of its own. The floor between trees — and the floor between trees is what there is, because the trees are spread wide apart, each commanding its circle of light and rain — is open and carpeted only in a low felt of liverwort and club moss, ankle-deep, the color of old bronze.

This is the strange arithmetic of the place: it is a green world, green as a green thing can be, but it is also an empty one. You can stand on the moss and look in every direction and see the next pillar half a kilometer off, and the next after that, and the next. The canopy is closed high above but the floor is the floor of a cathedral — vast, columned, hushed in the spaces between. There is room to walk in any direction for a day without striking another trunk. When the wind moves, it moves between the pillars without obstruction, and you can feel it come from a long way off.

The water that feeds all this green is patient. Rain falls in season — twice a year, heavy, theatrical, a sky turned to a single sheet for hours — but between the rains the place runs on mist. The plateau stands in a band of nearly permanent cloud. The clouds do not pour down, they merely drift against the slopes, and the trees drink them directly through their bark and through the long fringed leaves of the bromeliads and the ant-plants that crown the upper branches. What drips, drips slowly. There are no streams in the valleys; instead there are seep-lines along the lower trunks, dark streaks where water travels down the bark in a thin continuous film and feeds the moss at the roots. In the morning, after a cool night, every horizontal surface wears a skin of condensation so fine it looks like dust, until you brush a leaf and the whole leaf goes dark with it. If you cup your hands under a bromelia at dawn you can drink a mouthful before the sun takes it. The air smells of wet wood, of rot in its slow aristocratic phase, of orchids nobody has ever opened.

The animals are the thing that tells you, finally, that you are not alone.

It is not that you see them. You rarely see them. What you hear is the whole green world turning itself inside out with sound. From the canopy, continuously, from before dawn until after dark and on through the warm nights, the air is full of a deep throbbing chorus — a thousand throats calling from a thousand pillars, layered and overlapping so that no single call can be picked out of the noise. There are creatures here whose song is felt in the sternum rather than heard in the ears. There are clickers that snap the air into little detonations; there are booming frogs the size of melons wedged into the axils of leaves a hundred feet up; there are insects that grind their wings together and produce a sound like a rope being hauled over a wet drum. The combined volume is such that two people standing a meter apart must raise their voices to be heard. You learn to speak in gestures. You learn to read the shapes of mouths.

What lives in the trees is mostly hidden. Once, in a clearing between two pillars, a low branch shakes and you see something move — a long pale shape, the size of a small pony, with too many joints in its limbs — and then it is gone, and the moss where it stood is dark with the impression of its weight. You find their droppings on the path, fibrous and enormous. You find bark stripped from the trunks in long pale scars, forty feet up, that mark where something sharp and patient has been at work. The predators here are quiet and unhurried. They do not have to be loud. The prey is everywhere.

There are birds. You cannot avoid the birds. They are the size of crows and they are blue, an electric impossible blue, and they travel in bands of fifty or sixty between the pillars, screaming at one another in a language of long falling notes that sounds, if you are tired enough, like laughter. They build their nests of moss and spider-silk in the crooks of the highest branches. Their fledglings fall, often — a third of them, perhaps — and what lands on the moss does not always lie there long.

The few people who live here are thin in number and hard to find. They build their houses out of the lianas, weaving the curtain-vines into walls so tightly that the finished thing looks like a basket stood on end. There is no metal in their tools. They have stone axes, polished smooth by use; they have long knives of sharpened bone; they have baskets that they weave from the same vines in a day. They plant nothing — there is nothing to plant, in any case, that the forest has not already planted — but they cultivate the bromeliads in the high branches for the water that collects in their cups, and they trap the small mammals that come down at night to drink from the seep-lines. They have been here a long time. Their great-grandparents were here. There are faint marks in the stone near the ridgelines — chiseled lines, deliberate, almost worn away — that no one now living can explain, and a half-buried wall near one of the lower valleys that the moss is slowly digesting back into itself. Once in a great while a hut is built not from new vines but from old ones, from a hollow in a living trunk that has grown around it, and it becomes unclear, after enough years, whether the hut is inside the tree or the tree is inside the hut.

The light, when it comes, is the color of honey in water. It filters through the canopy in long shafts that move, slowly, as the sun crosses the sky. The pillars throw shadows the length of small valleys. In the afternoon the moss glows; in the morning it is blue-grey and crisp. The shadows do not cool anything — the air is warm — but they mark the time, and the people use them.

The weather does not arrive here so much as drift in. Fog moves between the trees for an hour, and then withdraws, and then returns. The rains, when they come, are not storms; they are curtains hung from the sky for a night and a morning. The rivers that appear in the valleys during a rain are brown and quick and gone again within hours; the moss drinks what the rivers miss, and within a day the seep-lines run again, and the bromeliads are full, and the air is loud with whatever it is the world is saying to itself today.

In the long evenings, when the shadows pool around the trunks and the birds have quieted to a single calling here and there, the place does not become silent. It becomes, if anything, louder with the smaller things. The clickers click. The frogs boom, low and steady as a heart. The leaves, half a kilometer up, rustle in a wind you cannot feel. If you lie down on the moss and close your eyes, the sound is of being held, gently, by something enormous and unhurried. The stone is old, and the trees are old, and the people are old, and the sounds are old, and nothing here is in any particular hurry to become anything other than what it already is. The plateau has been here a long time. It will be here a long time still. You are welcome to stay for a while, in the way that any visitor is welcome in a house that does not quite remember it has a door.
