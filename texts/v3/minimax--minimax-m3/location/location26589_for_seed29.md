# The Forge-Vein

You are inside a stone lung. That is the only way to begin — by admitting that the rock around you is breathing, even if it does so in spans that would make glaciers look jittery. The chamber is small enough that if you held out both arms, your fingertips would brush the opposing walls, and the ceiling hangs low enough to press the heat down onto the back of your neck. The heat is the first fact. It has no source you can point to, because the source is everything — the floor, the walls, the ceiling, the air itself, all of it at the temperature of bread fresh from an oven, and at the center of the floor a place where the heat crosses over and becomes something that does not forgive. That place is the color of a closed eye. Dull red. A wound in the rock that has not yet decided whether to seal or to open further.

The darkness is not absolute. It is the darkness of deep water, of a closed room at the far end of a hallway — a darkness that has texture. The red eye on the floor gives just enough to read the room by, the way a single ember in a fireplace gives shape to the furniture. The walls are working. You can see it. They are working the way a kiln works: the stone is not finished, it is being made, and the making is visible as a kind of slow skin — crusts and blooms and efflorescences pushing outward from cracks where the heat escapes. There are curls of yellow mineral that look like shavings. There are places where the rock has wept something dark and glassy that hardened mid-fall, so that the walls are hung with the petrified gestures of substances that tried to move and were caught. The floor is not a floor. It is a throat. A vent. The whole chamber is the inside of a breathing system belonging to a mountain, and the mountain is young.

The air is not air in any sense you would trust. It is a thin, dry, searing breath full of metallic grit. It tastes of iron and something sharper — sulfur, perhaps, or the bite of chloride, or the strange sweetness that hot stone gives off when you press your face against a wall in summer and feel it has been baking for a century. There is no water. There has perhaps never been water. The chemistry of this place is not the chemistry of rain and river; it is the chemistry of magma and gas, of things dissolved in their own heat, of minerals that will only form under conditions that would kill a man in two breaths. The stone is being born.

The sound is the most industrial thing here, and yet there is no machine, no engine, no human handiwork of any kind. The mountain itself is the engine. From the vent in the floor comes a low, sustained roaring — not loud in the way a furnace is loud, but loud in the way a held note is loud, a continuous pressure of sound that has no syllable and no silence. It presses on the ears. Underneath it, a deeper register, almost below hearing: the slow complaint of the chamber itself, the tick and ping of cooling stone, the occasional deeper concussion as some larger thermal fracture works its way through a wall and propagates outward into the body of the mountain. These sounds do not echo. The chamber is too small and too packed with the substance of its own making to give a sound anywhere to go. The sound stays. It lives in the room with you.

And the room is packed.

This is the second fact. Every surface, every cubic inch of available space, is occupied. The walls are crusted with growths that are not vegetable and not animal in any way your education will have prepared you for — fibrous mats of what may once have been classified as mineral and may now be classified as alive, or vice versa, depending on which century of biology you consult. They are the color of bone, of ash, of things left too long in the sun. They pulse, very faintly, in time with the roar from below, as if they are breathing in answer to the mountain. The ceiling hangs with them. They drip down in pale tendrils that brush your hair. The floor around the central vent is carpeted with a denser version, a felting of pale fibers that move when you shift your weight, parting and resettling like the fur of a disturbed animal.

The creatures are small. Most of them. The smallest are not creatures at all, by any honest definition, but they cluster and they swarm and they have a kind of intention. They cling to the walls in constellations of white, each one no larger than a grain of rice, each one radiating its own faint warmth, each one tethered to the rock by a thread of itself. They feed. You can see them feed. They are taking the rock apart. The yellow mineral blooms on the walls are not geological accidents; they are middens, the excreted residue of a million small transactions between the creatures and the stone. The creatures eat the heat. They eat the chemistry. They are the workers in this factory that has no foreman, and the factory is making them, and they are making the factory, and the only question is which came first and the answer is that the question is wrong.

There are larger ones. In the folds of the wall-felt, shapes move that have the heft of mice, the deliberate motion of something with a spine or its equivalent. They are pale, eyeless, slick-looking, and they seem to be made of the same substance as the walls — a kind of living stone wrapped in a membrane of heat-tolerant tissue. They are abundant. You cannot see a square foot of wall that does not host at least three or four of them, folded into the crevices, riding the thermal gradients the way a fish rides a current. They do not acknowledge you. They have no apparatus for acknowledgment. You are not a thing they have language for.

The slow change is everywhere but invisible. This is a place that is being built, but the building is on a timescale that makes the chamber itself look like a finished object. The vent on the floor is widening. Or narrowing. The mineral crusts on the walls are growing outward at a rate you could measure if you sat here for a week and returned, but you would have to be very still, and you would have to last, and you would not last. The temperature is dropping — or it is rising; the gradients are too weak and too gradual to read in any single moment, but over the long arc, the place is changing. The mountain is deciding what it is going to be. The creatures are deciding along with it. The chamber is a sentence the mountain is in the middle of speaking, and the period is a million years away, and the comma is something between now and then.

The darkness. The heat. The roar. The packed, pale, eyeless abundance, working the walls of a pocket of space that no one has ever named because no one has ever been here, inside the unfinished lung of a mountain that is still learning how to breathe.

The stone grows. The things feed. The heat holds. The sound does not stop.
