# The Hollow in the Column

The chamber opens in the side of the column above the cloud line, and for most of the year the cloud line is below the column. There is no approach that does not end in air. The chamber is reached by a fissure that runs roughly vertical through the rock — a wound, you would call it, if you came upon it from above — and the fissure ends, after some distance that the climber no longer thinks to count, in a horizontal crack, and the horizontal crack is just tall enough to enter if you are small or if you no longer care. The crack opens into the hollow.

Inside, the hollow is roughly the size of a small temple, or a generous room. The ceiling arches upward into shadow and then resolves, if you let your eyes climb it, into a ragged mouth that opens out into weather. The mouth is the size of a wagon and its lip is uneven where the stone has flaked away over the centuries, and weather pours through it as though poured — light when light is coming, otherwise grey, otherwise dark, otherwise stars. The floor is a tumble of stones tumbled into something like arrangement by water and time. The walls are wet. The walls are always wet.

This is the place. Almost no one has been in it. The few who have, did not stay.

---

Water is the first thing you notice, after the wind.

The walls weep in long thin sheets that catch what light comes through the opening and throw it back, sliding. Where the sheets meet the floor they pool in shallow hollows, and the hollows have their own small fauna — pale things, a finger-length long, that swim in the shallows and come up to the surface and breathe. The water finds its way downward in rivulets between the floor stones and falls, somewhere, into the long drop below. After a hard rain the mouth above turns into a chimney and the water comes straight down, and then the hollow is a cistern with a ceiling, and the noise is the noise of a millrace, and you crouch against the wall and wait it out. In the long dry spells — and there are dry spells, even at this altitude, even with the sea so close that the air tastes of it — the dripping slows but does not stop. The dripping is the metronome of the place. You learn it, and it stays in you.

The vegetation is whatever can root in wet stone. Mosses cover the lower half of the walls and the tops of the floor stones in dense green-black cushions, soft underfoot where they have spread, holding water like a sponge. Ferns have taken the crannies where light pools down through the opening; they grow in flat rosettes against the rock, each frond exactly the size of a hand. There is one small rowan, or something like a rowan, that has somehow taken hold in a horizontal crack halfway up the back wall; it is bent sideways, toward the opening, and it is the height of a child. In summer it puts out a few pale flowers. In winter it is a black stick. There are lichens everywhere in patches of grey and ochre and the faintest sour yellow. Nothing is taller than the rowan. Nothing can be.

The air in the hollow is cool even in midsummer, when the sun comes in through the mouth and lies in a single bright trapezoid on the floor for an hour and a half and is gone. In winter the trapezoid becomes a colder paler thing and the ferns curl and the rowan loses its leaves and the water freezes in the shallow pools and the pale things in the water go down into the mud and wait. The cold comes off the stone. The stone never warms.

---

The elevation is the fact of the place. Everything else is in service to it.

Through the mouth above, depending on weather, you see the upper face of a cloud blanket that goes out to the horizon in every direction — a level white plain, lit from the side, featureless, sometimes breaking apart to reveal, far far below, a black wrinkled landscape that is the country and is also, in a way you cannot quite take in, the world. The air in the hollow is thin. A stranger gasps a little coming in. Sounds carry strangely: a small noise made at one wall arrives at the opposite wall louder than it left, as though the chamber is repeating itself back at you. Voices do something here that voices should not do. If you sing, the chamber returns the song a half-beat later, a third lower, and you can hear yourself as someone else would.

The chamber has been here for a long time. The opening has the worn look of something that has been receiving weather since before there were people to name weather. The floor stones are rounded where water has flowed over them for so many seasons that their original edges are gone. The moss is a deep thing, layered, centuries thick in places. Nothing in the hollow is sharp. Everything has been made gentle by the long argument between water and rock.

---

The hollow is mostly empty.

It is empty the way a cathedral is empty: not because nothing is in it but because what is in it is small and far apart. There is space here for a great deal more than is here. The floor stretches from wall to wall with only a few stones, a few pools, the rowan, the moss. The ceiling rises to a height that dwarfs anything standing in it. The mouth above is far overhead. You can stand in the middle of the chamber and feel, despite the wet and the green and the dripping, that you are at the center of a great deal of nothing. The chamber was not made for you. It was not made for anyone. It was made by water, over time, and it has the indifference of a thing made by water.

But there are marks.

A cairn stands in the far corner where the back wall meets the floor — eight or nine stones stacked with care, the upper ones smaller than the lower, the whole thing about as tall as a man's knee. It is not new and it is not very old. The moss has begun to climb it. Beside the cairn, set into the wall, is a flat stone that has been fitted into a niche of its own size, and on the flat stone, in a line of marks cut with something sharp and patient, are seven figures. They are worn now and difficult to read. The first is a tall upright shape. The second is the same shape with a line across it. The third is a circle. The fourth is something you cannot name. The fifth is the first figure again. The sixth is the circle again. The seventh is the first figure once more, with two lines crossing it.

Elsewhere along the walls, at about the height of a hand, a row of small stones has been set into cracks in the rock. The stones are of a different color than the rock — a pale granite, where the chamber is basalt — and someone has carried them here, one at a time, over how long you cannot say. There are nineteen of them. The row is not quite straight. Beyond the row, near the mouth, a small shelf of stone has been built out from the wall. It is dry under it; the moss has not come there. On the shelf there is nothing. The shelf is about the size and shape of a sleeping cat, or a folded cloak.

No one knows who made these things. No one has been here in living memory except the climbers, and the climbers did not make them. They are old. They are simple. They were made by hand. They are not for us.

---

The place is loud.

It is loud the way a sea-cave is loud: not with any single sound but with the layered attention of many sounds at once. Wind through the mouth, always wind through the mouth, louder in winter and at night, but never absent. The constant drip and trickle of water down the walls and across the floor and from the ceiling. The cries of the birds — small sharp things, brown, that nest in the high crannies of the chamber and whose calls ricochet off the walls in a way that makes the chamber seem to hold many more birds than it does. The wind, again. The distant, occasional percussion of rockfall, somewhere far down the column, that arrives as a faint shudder in the floor. The water, again. Always the water.

The birds are the most visible of the fauna, but not the only. There is a pair of mountain goats — or something very like mountain goats, hard to say at this altitude, with longer faces than you would expect — that comes up the fissure in the late afternoons and stands on the lip of the horizontal crack and looks in and does not enter. They stand there for a long time. They chew. They leave. Inside, in the moss, there are small tunnels where something vole-sized lives; you see the tunnels more than you see the thing. In the pools, the pale finger-length creatures turn over their slow loops. On the wet walls, insects with pale wings cling in clusters and scatter when the light shifts.

The chamber is alive in the way a place is alive when it has been alive for a long time and has not been much disturbed.

---

What is strange about it, in the end, is not anything you can point at. The chamber is made of ordinary things — rock, water, moss, birds. The carvings are old but they are only carvings. The cairn is only a cairn. The row of stones is only a row of stones. The shelf is only a shelf.

But the chamber does not behave the way a room behaves. Sounds return wrong. The light, when it comes through the mouth, falls in a pattern that is never quite where you expect. The rowan grows toward the opening as though it is listening for something. The pale things in the pools swim in patterns that almost repeat. The figures on the flat stone, in certain lights, seem to be looking at you. The air is thin and tastes of nothing. You can stand in the chamber for an hour and feel, at the end of it, that you have been there for longer than an hour, and that the chamber, in some way you cannot account for, has been keeping time with you.

It is a small place. It is a high place. It is a wet place. It is mostly empty. It is loud with water and wind and the small bright cries of things that nest in stone. It is, in the way that some places are, awake.
