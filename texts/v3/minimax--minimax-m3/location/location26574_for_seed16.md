# The Conservatory

This is not a place that should exist, and yet it has settled into itself with the easy patience of something that stopped questioning its own existence centuries ago.

---

The first thing you notice, if you ever found your way in, is that the air is thick. Not humid—thick in the way that mineral powders are thick when they fill a room. It moves slowly across the skin and tastes of ferrous oxide, of crushed cinnamon, of something your tongue identifies as *warm* even though there is no moisture to carry that warmth. The atmosphere is heavy enough to feel in your chest when you inhale, a kind of breathing-resistance that settles over a newcomer like a blanket pulled to the chin.

The light is the second thing. There *is* light, but it does not come from anywhere you can point at. It is the color of the underside of a storm cloud at dusk, that bruised lavender-grey, and it seems to rise out of the structures themselves—from the pavement, from the leaves, from the faces of the buildings. Nothing casts a shadow in the conventional sense. Instead there are gradients of darkness, places where the grey deepens into ink-black pooling at corners, and you learn to read the world in those gradients the way a cave-fish reads the world in vibration. The light is *enough*. It is never bright. After an hour your pupils ache with the effort of finding shape.

---

## The Place Itself

The settlement is built into the floor of what was once, you might guess, an enormous basin. Picture a shallow bowl twenty kilometers across, its rim lost in the perpetual dim distance like the edge of a card table dissolving into fog. The basin sits low on some scale you have no reference for—is it mountain-bottom, sea-bottom, the underside of something larger? The walls of the bowl rise smooth and angled, and they are *warm*. You can press your back against them and feel the slow tectonic pulse, the patient movement of immense heat traveling outward from wherever the source is. The walls are made of something you eventually decide to call *basalt-silk*: layered, fluted, with the look of cooled magma that has been dragged into folds by its own weight while still plastic. The layers are colored in rusts and oxbloods and the deep aubergine of cooling slag, and they catch the lavender light in their grooves, so that the bowl walls faintly shimmer with directional grain.

The floor of the bowl is paved. Not paved with stone—with *interaction*. The ground is made of tiles, each about a handspan across, fitted together without mortar. They are a pale bone color and they ring faintly when you step on them, a high clear note like a struck tuning fork, and they are warm too, and they pulse in subtle rhythm with the walls. If you kneel and put your ear to the tile surface you can hear a deep rhythmic hum that is the settling of the basin's own cooling, the patient contraction of something that used to be molten and is now embodied and fixed. Living, you might say, if you were the kind of person who said that.

---

## The Buildings

The settlement looks like a city and behaves like a garden.

The buildings are clustered toward the center of the basin in a dense irregular necklace, leaving enormous empty margins between them and the bowl walls—miles of paved tile stretching out flat and uninterrupted, a stillness so complete it becomes a kind of substance. *Reserve*, you might call it. *Buffer*. The citymakers wanted their buildings to face each other across a void, the way coral polyps face each other across the lagoon they themselves built.

The architecture is upward and inward. Towers rise from the tile floor to heights of perhaps twelve meters, and they are everywhere constricted, narrow-shouldered, as if their builders knew that *wide* was somehow obscene. Spires of dark bronze-green stone, hand-cut and fitted without nails, lean toward one another across narrow streets. They are leafy at their tops—not roofed, *leafy*. From every cornice, every projecting ledge, every gap between stones, vegetation erupts and cascades and spills. But this vegetation has no chlorophyll-green in it. It is orange and oxblood and the deep purple-red of beet flesh, and it is *lush*. It is so lush it is almost obscene in a place with so little light. Leaves the size of human hands, plaited and pleated, fold over the bronze ledges in living curtains. Tendrils as thick as your wrist drop six meters from upper windows down to the pavement. Fern-like fronds the size of small trees unfurl from cupolas. The whole city wears its vegetation like a heavy crimson wig, and the dim lavender light makes the reds look almost black, makes the purples look like bruises, makes the whole visible spectrum of the place slide toward *deep*.

There are walkways strung between the spires at varying heights—rope and bone and something darkly metallic that is not quite iron—lashed and pegged and knotted with a craft that is clearly old and clearly maintained. Ladders are common. Doors are round and rare. The inhabitants prefer to enter their buildings through the leaves.

---

## The Sounds

The place is loud, but the loudness is structured.

There is a constant deep tone, the cooling-hum of the tiled floor and the basin walls, that runs beneath everything like a river beneath ice. Above it, the constant chiming of the tiles themselves: every footfall, every dropped object, every flap of a banner or swayed tendril produces a clear bell-note. The citymakers tuned this, you eventually realize. They selected their pavers for harmonic compatibility the way an organ builder selects pipes. Rain falling on tiles is a chord. A child running across a courtyard is a glissando.

Above the chiming, the vegetation rustles and chatters. Not with wind—there is no wind, the air is too heavy. With *growth*. The fronds uncurl with an audible creak. The tendrils lengthen with a low fibrous groan. Tendon-tight fibers pop as the living architecture rearranges itself hour by hour, redistributing weight, reaching toward the faintest differential in the ambient light. It sounds like a forest at night, an entire forest, but slowed by a factor of ten so that what would be crickets and leaf-bristling are instead the slow grinding and shifting of giants' bones. It is a sound that makes you feel like a small thing inside a sleeping animal.

There is also a percussive layer. Somewhere—everywhere—there is hammering. Not metal-on-metal. *Wood-on-stone, fiber-on-bone, stone-on-stone*. The hands in this place are perpetually working. Tapping. Knotting. Tying. Re-lashing the ladders. Adjusting the rope-walks. Re-fitting a tile that has walked itself loose. From every direction at every moment there is the small competent sound of construction and maintenance, and you cannot see the sources of it because the work is being done above you in the leafy crowns, or below you in passages cut into the tile substrate, or in the leaved interior of a tower you thought was empty.

And there is voice. Voices call back and forth across the gaps. Not words. Not song. Something in between—long vowels, rising and falling shapes, like the calls of seabirds on a colony at morning. The voices come from many directions at once and overlap into a kind of organized polyphony, an unconscious chorus, and it never stops, even in what is clearly the middle of the city's sleep-cycle.

---

## The Vegetation

It is *everywhere*, and it is fed by what?

The soil—if there is soil—is sparse, a thin smear of red dust caught between the tiles and in the planting bowls at every window. The plants grow out of that red dust and out of cracks in the bronze stone and out of the rough backs of the tiles themselves. They are not water-fed, you realize after watching a day. They are *fed by heat*. Their roots are wide and shallow and hot to the touch, and they lie pressed against the warm basalt-silk of the walls, against the warm tile floor, drinking warmth the way a desert plant drinks dew. The whole vegetation is a kind of solar panel inverted—it collects the geothermal pulse of the basin and converts it into the slow red lush life that cascades from every surface. The leaves are dark because they are metabolizing *down*, extracting energy from minimal light and abundant heat, a trick you associate with bacteria in deep-sea vents. These are not bacteria. They are ferns and vines and things that flower with blooms the size of dinner plates, blooms that smell of iron and warm dust and, when they open, briefly reveal interiors that glow a low amber as if lit from within.

The vegetation is the city's weather. It grows and grows and grows, and the city's workers are perpetually lopping and re-routing and re-training it, because if they stopped, in a season the whole settlement would be engulfed in living red rope, the buildings would become the bones of something unrecognizable, and the city would turn back into jungle, and then back into the wild red scrub that presumably carpets the upper reaches of the basin walls beyond sight. The boundary between city and *non-city* is maintained by hand. By hammer. By hook-and-pole.

---

## The Workers

You do not see them clearly, ever.

You catch glimpses. A hand, brown as old wood, taking a tile from a pile and fitting it into the floor. A shoulder under a red leaf. A pair of feet, callused and broad, walking the rope-walk fifteen meters above you with the easy balance of someone who has done this since they could walk. A voice overhead, throwing a long vowel into the canopy-sound, and a voice answering from three streets over, and the first voice laughs—a full laugh, a small surprised human laugh that drops into the polyphony and gets swallowed.

They are invisible because the place is dense with leaves and the light is dim and the ladders go up before you remember to look up. But you feel their attention. When you step onto a tile that has been recently re-laid—darker, sharper-note—it feels *placed for you*. The lunch counters along certain arcades are always half-full of fermented-grain cakes wrapped in leaves, and there is no till, no transaction, and you take one and the leaves around you rustle in a pattern that might be approval.

They are everywhere and you cannot find one, and the place *operates*. The hammers never stop. The lattice never fails. The city runs on itself the way a sleeping body runs on breath.

---

## The Peculiarities

You begin to notice things.

There are no water channels anywhere. No wells. No cisterns. Nothing that admits water is necessary. The inhabitants do not drink in any quantity you observe. Their mouths are wet when they laugh but they do not congregate around any water-source. They seem to metabolize their moisture from the air, from the fermentation of the grain cakes, from the breakdown of the leaves they eat. The whole system runs on minimal water and abundant heat, and it is *stable*. It has been stable for what you estimate is centuries. The bronze of the spires has the patina of centuries. The tile pavers have been walked down in troughs along the major ways, century-deep grooves worn into the floor by the same routes walked over and over.

There are no animals.

You do not see a single creature of any kind except the inhabitants. No insects in the leaves. No birds among the fronds. No rats in the lower streets. You turn a tile up out of curiosity once and find nothing under it—not an ant, not a worm, not an egg. The system of life here is a closed circuit. The plants eat heat. The inhabitants eat the plants. The inhabitants decay back into red dust. The plants eat the dust. Nothing is missing. The presence or absence of anything that walks or flies or burrows has been edited out of the equation, and what is left runs without them.

This is the strangeness you carry with you when you leave. Not the towers, not the red jungle in the dim light, not the bone-paved floor. The *absence*. The fact that an entire place, lush with its slow vegetable life, ringing with its hammers and voices and tile-chimes, can exist with no fauna at all but the two-legged ones who built it, who maintain it, who eat the leaves and lie down in the heat at night and rise in the morning and re-fit a tile that has cracked and go on.

---

## The Long Sound

In the late hours, when the chiming of footsteps dies down because most of the bodies are lying in their towers among their leaves, the sound simplifies.

The deep cooling-hum remains. The growth-creak of the vegetation continues at its slow pace. But the hammer-tapping thins to occasional strikes. The voices drop to murmured vowels that rise and fall like night-birds, or like the breathing of sleepers.

And you, if you are still awake, walking the lower streets under the dim lavender non-light, begin to hear the far horizon. The bowl walls echo. They hum. You become aware that somewhere out beyond the basin, beyond the rim you cannot see, there is a much larger sound, a sustained and continuous roar, the sound of enormous heat moving through enormous stone, the breathing of whatever sits below this bowl and gives it warmth.

The city is a cool spot on the surface of something vast and hot. The vegetation is the cool spot's skin. The inhabitants are the cool spot's immune system, perpetually adjusting, perpetually tapping and re-fitting, keeping the boundary stable so the cool spot does not overheat and collapse back into the source.

They are maintaining themselves.

You walk back to your chosen alcove under a tower whose leaves have grown closed over you like a hanging canopy. The tile beneath you rings faintly under your last step of the night, a clear high note that you hear travel out across the basin floor and come back to you diminished, an echo so faint it is more felt than heard.

You lie down. You sleep. Above you, the leaves grow another centimeter in the night, reaching for whatever differential in the dim light tells them where to reach.

In the morning the hammers will start again.
