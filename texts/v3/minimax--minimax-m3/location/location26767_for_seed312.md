# The High Stone

The wind comes first. Not as a sound but as a pressure against the eardrums, a thin bright insistence, and after it, gradually, the cold — a clean, dry cold that has not been warmed by passing over any sea. You arrive at the city from below, climbing, because there is no other way to arrive. Below you for many hours is the valley, and the long switchbacks of the road, and the small stone shrines that mark the stages of the ascent, each one carved by hands long enough dead that the chisel-marks have begun to be erased by weather. Above you, for as long as you can see, the city climbs.

It is built on the face of a young mountain. The mountain is young in the way a wound is young — recently made, not yet smoothed by the patient operations of time. Its slopes are sharp. The stone is a dark blue-grey, almost black in shadow, veined with quartz that catches the late light and refuses to let it go. The ridges above the city are knife-backed and unbroken. There is no tree line because there has never been a tree line; the elevation is too high and the soil too thin for trees to have ever remembered how to grow here. What grows is lichen, and a few low cushion plants that hug the rock in the crevices where snowmelt gathers for a few weeks each summer, and there are grasses — sparse, wind-flattened, the color of old rope — that the animals keep cropped close.

The city itself is dense. The first thing you understand from a distance is that the houses are not separate. They share walls. They step up the slope in unbroken tiers, terrace above terrace, and from across the valley the whole edifice reads as a single rough-hewn thing — a slow accretion of stone rooms pressed together like seeds in a pod. The roofs are flat. The stones are undressed, fitted with a craft that does not pretend to invisibility: you can see every joint, every place where a builder wedged a chip to make a surface meet. The walls are thick. They have to be. The wind off the glacier to the north would find any thin wall and speak through it.

You enter through a low gate cut into the city's outermost wall. The gate is not decorated. There is no carving above it except the slow inscription of weather — a blackening of the stone at shoulder height where generations of bodies have passed and rested. Beyond the gate the street is narrow. It is paved with fitted stone, also undressed, and worn in places to a polish by feet and hooves and the dragging of laden frames. The street climbs. Everything climbs. The city is built on a slope steep enough that a fall from the upper terraces would carry a stone for several seconds before it struck anything, and the streets are stepped where they are stepped, channeled where they are channeled, never quite level. You walk with your head slightly down. The thin air does not demand this; it only allows it.

The cold is everywhere. It is not a violent cold — it is too dry for that, too high — but it is constant. Breath condenses in front of the mouth from morning to evening, a small personal weather, and the shadows of the walls hold a harder frost than the sunlight warms away. Water is the city's negotiation with this cold. There is not much of it. The dampness you feel is not rainfall — rain is rare, and when it comes it is small and cold and finishes quickly — but condensation. Clouds come up the valley in the late afternoon and press themselves against the upper city like wet cloth, and the stone sweats, and the channels along the streets run with a thin black trickle for an hour before the clouds retreat. There are cisterns. They are everywhere, built into the foundations of houses, and the small boys of the city spend their mornings checking them, ladling out the night's gathering into clay jars, carrying them up. The sound of water being poured is one of the city's sounds.

The other sound is the animals.

There are many animals. They outnumber the people, perhaps, or are at least equal. Goats — small, pale, hard-coated, with horizontal pupils that watch you without seeming to. They are kept in the lower terraces and let out in the morning into the rough pasture above the city, where they move across the slope in a slow deliberate tide, browsing the cushion plants, pausing to look down at you with the incurious focus of creatures that have no predators. There are larger beasts too — oxen, heavy-shouldered, with coats that the cold has thickened into something like felt; they pull the carts that come up the road with what cannot be carried. There are dogs. The dogs are rangy and quick and they own the streets in shifts, and at night you hear them in chorus, a high almost-tuneful barking that carries a long way in the thin air and is answered from other parts of the city until the whole mountain seems to be holding a conversation in one tongue. There are birds. The birds are large — dark-feathered, with broad wings built for the up-drafts along the cliff face — and they sit on the roof edges in rows, shifting their weight from foot to foot in the wind, and they are not afraid of you. They watch you pass with the same flat attention the goats give. Nothing here is afraid of you. The predators of the valley do not come this high.

The light is the same light as anywhere. It enters the city from the east in the morning and leaves it from the west in the evening, and at noon it falls straight down into the streets and heats the upper surfaces of the stones until you can feel the warmth through the soles of your feet if you stand still. The shadows of the walls are sharp and short at midday and long and blue at dusk. There is nothing magical about the light. It is ordinary light, doing ordinary work on ordinary stone.

Inside the houses — and you are taken inside, because the cold is the kind of cold that makes hospitality a small urgent kindness — the rooms are low and dark and warm. Warmth here is a thing that has been made and is being kept, not a thing that arrives. There is a fire. The fire is small. The fuel is dried dung and the slow twigs of the cushion plants, gathered above the city in the season when it can be gathered. The smoke goes up into the roof and finds its way out through a chimney that is just a hole, and the smoke stains the ceiling black, and the smell of it — dung and wood-smoke and cold stone — is the smell of the city itself, and you carry it with you after you leave, in your hair and your clothes, for days. The floor is stone. There are woven mats, and on the mats you sit, and there is tea — bitter, dark, served in small wooden bowls — and there is a stew, mutton or goat, with the root of a small tough plant that grows only in the cracks of the upper rock. You eat with your hands. The bread is flat. The bread is dense. The bread is the color of the mountain.

The people are slow. They are slow because the air is thin and the cold is patient and the city was not built in a hurry and does not need to be hurried in. A man climbing the street moves at the pace of a man climbing a long staircase, and a woman carrying a water jar moves at the pace of a woman who has carried this jar every morning of her life. The children move faster. The children run along the rooftops with the dogs, and the sound of them — bare feet on stone, a high breathless laughter — is the most human sound in the city, and it is rare enough that you notice it when it comes and notice its absence when it passes. The rest is the wind, and the water, and the animals, and the small sounds of small tools — the chip of a chisel on stone, the scrape of a hide being worked on a stone, the tap of a mallet driving a wedge into a crack in a wall that needs to be made sound before the next hard cold. There is no metal that you have seen. There is copper, possibly, used sparingly. The blades are stone or are of a hardness that is not steel. The tools are old.

Above the city the mountain continues. Above the last terrace the pasture rises in a long broken sweep toward a ridge, and above the ridge there is another ridge, and above that another, until the peaks lose themselves in a haze that may be cloud or may be the beginning of the weather. The weather comes from there. You can see it being made. A system moves across the range, a slow darkening of the upper slopes, and then the wind picks up, and then the cloud comes down, and the city is inside it, and the world is reduced to the street you are standing in and the next street and the next, and the shapes of the people around you are softened and made grey. The cold sharpens. The condensation gathers on every surface. The dogs quiet. And then it passes, and the city is bright again, and the glacier to the north shows itself for a moment as a long white field high on the sky, and the birds shake themselves and take up their stations on the roof edges again, and the small boy with the water jar continues his rounds.

The city has been here for a long time. The stones of the oldest walls are darker and more worn than the stones of the newer walls, and the newer walls are darker and more worn than the stones of the newest houses, which are still the blue-grey of the mountain itself. The city is being added to, slowly. A house is finished in a season. A terrace is begun. A cistern is deepened. These are the events of the city — not births and deaths, which happen but are private and do not mark the stone — but the patient slow building and mending of the place itself, which is the city's way of continuing. It does not hurry. The mountain does not hurry. The cold is patient and the wind is patient and the lichen is patient, and the city has learned from them.

You will leave the way you came, down the switchbacks, and the city will close behind you, and from the valley below it will read again as a single rough thing on the face of the mountain, dark against the snow, and you will think of it as still. But it is not still. It is doing what it has always done. It is making itself, slowly, out of the stone of the place, and it is keeping the people in it alive, and it is keeping the cold out, and the water in, and the animals with it, and the weather moving over it, and the light falling on it, and it will continue to do this for a long time after you have gone down into the warmer air and lost the sound of the dogs and the smell of the smoke and the thin bright pressure of the wind against your ears. The mountain does not hurry. The city does not hurry. There is no need.
