# The Manufactured Plain

The ground here is not ground. There is no dirt, no stone, no mineral substrate in the older sense — only a single continuous surface that was finished, sealed, and laid down across a distance that takes weather to cross. From the rim of a maintenance ridge you can stand and see the horizon bend, and what you see curving away from you is one object. The seam lines run for kilometers before the next one begins. They are recent. The edges are sharp. Nothing here has had time to weather into the soft, dumb shapes of older things — every corner still catches the light at an angle, every extrusion still holds its original geometry, every vent still presents its original lip. The surface looks young because it is young. It is also old enough to be inhabited.

Water is everywhere. It condenses on the vertical faces of equipment housings and runs down in sheets fine enough to be felt as a mist. It gathers in the shallow channels that have been cut into the floor for it — not carved by erosion but drawn there by design, a capillary system at continental scale, dark with whatever it carries out of the substrate. The atmosphere holds it close. Warm, humid, with the closeness of a place that has no place to send its moisture except back down. Fog forms an inch above the floor and dissipates. Rain begins and ends inside a minute. The air tastes of warm mineral and faint polymer. You do not get dry here. You do not ever really get wet either. Everything is damp, all the time, at exactly the same dampness, and the dampness does not change.

The machinery is layered into the substrate like a second geology. Engines, generators, processing housings, conduits, junction blocks, the housings of equipment whose function you cannot read because the markings are in a script that doesn't loop or curve but only brackets — but the machinery is there, everywhere, in dense and interlocking arrangement. It is industrial in the older sense: scaled, electrified, made for production. There is heat radiating up through the floor. There are places where the heat is enough to shimmer the air. The factories are running. They are running the way a heartbeat runs — at a fixed rate, into a fixed output, into a fixed consumption. They are not silent. Beneath everything is a low rumble, felt more than heard, the way you feel a river under a bridge. From certain housings you can hear the cycling of pistons, the click of relays, the patient rotation of things turning in oil baths. None of it is loud. None of it changes.

The fauna is overwhelming. This is the fact of the place.

You notice them first as a texture. The surface of every flat panel is not the panel — it is the panel plus a moving carpet one or two creatures thick. The dark, sloping face of a housing is not dark; it is dark plus a moving crust. The shallow channels of water are not water; they are water plus things packed shoulder to shoulder in the slow current. The vertical walls are not walls; they are walls plus things climbing up and things climbing down and things stationary in between. The air near the floor — that fog band, the lowest meter of atmosphere — is not air; it is air plus things drifting on warm thermals and things launching from the substrate and things returning. They are small. Mostly they are small. The largest you see is the size of a thumb; the smallest you cannot resolve. They are near-touching, layered, packed. Their density is the density of a market crowd redistributed into three dimensions and held there by behavior and warmth.

They are quiet.

This is the strangeness, the first one. You would expect overwhelming fauna to be loud. You would expect a continental carpet of creatures to be a roar, a chorus, an unignorable thing. Instead the sound is a faint, continuous rustle — the noise a sleeve makes against itself, the noise a stack of paper makes when breathed across. The largest of them produces a chirp at intervals, but it is a single chirp, and the interval is long. The smallest of them produces nothing the ear can catch. Their collective sound, which is the sound of uncountable small bodies moving over surfaces, climbing over each other, displacing each other into the water channels, launching and resettling, is a hiss. That is the word. A hiss the way a wet thumb makes when pulled from a glass. It does not rise above the rumble of the machinery. It does not vary. It has been the same loudness for as long as anyone has listened.

There are no plants.

Not a leaf, not a blade, not a filament. Not a fungus, not a lichen, not a stain of algae on a wet surface. The place is wet, warm, and packed with living things, and none of them are photosynthetic. None of them are rooted. They eat — you can see them eat — they eat each other, and they eat the outputs of the machines, and whatever the relationship is, it is closed. It is a closed loop. The faunal mass stays the same. The machine output stays the same. The water cycles the same way. The atmosphere stays the same. Nothing is accumulating. Nothing is being depleted. The system is in equilibrium.

This is the second strangeness.

You stand on a surface that was built — by whom, by what process, you cannot tell, because the construction is finished and finished things forget their tools — and the construction is functioning. It is functioning at the same rate it has always functioned. The heat is the same heat. The humidity is the same humidity. The fauna is the same fauna in the same density in the same places. The fog forms at the same height. The rain falls at the same intervals. The pistons cycle in the same rhythm. The chirp comes at the same interval. The hiss is the same hiss. The light is normal — a sun, a sky, day, night, the cycle of a planet — but the weather is its own, and the weather does not change.

Nothing here changes.

You could come back in a year and find the same thing. The same channel of water will have the same dark current. The same sloping face of the same housing will have the same crust. The same corner will be sharp. The same fog will form at the same height. The same hiss will be the same loudness. There will be the same number of creatures on every panel, and they will be doing the same things, and they will be eating the same things, and they will have grown not one micron.

The vastness is what holds it. A place this size, with weather of its own, can absorb the outputs of its own machinery and the waste of its own fauna and the heat of its own engines without ever drifting. A continental surface is a large enough buffer that equilibrium becomes permanent. The whole thing is a steady state. The whole thing is a held breath.

When the wind comes, it is the same wind. It blows from the same direction at the same speed, because the differential heating under the surface is the same. It moves the same fog in the same direction at the same height. It lifts the same fauna into the air in the same densities. The fauna resettles in the same places.

You walk, and as you walk, the surface ahead of you is the same surface you have just left — the same damp, the same hiss, the same moving crust, the same sharp seam running to the vanishing point in the same fog. You could walk forever and find that nothing has changed, because nothing does change, because this is a place that has finished changing and is now only itself, a vast made thing holding itself together in the warm wet air while the small creatures go about their small lives on top of it in the same exact way they did the day before and the day before that and the day before that.
