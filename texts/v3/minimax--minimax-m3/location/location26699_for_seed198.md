**The Long Valley**

A high valley, narrow and curving, ringed by slopes that lose themselves halfway up in the same gray they sit inside. The valley has been filling for a hundred and forty years. It is full. There is no longer any way to tell where the town ends and the mountain begins, because the town has built itself up the mountain in some places and the mountain has come down into the town in others — slides of scree and slag, terraced and re-terraced until the terraces are the only ground anyone walks on. The floor of the valley is not visible from anywhere within it. The eye meets walls.

The climate is damp. Always damp. The ridge catches weather and wrings it. Rain comes often but mostly the air is in a state of partial precipitation — fog, mist, the slow condensation that forms on every surface that has any temperature below the air. The factories vent steam that never rises more than a few meters before it merges with everything else. Pipes sweat along every wall. The cobbles are slick. Water runs in shallow channels beside every street, the color of weak tea with iron, carrying the runoff of the city toward a river that is also brown.

The industry is old industry, the kind that put itself here and then put down roots. Smelting, then forging, then machining, then a hundred branches off those — chemical works, dye-makers, small precision shops that grew because the foundries were already there to supply them. The brick stacks are grown thick with soot and moss, the moss and the soot nearly the same color. The narrow-gauge railway runs along the main street, tracks polished to a dull shine by a century of use; the locomotives are sooty little affairs that chuff rather than clatter, venting white plumes into the white air. Electric lights are strung between buildings in sagging festoons, casting a yellow glow that the fog turns to gauze. The original generators still run, somewhere down by the river, making a sound like a very large heart heard through a wall.

The buildings are layered. Local stone, brick, the slag from their own production re-cast as blocks, plaster over brick over timber over brick again. Additions on additions. Roofs are a mosaic of tin and slate and corrugated iron, replaced piecemeal, never all at once, each patch showing its own weather. A building tells you what year it was added by the color of its brick, which is also the color of the moss on its north face, which is also the color of the fog.

The vegetation is part of the construction. The terraces cut into the valley walls were planted a century ago — fruit trees, hedges, vines — and have been pruned and trained and re-trained ever since, until they are not so much plants as architectural elements: green walls, edible eaves, hedges that have become parapets. The slag heaps are moss-carpeted and lichen-painted, soft-looking from a distance though the iron shows through at any approach. Ivy has climbed every pipe. In the cracks between cobbles, in the hollows of walls, in the unused corners of yards — moss, fern, a creeping thing with small white flowers no one has named. The original purpose of these plantings is forgotten. They are part of the city's body now, the way bone is part of a body.

The fauna is sparse. Pigeons in the roof spaces. Rats in the drains, large and unafraid. A few cats, lean and territorial. The dogs are mostly kept indoors; the streets are too wet. Sometimes a heron comes up the river and fishes for a day before the neighborhood dogs drive it off. Once, very rarely, a fox. Otherwise the city is without wildlife. The density does not permit it.

The light is always low. Midday in summer, the sun is a pale disc behind gauze. The electric lights stay on at all hours, supplemented by the orange glow of forge windows and the greenish flicker of the chemical works down by the water. The fog carries each light's color and blurs it into the next. Colors run.

The sounds are muffled. The place has the quietness of a forest — not silence, but dispersed sound. The factory rhythm is everywhere but soft, because the factories are stone-built and the stone is damp. A low constant rumble felt more in the chest than heard. The hiss of venting steam. Water running in the channels. The whistle of a locomotive three streets away, arriving as a tone without edges. Conversations at a distance. The slap of boots on wet stone. The bells of the guild halls and the union temple, ringing at certain hours, but muffled.

The dynamism is in the eye, not the ear. The city never stops. There is always a shift happening. Ore coming down from the upper valley, ingots going out. The river is always moving with something. The mist is always forming and re-forming around the chimneys and the bridge cables. The moss is always growing on the next pipe. A worker is always up on a ladder re-laying a tile. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here will be finished. The city is its own weather.

The odd thing is that it holds. That this much density, this much damp, this much age, this much industry, keeps running. That the moss has not buried the rails. That the workers come every morning through the wet streets and the whistles still blow and the ingots still go out. The valley was not designed to hold this; it was filled because it could be filled, and once filled it kept filling, and what emerged was not a plan but an organism. Cool, damp, dim, industrious, packed, alive in the way a hive is alive — not warm-blooded, not quiet, not still, but coordinated, and full, and never finished.
