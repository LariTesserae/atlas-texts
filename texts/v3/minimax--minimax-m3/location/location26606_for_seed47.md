# A Plateau of Settled Stone

The ground is dark. It is older than anything you have language for. It looks like the bones of a thing that finished dying before dying was invented — a plateau, mostly, with a barely-perceptible roll to it, the kind of land that has stopped deciding things. Every edge has been argued down to its final form by time. The stones are not sharp. They are not really stones anymore. They are a kind of compacted memory of stone. If you picked one up it would feel heavier than it looked, and it would be warm from the sun, and it would not ring if you struck it — it would thud, the way a thing does when its grain has been settled by an unimaginable number of years.

The cool here is a high cool. A thin cool. The kind that comes from being somewhere up where the air has been asked to do less. The temperature varies by no more than a degree or two across the whole vast surface, and across the whole day. The plateau has decided on this cool. The plateau is committed to it.

The light is honest. It does not flatter. It falls on a surface that doesn't quite catch it — the ground takes the light and gives back something close to itself, a muted gray that sometimes tilts toward violet, sometimes toward rust, depending on which mineral has quietly dominated a particular square kilometer. The sun is thin. The sky is not quite black but a deep blue you have to look at for a while before you decide it is blue. A few stars are visible in the daytime because the air is too thin to wash them out.

There is almost no water. You could walk a long time and not find any. What air there is holds very little of it. You breathe and what you breathe is mostly nitrogen with a dry metallic aftertaste. The plateau does not weep. It does not pool. In a few low places there is a faint darkening of the stone, a residue of something that once moved through here and is no longer in a hurry to do so again.

Vegetation is sparse. It is there. You can find it if you know what to look for. It grows in the lower places, where a little moisture collects briefly when the temperature swings, and in the lee of the few stones tall enough to throw a small shadow. Black crusty lichens. A low mat of something with a metallic sheen. Tufts that look almost like moss but are not. They survive on very little. They do not look enthusiastic about surviving. They look like the last representatives of a thing that used to be more common, and that has made its peace with being less.

Animals are rarer still. You might see one in a long day. A small thing, low to the ground, dark, repositioning rather than running. If you got close to it — closer than seems reasonable — it would not panic. It would pause. Then it would continue. It has, in some sense, been here longer than you, even if it just arrived.

Then there are the structures.

They are scattered. You can stand at one and not see another, and walk for half a day and see one, and then not see another for a day. They are not all the same. Some are tall — a single column, a slim wedge, a thing without a clear name. Some are low and broad, almost flush with the stone, like a thought the ground has had. A few are made of the same dark material as the plateau, indistinguishable from it except by their intent. A few are made of something else — lighter, with a faint internal shimmer, a material that is doing something you cannot see and is not going to explain itself.

Their engineering is past ours. They are not ruins. They are operating. They are operating in a way our physics would have to apologize for and then look up the answer to. Some are present and not-present at once; you can tell because the way the light falls around them is slightly wrong. Some make the air nearby feel not heavier but more attentive, as if the air has been asked to pay closer attention to itself.

And so to the turbulence.

The plateau itself is quiet. The plateau has been quiet for an extremely long time. The stones do not move. The lichen does not move. The small dark fauna does not move in any way that registers as motion. But the structures are busy. They are very busy. They are busy in a way that looks almost like weather — flickers, slow pulses, faint distortions in the air above them, a turning of something invisible. They are busy in a way that sounds like nothing, because the air is too thin to carry what they might be saying. If you stood near one, you would not hear it. You would feel a faint wrongness in your teeth. You would feel something happening in the ground beneath your feet that the ground is politely not telling you about. The activity is on a timescale and at a register the plateau has decided not to share with you.

The vastness of the place is the point of the place. You can stand on a slight rise and look in any direction and see the same thing — dark ground, faint horizon, structures far away or not at all. The horizon is not where the ground ends; the ground just gets smaller. You could walk for what feels like a week and not arrive at anything. The emptiness is not absence. It is the room the place needs in order to do what it does.

Sound, when there is sound, is the sound of your own boots. The faint click of stone against stone. Very rarely a small wind — not a real wind, just a thin suggestion that the air has briefly moved. There are no birds. No insects. No water to trickle. The plateau is silent in the way a library is silent — not because nothing is happening, but because the things that are happening have chosen to be quiet about it.

The structures, when the light is right, cast shadows that do not quite match the light. This is the closest the plateau comes to lying to you.

You can spend a long time here and not understand it. That is fine. The plateau has spent a long time not understanding you, either.
