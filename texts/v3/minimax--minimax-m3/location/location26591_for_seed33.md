# The Plateau of Unfinished Air

The settlement exists at a height that strains the meaning of height. Below it, somewhere, there is a world of warmer air and wetter stone, but no one currently in the city remembers it with any confidence, and the few who claim to have climbed up from below describe the ascent in terms that don't quite translate — as if they'd climbed through the rock itself rather than over it. The plateau sits at the lip of something. Above it, the sky is not so much a sky as a suspension: a thin, tarnished pewter, neither overcast nor clear, holding a kind of dim flat illumination that arrives without warmth and leaves no shadows in the conventional sense. Instead, each surface casts itself, slightly, onto its neighbor. Every stone has a soft duplicate nearby, an afterimage that cannot be looked at directly.

The city is layered. The lowest stratum lies almost fused with the bedrock — cyclopean blocks of pale, almost bone-colored stone fitted without mortar and so long unworked that the joinery has dissolved into a single continuous mass. They are not foundations in any meaningful sense; they are geology with intent. No one remembers who set them. There are no inscriptions. There are no tool marks that anyone has been able to find, only an inhuman smoothness. Above this first stratum rises a second city of dressed stone, the blocks here squared with a precision that feels almost nervous — as if the builders had been in a hurry to finish before something changed. The streets are narrow, the walls lean in slow conversation with their own weight, and doorways often admit to interiors that don't quite agree with their exteriors. A door that appears to open onto a small chamber may, in fact, open onto a corridor that runs for what feels like a city block before doubling back or rising through the floor.

Above the second stratum, the third is partial. Walls rise two or three meters and stop, their tops rough as if interrupted mid-stone. Streets begin and fade into the plateau's edge. Whole quarters are missing — not ruined, but absent, as if excised with a careful knife. In some places the gaps between standing structures are filled with a kind of featureless plane of the same pale stone, polished flat and meeting the walls without seam, as though someone had poured a city and only part of it had set.

There is no water. There has not been water in this place for as long as anyone can attest, and the air has the bone-dry mineral taste of altitude rather than heat. The cool is constant — not cold enough to bite, but never warming. Body heat leaves reluctantly. In the deeper chambers, the chill is almost ceremonial. Breath does not cloud. Skin tightens.

The silence is not absence. It is presence. The thin air holds sound the way a cupped hand holds water — briefly, reluctantly, and then not at all. A footstep on the pale stone is swallowed almost before the foot lifts. A spoken word sinks into the walls and does not return as echo. The rare fauna, when they appear, move without sound. They are small and pale and almost translucent, with too many joints in their limbs, and they do not look directly at anything. They live in cracks and corners, in the dark undersides of fallen lintels, in the lee of stones that lean. When they emerge, in the deeper hours, they trace faint lines along the rock with the tips of their forelimbs and vanish again. Their lines are not markings anyone has learned to read.

This is where the unreliability begins.

Geometry here is not quite contractual. The street that runs from the western gate appears, by all the senses, to terminate at a blank wall some forty paces in. Trust the wall, and it does. But in the second and third stratum, certain corners fail to meet. A building's interior can be larger than its exterior, sometimes substantially so — one doorway admits to a hall that, by the floor area, could not fit inside the walls that contain it. Distances disagree with themselves. Walking what feels like a hundred paces in a straight line has been known to deposit a person at the city's edge, or back at the gate from which they started, or in a different street altogether, one not previously noted on any mental map. The few residents still here have learned not to argue with this. They take the route that doesn't contradict itself. They learn the angles of trust.

The plateau is not stable. It breathes, slightly. There are tremors — not earthquakes, exactly, but something more localized, more polite. A column of buildings might shift overnight, rotating three degrees around an axis no one can identify, without dislodging the people sleeping inside them. A wall might unspool, stone by stone, into the air — the stones hanging there in the dim pewter light until morning, when they are found again in their places, sometimes in a different order, sometimes re-used in walls a quarter-mile away. There is no explanation offered, because there is no explanation that holds. The city accepts these corrections. It has been accepting them for longer than the third stratum has existed.

At the city's edge, the plateau simply stops. What lies below the rim cannot be agreed upon. Standing at the western edge in the cool afternoon, one looks out over what appears to be a vast grey plain stretching to a darker horizon, where things that are not quite mountains seem to be massed. An hour later, the same rim may show only a true dark — a void that has texture but no floor. Or a sea of pale vapor that breathes, rising and falling in a rhythm too slow to be weather. Or a mirror, perfectly reflecting the pewter sky, so that the city appears to sit at the lip of an inverted twin. The plateau has many edges, and they do not consult.

There are no plants. The stone itself, in certain places, carries a film — a kind of pale growth that is neither lichen nor fungus nor mineral, but is often described as all three by those who have tried. It spreads in patterns that suggest intention but cannot be proven to have any; in places it branches like a tree seen through water, in others it pools like spilled milk that has dried in place. In certain chambers deep in the second stratum, the films organize themselves into something almost resembling writing. The shapes are repeated, but never quite. Nobody reads them. They are not in any language anyone carries, and they shift, slightly, between visits.

Light comes from where the sky is thinnest. Sometimes, in the cool late hours — there is no real night here, only a slow deepening of the pewter — a glow gathers along the upper walls of the unfinished third stratum. Not sun. Not moon. A phosphorescence that seems to belong to the stone, or to the air, or to the unreliability itself. It pools in the hollows of unfinished walls and climbs the dressed stone of the stratum below. The city is at its quietest then. The pale fauna emerge in greater numbers. They trace their lines. The glow listens, perhaps, or perhaps only looks.

There is no technology here. There are no devices, no mechanisms beyond the lever and the wedge, no fire. The cool air is too thin and too dry to carry flame well, and no one in living memory has bothered to try. Stone is moved by hand. Water — there is no water. Food is whatever the pale fauna leave behind, which is not much, or whatever grows in the films, which is less, or whatever the body can extract from the mineral air over weeks of careful rationing. The residents are thin. They move slowly. They are not unhappy.

The breakdown is not hostile. It is not catastrophic. It is, if anything, gentle — a slow drift of the world's rules, a softening of the line between what is and what could be. Things continue. The cool holds. The dim light holds. The pale fauna trace their lines. The walls correct themselves overnight. The edges disagree. The films almost write. Nothing ends, and nothing begins, and the plateau breathes, and the city is layered upon itself in slow stone patience, and above it all the pewter sky neither brightens nor darkens, and the silence is held like a small, careful thing in the cupped hands of the air.
