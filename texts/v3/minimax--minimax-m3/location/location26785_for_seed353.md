A high plateau, flat as a hand. The heat here is a weight, not a temperature — it presses on the back of the neck and stays. Above, the sky has the color of old iron; the light that reaches the ground is meager, filtered through what looks like smoke but is, when you trace it down, the exhalation of a million trunks. The elevation is real: this used to be a mountainside, perhaps, but erosion has done its work over a span of time so long the mountains themselves are a rumor, and what remains is a tableland of packed vegetable matter, an entire biome compressed into the consistency of soft stone.

Everywhere: vegetation. Not in the way of a forest — that implies gaps, sky, a sense of leaves catching light — but in the way of a fabric. Trunks press against trunks. Canopies mesh. Roots are felted together beneath the surface into a continuous mat several meters thick, which is itself the substrate. There is no soil. There is no rock. There is only this: a vast, slow, packed body of living wood that extends to the rim of vision, dark and sweating resin in the heat.

It is also, unmistakably, a factory.

The trunks are not random. They are aligned. They form rows — corridors — and at regular intervals they thicken into bulges that could be vessels, could be chambers, could be presses. Some are hollow; you can hear the air moving inside them, drawn up and pushed out by contractions of the woody walls. Some are filled with something dense and dark, fibrous, that pulses with a low hydraulic insistence. Branch-beams arch overhead, fitted to one another in mortise-and-tenon joins that have grown over themselves and become seamless. The whole thing is built, was built, has been built and rebuilt so many times that the boundary between structure and organism has dissolved.

There is no water. The sap here is not water; it is a thicker thing, resinous, and it moves sluggishly through the trunks with a sound like a slow drum heard through a wall. Where it surfaces — at the joins, at the vents — it oxidizes in the heat into a hard amber glaze. The place smells like a cabinetmaker's shop after a long fire: caramelized wood, scorched oil, the ghost of turpentine.

The machines are alive. That is the strangeness, and it is not strange anymore, not after a few hours here, because nothing here is dead. The presses breathe. The conveyors creep forward in a slow peristalsis of tendrils, carrying nothing you can see — perhaps dust, perhaps powdered bark, perhaps the fine particulate of the place's own attrition — toward intake ports that open and close like mouths. The crushers are knot-fisted trunks that squeeze in a rhythm, patient as a heart. Piston-trunks rise and fall in stands, driven by the expansion of heated air within them, and their movement makes the only sound that reaches any distance: a deep, periodic thump, the heartbeat of the place, felt more in the chest than heard.

It is old. Erosion has had a long time here, but erosion of what? Not stone — there is no stone. Erosion of the machine itself. The once-sharp geometries of the original construction are gone, softened, rounded, polished by the slow abrasion of growth and death. Where there were angles there are curves. Where there were gears there are fused masses of trunk that turn, still, in a slow and grinding way, their tooth-profiles worn into lozenges. The place has settled into its final form. It is no longer being built toward something; it is being maintained. The factory is its own restoration.

The dynamics here are turbulent in the way a tide is turbulent — patient, cyclical, immense. Every trunk is at once growing and rotting, expanding and being absorbed. The packed mass is never still; it creeps, redistributes, flows around its own immobility like a very slow fluid. A wall will lean over decades and become a bridge. A chamber will inflate and split, its halves becoming two chambers. The whole is in a state of constant, churning self-replacement that is, to the eye, indistinguishable from stillness. You have to come back after a week to see that the press by the eastern corridor has moved three meters to the north. You have to come back after a year to find that the corridor itself no longer exists.

The sound is low and intimate. The deep thump of the pistons. The hiss of vents exhaling superheated resin-vapor. The creak of fibers in tension — the small percussive ticks of trunks splitting along their grain, then the long slow sigh of them settling. A faint distant roaring, like wind in a chimney, where the great central stack draws air upward through the entire mass, cooling the inner workings, expelling the heat. The roar is the loudest sound, and it never stops, but it is the roar of something that has been roaring for so long it has worn itself into the texture of the air. It is the sound of an old machine breathing out.

No animals. There is nothing here that eats, nothing that moves on legs or wings. The processes of the place are internal. The trunks do not fruit; the canopies do not flower. There is no reproduction in the way we would recognize it. The place grows by accretion and is dismantled by the same forces — heat, internal pressure, the relentless chemistry of hot dry matter seeking equilibrium. It does not need to seed itself. It is already everywhere.

The light is dim. It comes in from above through the canopy, which is not a canopy of leaves but a lattice of interwoven branch-tips, each one pressed against its neighbor, forming a continuous shell several meters thick that admits only a faint orange glow from the sky. At ground level it is the light of a deep sunset held permanently in place. The shadows are dense and do not shift. When you move through the corridors, the trunks glow with their own faint luminescence in the depths of the wood — a deep amber, a slow phosphorescence of metabolizing tissue.

The place is strange, yes. It is strange because it is the end-state of something. An industrial complex, once, of which no record remains — built by hands that are not here anymore, for purposes that are not apparent anymore — but the plant matter of the original site, the boundary plantings, the wood of the original scaffolds, the seeds that fell in the rafters — all of that has, over the unaccountable time since, taken the place over. Remade itself in the image of the factory. Become the factory. Or, perhaps: the factory was always a plant, and the operators only thought they had built it. The boundary between cultivated and wild has been eroded away, just as the angles of the gears have been worn round.

You stand in a corridor. The trunks press close on either side, dark and sweating. The pistons thump. A vent somewhere ahead exhales a long breath of hot resin-scented air that ripples your hair. Above you, the lattice of branches is the ceiling; around you, the architecture is the architecture of trees that have been told, by some patience that has outlasted the tellers, exactly what shape to be. The heat is a weight on your shoulders. The darkness is a fabric drawn over everything. The silence is full. The place is working, still, at its work, and you are a small and foreign thing inside it, and it does not notice you, and it will continue not noticing you long after you have gone.
