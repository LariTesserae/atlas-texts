The plateau rises like a table cut from the dark rock of a young continent. There is no gentle approach to it; the land simply stops being land elsewhere and begins being something else here, a flatness that extends so far in every direction that the curvature of the world begins to be a felt thing rather than a known thing. You can stand on the surface and watch the weather form at one edge of your vision and arrive at the other edge later, having crossed your entire field of view like a slow parade. Fog comes first, usually, low and gray and thick enough to make the next tower disappear before you can walk to it. Then the rain, when it comes, falls in straight windless columns onto the basalt. The rock is young — geologically young — and has the sharp edges of things that have not yet learned to be smooth. The rain does not roll off of it but pools in the hollows and stays there, trembling.

It is a cold place. Not the killing cold of inner winter, not the locked-frost cold that takes the breath, but the persistent clarifying cold that lets you see your own thinking as if it were laid out on a table. The air is wet and thin and it moves constantly. Condensation forms on everything. The vertical faces of the rock weep without pause, and the lichen that has begun, here and there, to colonize the basalt is the color of old bruises — purple, ochre, a yellow that is not quite yellow. Where the water collects in the natural bowls of the young stone, the pools are clear and very still, and you can see to the bottom of them, and the bottom is often the same stone as the top, unaltered.

The city — though calling it a city is generous; calling it a settlement is generous; it is more accurate to call it a constellation — is built out of the rock and into the rock. There are towers that rise from the plateau like fingers held up to read the wind. There are domes that sit low against the stone and look, from a distance, like nothing more than a particularly shaped boulder. There are walls, but the walls are mostly interior; the city does not defend itself against anything that lives here. The structures are sparse. There might be two of them visible from any given point, and the next two are half a day's walk or a full day's drive away. The routes between them are not roads so much as paths of least resistance that the machines have worn into the stone, or paths of least resistance that the animals have worn into the machines' paths, and it is no longer clear which came first.

The materials of the construction are not the materials of any engineering you would recognize. The walls do not seem to have been made. They seem to have been persuaded. The stone the city is built of is the stone that was already there, but it has been rearranged at a scale that is hard to perceive — at the scale of the molecules, or the bonds, or something below the bonds. A tower will have the grain of the basalt continuing through it as if the tower were a single crystal that had grown up out of the plateau. A dome will have a window that is not a window but a thinning of the wall, and the light that comes through it is the same color as the light outside, which is to say it is not a special color; but the room inside the dome will have a quality of stillness that the outside does not have, as if the air in there were being held in place by something patient.

The animals are many. This is the part of the place that anyone who arrives here notices first, and the part that is hardest to account for. The land, by every visible measure, should not support them. There is no soil. The vegetation is sparse — small gray-green bunches of something that might be grass or might be something else, a few mosses that have found a permanent wet place, the lichen in its bruise colors. The water is mostly thin films and small pools. And yet the animals are everywhere. They move across the plateau in their slow migrations, the great furred shapes and the small quick ones, and they have done so for as long as anyone has been here to watch. The biologists — if that word still means anything — say they are adapted. The engineers — if that word still means anything — say they are sustained. The animals themselves do not seem to have an opinion. They graze, when they graze, on the bunches of almost-grass. They drink from the pools. They watch the towers with yellow or amber eyes and do not startle at them. The predators among them — there are predators; the food chain is intact and operates with the patience of a well-made machine — take what they take, and the rest continue.

The light is the light of a place that does not have a special relationship with the sun. It is the same in the morning as in the evening, which is to say it changes but does not dramatize. The shadows of the towers are long in the morning and short in the middle of the day and long again toward evening, and the color of the basalt shifts from black to brown to gray and back again. The fog, when it comes, diffuses everything and turns the world into a pencil drawing. The rain, when it comes, makes the world a darker pencil drawing and adds the small percussion of drops on stone, which is a sound that goes on for hours if the rain is committed to its work.

It is mostly quiet here. The wind is the loudest thing, and the wind is not loud, only persistent. You can hear the water running off the rock faces in a thin continuous hush. You can hear, if you stop walking, the small movements of the animals — the click of a hoof on stone, the rustle of something moving through the bunch-grass, the distant lowing of something large. Occasionally there is a sound from the city — not a mechanical sound, nothing with an engine or a rotor, but a sound that is hard to place, a hum that lives at the bottom of the audible range, that seems to come from the stone itself rather than from anything built upon it. Some of the people who live here say they do not hear it anymore. Some of them say they never hear it at all.

The weather moves. This is a place with weather of its own, the size of it ensures that, and on any given day you can watch a system form on one horizon, cross the entire visible plateau, and dissipate at the other, and the crossing takes most of a day, and you can feel the air pressure change as it approaches and as it leaves. There are days of clear cold and long light. There are days of fog so thick that the nearest tower is a rumor. There are days of rain that comes in sideways under a wind that has nowhere else to be. The animals move with the weather, or before it, or after it — the biologists and the engineers disagree about which, and the animals continue to not have an opinion. The pools fill and empty and fill again. The lichen creeps, very slowly, across a new square millimeter of stone.

This is a place that is being made. Not finished — never finished, perhaps — but made, in the way that a thing is made when the maker has time and the thing is large. The plateau itself is young. The city is younger. The route between two towers you can see in the distance has been there for a hundred years, which is not long, by the standards of the rock it crosses. The animals are older than the city but younger than the plateau. There is a sense, standing here, of a place still deciding what it is. The wind writes on the stone and the rain erases what the wind has written and the lichen colonizes the erasure and the towers stand in their sparse and patient arrangement and the animals pass through all of it, indifferent to the timescales involved, occupied only with the immediate business of being alive.

There is no fence. There is no border. There is just the plateau, going on, and the weather crossing it, and the city embedded in it, and the life that has found a way to be abundant here despite every reason it should not be, and the quiet that is not silence but the held breath of a place that has not yet finished becoming a place.
