# A Basin Held in Breath

A basin. Vast, low, and steaming. The air lies on the land like wet wool, heavy with water that never quite falls and never quite rises — a permanent mist that diffuses the sky into a pale smear, visible only as a brighter bruise overhead when you look straight up.

The ground is *recent*. That is the word that comes to mind, though recent is wrong, because nothing here has happened recently. Nothing has happened here at all, in the way that things happen in places where time works as it should. The substrate is a deep, soft, unformed thing — peat and silt and the slow rain of uncounted dead leaves, piled into something that holds weight only because it has forgotten how to give way. Step and it dimples. Pause and it remembers. Nothing collapses. Nothing firms. The land is held in suspension, neither earth nor water, neither floor nor fluid, and has been so for a very long time.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It weeps from the underside of every leaf. It condenses on every surface in beads the size of small grapes, gathers, runs down in threads so fine you only notice them when the light catches. The air tastes of it. The mud tastes of it. To breathe is to drink a thin, warm water.

The vegetation is layered and layered and layered again. Somewhere above, a canopy so tangled that the eye cannot follow it — only its effect, which is the dimness. Below it: trunks, some slender, some fat and buttressed, some with no clear seam between root and stem. Ferns whose fronds arc like questions and never resolve. Vines that fall straight down for the length of a tall tree and end in pools. Things that flower in colors you almost have to coin names for — a red more felt than seen, a purple that hums at the edge of hearing.

And the animals.

The animals are the place.

You do not see the basin and then notice the animals. You see the animals and the basin is what they are doing. They occupy every cubic inch. Beetles the size of a thumbnail, dozens on any square foot of leaf. Lizards that hold still and look like part of the bark until they are not. Things in the canopy that travel in pulses — flocks, colonies, traveling minds that move through the branches like a single thought. Things in the water that surface and break the meniscus and are gone. Things that fly, things that swim, things that burrow and surface, things that simply sit and breathe.

The sound is a wall without edges. Cicadas, frogs, the wet percussion of a rain that is always about to begin and never does, the low distant complaint of something large moving somewhere beyond sight, the high thin whine of insects so small no language has named them. The sound does not change. That is the strangest thing, the only strange thing, and it is strange in a small way. The chorus is the same at every hour.

There are no hours here.

There is no dawn. There is no dusk. There is the dim green and the steam and the unchanging wall of sound. What light comes from above is eaten by the canopy before it can arrive. What little arrives is taken by something with a leaf, and then by something else with a leaf, until what reaches the floor is a sourceless pale glow the color of old copper.

There are no paths. There are no clearings. There is nothing piled, woven, split, or stood upright by intention. No hand has ever entered this place. There are no hands. There are only claws and beaks and the slow turning of mouths. The mud holds no footprint older than a day because nothing walks here twice, but also because the mud is the mud and the rain of leaves is the rain of leaves and the form of the place does not remember what touches it.

The strangeness — and it is strange, though only just, only at the edge where one might miss it — is this: nothing is becoming anything else. The mud is not becoming soil. The fallen trunk is not becoming a hollow. The water is not wearing a channel. The hills are not lowering. Things grow and things die and the dying things are eaten by things that are also dying, and the shape of the whole remains exactly as it was, as if it had arrived, once, at some final arrangement and now simply continues. A standing wave. A held breath. A geology that is in the act of becoming and has forgotten to finish.

It is hot. That is the dominant fact, more dominant than the water or the green or the sound. The heat is not the heat of a sun on shoulders. It is the heat of being inside something alive — of compost, of ferment, of a mouth. The water is warm. The mud is warm. The air is warm and close. To be inside this basin is to be inside a slow cooking that has no dish and no end.

One could stay a long time. The basin would not change around the staying. The chorus would not vary. The mist would not lift. The mud would not yield or harden. The animals would continue their unchanging business. The place does not do anything to a visitor. It only offers the same offer, hour after hour, that it has always offered: the green dim, the warm wet, the held breath, the unending wall of small and loud life, the strange and patient refusal to become anything other than what it is, right now, as it was, as it will be.
