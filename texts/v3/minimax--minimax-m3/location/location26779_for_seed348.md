# The Lower Engine

The valley sits low and does not drain. There is no breeze to speak of — only a slow convective drift up the basalt walls, carrying the smell of warm iron and something gamier, almost sweet, the way old tallow smells in a cold kitchen. Everything here is built close to the ground. The horizon, if you could see it from inside, would barely crest your shoulder. You don't see the horizon from inside. You see walls.

The place is a single compound, or perhaps a single very large machine dressed up as a compound — the difference has been forgotten. Brick and riveted plate, low rooflines, a single stack that doesn't smoke so much as *exhale*, a continuous pale breath that drifts off into nothing. The bricks are dark with age and with moisture. They have been here longer than anything alive has been anywhere. The mortar between them has gone slightly green in places, though not from moss — moss requires more light than this, and more intention. The green is a stain from the damp, mineral-fed, like verdigris without the copper.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It condenses on every cold surface — the iron ties, the window-glass, the backs of the standing pipes along the central spine — and runs down in threads, then gathers, then is taken up again by the long troughs that line every walkway. The troughs are always half-full. Something in them moves, sluggish. Nobody tends the troughs; they are tended by the gradient of the floor, which was set, long ago, at exactly the right tilt, and has not shifted. Nothing shifts. The floor is true. The troughs run true. The water goes where it has always gone.

There are no plants. There have never been plants in this valley, or there were once and the place has moved past them, the way a fire moves past a forest. The architecture is the vegetation. Pipes stand in for trunks. The latticework of catwalks, in the upper reaches that you cannot quite see into, stands in for canopy — a hard canopy, sweating moisture, hung with condensation like pale leaves. The light that does reach the floor is filtered through this metal leafage into a moving mesh of shadows that does not, in fact, move. The shadows are stationary because the things casting them do not move. Nothing sways. Nothing rotates. Nothing turns. The shadows are just shadows.

The machines are loud. That is the first thing, and the second thing, and the last thing. They are loud in the way a sea is loud — not in events but in mass. A continuous mid-frequency roar with undertones: a deeper throb from somewhere below, a higher hiss from the steam traps along the walls, a faint mechanical clicking from a hundred small reciprocating parts that are going through their motions in a register below conscious attention. If you listen past it you can pick out individual voices — a valve somewhere cycling open and closed, a belt slipping, a pulley making its small complaint — but they are voices in a chorus, and the chorus does not pause for soloists. After the first day, you do not hear it. After the second, you cannot remember silence. After the third, you understand that the roar *is* the silence, the silence this place has instead of quiet.

And then there is the fauna.

This is the thing that makes you understand you are not in a town anymore, if you ever were. The fauna is the place. The fauna is what the place is for.

It is not birds. It is not anything with a spine you would recognize. It is moths, but moths at a scale the word was not built for — pale, dust-colored, the size of your spread hand, with wings like dry vellum that have given up on pattern and gone entirely to texture. They fill the air of the central hall in a slow column from floor to roof, a living chimney-draft, never landing, only ever passing through. Their sound is part of the under-noise: a dry whisper, a paper-rustle, multiplied by tens of thousands of bodies in continuous flight. Where they go when they leave the column, where they come from when they rejoin, you cannot tell. They are not migrating. They are circulating. They are the weather of this room, the way snow is weather in a colder place.

Beneath them, on every surface, the beetles. Black, lacquered, segmented, slow as poured oil, in layers three and four deep where the warmth is best — along the hot-face of the great drum in the center, along the flanges of the pipes that carry whatever the pipes carry. They do not scatter when you approach. They have nowhere to scatter to. They part around your boot and close behind it, and if you stood still long enough they would climb you. Their contribution to the sound is the faintest continuous ticking, like a clock heard through a wall, the sound of their parts fitting and refitting as they crawl over one another. They feed on the condensate. They feed on each other's castings. They feed on whatever the small orange fungus — not a plant, not animal, something in between, a smear, a lividness — grows in the joins of the brickwork where the heat meets the damp. The food web is complete. It has been complete for longer than the bricks.

There are things in the troughs. You see them only as movement when you cross the walkway above — pale shapes, eyeless, the length of your finger, that flex and settle in the slow water. They eat what falls into the troughs. The troughs are slow. The water in them moves at the pace of a hand-drawn breath.

And in the upper catwalks, in the latticework you cannot see into clearly, there are the watchers. You never see them well. You see a length of limb, sometimes, across a gap in the grating — long, jointed more times than is usual, tufted with the same dust-colored fine hair as the moths. You see an eye, occasionally, at the wrong angle for the head to be where you expect the head to be. They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are the predators that close the loop, that keep the beetles from filling the world, that keep the moths from filling the air until there is no air left. They move in the dark above you, and the dark above you is where they live, and you do not go into the catwalks, and they do not come down. This is the arrangement. The arrangement is older than you. The arrangement will outlast you. The arrangement is the law of the place.

The light is normal, insofar as light can be normal in a valley with a low overcast that has never broken in memory. There is a diffuse gray from above, where the stack exhales into a sky you cannot see. There is a warmer light from within — a yellow-orange from the small grated windows of the furnaces, which are not stoked and never go out. They burn. They have burned. The fuel is not visible. The ash is not removed. Whatever the fuel is, it is being consumed at a rate that exactly balances its replenishment, which is also not visible, and the furnace is therefore, by definition, perpetual, and therefore, by the place's own accounting, not burning at all. It is *being*. It is in equilibrium.

You can walk through. There are walkways, there are doors, there is a route from the receiving yard at the entrance to the loading bay at the far end, and the route is clear, because the fauna keeps to its own channels and the machines keep to their own, and the geometry of the place has had time to settle into its own logic. You can walk through. You can count your steps. You will arrive where you expected to arrive.

You will not be alone. The watchers will know you passed. The moths will taste your warmth. The beetles will part around you and close behind, and the trough-creatures will flex beneath your shadow as you cross the bridge. The machines will not adjust their rhythm for you. They will not pause. They will not speed up. They will not, in any way that you can measure, acknowledge that you are here. And yet when you leave — when you step out past the receiving yard, past the low stone gateposts with the iron numbers no one reads anymore, out into the higher country where the wind lives and the scrub-grass, such as it is, begins — you will find, in the silence of the open air, that you are listening still. You will hear the place behind you for as long as you live. You will hear it the way a sailor hears the sea after coming ashore. It will be in your chest, a faint reciprocal motion, a matching pulse, as if a small part of the engine has been left ticking inside you, faithfully, for the rest of your walking.

The place does not change. The place is the change. The place is the place.
