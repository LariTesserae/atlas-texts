# The Garden of the Long Calm

What water has done here, it has done well, and what it has not yet done, it is considering.

You stand — if standing is the right word — at the bottom of an ocean that has decided to become a forest. The transition took longer than memory. It is still going on. Somewhere above you, if "above" still refers to anything, the surface of the water hangs at a height that varies with the patience of whoever watches. Today it hangs low. Today you can see the treetops, or what were treetops, dimpling the underside of a sea that has forgotten the taste of sun.

The light is the color of a coin held under cloth. It does not arrive so much as *resolve*, from somewhere far overhead, after passing through what must be miles of patient dark water. By the time it reaches you, it has been leached of everything urgent. There is no shadow in this place. There is only the slow gradient between things-seen-barely and things-imagined-very-still.

The trees are not trees.

Or they were trees once, and the water loved them so completely that it kept the shape of them while changing the substance. They are columns now — vertical things, rough-skinned, branched — made of something between coral and bone. They do not sway. They have not swayed in a long time. Some of them have grown together at the crown, fused into arches, into vaults, into the suggestion of architecture that no one designed. The roots, if there are still roots, are buried in the floor of this drowned country, which is not the floor of any sea you would recognize. It is soft. It is the color of old tea. Your weight on it is received, not resisted.

The water itself is warm. This is the first strange thing. You had expected cold, the patient cold of deep places, but the water is the temperature of a bath left too long, of milk, of blood. It moves, but barely. It moves the way air moves in a room where someone has been sitting for years — in currents that remember the body. If you listen — and here you must listen, because the silence is so total that listening becomes a kind of swimming — you can hear the water breathing. Not a sound. A pressure. A slow pulse that travels through your chest before it travels through your ears.

Vegetation clings to everything.

Not the green you remember. A deeper green, the green of olives, of moss that has forgotten the sun, of lichen on gravestones. It grows on the columns. It grows on the floor. It grows on itself, layer after patient layer, until the original surface is only a rumor. Ferns the size of rooms unfurl and never finish unfurling. There are flowers — if they are flowers — pale and heavy, that open and close on a rhythm no insect has ever set. They smell of nothing. They smell of *old*. In some places the growth has buried itself; whole groves have been swallowed by their own descendants, and you walk (swim, drift) over the smooth backs of forests that have become hills that are becoming, very slowly, geography.

The elevation is wrong. That is the second strange thing. You have the sense of being high up, of being on a plateau, of having climbed — and yet there is no climb, only a slow rising of the floor, a gentle shoaling of the drowned land. When you look up through the dimming water, you see, very far above, the suggestion of peaks. Other drowned mountains. The spines of a continent that settled, ages ago, into its final arrangement and has not objected since. They are dark against a darker dark. They have been there longer than you can think.

The erosion here is not erosion. It is *forgiveness*.

Every surface has been worked on for so long that the working has become the surface. There are no edges here. The columns taper into the floor; the floor ripples into the columns. The hills roll into the troughs. If there were ever sharp stones, they have been petted into smoothness by hands that are no longer hands. The whole of this place has the texture of something that has been held in a mouth for a thousand years — worn, warm, given over.

It is vast. You cannot see the edges of it. The water extends in every direction until the light gives up, and beyond that giving-up, the place continues. There is weather here, but it is a weather of pressures — fronts of warm water meeting cooler water, slow tides that travel for days before they arrive, currents that take weeks to decide to be currents. You can stand in one place and feel the temperature shift around you as some immense but lazy system passes. Sometimes the water darkens for a while, as if a cloud has crossed the surface far above, and you understand that this place has its own sky, and its own days.

There are animals, but they are rare.

You will see, if you are lucky, a shape move at the very edge of seeing — a long pale thing, eyeless, slow as a thought, sliding between the columns. It does not look at you. It has no mechanism for looking at you. It is moving from one silence to another. Once, something larger passed — you felt it more than saw it, a displacement of water, a shadow within the shadow, gone before you could form the question of what it was. The fauna here are not numerous. They are not in conversation with each other. Each one seems to be pursuing a private errand through a country that no longer requires errands.

There are structures.

Or there were. In one place, half-buried in the soft floor, you find a wall. Not a wall — a *suggestion* of a wall: a length of stone that has been laid (built, placed, arranged) and then forgotten for so long that the vegetation has made it into a ridge, and the ridge has made itself into a hill, and the hill has forgotten it was ever a wall. In another place, a flight of what might be steps rises into a column and disappears inside it, as if the column ate them politely. In yet another, a ring of stones stands in a clearing among the drowned trees, and you cannot tell if it is a foundation, a well, an altar, or a game someone played a very long time ago. The marks of making are here, but they are so patient, so thoroughly reabsorbed, that they read as geology. Whatever was built here was built to be lost, or was loved until it stopped being separate.

There are no devices. There are no tools. There is no *need* of them, and there has not been for longer than this place has had a memory of needing. The few animals have their bodies. The water has its patience. The columns have their slowness. Nothing here is waiting to be improved. Nothing here remembers the word.

And the silence.

The silence is not the absence of sound. It is the *presence* of the ending of sound. Every noise that begins in this place is gently completed before it can travel far. A stone falls from a column and the water swallows its report. A flower opens and the click of it is eaten at birth. The large creatures move in such slow currents that they make no complaint. So what you hear, when you listen, is the inside of your own listening. Your own pulse. The faint hydraulic whisper of your own blood, which is — you realize with a small start — the same temperature as the water.

This is the place where things come to be old.

This is where the waves go when they are tired. This is where the trunks of trees decide they would rather be coral. This is where mountains, having discovered what they were for, sink into their own idea of themselves and stop complaining. The water is high, the water is warm, the water has been here longer than the word for *long*, and it has done its work.

It is not a dead place. That is important.

Look — there, between the columns. A pulse of light. Not sunlight. Something bioluminescent, something faint and blue-green, traveling slowly up the trunk of one of the bone-trees and arriving, after a long ascent, at a flower that opens to receive it. This is a transaction. This is an exchange. A contract between one living shape and another, performed in a currency of dim light, witnessed only by the water. It happens, and then it is over, and the tree is one flower fuller, and the small bright creature goes about its slow day. This is happening throughout the drowned forest, this quiet commerce, this unspeaking agreement. The place is active in the way that old places are active — not with violence, not with hurry, but with the long business of continuing.

The water moves. The columns grow, a millimeter a century. The flowers open. The pale things pass between the trunks. The light shifts as some weather, far above, decides how much of itself to admit. The walls forget they were walls. The floor remembers, gently, that it was once a field, once a forest floor, once a beach, once a mountain top, and decides to be all of these at once.

And you —

You are standing in it. You are breathing it (somehow, the warm water). You are one more strange shape in a country of strange shapes. You are being slowly, patiently, very gently, reabsorbed. Not today. Not for many todays. But the place has time. The place has nothing but time, and warm water, and the memory of having once been something else.

You put your hand against one of the columns. It is warm. It is the warmth of skin. It is the warmth of a hand held for a long time.

The column does not respond. It does not need to.

It has been here longer than your hand. It will be here longer than your hand.

It is, very quietly, glad you came.
