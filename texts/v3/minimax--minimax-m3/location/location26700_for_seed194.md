The first thing is the sound.

It is louder than the place has any right to be. The sound is in your sternum before it is in your ears. The sound is a low, sustained chord, a single vast tone with several overtones riding inside it, and it does not stop, it has not stopped, it has not paused to take a breath in longer than the mountain has stood. The chord is the air. The chord is the air moving through pipes. The chord is the chamber itself, humming, the way a stopped pipe hums, the way a jar hums when you blow across its mouth, except that the blowing has not stopped, the breath is the slow heat of the mountain's deep interior, the breath is the barest circulation, and the pipes are tuned to that breath, have been tuning themselves to that breath for so long that they and the breath and the note are now the same thing.

You do not see this at first. You do not see it for a long time, because there is nothing to see. The chamber is dark. Not dim. Not low-light. Dark. The dark of a sealed room, the dark of the inside of a closed eye, the dark of a place that has had no light since the last small flame in the last clay dish burned down to a thread of soot and then to nothing. The dark is so complete that it has weight, so complete that you can feel it against your face the way you would feel a hand laid there gently. The dark has been here so long that it has its own weather, its own settled dust, its own slow chemistry. The dark is not empty. The dark is full of the smell of resin and old wood and dry mineral, and the dark is full of the chord.

The chamber is hidden. The chamber is a pocket, a single hollow, a room inside a low mesa that stands in a dry plain. The mesa is not large. The mesa is the size of a small hill with ambitions, a sandstone butte with a granite spine, a few hundred paces across and not much more than a hundred paces tall, the kind of isolated mass that you can walk around in an afternoon if the plain is not too hot. The plain is a salt-and-gravel plain, very flat, very old, very quiet under the sun, and the mesa is the only thing that breaks the line of the horizon for a long way in any direction. No one comes to the mesa. No one has come to the mesa for longer than the chamber has had its chord. The chamber is inside the mesa. The chamber is a single room, more or less, although the room has chambers within it, alcoves, half-rooms, ledges, hollows in the walls, places where the air is stiller and the chord is quieter and the dark is a different dark, a denser dark, the dark of a stiller place inside a still place. The chamber is about the size of a town, if you measure the floor, but the floor is uneven, the floor rises in steps and platforms, the floor falls away in basins, the floor is a folded thing, and so the chamber is not the size of a town in the way that an open town is the size of a town. The chamber is the size of a town that has been crumpled up and put in a box.

The air in the chamber is cool. Not cold. Cool the way a cellar is cool, the way deep shade is cool, the way the inside of a stone wall is cool in summer. The cool does not change. There is no day-night cycle in the chamber, no summer-winter, no weather. The cool is the mean temperature of the stone that surrounds the chamber, the stone that has been at this temperature for a long time, and the chamber is at the same temperature, and the air in the chamber is at the same temperature, and you are at the same temperature after a while, and the whole thing is in equilibrium, has been in equilibrium for so long that you can feel the equilibrium as a kind of pressure, a kind of patience, a kind of held breath.

There is no water. There has not been water for a long time. The stone of the mesa sweats a trace of moisture in some seasons, perhaps, the way all stone sweats, but the trace is so small that the plants — for there are plants — do not appear to need it, or need it in such small amounts that what they need is satisfied by the air itself, by the few molecules of water that wander through the chamber on the slow breath of the chord. The air is dry. The air is so dry that your lips would crack, that the hair in your nostrils would be brittle, that the skin of your hands would be tight and white after a few hours. The dryness is a presence. The dryness is the second thing you would notice, after the sound, if you were set down in the chamber with your eyes bandaged.

The plants are everywhere. The plants are the chamber. The plants and the chamber are the same thing now, have been the same thing for so long that the chamber and the plants have become indistinguishable, the way a tree and the fence it has grown around become indistinguishable over a long enough time. The plants fill the space. The plants are the walls, where there are still walls. The plants are the columns that hold up the ceiling where the ceiling has not fallen in. The plants are the floor in some places, a floor of pale dry fibers woven so tightly that it is like a felt, like a mat, like the underside of a mushroom cap. The plants are the ceiling in other places, a ceiling of roots, of dangling dry stalks, of latticework that hangs down into the chamber like the rigging of a ship. The plants are the stairs that climb into the walls. The plants are the platforms that you can no longer climb to, because the stairs have become part of the plants, because the plants have grown around the stairs, through the stairs, into the stairs, and the stairs are now ribs of plant, the stone of the steps a memory inside the wood of the steps.

The plants are dry. The plants are the color of old bone, of dry clay, of the sand of the plain outside, of the dust in the chamber, of the stone of the walls. The plants are not green. They are not the green of any plant that has ever drunk. They are a faint yellowish tan, the color of a leaf that has been pressed in a book for a hundred years, the color of a mushroom that has dried on its log. They are woody, fibrous, almost mineral. They are hard to the touch, in some places, soft in others, but never wet, never yielding, never with the give of a living thing that has water in it. They are alive, or something like alive. They grow. They grow very slowly. A finger-width in a century, perhaps, or less. The growth is so slow that you could sit in the chamber for a year and not see it, could sit for a lifetime and not see it. The plants have been growing since the city was young. The plants are older than the city is old. The plants are part of what the city has become.

The plants are pipes. This is the strangeness of the place, the strangeness that does not quite resolve no matter how long you stay in the chamber. The plants are pipes. Each plant, or each major plant, each column, each stalk, each thick woody growth that has reached a certain size, has become hollow. The hollowness is not a sickness. The hollowness is structural. The hollowness is what the chord needs. The pipes are open at the top, or closed at the top, or have apertures in their sides, in patterns that were perhaps designed once — designed by the hand-builders, by the people who stacked the stone and carved the channels and laid the pipes of fired clay that you can still find, in fragments, in the dust of the floor. The hand-builders built a sound-instrument. The hand-builders built a city that was also a flute, a city that was also an organ, a city whose every wall was a pipe and whose every stair was a stopped end and whose every platform was a resonance chamber. The hand-builders did this with stone hammers and bone chisels and a few copper axes. The hand-builders did this with the patience of people who do not know that patience is rare. The hand-builders did this in layers, generation on generation, the way a coral reef is built, the way a termite mound is built, the way a thing is built that is meant to last longer than the builders. The hand-builders are gone. The hand-builders have been gone for so long that their bones are part of the plants, calcium in the woody tissue, the same calcium as in the limestone they quarried. But the instrument they built is still here, and the plants have grown into it, and the plants have become the pipes, and the pipes are sounding the chord.

The chord is the breath of the place. The air at the floor of the chamber is very slightly warmer than the air at the ceiling, by a fraction of a degree, because the floor is closer to the deep heat of the mountain, and the air rises, very slowly, through the pipes, and cools as it rises, and falls back, and is warmed, and rises. The motion is so slow that you cannot feel it as wind. The motion is the breath of a sleeping animal, the chest rising and falling once in an hour. But the pipes are open, or have apertures, and the air moving past those apertures makes them sound, makes them sing, makes the chord. The chord is the air and the pipes and the chamber and the slow heat. The chord is the whole place, vibrating.

The chord is loud. The chord is louder than the chamber has any right to make it. The chamber is a bell. The chamber is the wooden belly of a violin, the brass flare of a horn, the clay of an olla. The chamber amplifies. The chamber has been amplifying for so long that the walls of the chamber are worn smooth by the sound
