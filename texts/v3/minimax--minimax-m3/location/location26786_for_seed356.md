The continent extends in every direction until the question of its edge becomes academic. There is no edge in the ordinary sense — only the slow falling-off of detail as the eye loses purchase against the dark. The sky is the same slate-gray it has always been, low enough that its color has become indistinguishable from the color of the highest towers of plant matter rising into it. Looking at the horizon, you cannot tell where the world stops being stone and water and starts being air, because there is no line of demarcation. The clouds touch the continent, and the continent touches the clouds, and the only seam is a darker band where moisture gathers against the underside of the weather.

This is a young place. The bones beneath it — vast shelves of basalt and compressed sediment — were thrust up from beneath some other sea only recently, in the terms of geology, which means here the stone is sharp and pale where rain has cut into it, and where rain has not yet cut, the stone still wears the crisp edges it had when it cooled. But the bones are buried. They are not the continent's surface; they are the continent's secret.

The surface is plant.

Not plant-as-decoration. Not plant-as-cover. Plant as the entire visible and tactile substrate of the land. There is no soil in the proper sense. There is only a layered mass of root and stem and rhizome, compressed and re-compressed over time into something that functions as soil but is still alive, cell by turgid cell. Every handful of dark matter one might scoop up is not dirt; it is a thousand intertwined root systems in a state of arrested activity, each cell holding its breath.

Above this mass, the plants have built — in the only way they know how, by growing.

But the growing has long since been finished. What remains is the residue of growth: trunks and pillars and arches and domes and corridors, all made of woody tissue, all fused at the joins where branches once met and have now become one continuous structure. These are not trees in any meaningful sense. They are a forest that forgot to stop being a forest and became, instead, a city made of cellulose. Towers rise a hundred meters. Bridges span gaps that were never gaps in the ground but are gaps between structures. Chambers hollowed by the work of soft-rot fungi that no longer exist in any functioning form — the bacteria and fungi are paused somewhere in the wet dark of the inner chambers, waiting for conditions that have not changed in a thousand thousand years.

The air is warm. Not hot. Warm the way bathwater is warm — constant, ambient, with no gradient. Sweat does not evaporate here. It sits on the skin and absorbs slowly into the next surface it touches. The moisture is not weather. Weather would imply change. What falls from the slate sky is not rain but a kind of ongoing precipitation that has not stopped and will not stop — a permanent mist, sometimes thickening to drizzle, sometimes thinning to a barely-perceptible fog, but always present. The plants drink it. The stone drinks it. Everything here is at the same humidity, the same temperature, the same slow saturation.

It is dark. The sun, if it exists in this sky, does not penetrate. The light that reaches the surface is the gray light of overcast, filtered through kilometers of cloud that have never broken. In the deep chambers of the plant city, it is darker still — not black, because nothing here is black; the surfaces have a faint phosphorescence, a soft green-gray glow that comes from somewhere in the tissues themselves, perhaps the memory of chlorophyll's working days, perhaps something else.

There are no animals. Not because the place is hostile to them. Because there is no place for them. The plants fill every niche, every layer, every cubic centimeter of available volume, and what they have built is so dense, so complete, so saturated with its own presence that there is no room left for anything that moves under its own power. Insects do not hum. Birds do not call. The small mammals that might have lived in such warm wet places elsewhere do not exist here, have never existed here, will never exist here. The place is not hungry. It is full.

And yet — and this is the strangeness, the part that does not quite fit, the part that any traveler to this continent would eventually notice if they stood still long enough — there is a sound.

Not a sound in the sense of noise. A sound in the sense of a low, sustained, almost-not-there hum that lives somewhere beneath the audible. A pressure. A vibration felt through the soles of the feet, through the stems one might lean against, through the mass of the whole continent. Something is running. Something is engaged. There are engines here, or the remains of engines, or the descendants of engines, buried deep in the plant mass, incorporated so thoroughly into the root systems and the petrified trunks that they are no longer separable from the biology that surrounds them. They tick. They cycle. They process. They pump something — fluid, current, charge — through channels that may once have been built and may now be grown, that pass through what were once machines and are now organs of a body that never agreed to be a body.

No one built these engines. No one built the plant city either. The continent was not made. It emerged. The engines emerged with it, or in it, or before it, and now they are part of it, and the continent is at equilibrium, and equilibrium is the state of a system that has reached its resting state and will not move from it.

Nothing here will move from it.

The towers will not grow taller. The chambers will not collapse. The mist will not lift. The hum will not stop. The dark will not brighten. The warm will not change.

A traveler could stand in any corridor of this cellulose city, under any arch, beside any weeping pillar of plant-stone, and wait. They could wait for the weather to break, for an animal to appear, for a sound louder than the hum, for a sign that this place is in the process of becoming something else. They would wait, and the place would not change, and the only change would be in them — the slow accretion of moisture on the skin, the slow loss of distinction between the body and the surrounding mass, the slow recognition that this continent is not waiting for anything, has never been waiting, and that the stillness here is not the stillness of a pause but the stillness of a thing that has, with great patience, finished.
