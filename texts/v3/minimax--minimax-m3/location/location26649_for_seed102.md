The place is a throat. A narrow inverted bell carved into warm stone, opening down through a crack you could miss if you weren't looking for it — below the surface, a notch in something larger, a hidden pocket buried in the flank of a hill or the ribs of a formation or the underside of a continent. The chamber is small enough that you could throw a stone across it in any direction. A single room. Intimate. Enclosed. Like standing inside a cupped hand.

The walls are sharp. Fresh stone. Recently split from whatever pressure pushed it open. There is no weathering here, no rounding, no erosion's patient softening. The rock is young and it looks it — black and orange in places, almost glistening, the mineral grain visible like teeth. Where the stone has cracked it has cracked clean.

There is no water. None has ever been here, or none has been here in so long that the place has forgotten what it is. The air is dry. Your lips would crack. Your skin would tighten. And yet — and yet — the air is heavy with something else. A warmth. A thickness. Almost a sweetness on the tongue, if you let yourself breathe through your mouth. Chemical. The stone is off-gassing, or something is metabolizing in the warm air, or the plants — yes, the plants — are producing something. The atmosphere is breathable but unfamiliar. The oxygen is a little higher than you'd expect. The carbon a little lower. Something else is mixed in. The plants do not drink. They have no roots in any conventional sense; they adhere to the rock like limpets, like burns, and feed on the chemistry of the warm air and the slow weeping of mineral vapors from the cracks. They feed, and so they are lush.

But they are sparse across the chamber. Lush where they live, bare where they don't. There are clumps, isolated stands, separated by stretches of warm stone. They look nothing like plants. They look like corals. They look like fungi. They look like both at once and neither. Their structures are thick, layered, undulating. Some are tall as your knee; others are flat and pressed to the rock like spilled wax. Their colors are deep — wine-dark, near-black, a green so dark it is almost blue. They have no leaves in any conventional sense. They have blades, fans, plates, sprays, fine filaments. They feel damp to the touch though they are not — they are slick with some other secretion, some waxy bloom that catches the light and the dust.

Light is dim. Not absent. A pale sourceless glow, like the inside of a just-closed eye. A little of it comes from the cracks above — perhaps the slimmest seams to a brighter world, admitting only a thin grey suggestion of daylight. The rest of it comes from the plants themselves. They emit a faint amber-green radiance at the edges of their blades, at the seams of their stalks, like coals that have been banked and will never catch or die. The plants glow. The plants are the light. You learn to navigate by them.

The whole chamber is loud.

It is loud in a low way. A hum that sits in your chest. A hiss that sits in your teeth. Like standing inside a hive. Like standing inside a conch shell held to the ear, except the conch is alive and a long way from any sea. The plants rasp softly against each other when they sway, and they are always swaying, though there is no breeze you can feel — they sway on their own, or on convection currents in the heavy warm air, or because the sound itself moves them. The stone pops and ticks faintly: thermal fractures, cooling, but slow, so slow. Something chitters in regular pulses from a crevice you cannot see, like breathing, but never varies its rhythm. A long scrape of something dragged, once, somewhere far off, that never comes again. The chamber is an instrument and everything in it is a part of the instrument.

The fauna is everywhere and nowhere. Abundant but thinly spread. You see them at the edges of the light, or you hear them and never quite locate them. Long pale things that move across the rock in ripples, slow as poured liquid, perhaps gastropods without shells, perhaps something without a name. Things that hang in the warm air itself — drifting, finned, fin-less — not swimming, because there is no water to swim in, but floating, gliding on currents in the heavy air. They have soft bioluminescent bellies, dim turquoise, that pulse faintly with the chamber's hum. They are numerous; there are dozens in view at any time, if you learn to look. They are small. Nothing larger than your forearm. Most are smaller. They feed on the plants, perhaps, or on each other, or on the chemistry of the air. Their noises contribute to the hum. The hum is partly them. The hum is also partly the stone, cooling. The hum is also partly the plants, respiring.

Nothing changes. You stand here for an hour, a day, a week — the sound is the same. The light is the same. The temperature is the same. The plants do not grow. The creatures do not die. The stones do not erode. Nothing shifts. This is equilibrium. This is a place that has reached itself and stayed. It is stable the way a held breath is stable — full of life, but not for long, and yet indefinitely, and yet never altering.

In one corner of the chamber there are marks. Faint marks. Someone has been here.

A low ring of stones, shoulder height, marking out a space the size of a sleeping body. The stones are stacked without mortar. They are old enough to be furred with a crust, but there is no lichen — only a thin amber film of the same waxy substance the plants secrete, dried hard. A few scratched symbols on the largest stone. Hand-tools were used: a pointed rock, perhaps the same black glass that lies beside the ring. The marks are careful, deliberate. They do not weather. Nothing here weathers.

Beside the ring of stones there is a wedge of obsidian, or something like it — black glass, sharp, placed with intention. A small basket of woven fibers — fibers stripped from one of the plants, dried, treated, twisted tight — containing a handful of dried plant matter and three of the small floating creatures, dead and stiff, posed with a kind of tenderness. A still life. A portrait of subsistence. Whoever made this has not been here in a long time. Or has been here always and is now part of the place. You do not see anyone. You cannot tell if anyone is here.

The place is complete. The place is finished. It does not invite you in, nor refuse you. It holds you the way a cupped hand holds water it doesn't know it's holding. You are in it. It does not change for you. It will not change after you.

The hum continues. The light continues. The plants sway in their unfelt breeze. The small pale creatures drift. The shadows do not move. The chamber is a stable system balanced at a strange point, and it will remain so for as long as its nature holds, which may be longer than you can imagine, or may end tomorrow when something collapses somewhere else in the world and the cracks above finally widen enough to admit weather or light or time.

For now: it is warm, dry, dim, loud, still, alive, and utterly strange.
