The cavern is still becoming. That is the first thing to understand about it: it is not finished. The limestone has not finished surrendering to the water above, and the water above has not finished finding its way down. The ceiling weeps in a thousand places and will continue weeping long after anyone present has gone, and the slow patient dissolution of the rock goes on regardless of who is listening, which is everyone, because the sound of it never stops.

You arrive — if you arrive — by a freight elevator or a vertical shaft or a slanting rail tunnel that descends through three kinds of stone, and then through a fourth that is wet to the touch, and then the shaft opens, and you step out onto a metal grating, and the cavern swallows you. Not metaphorically. Acoustically. The sound closes around you like water. You can feel it in your sternum.

It is a great space. That is not quite the right word either, because "space" suggests emptiness and there is no emptiness here. Density is the rule. Every surface is doing something. The ceiling bristles with stalactites that have not yet joined their stalagmite partners below — they hang in pale congregations, thickening drop by drop, blunt as thumbs, some of them reaching a meter, some three, none of them finished. The walls are furred with flowstone, that orange-and-cream plaster of ancient mineral runoff, ribbed and curtained where the water has been patient. Rimstone dams step down across the floor in tiered basins, each pool shallow, each overflow feeding the next. The floor itself is a confusion: standing pools, terraced calcite, loose rock from last week's collapse, mud that sucks at boots.

The air is warm. Closer to body temperature than to anything you'd call cold, and saturated — water hangs in it visibly, a faint white haze where the few lights catch it. You sweat within minutes of stepping off the elevator, and the sweat does not evaporate, it just sits on the skin and runs. There is the smell of wet limestone, which is not unpleasant, and beneath it the iron smell of old machinery, and beneath that something organic, a sweet rot, bat-guano generations old compressed into the sediments of forgotten ledges.

And the sound. There is no quiet here, has never been quiet here, will not be quiet. It is the sound of water in volume: a torrent somewhere unseen, plunging into a sump, producing a sustained oceanic roar that fills the chamber and is reflected off every wet surface until you cannot localize it. That is one layer. Below it — the bass thrum of pumps, three of them or four, running continuously, their vibration transmitted through the rock itself so that the floor shivers faintly under your feet. Above it — the higher register of water in pipes, the hiss of pressure valves, the slow rhythmic clunk of a piston somewhere on the far side of the cave. And above even that, woven through everything, the percussive drop of individual drips and trickles, thousands of them, each multiplied by the cave's acoustics into a faint glittering static. If you stand still long enough you begin to pick out individual notes: a regular *tok-tok* from a stalactite above you, a different interval from the one beside it, so the whole cave is playing an unending aleatoric percussion that never resolves. The machines and the falling water are louder. They are always louder. They drown the patient music of the geology the way a highway drowns birdsong.

Light is scarce. A string of caged work-lamps runs along the catwalk — forty-watt bulbs in steel cages, half of them dead, the rest casting yellow puddles that reach maybe five meters before the wet air eats them. There is a bank of fluorescent tubes inside the pump-shed that you can see from the walkway, humming faintly, their light reflecting off the sweating metal of the pipes. Far across the chamber, perhaps a hundred meters off, the head-lamps of a maintenance crew move in the dark like deep-sea fish — three pale spots that drift, dip, rise. And there is a fourth source of light, and this is the strangest: a faint blue-green glow along the rim of one of the rimstone pools, a low continuous phosphorescence, probably bacterial, probably unrelated to the industrial presence, probably older. It does not illuminate anything. It only marks its own edges.

The built things are scattered, as if a small operation has been set down inside a much larger cathedral and has never grown to fill it. The catwalk is the largest — a steel-and-grating walkway on stanchions bolted into the rock, snaking from the elevator shaft across a saddle of dry ground and ending at a metal shed perhaps eight meters square. Inside the shed there are gauges, valves, a control panel with most of its labels illegible, a wooden stool, a clipboard hanging from a nail, an unlit lantern, a calendar on the wall from a year you would have to climb up to read. The shed is welded rather than riveted, which dates it to the second era of operations, after the original wooden structures burned. Pipes of varying diameter run from the shed in several directions: a fat one, painted green and now mostly orange with rust, disappears into the rock above and reappears far overhead, held by brackets; a smaller black one runs along the catwalk itself; a third, much older and not painted at all, has been bypassed and lies on the floor like a shed skin, half-buried in calcite. There is a rail track that runs along the catwalk and then, improbably, plunges down a slope into one of the rimstone pools — an old mining tramway, its cars long rusted into immobility. There are ladders bolted into the walls at intervals, leading to ledges whose purpose is no longer obvious. There is a hand-painted sign at the head of the main catwalk, faded to the point of illegibility except for the word *CAUTION*, which is itself cautious about its vowels.

Outside the shed and the walkway, the cave is not empty, only unstructured. There is a winch on a tripod near one of the deeper pools, used for lowering instruments or samples. There is a stack of PVC pipe, twenty-foot lengths, plastic-wrapped against the damp, slowly fogging with condensation. There is a coil of rope on a cleat. There is a wheeled plastic bin labeled in marker: *SAMPLE — DO NOT EMPTY.* There is a folding chair, also plastic, set up facing the largest of the rimstone pools as if someone intended to come back and sit in it. They have not come back, or they have come back and not stayed. Either way the chair is there.

The fauna has accommodated the sound. It had to. Anything that could not tolerate a sustained roar of ninety-plus decibels either died here or left long ago, and the things that remain are the things that cannot hear it, or no longer try. Midges in pale drifting clouds above each of the few warm lamps, so dense they look like smoke from a distance. Pale isopods in the rimstone pools, tiny blind things walking upside-down on the underside of the water film, undisturbed when you dip a hand in. A salamander — or what looks like a salamander, perhaps *Eurycea* of some species, slick and pinkish-white — on the damp rock beside the catwalk, motionless, watching nothing. Worms in the sediments of the guano ledges, where no human goes. A frog, somehow, somewhere in the deeper chambers, calling or not calling — you cannot hear it over the pumps and the falling water, which may be why it still bothers.

There are bats, but fewer than you would expect. A few thousand, roosting in a high fissure on the far side of the cave, visible from the catwalk as a moving dark mass when the head-lamps swing past. They emerge at dusk from a chimney that opens somewhere above and they have to fly past the pumps to do it, and the pumps have been running long enough that their echolocation has been adjusted, or so the theory goes. They come and go and the cave does not much notice them.

The water is everywhere and is the reason for everything. It emerges from a thousand fissures in the ceiling and from a dozen larger openings — one of them, near the far wall, a true stream that pours out of a horizontal slot in the rock and falls perhaps fifteen meters into a plunge pool that is the source of most of the noise. This is the river above, having found its way down through a passage too narrow and too deep for anyone to follow, and arriving here with all the force of gravity and a head of water from the surface. The plunge pool is the largest body of water in the chamber — perhaps forty meters across, dark, its surface disturbed by the falling stream and the vibration of the pumps, which draw from it through a screened intake on the far side. The screened intake is the reason for the shed, the gauges, the pipes, the whole industrial project: somewhere, up on the surface, this water is needed, or processed, or sold, and so it must be lifted. The pumps take it from the pool and push it upward through the green-rusted pipe, and the pumps are loud, and the pumps run constantly, and they have been running for decades.

The cave is still forming. The dissolution has not stopped. The stalactites thicken by a millimeter a decade in some places, by a centimeter in others where the water is rich. A section of flowstone on the western wall is encroaching on an old ladder, slowly burying the lowest rungs in cream-colored stone that was liquid a hundred years ago and is solid now. The rimstone dams creep outward. New drips find new paths; an old stalactite that had been building steadily for centuries was undermined last winter by a shift in the fracture above and now hangs at a different angle, its tip displaced by two degrees from where it was last surveyed. A small collapse from the ceiling has partly blocked the catwalk; the loose rock has been swept aside but the scar remains, a fresh paler patch against the darker older ceiling.

The slow machinery of mineral deposition, the slow machinery of the pumps. Two processes on different time scales, both continuous, both indifferent. The cave does not care that humans have come down into it. The cave would be doing what it does whether they were here or not, and will be doing it long after the pumps have rusted through and the catwalk has fallen and the chair has become a calcified lump on the floor. But for now — for as long as the infrastructure holds — the place is both: a raw geology still in the act of becoming, and a working industrial site, packed with machines and formations and fauna and the unceasing roar of water moving, water falling, water being pushed back up to the surface by engines bolted to the rock.

You stand on the catwalk and the sound holds you and the warm wet air sits on you and the dim lights barely reach the walls. A drip falls on your shoulder. Somewhere a salamander does not move. Somewhere a pump exhales. The cave grows by a thickness you will never measure.
