# The Empty Bowl

It sits high, and it sits alone. The whole thing fits inside an area smaller than a hamlet — a fractured pocket of stone cradled between two ridges, with the sky pressing down through it like a thumb into wet clay. The elevation is what announces itself first: the air thins, and the light comes pale, washed clean of its warmer colors. Everything here has the clean blue-grey cast of cold.

The stone is young. That is the strangest thing about the place, and you'll notice it before you've reached the walls. Cliffs and outcrops thrust up with edges still sharp, still serrated, as though the mountain finished building itself only recently and hasn't yet had time to round itself into age. The fractures are crisp. The facets where something has broken away look sawn. There is no dust to speak of; the rock hasn't powdered itself into submission yet. Everything mineral here looks surgical, deliberate, freshly made. Glacial polish? No — there are no glacial striations. Tectonic? Possibly. It doesn't matter. The shapes haven't yet been argued with by weather.

And yet the water has arrived. Even at this height, even in this coolness, water finds its way. You can hear it before you see the place — a constant muffled roar that drifts down with the wind and then back up again, looping through the hollow. Inside the bowl, after you crest the rim, the sound is enormous: not crashing, but *full*, like standing inside a seashell. Every drop of water that falls, every trickle that escapes a fissure, every slow seep from the saturated rock face, throws itself against the wet stone walls and joins its small voice to a thousand others. The place is loud with water the way a forest is loud with birdsong, except there are no birds. The mountains are too high and too cold, and the wind comes out of somewhere without feeders. So the water speaks alone, and louder for the lack.

There are buildings.

This is the part that makes you stop and tilt your head. They are built from the raw stone — quarried, presumably, though you can see no evidence of a quarry — and laid up into walls, arches, plinths. The construction is human-scale, hand-tooled. Chisel and adze and patience. No metalwork, or only the simplest: no flush seams, no iron cramps. Each block was dressed to its neighbor by eye and touch. The walls course around the bowl like a rim, half-broken, half-standing, and at the lowest point — the place the water wants to leave — they cross each other in a structure that might once have been a gate, or a dam, or both. There is no roof. Most of the buildings have collapsed to their own first floor, leaving toothed silhouettes against the sky. The rest are cellars, low chambers, hollowed rooms whose tops have gone to weather. You can stand inside any of them and look up past the broken courses of wall directly into the cold and the cloud.

But they are not ruins exactly. They are too unfinished for that. They are constructions that were *interrupted*, or *abandoned*, and what remains looks more like the scaffolding of something that hadn't decided yet what to be. Inside one chamber the floor is still unlaid — bare stone, with a shallow pool standing in it. Inside another, the wall rises only to shoulder height, then breaks into an open lattice of stacked stone the wind can whistle through. The town is half-born. The town is mid-sentence.

And into all this — the broken walls, the unfinished floors, the sharp-edged bones of the rock — the water and the plants have come without resistance.

Mosses first. They are the architects now. Every horizontal surface holds a depth of spongy green, deep enough that a footprint will leave a dark wet print and water will rise slowly into the depression and stay there, jeweling, for an hour. Ferns have colonized the cracks — pale green fiddleheads trembling in the wet air. There are liverworts, filmy and translucent, pluming down the inside faces of walls. A few shrubs have got their roots into the wet joints between two stones and grown to chest height, spilling over the tops of the broken walls. None of it is tall. None of it is woody. The growing season is short and the cold keeps things low and patient. But the color — the saturation — is astonishing. After the grey of the rock and the white of the sky, the green of it almost hurts. It looks like the hills have been wrapped in velvet, but the velvet is breathing, and if you put your hand flat on it the whole surface gives and stays dented for a while after.

Mist lives here. It comes in from the west on a regular schedule, and when it arrives the whole bowl seals itself under a low lid of cloud. Visibility falls to the next wall. Sound seems to thicken, because every surface is wet now, and the acoustic of the space shifts each time the water load on the rock changes. Drips become rivulets. Rivulets become small falls. The loud, hollow roar inside the broken buildings grows louder when the mist is in. The walls pour. Every horizontal ledge runs water. The bowl becomes a cistern.

There are no people. There may have been. The construction argues for them. But the place gives you the feeling of a room the inhabitant has just stepped out of — any moment now, the chair will scrape, the kettle will speak — except nobody steps back in. You cannot find a footprint in the moss that isn't your own. You cannot find a hearth that has been used. Yet. The water dripping from the eaves of the unfinished cathedral at the bowl's center has been doing so for a long time; there's an inverted stalactite of lime and moss where the flow has nurtured a downward growth, like a green icicle.

Animals are scarce. A bird, occasionally — once in an hour, the high thin note of something that doesn't live here but is crossing. A furtive mammal-print in the deepest moss of a roofless corner, but you don't see the maker, only that it passed through. The ecosystem is essentially plants and water and rock and the occasional transit of something else. This is part of why the place feels so loud. With so few voices, the few that remain take up the whole conversation.

There is something else, which is harder to name.

The walls don't quite line up. You keep catching it out of the corner of your eye — two courses that should meet but pass each other and resume an inch apart. A doorway whose threshold slopes a quarter-turn. A window opening that looks through to another window opening three rooms away, and through that, to a third, and through that, to sky. The geometry is just slightly warped, the way a sentence can be slightly wrong and still mean something. Some of this is the slow shifting of the young rock beneath the construction; the foundations are not yet trusted by the ground they stand on. Some of it is the builders' hand, which was learning as it went. But the cumulative effect is that the architecture gives you a subtle, repeated *wrong* feeling, as if the bowl is breathing very slowly in and out and you are only catching the edges of its motion.

What changes here? Almost nothing, and almost everything. Erosion has barely begun. The sharp edges will one day round themselves. The walls will settle further into the wet stone they sit on. The mosses will slowly lift the courses they have invaded; frost will prise open the joints. Trees will arrive, in the fullness of time, and begin the long argument about whether the place should be a forest or a ruin or both. But for now, the bowl is doing the slow, patient work of becoming. Stone to soil. Sharp to worn. Built to grown-over. The whole place is rotating very gently forward into its own future, and we are only watching one frame of it.

Stand in the central ruin, where the unfinished arch lifts itself halfway to heaven. Listen. Even with almost nothing alive, the place is loud as a sea. Every drop of water that has ever fallen here is still, in some sense, falling here.
