A high plateau torn by water long ago and left to sit in the wind ever since. The elevation is the kind that presses the temples and quickens the breath; nothing grows above knee height because nothing has the patience for it. Stone, mostly — laid down in pale horizontal layers, lifted up by some old force, and now resting in the wind, waiting for nothing.

The valley is wide enough that the far rim is a suggestion more than a line, perhaps twelve miles across, but the floor is broken by ridges and channels that give it complexity. A central spine of harder rock runs north–south, divided from the surrounding flats by gouged gullies where meltwater runs in the morning and evaporates by afternoon. The gullies are damp now, even though the season has not decided what it is — a grey light at this elevation, holding something in reserve. The dampness is everywhere and nowhere. It beads on the underside of every flat stone. It darkens the sand in the lee of every boulder. It pools in the long shadows the ridges throw to the east in the early hours. Moisture here is a visitor that never quite arrives and never quite leaves.

The wind is the constant. It does not stop. It is the loudest thing in this place and the only loud thing that is not made by something alive. It comes down the long axis of the valley, and it has been coming down this axis longer than anything can say. When it hits the spine of harder rock it lifts over it and the sound changes — a hollow whistle, a low moan, a sudden bark where it catches the lip of a particular shelf. The wind has been wearing the rock, and the rock has accepted the wearing. The shapes are smooth where the wind touches them and sharp where it doesn't, a polished-stone vocabulary of curves and undercuts the eye can read for hours without exhausting.

Vegetation: almost none. Where the gullies hold meltwater longest there are cushions of something — a grey-green moss, a few stalks of grass bent sideways by the prevailing wind, the occasional shrub as wide as it is tall, twisted into a low dome. These are the only colors besides the rock and the snow. Most of the visible landscape is pale: limestone pale, granite pale, the bleached brown of old streambeds. Against this the sparse plants are darker than they should be, almost black in the raking light.

The light is merciless because so little of it is being absorbed. It comes down flat and throws shadows sharp as incisions. In the morning the shadows are long and the rocks look like teeth. By midday the land flattens to its true color, which is the color of dust and old bone. In the afternoon the light angles back, the gullies fill with shadow, and the place looks different — older, more crumpled, more like itself.

Animals are abundant, though you don't see them so much as sense them. The wind pushes them into the lee of things; they live in the spaces the wind cannot easily reach. Marmots whistle from among the rocks, and the sound is small, and the wind swallows it. There are birds — many birds — riding the updrafts along the ridges, their cries thin and high and almost eaten by the air. Foxes cross the open flats at certain hours, their fur the same color as the rock; you only see them when they move. Insects come out in summer, brought by the sun, and they are the loudest creatures for their size — a steady hum from any patch of damp vegetation, layered beneath the wind. Herds of something — ibex, perhaps, or mountain sheep — move along the upper ridges in early morning, small as ants against the stone.

The dampness draws them all. Where there is water there is life. The morning channels are the highways of this place; every creature visits them at some point in the day. By afternoon they are dry and the traffic moves elsewhere. There are pockets of standing water in certain bowls of the rock — places where runoff collects and the wind cannot reach it — and these pools are the most populated things here. They have algae at the bottom. They have insects. They have, sometimes, a fox drinking at the lip. They are mirrors in a landscape otherwise without mirrors.

No structures. No walls. No roads. But on the lee side of some large boulder you can find a faint circle of darker stone where someone sat long enough that their body oils marked the rock, or where a fire was lit one night and the heat changed the patina. A few flakes of worked chert in the dry streambeds — sharp, translucent, the same color as the stone they were struck from. A horn core, polished, half-buried. The traces are so faint they almost aren't there. People came here. People used to come here. They probably still come, in the seasons when the passes are open, and they leave almost nothing behind.

The wind is the main sound, and it is not a gentle wind. It is the sound of air pushed through stone for a long time, with texture — sometimes a hiss, sometimes a roar, sometimes a sound like tearing cloth when it goes over a sharp edge. So constant that you stop hearing it after a few minutes, and then you hear everything else: the marmots, the birds, the faint sound of water in the channels, the tiny tick of grit against grit as the wind moves the sand grain by grain. The wind makes the silence loud by contrast. When it pauses — and it does pause, briefly, between weather systems — the world seems deaf. Then it returns and the world is loud again, and the body settles back into the small adjustments: turning a shoulder to the gust, lowering the head, breathing through the nose because the mouth dries too fast.

What is strange about this place. The wind has done something to the rock that doesn't quite look like wind-eroded rock. Some of the boulders are too smooth, too balanced — they look almost placed, almost deliberate. The channels are too even in their spacing. The pools are too round, as if they had been drilled. It isn't that anything is wrong, exactly; it is that everything is slightly too right, slightly too consistent, as though some hand had smoothed the rough edges without quite finishing the job. The land has a quality of being worked. It hasn't been worked. But it looks worked. This is the wrongness — a feeling more than a fact, the kind of thing you stop noticing after an hour and then notice again a day later.

The temperature is cool and remains cool. Snow comes and goes; sometimes the whole valley is white for a week, sometimes there is only a dusting in the gullies. The coolness comes from the elevation more than the season. Even at noon in summer the rocks stay cool to the touch on their shaded sides. Even in winter the sun is strong enough to warm the skin through the wind-chill. The body oscillates between cold and warm in a way that becomes almost rhythmic, almost pleasant.

Everything is in equilibrium. The gullies erode a little each year and the rock weathers a little each year, and the amounts balance. The water comes and goes and comes and goes in the same measure. The animals live and die and the population stays roughly the same. The wind blows. The light arrives. Nothing is changing. Nothing has changed in a long time. Nothing will change for a long time. This is a place that has arrived at its final shape and is content to stay there — stone, wind, dampness, light, the small persistent noise of things that are alive, and the silence that lives between the gusts.
