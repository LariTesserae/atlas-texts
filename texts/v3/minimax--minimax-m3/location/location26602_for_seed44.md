# The Hothouse Quarter

The first thing is the wet.

It is not rain anymore, hasn't been rain for weeks — rain here is an event with a beginning and middle, and this is something else, this is the air itself deciding to become heavier than air, droplets condensing on every surface until the surfaces stop being surfaces and become part of the humidity. You walk into the Hothouse Quarter and within thirty seconds your clothes are not your clothes. They are a membrane. You are breathing water.

The second thing is the green.

There is no ground in the conventional sense. There is a stratum — call it the first layer — and it consists of a thing like packed clay but it is not clay, it is the pressed and composted remains of a thousand thousand generations of growth, dark as good soil, smelling of fermentation and faintly sweet, and from this stratum the green erupts upward in a way that has more in common with crowd than with vegetation. Stems. Stems is the wrong word. Trunks. Trunks is wrong too. They are vertical bodies, rising five, six, seven meters, but they branch and rebranch so quickly that the impression is of green foam, of chlorophyll given ambition. The trunks are not woody, or not only woody. They are fibrous, layered, ringed inside like a tree but soft as rope outside, and where a branch fails the plant knits around the failure and continues. There are no dead branches. There is no dead wood. Everything that dies is immediately taken up, composted by the same growth that produced it, so the bodies of these plants are also the bodies of their grandparents, and the floor of the Quarter is, if you scrape it back, the floor of the Quarter forty years ago, and forty years before that, all the way down to the original paving, which is still visible in the deepest cellars, flagstones the color of bone.

The buildings are inside this.

That is the trick of the Hothouse Quarter, the thing the cartographers argue about. The city built here first. The city came first, and then the plants came, and then the plants decided not to leave, and now it is impossible to say which is the host and which is the parasite. There are three- and four-story tenements along the main artery, brick, mortared, windowed, built maybe a hundred and fifty years ago in the damp-warm style of the southern provinces — thick walls, shallow window reveals, slate roofs that turn out to be unnecessary because the plants have grown over the slate and made a continuous green cap that sheds water better than slate ever did. The buildings stand. The buildings are sound. It is the plants that are precarious, not the architecture, and yet when you walk through the Quarter you feel as though the buildings are precarious, as though the brick is the membrane and the green is the muscle. This is an illusion. The brick is the muscle. The green is the membrane. But the illusion persists.

Light enters the Quarter as an orange suggestion. The canopy — if canopy is the word, if canopy can refer to a thing this irregular — is at maybe eight meters, ragged, and below it everything is filtered. You do not see direct sun. You see a warm diffuse glow, slightly amber, slightly green, and your shadow, if you have one, is not sharp. The light is consistent through the day. Dusk does not happen here, or dusk is a four-hour event rather than a twenty-minute one. Mornings begin as a faint brightening of the same glow. People who live here lose the habit of looking up.

The water is everywhere because the water has nowhere else to go.

There are drainage channels — there have to be drainage channels — cut between the buildings in some distant past when the city still believed the plants were a problem to be managed. They are clogged now, clogged for decades, but the water does not need them. The water enters through the canopy, is absorbed by the green mass, is transpired back out, condenses on the next cool surface (which is always present, because the canopy is so thick that interior surfaces are five degrees cooler than exterior), and falls again, and is absorbed again. The Quarter runs on its own weather. If you stand under a thick patch of growth on a still afternoon you will be rained on by the plants themselves. They are louder than you'd think, dripping. A whole district of dripping.

And the sound.

The sound is industrial.

That is the other thing, the second thing after the wet and the green, that surprises you, because you would expect — looking at the photos, reading the descriptions — that a place like this would be quiet. It is not quiet. It is loud. It is loud the way a steam laundry is loud, the way a paper mill is loud, the way any place where water meets heat meets mechanical effort meets human presence will be loud, and the Hothouse Quarter is all of those.

The first sound is the hiss and tick of the steam vents. The Quarter is heated from below. Geothermal — there is a hot spring somewhere under the south edge, an old thermal feature of the region, and someone, a long time ago, piped it. The pipes run under the buildings, under the green stratum, under the original paving, and they vent in a hundred places: iron grates set into the curb, brass roses mounted on walls, bare pipe-ends that simply open into the air. Steam comes out. The steam is warm, smells of sulfur faintly, and the plants love it. This is why the plants are here. The heat and the water together, the year-round seventy-five degrees, the never-drying humidity — this is a hothouse. An accidental hothouse, built on top of a city.

The second sound is the engines. There are engines in the Hothouse Quarter because there are factories in the Hothouse Quarter, because a place this warm and this wet is good for certain kinds of work. Tanning. Paper-making. The cultivation of certain fungi for dye and for food. There are small operations here, family-scale, two or three workers, and the machines they use are old — belt-driven, foot-treadled, the kind of thing that runs on the geothermal steam tapped through a small auxiliary pipe. You can hear them. A rhythmic clunk. A wet slap. A steady wheeze of steam through a valve. From any given point in the Quarter you can hear at least three of these rhythms layered over each other, none of them in sync, all of them continuous.

The third sound is the people. There are not many people. The Quarter holds, perhaps, three thousand souls in a space meant for eight. The buildings are dense — built-up, party-wall, four stories of apartments over ground-floor workshops — but the Quarter as a whole is sparsely populated, because the Quarter is hard. It is damp. It smells. The light is bad. People leave when they can. Those who stay are the ones who need the wet, the warm, the steam, the cheap rents, the proximity to a particular job. Tanners stay. Paper-makers stay. Fungalists stay. There is a small community of people who run the geothermal system itself — plumbers, steamfitters, pressure-watchers — and they stay because their fathers stayed, and they are the closest thing the Quarter has to an old aristocracy. You hear them in the evenings, more than the workers: voices, calling up between buildings, hanging laundry, arguing over a pipe. The Quarter is loud with them.

There are almost no animals.

A few cats. A few chickens in some of the ground-floor workshops. Pigeons roost on the upper stories and come down in the morning to peck at the compost layer. Rats, certainly, in the deep strata. A pig or two in some yards. No dogs, or very few dogs — dogs don't like the wet, it gets into their coats. The animals that thrive here are small and wet themselves: snails on the walls, pale slugs on the brick paths, insects, beetles, a fat-bodied spider that lives in the steam vents and eats the insects. The fauna is below the knee. The fauna is under the leaf. The fauna is in the compost.

There is no wind.

There is never wind in the Hothouse Quarter. The canopy is too thick, the buildings too close, the air too damp to move. Air moves by convection — warm air rising from the vents, cool air sinking from the canopy — and that is all. Smoke from the small chimneys of the workshops rises straight up until it hits the underside of the green mass and is absorbed. You cannot smell the tanneries from inside the Quarter. You can smell the tanneries from a block away, outside the canopy, where the smell escapes in a humid breath at street level, and then it is gone.

The pace is slow.

This is a place that runs on patience. The plants grow an inch a day in the wet season and almost not at all in the dry season, but the wet season is eight months long and the dry season is only a suggestion. The compost accumulates a centimeter a year. The buildings settle a millimeter a year into the soft strata. The pipes corrode and are replaced, slowly. The people age and are replaced, slowly. The Quarter has been here for a hundred and fifty years. It will be here for a hundred and fifty more, unless someone drains the spring, which no one wants to do because the spring is the whole reason for the Quarter being what it is.

There is no weirdness here. There is no magic. There is no strangeness beyond the ordinary strangeness of a city that has agreed to share itself with a forest and has not yet decided which is the guest.

You stand in a courtyard. There is brick underfoot, slick with condensation. There is green over you, dripping. There is steam hissing from a grate to your left, and beyond the grate, in the workshop, you can hear a man working a treadle, slow and steady, a wet rhythmic sound like a slow heart. Somewhere above you, a woman is calling her child in for the evening meal. Somewhere below you, in the dark, in the compost, in the deep stratum, something is being made ready for the next layer.

The light is the same. The wet is the same. The sound is the same. It will be the same tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, and the Hothouse Quarter will continue to be itself, slowly, patiently, in balance, in its own state, in its own weather, in its own green and wet and warm and loud and quiet and ancient and ongoing way.
