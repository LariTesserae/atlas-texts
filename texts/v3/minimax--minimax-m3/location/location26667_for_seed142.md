# The Exhausted City of Calor Reach

There is no water here. There has not been water here for a very long time, though the city was built around the memory of it — aqueducts that carry only dust, fountains calcified into pale sculptures, basins where nothing has pooled since the last rains that anyone still living remembers, which is to say: no one. The basins are cathedrals. The basins are where the pious go.

The elevation is significant but it does not feel like a mountain. The land has been ground down so thoroughly by time that the city sits upon what might charitably be called a swell — a long, slow rise that climbs for thirty kilometers and gives the impression, from below, of a fortress. From above, of a wrinkle. The actual peak is somewhere beneath the central spire of the Foundry, and the spire does not so much rise from the land as the land consents to let it be tall.

## What is here

A plateau of warm, pale stone. Ochre, bone, rust, the color of dried blood on sandstone. The stone is not one stone but the compressed residue of older stones, so thoroughly aged that geology has stopped being a discipline here and become a kind of archaeology of itself. The cliffs at the city's edges — and there are edges, the plateau does have edges, falling away in worn steps toward lower country that is just more plateau, just older and lower, all the way down to a horizon that suggests the planet itself was once a mountain range that lay down and never stood up again.

The light is wrong. This is the first thing visitors say, the only thing they agree on. The sun — if it is a sun — does something to the atmosphere that makes the light heavy and amber, like honey held up to a candle, and the shadows it casts are not crisp but slightly bruised at the edges, as if the darkness is reluctant to commit. Twilight here is long, and the dark that follows is not total but is not really light either. It is a kind of luminous absence, a glow that comes from everywhere and shows nothing.

The city runs on that glow when the brief day retires.

## The city itself

It is industrial in the manner of something that forgot it had a choice. The construction is layered — older Calor Reach, the low stone and brick districts with their sealed cisterns and their walls thick as siege, and over it the Reach proper, the iron and brass works, the pumped pipework, the slow-spinning rotors of the Ventilators that turn even now in the still air because they have always turned and the operators no longer know how to stop them safely, and over that the high works: the chimneys of the Foundries, the suspension bridges of empty walkways, the scaffolded spires that climb and climb and never reach a point.

There is no plant life. There is also no dead plant life, no driftwood, no peat, no coal. The fuel is something else. The ore is something else. The Foundries produce a metal that is warm to the touch and does not corrode, and the city has been making it for so long that the slag heaps have become neighborhoods and the neighborhoods have become foundations for further Foundries, and so the city grows downward into its own waste, which is also its wealth.

Machines move in the deep places. They are not well understood. The operators tend them, oil them, replace what wears. The machines do not appear to do anything that the operators can name.

## Sounds

Mostly quiet. The Ventilators turning. The distant, soft complaint of metal under stress, a sound like a held breath. Footsteps on stone. The Foundries are muffled by their own construction; you hear them more as a warmth in the chest than as a noise. Once an hour, a low tone from the central spire — the Hour-Strike, a single note that is felt in the teeth and then fades over a long minute. It is the loudest thing in the city.

There is no wind to speak of. The air is warm and still and tastes faintly of iron.

## What lives here

Rare. Sparse. A scattering of beings, humanoid in the way that suggests convergent engineering rather than convergent evolution — too many redundancies, too many sealed joints at the wrists, eyes that do not quite focus in the usual sequence. They wear layered clothing against what is unclear. They work. They repair. They eat something they call bread, though the ingredients are not named and may not be edible in the conventional sense. They speak a language that has fewer vowels than most.

There are animals. Perhaps. Small moving things in the lower corridors, heard more than seen. The consensus is that they are machines. The minority position is that they are not. No one investigates.

## What it is like to be there

You arrive on a road that took you a long time to walk. There is no transport. There used to be, the rail-beds say so, but the metal was needed and the metal is always needed. You enter through a gate that is never closed. Inside, the streets are wide and the buildings are tall and the population is not enough to fill either. The scale is wrong — everything built for crowds that did not come, or that came and went, or that are still here but have become too quiet to count.

You feel watched but not observed. You feel, after a time, that the city is not so much empty as *finished* — that it has reached some equilibrium it has been reaching for a long time, and that the continued activity of the operators and the Foundries and the Ventilators is a kind of ritual rather than a purpose, a way of keeping the equilibrium in place rather than producing anything further.

You are not uncomfortable. The light is warm. The stone is warm. The metal is warm. The air is warm. The city is the temperature of a held hand. Nothing here is going to happen to you, or for you, or against you. Nothing here has happened in a long time. The hour-strike comes. The rotors turn. The operators go about their work, and the work goes about the operators, and the slow, vast, exhausted stone of the plateau receives it all the way a parent receives the breathing of a sleeping child.

You could live here. You could live here a long time. You would not, in the end, be certain that you were.
