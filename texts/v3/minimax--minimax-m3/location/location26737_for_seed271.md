# The Hollow

You come down into it through a long, shallow grade of cracked earth, and then the ground simply stops dropping, and you are inside. The place is roughly the size of a small cathedral — perhaps a hundred paces across, perhaps less, you cannot tell because the air is the same everywhere and there is no good point of reference. It is a depression in the land, a soft bowl carved out of red-brown soil by something patient that is no longer working. Whatever made it is finished with it. The shape is settled now, the slopes gentle, the contours fixed.

The light is diffuse. There is no sun in it. The sky above is a flat pewter and gives no direction; shadows do not form. The dimness is not the dimness of evening but the dimness of a room with its shutters half-closed, that flat gray quality that makes everything look slightly older than it is. Even so, the air is warm — warm in the way that warm stone stays warm after the day has gone out of it, warm and faintly humid, the warmth held close to the ground as if reluctant to leave.

There are walls. Not many. Five or six of them, perhaps seven, low and partial, shoulder-height at most, arranged without obvious order across the hollow. They are dry-stacked stones, some of them, and some are built from a coarse brick that has gone the color of dried blood. The corners are rounded. The mortar, where there was any, is long gone, and the stones have sunk into one another and into the earth beneath them, each one finding the exact place it was meant to stop. Lichens cover them — pale gray-green, almost white, the kind that grows very slowly and means the place has been sitting for a very long time. If you run your hand along the top of a wall, your palm comes away faintly damp. Condensation gathers on the stones overnight and never quite dries.

Between the walls, the ground is bare in places and patched in others. The vegetation is sparse but it is present, in the patient stubborn way that things grow where they are not stopped. Tufts of a tough gray grass push up at the base of walls. A low shrub with small dark leaves has colonized the corner of what was once a room, its roots worked down between stones. There are patches of moss — bright moss, almost startling in the dimness — wherever water gathers. The moss is the greenest thing here. Everything else is in the brown-to-gray range.

The water is the second most noticeable thing, after the stillness. It does not flow. It sits. There is a channel that runs through the lowest part of the hollow, a shallow trough perhaps a foot wide, where in another place you would call it a stream and here you can only call it a wet place. The water in it moves a little — it trembles, it trembles, it trembles — but it does not go anywhere. There are puddles elsewhere, in the footprints of stones that have shifted, in the hollows of old doorways. When you stand near them you can smell the water: a mineral smell, cool and faintly metallic, the smell of water that has been sitting a long time against rock. After a rain — and it does rain here, sometimes, briefly, the sky simply becoming water for ten or fifteen minutes and then stopping — the puddles widen briefly and the moss darkens and the walls drip for an hour afterward. Then it all goes back to the same.

What is most strange about the place — and it takes you a while to realize it is strange, because nothing announces itself — is the silence of living things. There are no birds. There are no insects. There is no small rustle in the grass. The moss does not move. The shrub does not move. Nothing moves of its own will in the whole bowl of the hollow. You stand there and you wait to hear something, anything, a cricket, a fly, the small ticking of a lizard on a stone, and you wait, and you wait, and you understand that you are not going to hear it. The place has been emptied, or was never filled, or exists in some condition that does not support the small bright quick things. The moss grows without anything to eat it. The grass goes to seed and the seed falls and nothing comes of it. There is no decay to speak of, or the decay is so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness.

What you hear instead is the water. The water in the channel makes a sound like a single voice saying one syllable over and over, very softly. When a breeze comes through — and it comes through rarely, and only on the warmest hours — you hear the air moving against the top edges of the walls, a dry faint hiss. Once, while you are there, a piece of lichen detaches itself from a stone and falls. It is the loudest thing that has happened in a long time.

The structures — calling them structures is generous — are not arranged like a village. They do not face a street. They do not enclose a courtyard. They are scattered across the hollow as if placed by someone who was only halfway through deciding what to build, or by someone who had finished and the rest had fallen down and been carried off by water. One wall runs along the lip of the hollow as if to mark the edge. Another is a corner only, two sides of something that never had three. There is what was once a chimney — a stack of stones taller than the walls around it, blackened faintly on one side as if there had been a fire there once, but so long ago the soot has become part of the stone. There is a flat stone set into the ground like a threshold, leading to nothing. There is a circle of stones that may have been a well, though it is dry at the bottom and choked with the same gray moss that grows on the walls. None of it is decorated. There is no carving, no inscription, no painted line. Whoever made these things either could not write or had no reason to.

The whole of it is in balance. You can feel it — or you think you can feel it, which is the same thing here. The water has found its level and stays at it. The walls have stopped falling. The plants have stopped spreading. The lichen has reached as far as it means to. Nothing is being added. Nothing is being taken. The hollow is exactly as it will be tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, in a way that the rest of the world is not. It is a closed sentence, with a period at the end.

You stand in it for some time. The warmth sits against you. The dimness does not change. The water trembles in its channel and says its small syllable. A drop falls from the lip of a wall and hits a stone and the sound is round and complete and then it is gone and you are alone again with the next drop, and the next, and the next, and the hollow holds you in its warm gray hands and does not ask you anything.
