# The Upper Stoup

The place is older than its name. The name is recent — a few generations old, hung on it by the inhabitants who needed something to call it when strangers began, very rarely, to arrive. Before the name there was only the wind, and before the wind the same wind.

It is a plateau. That is the first thing to understand, and also the last, because everything else is contained inside that fact. The plateau sits high — high enough that the air tastes thin and metallic on the tongue, high enough that a man walking hard will feel his lungs complain within the hour. The stone beneath it is grey-green, shot through with veins of something darker that the old people say is the dried blood of whatever mountain stood here before the mountain wore down. Whether mountains ever stood here, in the sense we mean, is uncertain. What is certain is that the stone is very old. It has been worked on. Wind, water, frost, the patient chemistry of lichen — these have taken the plateau almost to its final form. Another few thousand years and there will be nothing left but a stain on the bedrock, a slightly raised place where the rocks remember being a hill.

The plateau is not flat. That would be too easy. It is *almost* flat — a long, slow undulation, like cloth draped over a sleeping animal. The eye reads it as level for an hour at a time, and then a fold reveals itself: a shallow valley, a hump of harder stone, a place where the ground drops half a meter over the distance of a field and then rises again. Into these folds the moisture gathers. Into these folds the people have gathered too.

---

The town is small. Forty-some structures, perhaps fewer if you count the ones that are no longer entirely separate from one another. They sit in the largest of the folds, where three shallow valleys meet, and where the ground dips just enough that the fog, which is the principal weather of this place, pools instead of sweeping past. The fog is what makes it habitable. Without it the plateau would be a desert of stone — bone-cold at night, sun-blind by day, scoured by a wind that never stops. With the fog there is water. Not much, and never falling, but condensing: onto every surface, every blade of moss, every worn stone, every back bent under a load. The fog moves through the town like a slow creature, thinning and thickening with the hours. By afternoon it is often opaque, and the town disappears into itself, and a person standing at the edge of it can hear voices and the clack of a loom and the slow complaint of a door but see nothing. By morning it has drawn back to the eastern rim, leaving every horizontal surface dark with moisture, and then the sun (if there is sun) finds the droplets and the whole plateau glitters for an hour before the wind dries it.

This is the damp of the place. Not rain, mostly. Rain comes maybe six times a year, in brief violent showers that turn the dirt lanes to rivers for a quarter of an hour and then are gone. The rest of the time it is this: fog, condensation, the slow weeping of water from seams in the rock. There is a spring at the head of one of the folds — a place where water emerges from a crack in the stone, clear and very cold, and runs for perhaps two hundred paces before it disappears again into the gravel. This is the town's water. The women go there with jars. They have been going there since before the town was a town.

---

The buildings are made of what is here. There is no metal — there has never been metal here, and the few tools of harder stone came up the goat trail from somewhere far below, a generation ago, traded for a winter's worth of dried moss and a child to carry them. So the buildings are of the local stone, broken and stacked, the gaps chinked with a mortar that is mostly moss pounded into clay. The roofs are of timber, but there is no timber up here, so the timber came too — carried up, piece by piece, over years. The timber is now older than the people. It has gone silver, then grey, then dark with the lichen that has learned to grow on it. Some of the roof-beams are still sound. Some are not, and have been replaced by more recently carried-up poles leaned against the walls, and these in turn have begun to silver, and so the town is in a slow, perpetual state of repair that never quite catches up with the decay.

The architecture is strange. Not deliberately — there was no one to teach these people a style, no tradition of straight lines or right angles imposed from outside. They built as the ground allowed. So the walls curve. They follow the natural curves of the stone outcrops they are built against. The floors tilt where the rock tilts. Doorways are the height of a stooping person because there was no need for taller ones. Roofs are steep, almost conical, because the snow (which comes rarely but heavily when it comes) must slide off, and because the wind, which is merciless, must be given nothing flat to push against. Seen in the fog, these buildings look almost grown — like a cluster of stones that has decided, over a long time, to become a village. You cannot always tell where one ends and the next begins. There are places where the wall of a house and the face of a natural boulder are the same surface, and a stranger would not know which side of it to knock on.

This is part of what is strange about the Upper Stoup. The boundary between the human and the not-human is unclear. The people have built here for — no one knows how long. Long enough that the original settlement is now geology, indistinguishable from the natural rock. The houses of this generation are built on top of the houses of the last several generations, and where the older ones have crumbled, their stones have been re-used in the newer ones, and where they have not crumbled they are simply incorporated, low-ceilinged rooms beneath the floors of newer rooms, passages that lead nowhere. A child growing up here learns the town's under-town as it learns its above-town, and they are not really two things.

---

The vegetation is lush, and this surprises people who arrive expecting, at this altitude and this cold, a kind of grey austerity. But the fog, again. The fog is the garden. Everything that grows here grows because of the fog, and most of what grows is built to catch it. Mosses: the dominant form. They cover the stones, the roofs, the walls, the lanes between the houses. They are thick, and they are many colors — a deep bottle green in the damp pockets, a pale almost-yellow on the exposed faces, a near-black where the shadows never lift. Walking through the town in summer (such as summer is — a brief slackening of the wind, a few weeks when the moss grows visibly) is to walk on a sponge. The footsteps are quiet. The smell is of cold vegetable rot and clean water.

Ferns. There are ferns in the deeper folds, where the stone shelves have collected actual soil. They grow shoulder-high in places, though a shoulder here is lower than a shoulder below. They are the only tall living thing, and they move constantly in the wind, which gives the town an odd quality of having a forest above it, a forest that is somehow only its own height. When the wind is strong the fern-fronds all lean the same way and the town looks combed.

Lichens. On every stone, every beam. White, orange, grey, a black-green that is almost black. They are the slowest painters imaginable, and they have been painting this place for so long that the town and its substrate are, colorwise, continuous. The lichen is the same lichen on the natural rock and on the cut stones of the houses. You cannot, from a distance, tell which stones have been moved by hand and which have not. This is another piece of the strangeness.

There are no trees. There have never been trees here. The shrubs are low and twisted, hugging the ground, growing in the lee of stones. They are dark, and tough, and they have small waxy leaves. They do not look like much. They are the principal fuel and the principal food of the place — the berries, in season, are gathered and stored in stone-lined pits, and the dried twigs are burned in the small hearths that are the only indoor heat.

---

The fauna is sparse. There are birds. Some kind of small dark bird that lives in the ferns and calls at dawn with a single repeated note — not a song, just a fact, given over and over until the fog lifts. There are larger birds that come up from below on certain winds, big pale things with slow wings, that circle the plateau once or twice and then are gone. There are insects, in summer: small flies, a few beetles, something that might be a cricket but does not chirp. There are said to be, in the deeper folds where the plateau breaks into broken country, a few small grey animals that the old people trap and eat, but these are rarely seen. The dogs of the town are half-wild, living off scraps and whatever they can catch, and they are the only fauna that pays attention to the people and vice versa.

The dogs are how the people mostly learn the weather. The dogs know before the people do, and a certain restlessness among the dogs is a signal that the wind is going to change, or that the fog will thicken, or — very rarely — that rain is coming. The dogs do not bark much. They are quiet animals. When they do bark, at some shape in the fog, every person in hearing stops what they are doing.

---

The sound. This is one of the first things a visitor, if a visitor ever comes, will notice. The plateau is loud. The thin cold air carries sound far. The wind, which is essentially constant, is not a gentle wind — it is a working wind, and it moves around the buildings and through the gaps in the walls and across the moss in a way that produces tones. The town hums, in weather. There are days when the wind is such that the whole place rings — a low, almost musical sound, like a great many bottles being blown across simultaneously. This is not the wind alone. It is the wind finding every hollow in the rock, every loose stone, every gap in the masonry, and making of each one an instrument. The people do not remark on it. They have heard it all their lives.

The water, too, is loud. The spring. The drips from every roof. The little channels, cut over generations, that move the spring-water from place to place. In the stillest weather, when the wind drops for an hour, the sound of water is the dominant sound: a constant ticking and trickling, the whole town weeping gently onto itself.

And the voices. Because the air carries, the voices carry. A shout from one end of the town is heard at the other. A conversation on the eastern ridge — a kilometer away — can be made out, in pieces, in the right weather. The people have learned to speak softly. They do not shout. They have also learned to listen: a certain quality of distant sound, the way it breaks up across the folds of the stone, is information. Where a visitor would hear nothing, the people hear who is speaking, and how many, and roughly what mood they are in, and how far away.

There is no bell. There is a stone, a flat one, set up near the spring, that is struck with another stone when there is need — a death, a birth, a stranger arrived. It produces a flat clear note that travels very far. The people say it can be heard from the goat-trail, which is the only way up. Whether this is true is uncertain. The sound of it is certainly heard at the edges of the town, and that is enough.

---

The town is empty in the way that small things are empty when you stand inside them. Each house holds a family, or the remains of a family — the population is small and is, by the reckoning of the people themselves, slowly shrinking. There are not many children. There have never been many children here. The cold and the thinness of the air and the difficulty of getting enough to eat see to that. The houses are spaced because there was never pressure to build close. Between them there are the lanes — beaten earth, sometimes stone-flagged where the work has been done — and the small gardens, and the fern-patches, and the moss, and the long slow space of the plateau going off in every direction, empty, vast, the same grey-green stone with its lichen and its fog and its wind.

From the edge of the town, looking out, there is very little to see. In good weather, when the fog has lifted, you can see the plateau extending perhaps to the rim — a distance that is uncertain because of the folds, but that feels like hours of walking. There are other folds out there, where water might gather, where a person could build if they wanted to. The people do not want to. They stay close. The plateau is too large, and they are too few. To go out into it alone is to walk into a silence and a wind that do not know you. A few do this — the trappers, the people who go out for the small grey animals — and they are changed by it, and do not talk much about it when they come back.

---

The weirdness. It is hard to put a finger on. The place obeys its own logic. The rules of the place are not the rules below. Time moves differently here — or rather, time is experienced differently. A visitor, if a visitor came, would feel that the day was longer and the year shorter, and that nothing much was happening, and yet that everything was happening, very slowly, all the time. The moss growing. The stones wearing. The fog moving. The people getting older and being replaced, one by one, by people who look like them and speak like them and who will, in their turn, get older and be replaced.

The town is at the end of its life, and has been at the end of its life for a long time. It is not dying in any dramatic way. It is simply settling. The stones are settling. The walls are settling. The people are settling, into the routines that the cold and the fog and the moss require. The plateau is settling, down toward its eventual flatness, toward the day when it will be no more than a memory in the bedrock. The town will go with it, when it goes. The two will not be distinguishable at the end. They are already, in many places, not distinguishable now.

This is what is strange. Not that the place is harsh, or cold, or strange in the way that a ruin is strange. It is that the place is so patient. That everything here is moving at the speed of stone, and that the people have agreed, without ever discussing it, to move at that speed too. A child grows up here and learns to wait. To wait for the fog to lift. To wait for the spring to give its water. To wait for the lichen to soften a stone enough to be broken. To wait, in the end, for the plateau to take them back.

The stranger, if a stranger comes, will feel watched. Not by anything alive. By the place itself, which has very little to do and has been doing it for a long time, and which notices a new thing when one appears. The fog will close around the stranger. The wind will find the gaps in the stranger's clothing. The dogs will come and sit at a distance and watch. The people will come out of their doorways — low doorways, requiring a stoop — and look, and not say much, and after a while offer the stranger a seat by a hearth and a bowl of something warm. They will not ask where the stranger has come from, because they know the answer is far below, and because they know the stranger will not stay.

No one stays.
