# The Terraces at the High Place

The light comes down hard here.

That is the first thing you would notice, even before your lungs registered the thinness of the air. The light is not the polite, diffused light of lowland mornings — it is poured light, struck light, light that arrives with the weight of distance behind it. Everything it touches receives it cleanly, because the air up here is too thin to be courteous, and shadows fall in dark, sharp-edged rectangles against surfaces of pale grey stone. By noon there is nowhere to stand without being fully lit. By three in the afternoon the shadows have leaned out long and the stone begins to radiate back what it took, gently, like a body cooling after fever.

The plateau sits high. You can tell because the horizon is wrong — too close, too low, and curved in a way the eye has to keep correcting for. To the east the land drops away in a long scarp of freshly exposed rock, raw as a broken tooth, the color of milk where the sun strikes it and the color of bruise where the shadow pools. To the west the plateau continues, level, until it meets weather. Cloud sits against the western edge most afternoons, a low white wall that breathes inward and outward across the stones. Beyond the cloud is a country no one has described, because from here, looking out, there is only weather.

The surface of the place is not a surface. That is the second thing you would learn.

It is construction, laid down in such density that there is no longer any meaningful difference between wall and floor and stair and basin. The plateau was not built upon; it was built *of*. Every handspan of ground is stone, or stone covered by stone, or stone holding stone. Terraces climb into terraces. Channels run along channels. Platforms the size of rooms abut platforms the size of rooms, separated by walls only as wide as a body could not pass, and beyond those walls, more platforms. There are stairs that climb to nowhere and stairs that descend into chambers that open into chambers. There are basins that hold water and basins that hold only the memory of water — rings of mineral deposit on their floors, white and rust and the deep pale blue of copper eaten by time, marking the levels of seasons no one now keeps.

The stone is pale. A soft grey, almost chalky, veined with darker threads. It was quarried locally — you can see the scars in the scarp to the east, the wedge-cuts, the ledges left where blocks were levered free. It was shaped by hand. There are tool marks everywhere, if you learn to see them: the shallow round gouges of a ball-peen, the long dragging scratches of a rasp, the matte pocked planes where a chisel bit was driven repeatedly and gave up. The joins between blocks are tight without mortar. The geometry is not intricate — no carved ornament, no inlay, no sculpture — but it is *precise*, in the way that a person can be precise when they have a lifetime and only one task. Some walls run for hundreds of paces without a single block out of true.

This is not a city.

A city implies streets, and there are no streets. There are paths — narrow, switching, sometimes paved, sometimes just the polished bare stone where feet have walked for so long the tool marks are gone — but they do not connect. They begin at walls, end at walls, climb stairs, descend, climb again. A city implies destinations. Here there are only approaches. The whole place is approach, with no place being approached.

It is wet. Constantly wet. The plateau is high but it is not dry — weather comes across it from the west and is caught, held, condensed. Every morning, even in the dry season, water appears on the stone. It does not fall from the sky. It emerges. It condenses on the cool surfaces of the walls and the floors, gathers into beads that run together, finds a low point, and drips. The sound of dripping is the sound of the place — a soft irregular percussion, sometimes a single drop for minutes at a time, sometimes a chorus from a hundred basins and channels as the morning warms and the stones breathe out their moisture. There are channels cut everywhere for this water, narrow grooves that run along the tops of walls, around the perimeters of platforms, down the risers of stairs. They feed basins. The basins overflow into other basins. The system is not for drinking. Nothing drinks it.

Water also runs in deeper cuts — narrow canals a hand's width across, some of them roofed with overlapping slabs, some of them open to the sky. These carry water that has come from somewhere higher, possibly from melting snow in a season when snow falls, possibly from springs at the western edge. The water in them is clear and cold and moves slowly. Where it slows further, in the wider basins, it is the color of glass with a faint green stain. On the walls of these basins, below the waterline, things grow — but not plants. A crust. A bloom. Mineral, fungal, something in between. It is the color of old iron, the color of dried blood, the color of nothing recognizable. It spreads in slow lobes, a millimeter in a month perhaps, and where it dies it leaves a pale ghost on the stone.

The light makes the wet stone shine. That is the third thing. The whole place is always a little glossy, like the skin of something just out of water. In the morning the condensation catches the low sun and the terraces flicker with small bright points, like a constellation laid down on the ground. By midday the shine has become glare, and the channels run with white reflections that hurt to look at for long. In the late afternoon, when the shadows come, the wet surfaces hold the light longest — the channels and basins keep glowing after the dry stone has gone grey, and the place reads itself from below as a dark mass stitched with liquid gold.

Nothing moves.

That is the third thing, and the most disorienting.

There are no insects. There are no birds. The wind moves the cloud against the western edge and sometimes pushes a thin film of water across a basin, but these are motions of the place itself. There is no life in the sense that one usually means. The crusts on the walls do not stir. The water does not ripple from beneath. There are no droppings, no nests, no tracks. The paths are not worn by use but by time and water and the rare slide of stone. Whatever was here, or built this, is not here now. Or is here in a way that does not register as presence.

The quiet is profound. The dripping is the loudest sound. After that, the wind, low and intermittent, hissing along the tops of walls, sighing into the narrow cuts between platforms. After that, very rarely, the deep groan of a block settling as the temperature changes — a sound like a sleeping animal turning over, which is unsettling because there are no sleeping animals. The stone expands and contracts very slowly in the sun and you can hear it if you are patient enough to sit and listen for an hour. The stone also weathers. Slowly. A grain loosens. A flake lifts at the edge. The crusts push their lobes imperceptibly into the channels. Water carries dissolved mineral along the grooves and deposits it a little further each season, a pale line on a basin floor that creeps upward year by year like a tide measured in millimeters.

The place was made for something.

This is the last thing you would understand, if you stayed long enough. Not for living in — there are no hearths, no kitchens, no signs of habitation in any ordinary sense. Not for looking at — there is no ornament, no vista arranged to be seen from a particular place. Not for storing — the basins hold water but not the right kind of water, not in the right quantities, and the chambers are too shallow, too open. Something was being done here, on a scale and in a pattern that does not parse into the usual human purposes. The geometry is too consistent to be accident. The construction is too careful. The water system is too deliberate. But it is also too *much*. Too pervasive. Too total. Whatever the purpose was, the answer to it was not a building but a landscape, and the builders answered it by making the entire plateau into the answer.

Some of the walls are very old. You can tell by the rounding of the edges, by the way the tool marks have softened into a matte smoothness, by the depth of the mineral crusts along the bases. Some of the walls are very new. You can tell because the chisel marks still have crisp shoulders, because the join lines are knife-sharp, because the blocks have not yet found their final color and sit pale against their weathered neighbors. The construction did not stop. It continued, or it continues. Pieces are being replaced, or added, or removed. The slow patient engineering of the place is ongoing, though you cannot see who does it.

In the highest part of the plateau, where the air is thinnest and the light is cleanest, there is a single broad platform with no walls around it, only a low parapet. From here, looking out, you can see the whole construction spread away below you — the dense pale field of platforms and basins and channels, all of it wet, all of it shining, all of it quiet. The cloud comes and goes against the western edge. The light moves across the terraces in slow geometry. A drop of water, gathering at the parapet's edge, swells, holds, and falls.

The drop falls a long time before it strikes.
