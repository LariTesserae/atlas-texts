# The Furnace Plateau

The air hits you first. It comes off the rock in sheets, shimmering, the kind of heat that makes the horizon bend. Even at this elevation — and you are *high* — even here, the stone sweats. Condensation runs in slow rivulets down every vertical face, darkening the basalt and the sandstone alike, and the rocks themselves weep, weep, weep into the basins below. There is no snow on these heights. There has not been snow for a thousand years. What there is, instead, is *wet*: a perpetual film of moisture that clings to everything, drawn up from below by a sun that never quite lets go.

The plateau is a ruin that has forgotten it was ever ruined. Somewhere in the deep past — so far back the bones of it have become geology — something immense pushed the land upward, twisted it, cracked it open. The erosion since has been patient and complete. There are no jagged peaks here. The ridges roll, broad-shouldered, their edges softened into long curves, their flanks mantled in talus and the slow creep of regolith. The valleys between them are wide and shallow, scoop-shaped, and they are filled, *always* filled, with the green.

Because the green. Oh, the green.

It is the wetness made visible. Every cupped valley, every ledge where a seam of impervious clay holds the rain, every north-facing hollow is a garden. Ferns the size of doorways unfurl in the drawdown of seasonal creeks. Moss — thick, sprung, electric green — carpets the stones and climbs the trunks of trees whose roots have split the basalt apart. Orchids erupt from crotches of branches in pale constellations. The air smells of rot and bloom in equal measure, of warm mud and jasmine and the mineral tang of seeping groundwater. Vines rope between the trunks, between the trunks and the rocks, between the rocks and the sky. The understory is layered so thickly you cannot see your boots.

And rising through it all — through the green, through the wet, through the heat — the stacks.

They are everywhere. You cannot walk a hundred paces without encountering another. They are tall, slender, made of riveted iron and ceramic tile and brass that has gone green with verdigris. Some are cold, their mouths open to the weeping sky; some are hot enough to make the air above them wobble. From the working ones, a faint haze rises, sulfurous, faintly sweet, and threads of steam curl sideways into the canopy. They were not built. They *grew* — or rather, the rock grew them, the way a tree grows a burl: slowly, mineral by mineral, around the buried bones of something mechanical. Here a cylinder head protrudes from a matrix of ore, its bolts still threaded, its casting mark still legible. There a section of rail curves out of a hillside, runs fifty yards into the ferns, and ends in nothing. Gears the size of millstones lie half-buried in moss, their teeth worn to nubs. A flywheel spins, slowly, in a clearing, with nothing attached to it and no one turning it.

No one is here. There has been no one here for a very long time.

But the engines — the *system* — still runs.

It runs in the pipes that hum beneath your feet, in the faint subsonic throb you feel through the soles of your boots before you hear it. It runs in the way the groundwater moves with strange directionality, drawn sideways through the rock by pressures no aquifer would produce. It runs in the heat itself — heat that has nothing to do with the sun, that wells up from deep below, that keeps the ferns unfurling even at this altitude, that turns every rainstorm into a hothouse. Something down there, in the basal rock, in the magma or below it, is *burning*, and the burn is being managed, distributed, kept in its channels, by a machine that has long since forgotten how to stop.

So the place is alive. The water cycles faster here than it should: rain falls, is sucked into hot stone, returns as steam from a hundred flues, condenses on cold iron, drips back down. The ferns drink it. The orchids bloom. Things eat the ferns.

And the things —

You hear them before you see them, mostly. A high, piping chatter from the canopy. A heavy, wet splashing from the next valley over, the sound of something large moving through a marsh that should not exist at this elevation. A scraping, bark-peeling sound as something with claws climbs one of the dead stacks. In a glade you come across, a herd of six-legged grazers lifts their heads at your approach — short, dense, copper-colored, with throat sacs that inflate and defl ate as they breathe. They watch you with horizontal pupils and go back to their feeding. They are not afraid of you. Nothing here is afraid of you. Nothing here has learned to be afraid of anything in a very long time, because the system has been balanced, has been *running*, for longer than fear makes sense.

Higher up the food chain: you glimpse it once, across a ravine. A long shape, low-slung, the color of wet iron, moving with the unhurried precision of something that has never been hungry. It pauses at a stream to drink. The water steams faintly around its muzzle.

Above it all, the sky. The sky is enormous here — that is the only word. It is the sky of elevation, of thin air, of the upper troposphere, and it stretches from one rim of the world to the other in a dome of pale, bleaching blue. Clouds form against the peaks and *dissolve against the peaks*, their moisture wrung out by the heat below and recycled endlessly into the basins. The light is strong and clean and directionless, the light of a place where the sun has had a long time to think about what it wants to do.

And the sound. Mostly: quiet. The hum of the pipes, very low. The drip of condensation. The wind in the ferns, which sounds, at this scale, almost like rain. The occasional metallic *ping* of cooling iron. The distant, hollow moan of a flue that has found a particular resonance in the wind. The place breathes, in and out, in and out, and asks nothing of you.

It will be here long after you leave. It was here long before you came.

It runs itself.
