# The Stack at the Edge of the New Sea

The world has only just remembered how to drown, and this is one of the first things it has remembered to leave behind.

A single column of stone stands in the open water — fifty meters tall, perhaps seventy, no one has measured it because no one has come to measure. It is the color of bread-crust and wet chalk, an apricot-grey, faintly banded. From a distance it looks like a finger pushed up through a quilt. From inside it, the sea is the quilt. There is no land anywhere on the horizon. There is only the column, and the water, and the place where the column meets the water.

The column is not finished.

That is the first thing to know about it. The erosion has not done its work yet. The stone is new to the air. Its flanks are raw, almost soft in places, and where the wind has begun to take them they show the grain of the rock like the inside of an unworked beam. Every few minutes something small comes loose and tumbles down into the sea with a percussive, indifferent *tock*. The sea doesn't notice. The column doesn't seem to notice either. It is in the patient, distracted process of being born — or, possibly, of being unmade — and it does not distinguish between the two.

## The Pocket

Up near the top, where the column narrows, there is a place where the wind has eaten a concavity out of the western face. It is not large — a person could stand in it with arms spread and almost touch both walls. The floor of the concavity is a rough shelf, a little higher than the rest, and on this shelf the world has collected a few stray things.

A scatter of dark stones, arranged. Not by anyone — by the storm. Five or six of them in a crescent, larger ones toward the back. A fist-sized one has been laid on top of two of the others, and one of its facets is rubbed smooth, as if a thumb has been worried across it for many years. Beside the stones, a single standing piece of slate, maybe a forearm tall, with a notch cut into one edge. The notch is clean. The tools that cut it were hand-tools — a hammer-stone, a punch of antler, something patient and rubbing and slow. The notch is not a letter. It is not a tally. It is a notch, and that is all, and it is enough.

A pile of shells. Not a midden. Just shells — stacked, or rinsed, or simply left. Limpets and a single white scallop, the kind that lives in deep water. The scallop is the wrong shape for here; it should not exist in the pocket. Someone brought it up. There is no one to ask.

And a fire-pit, in the deepest part of the concavity, ringed by seven small stones. The pit is cold. It has been cold for a long time. There is no soot on the concavity walls — only a faint bloom, the memory of smoke, like a thumbprint in dust. It rained here, once, in a way that rain does not rain anymore. Whoever used the pit did not need to keep the weather out. They needed it to be somewhere else.

## The Water

The sea is everywhere. It is below, and it is around, and on a day like today it is also above — a low pewter ceiling of cloud that hangs only a hand's breadth above the top of the column. From the pocket you cannot tell where the cloud ends and the sea begins. The whole world is the same grey-white muscle.

The sea is not quiet.

This is the part that has to be lived to be believed. The column stands in a place where two currents meet — one coming down from the drowned north, one coming up from the drowned south — and they collide at the base of the stack and throw themselves upward. There is no surf line, exactly. There is just a continuous, churning **roar**, a sound that is not coming from one direction but from all directions at once, like being inside a bell that someone is forever striking. The pocket itself funnels it. The concavity catches the noise and turns it, and what comes into your ears is the roar plus the roar plus a higher harmonic that is the wind passing the notch in the slate and making a thin, keen, almost-not-there whistle that you only hear when the roar briefly hesitates, which it never quite does.

If you cup your hands over your ears you can hear your own blood. If you take them away you cannot hear your thoughts. The deafness is not silence. It is fullness.

Water comes into the pocket in three ways. There is the spray, which is constant, which soaks everything to a fine mist within minutes and turns the dark stones to the color of a wet mouse. There is the rain, which is not rain in the usual sense but a horizontal thing, a blown water, a gray veil that moves sideways across the concavity and pools in the cracks and runs off in silver threads. And there is the column itself, weeping. The stone is porous at this height; it sweats a slow mineral water, and in places it has crusted to a pale orange where the weeping has been going on longest. The crust is soft. You can flake it with a fingernail.

## The Living Things

Sparse. That is the kind word. The kind honest word is *almost not*.

A single plant, on the lip of the pocket, where a crack runs down toward the sea. It is a kind of cress, or a kind of grass — there is no one left to argue about it — and it is bright green against the grey. It has three leaves and what may be a fourth coming. It is not obviously dying. It is not obviously thriving. It is doing the only thing available to it, which is to be a plant, here, on a column that is being born, in a sea that has drowned whatever used to be a continent. Each morning it is wetter than it would like. Each afternoon it is drier than it would like. It endures.

A lichen, pale yellow-green, fanning across the back wall of the concavity in a pattern that suggests the inside of a lung. It has been there longer than the cress. The lichen is the slowest thing in the pocket.

A small bird, on most days. Not a songbird. Something grouse-shaped, dun, with a too-long neck and feet made for gripping wet stone. It comes in from the southeast, usually, when the weather is least bad, and it stands on the slate for ten or twenty minutes and looks out at the sea with one eye and then the other and does not make a sound. Then it leaves. It does not nest here. There is nothing to nest with.

A crab, in the lower part of the column, where the rock dips toward the water. It is not visible from the pocket. It is a fact about the column rather than the place.

That is all.

## The Weird

The strange thing is not the sea. The strange thing is not the wind, or the rain, or the fact of the column itself. The strange thing is the slate.

It is the wrong slate. The stone of the column is sedimentary, a soft limestone-marl in the apricot band, a denser grey shale lower down. The slate in the pocket is metamorphic. It came from somewhere else. It is the right size to have been carried — by a person, in a pack, up whatever path or ladder used to lead to this place, before the path or ladder was drowned. It stands here now with the patience of an object that knows it has outlived its purpose.

But the notch.

The notch is a strange notch. It is the right shape for a notch. It is at the right height for a notch to have been cut by someone kneeling. But the angle is not quite the angle of a person. It is too precise. It is the angle that stone takes when stone cuts stone, slowly, under pressure, over a long time. The notch could have been made by a person with an antler punch. It could also have been made by the column itself, as the column flexed in some storm a hundred years ago, by the rock of the slate against the rock of the column, the way a river makes a notch in its own bed. You cannot tell which. No one has been able to tell which, for a long time.

There is a third possibility, which the pocket does not name: that whoever made the fire-pit, and arranged the dark stones, and brought the scallop up out of the deep water, knew that the notch was already there, and placed the slate near it on purpose, and left the rest of the marks on purpose, so that the difference between a person's notch and a stone's notch would become a question that the pocket would carry forward into whatever came next.

## The Time of It

The column is emerging. That is the verb for this place — **emerging**. Not emerged. Not emerged at all. The water has been here longer than the rock has been out of it. The world is still mostly sea. The column is one of the first vertical things to come back, and it is coming back slowly, with the patience of an object that knows it has not been asked.

But the water is restless. The dynamic of the place is the dynamic of a tide that is not a tide, a storm that is not a storm, a sea that is in the process of deciding whether to fall or to rise further. Sometimes, in the mornings, you can stand in the pocket and watch the waterline move down the column by a foot or two — the rock exhaling, the sea remembering itself. And sometimes, in the afternoons, the sea comes back up with a surge and the spray leaps over the lip of the pocket and the dark stones are submerged for an hour, and the fire-pit fills, and the cress is beaten flat, and you think the column is being taken back.

It is never taken back all the way. Something — the rain, the wind, the slow upward push of the bedrock — keeps nudging the rock into the air by a thumb's width at a time. The column rises. The sea rises. The column rises faster, by a little. The difference between them, on most days, is the difference between the pocket existing and the pocket not existing, and that difference is held in the sound.

The roar, the constant roar. It is the sound of the column being argued over. It is the sound of two emergences. If you stay in the pocket long enough, you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as a kind of speech — a long, slow, ongoing argument between the stone and the water, in a language that has no words but which is, unmistakably, **about** something, and which will go on being about something for as long as either of them remembers to keep going.

The slate stands. The notch points at nothing. The cress puts out its fourth leaf.
