A high col, saddle-shaped, pressed between two shoulders of darker rock. Below the level of the surrounding summits by a body's height or so — just enough to gather a little snow in the deepest winter, just enough to escape the worst of the wind for an hour or two. It is not a large place. You could walk across its widest part in five unhurried minutes. The walls of the col rise steeply on three sides, and on the fourth a long ridge slopes away down toward a country you cannot see from here, only feel: warmer air rising, a faint sourceless scent of greenness that never quite arrives.

The ground is stone. That is the first thing and the last thing. There is no soil to speak of — only a thin grit of mineral dust worked down between the blocks by centuries of freeze-thaw, finer than sand, pale gray to nearly white, the only light color in the place. The blocks themselves are dark. Basalt, probably, or some cousin of basalt: dense, fine-grained, the color of dried ink. In certain lights they show a sub-metallic sheen along their cleaved faces, almost blue, almost the color of the sky. In other lights they are simply black. The high sun of this altitude — so thin and so bright that the shadow of your hand on the stone is hard-edged as a knife-cut — turns them both ways by the hour.

The blocks are packed tight. This is not a scree field where stones roll and resettle; this is a field that has come to rest. Frost has split the original bedrock into roughly rectangular pieces, perhaps the size of bread loaves down to the size of skulls, and then frost has lifted them and let them down and lifted them again until they fit each other with almost no space. In places you could not slide a fingernail between them. In other places there are gaps of a hand's width, narrow channels that run more or less downslope and carry the day's melt. The whole col is, in this sense, a kind of paving — not laid by anyone, but laid nonetheless. Erosion has spent a long time on this place and has produced something close to order.

Up at the eastern lip of the col, where the rock begins to rise again toward the higher summit, there is a wall. Dry-stacked. Stones chosen for their flat faces and laid in courses without mortar, the way you might build a low garden wall, only here the stones are heavier and the courses are wobbly with age. The wall runs for perhaps eight meters along the contour, then turns a corner and runs three meters more, then stops. There is no fourth wall. The gap where it should be is open to the col. Inside the L it has stood, the floor is the same close-packed stone as everywhere else, but slightly cleaner — the wind keeps it swept. A small hollow in the corner might once have been a hearth; it is only a depression now, with a blackening on the surrounding stones that no longer smells of anything.

The wall is not the only structure. Higher up the slope, just below where the rock turns to cliffs, there is a cairn. A proper one — taller than a man, conical, built of stones sorted by eye so that the larger ones ring the base and the smaller ones crowd the apex. It looks heavier than it should; it looks as though it has been there longer than the wall. Beside it, slightly downslope, there is a second cairn, smaller, more tumbled. And a third, almost lost, little more than a knee-high pile. They are not on a straight line, but they are not scattered either; there is a sense of intended arrangement, although the intention is no longer readable.

There is a slab. At the western edge of the col, set on end in a gap between two blocks as if it had always been waiting there to be stood up, a single dark slab about the height of a man's chest. Its face has been worked. Not carved — worked, in the patient sense: rubbed smooth with sand and water over years, perhaps, until the surface holds a kind of polish. Nothing is incised on it. There is no writing here, in any alphabet. But the polish itself is the writing, or it is the only part of the writing that survived. The slab catches the late sun along its edge and throws a thin bright line across the stone floor of the col for an hour before sundown.

There is water, but only just. In the morning the stone is furred with frost, white as a salt flat, and the air smells of nothing. By mid-morning the frost is gone and a faint dampness has come into the cracks. A short channel, a finger's width, has been cut — or has chosen — to run from the upper cairn down to the center of the col, and along it a thin line of meltwater moves all day, glinting, never more than a thread. It collects in a basin. The basin is too regular to be entirely natural: a circular depression the size of a large bowl, hammered perhaps, or worn by hands and water working together over a long time. The water stands in it a few inches deep, perfectly clear, black-bottomed against the dark stone. At night it freezes. By the next morning a clear disc of ice sits in the basin, and around its edges the meltwater has begun again. There are small icicles under the lee side of the cairns in the early hours, fat and blunt; they go by noon.

The light here is the kind of light you do not get lower down. Even at this cold it is strong enough to make you narrow your eyes. The sky is a deep, depthless blue, almost violet at the zenith, paling to a chalky white at the horizon where it meets the haze of the lower air. The sun comes from a long way above the horizon and casts shadows that are short and very black. When a cloud passes — they pass below you, often, big slow shadows sliding across the country far beneath your feet — the col dims and the dark stone turns the color of slate, and the wet places in the channels go suddenly bright as the cloud-shadow lifts. When the sun is back, the stones ring faintly under your boots and the air feels almost warm for a few minutes. It is a trick. The air is not warm. Your face knows.

The place is quiet. Not silent — there is always the wind, and the wind has a particular voice here because the close-packed stones give it something to play against. It comes in from the west in the afternoons and draws through the channels between the blocks with a low, slow note, almost a hum, that you feel in the soles of your feet as much as you hear. In the mornings the wind is usually still, and what you hear instead is the ice. Stone contracting in the cold makes small dry sounds, a kind of ticking, irregular, and somewhere a piece of meltwater falls from an icicle's root with a single soft *tok*. That is the loudest it gets. Once in a long while there is a heavier sound from somewhere up on the cliffs — a boulder giving way — and the silence afterward is a different, larger silence than before.

Nothing lives here. There are no birds. There are no insects. There is not even lichen, although at first you think there must be — the paler staining along the north faces of some of the blocks looks so much like growth that the eye insists. But it is only mineral: a salt efflorescence, perhaps, or a different rock showing through where the surface has flaked. The col is too high, too cold, too dry for the simplest things to take hold. Whatever animals might have wandered up here once do not come now; you can tell because there is no scat, no sign of bedding, no gnawed bones. The cairns were not made by creatures, only by people who came and went.

There is one last thing, and it is the reason the place unsettles, slightly, although you cannot quite say why. Toward evening, when the sun is low and the long shadows of the cairns reach across the col toward the standing slab, the dark stones do something with the light that is hard to describe. They do not shine, exactly. They do not glow. They deepen. The black of them becomes a black that has texture in it, and the polished face of the slab lights up along its edge as if it held a wire of the sun's heat inside it, and the water in the basin, which had been only black-bottomed all day, suddenly shows its green — a faint, cold, almost submarine green — and holds it for as long as the angle lasts. It is the kind of light that suggests the place is paying attention to you. It is the kind of light that makes you feel you arrived a long time after whoever built the cairns, and a long time before whoever comes next, and that the col itself, in its close dark patience, will be here for all of it.
