# The Cathedral of the Long Drain

You arrive, or imagine you arrive, through a corridor that gives way without warning to vastness. The hollow has the proportions of a cathedral nave, but the walls are wrong in a way your eye keeps refusing to accept — they curve in directions that should imply edges but refuse to produce them, so the room reads as both smaller and larger than it is, simultaneously more intimate and more immense. The place is roughly the size of a small town square. That is the scale. The volume, though, has been gutted. Most of it is air. The architecture hangs at the edges, floats above, descends from a height you cannot quite place, and the middle is mostly nothing — a tall, dim, hollow well where sound is allowed to gather and roll.

Everything here is young. That is the first thing your hand understands, even if your eyes are slow to follow. The stone, the metal, the strange other-substance the walls are made of where stone would normally end — all of it has the texture of something that was, very recently, not here. The cuts are crisp. The fractures are sharp and unweathered at their cores. The edges have not yet been sanded by time into anything soft. This place has not been here long. You can almost taste the newness, mineral and metallic and faintly sweet, like the air after a struck match.

It is built. This is unambiguous. There are walls — or things that perform the function of walls. There are floors — or surfaces that agree to be walked on. There are towers or columns or hanging geometries suspended in the empty middle by means you cannot identify; they do not appear to be attached to anything. They simply occupy. The construction is layered, vertical, and dense at the margins, thinning as it rises or falls, as if the architects only felt obligated to fill the edges. Some of the structures look civic — wide, communal, plainly public in their proportions. Others look private, narrow, folded inward, the way a person folds inward when they are waiting. Everything is built but most of the building is not for you. You feel, standing in the middle, that you are a thing permitted, not a thing invited.

The light is dim but it is everywhere. There is no source you can point to. The walls themselves seem to remember a glow and offer it back reluctantly, a low, even, warm-grey luminescence that does not behave like reflected light — it does not brighten where it should, it does not darken where shadows ought to fall. Your shadow is uncertain. It is there if you want it and not there if you don't. The light is roughly the color of a cloud seen from inside a tunnel.

It is warm. The temperature is something like a held breath, a hand pressed to a sleeping forehead. The wetness of the place is not rain — there is no weather here, no sky to fall from — but condensation. Every surface weeps. The walls run with a thin continuous skin of water. The columns run. The floating geometries run, and the water that drips from them falls a long, long way before it reaches anything, and the sound it makes when it arrives is one of the loudest things in the room.

There is vegetation. Not in spite of the architecture but because of it. Plants have arrived in the cracks of the construction — pale root systems threading between stone and that other substance, ferns uncurling from places where the engineered meets the natural and finding the seam. There are vines that hang for what feels like ten meters without branching. There are flowers you do not recognize, opening and closing in slow patient cycles no animal is around to witness. The greenery is lush but it is quiet-lush, the kind of growth that occurs when nothing eats it and nothing competes with it — a lushness that has never had to defend itself. The water feeds it. The dim light is enough. The warmth is enough. The plants have, in the absence of any reason to be cautious, taken the entire room as their inheritance.

There are no animals. None. Not even insect, not even the suggestion of insect. The absence is so total that it becomes a presence in its own right, a silence-within-the-sound, the way the middle of the room is a hush-within-the-construction. Whatever built this place either chose to leave no creatures in it, or the conditions of the place do not permit creatures, or creatures were removed, or creatures never applied. The lushness is uninterrupted. Nothing chews. Nothing hunts. The plants grow and grow and nothing answers them.

The sound is loud. This surprises you because of the emptiness, because you expect an empty room to swallow noise. It does not. The hollow is shaped to carry. Every drop of condensed water that falls from a height becomes an event, and the events never stop. There is a continuous percussion, soft but unending, layered and re-layered against itself as the sound rings off the columns, the walls, the floating geometries. Underneath this, deeper, there is a hum — not mechanical, not quite — a tone that lives in the floor, that you feel through the soles of your feet before you hear it with your ears. It is the technology of the place, or its ghost. It is the hum of something running that no one is currently watching. Sometimes a higher note enters, briefly, and then withdraws. The room is full of a constant quiet roar that you gradually realize is not coming from any one thing but from the room itself, from the way the architecture and the water have agreed to behave together.

The technology is the part you cannot parse. The walls are made of a material that should not be possible — it is heavy when struck but does not ring, it holds condensation but the condensation never stains it, it has no grain, no seam, no history you can read. The suspended structures do not visibly connect to anything and do not fall. The floor accepts your weight but you are unsure, if you knelt, whether you would find it hard all the way down, or whether it has only been made hard for as deep as you might reasonably need to go. The light is its own explanation — it is not generated, exactly. It is *remembered*, as if the walls have been told to glow and have agreed to do so for some indefinite interval. The whole engineering is operating in a register that physics has not yet caught up to, and the place hums along at the edge of what you can describe.

It is active. Not in any way you would call inhabited. The water moves — it condenses, it runs, it gathers at low points and slowly circulates back upward through the walls by some process that is not quite capillary action and not quite anything else. The plants move — slowly, opening, closing, sending roots into seams, retreating from surfaces they find inhospitable. The light shifts in long slow rhythms that have nothing to do with any day you can identify. The hum modulates without ever truly changing its pitch, the way a held breath changes without ending. The dripping continues. The place is doing what it was built to do, and it has been doing it for what you estimate is a short time but you cannot be sure, because nothing in here is wearing down.

There is a rule, here, that you begin to feel after a while. It is not spoken. It is not written on any of the walls, and there are no walls here that could bear writing in any case. The rule is: nothing is permitted to decay. The water does not erode. The plants do not rot. The light does not dim. The hum does not fade. This is a young place and it intends to remain young. You are standing in a cathedral that has decided to stay new forever, and the decision is enforced by an engineering you do not understand, in a hollow carved out of the world and lit by its own quiet refusal to age.

You can walk its perimeter. You can sit on one of the floating geometries and feel the hum come up through you. You can let the condensation touch your hand. It is warm. It is faintly sweet. Nothing here will answer you, and nothing here will refuse you. The room continues its small, active, ancient-not-yet work. The water falls. The plants grow toward a light that does not have a source. The hum holds its single long note. You are a guest in a place that has only just decided to exist, and is in no hurry to learn what that means.
