# The Hollow of the Steeple-Vines

There is a place where the city has forgotten it is a city. Or perhaps it is the city that has been forgotten by everything else. Either way, the result is the same: a small, low, packed, ancient pocket of dwelling set in a cold and damp place, and nothing about it has changed in a very long time.

---

You come upon it from above, if you come upon it at all. There is no road that announces it. The land here is low — not a swamp, not a floodplain, but something lower than a hill and wetter than a hill ought to be. The ground gives slightly when you walk, as though somewhere beneath the packed earth and root there is water holding its breath. Rain comes here often but never hard, a fine persistent drift, the kind of moisture that does not fall so much as gather. Condensation beads on every vertical surface by morning. The stones of the place are never dry. They are dark with it, perpetually, the way a throat is dark.

Cold. Not the brittle, killing cold of open country, but the saturated, inward cold of a basement or a stone chapel. It lives in the joints. It does not move through you so much as settle into you, slowly, like dye into cloth. The sun, when it comes, is white and diffuse, the way light is in places where the air always has weight in it. It arrives without warmth. It simply makes the wet surfaces of everything into small mirrors, so the whole hollow glints and blinks at nothing.

The sound of the place is the sound of water held in place. Drip. Drip. Drip, but not from one source — from everywhere, from the moss, from the eaves, from the lips of carved figures, from somewhere under the floor. And below that, almost beneath perception, the low, slow grind of the place itself: wood settling, stone breathing, root against stone, stone against root. Nothing louder than a human voice has been made here in living memory. Conversation, when it happens, is conducted at the volume of weather.

---

The vegetation is older than the buildings. That is the first thing to understand, and the second thing to understand is that no one here quite admits it. The walls are stone — small dark stones, river-rounded, fitted with the kind of patience that does not exist in a hurry — but the walls are wearing green. Moss. Liverwort. A certain bright lichen that has no name in any language still spoken. Ferns have colonized every horizontal surface not explicitly defended against them: the rims of basins, the treads of stairs worn concave by a hundred generations of feet, the flat tops of boundary stones. Where the buildings touch, the plants have bridged. Where a roof has gone, a tree has come up through the floor of the upper room and is now the room. Vines with thick, ropey stems climb the interior walls, and they have been climbing so long that they have become structural. No one remembers which stones they hold in place. No one wants to find out.

The trees here are short and dense and dark. They grew up crowded, so they grew up together, and the canopy is a single low thing you can almost touch. Beneath it: dimness, but a dimness full of the small, persistent light that filters through wet leaves. The forest floor is a sprung mattress of moss and old wood, and it never dries.

The animals are everywhere and quiet. A certain small deer, the size of a dog, moves through the understory in loose groups, their hooves making no sound on the moss. They will watch you from twenty feet away and not move. Bright birds — too many kinds, none of them brightly colored; the colors here are damp colors, browns and moss-greens and a certain gray that means rain — work the bark and the eaves. Something lives in the roofs that makes a soft, repeated call, two notes, like a question with no verb. At night, in the deeper cold, a fox or its analogue passes through the lanes with that particular fox-gait, unhurried, as if it had errands that would wait.

There are no large predators. There have been no large predators for a long time. The bones of one are carved into the lintel of the oldest standing door, and no one alive can tell you what it was.

---

The settlement is small. You could walk from one edge to the other in less than an hour, and the edges are not sharp — the buildings thin out, the trees thicken, the streets become paths become suggestion. But within that hour, you pass through a place that is more made than almost any place you have seen. The buildings are tight. They share walls. They share eaves. They share, in some cases, floors — there are houses built over a continuous crypt, and the doors to the lower level open from inside the houses above, and the people here do not think this is strange. They do not think about it at all. It is just how it is.

The streets are not streets. They are the leftover spaces between buildings that no one was willing to give up any further to the public, and so they are narrow, and they are dark, and they are paved in the same dark wet stones as the walls, and they hold shallow green channels of slow water along their middles. You walk single file here. Two people passing must turn sideways, or one must step up onto a stoop, or one must wait. The architecture has not been planned. It has been *negotiated*, over centuries, in the language of who needed what where, and the result is a kind of dense, low, almost organic settlement that looks less like a town than like a single very old organism that has calcified around its own center.

There is a center.

---

You do not find it by looking. You find it by going downhill, because everything here drains toward it, and the stones underfoot become steadily more worn, and the channels of water become steadily more deliberate, until you are standing in a small paved space — really just the absence of buildings — with a low stone at its middle, and a ring of worn figures around it, and the water from six lanes meeting at a single iron grate that is older than the iron is supposed to be.

This is the place the place is organized around. No one will explain it to you. They will shrug, or nod, or change the subject. There is a basin here, fed by a spring that has never been known to fail, and people come to it for water, and the water is cold and clean and faintly mineral, and it tastes of the place. They pour some of it back. This is observed. It is not discussed.

Above the basin, on a stone post, a small carved figure leans forward as if listening to the water. The figure is too worn to identify. It may be a person. It may be something else. Its face is gone. Its hands rest on its knees. It has been sitting there for a very long time, and the moss has crept up its back, and no one cleans it off, and no one says why.

---

The technology of the place is, on inspection, more sophisticated than it first appears. The stonework alone is beyond what one would expect — the fitted walls have no visible mortar, and yet they have stood through centuries of freeze and damp, and the joints are still tight. The water channels are deliberate. The crypt below the houses is ventilated by some system of shafts that no one can describe, because no one has ever needed to; the air in the lower rooms is always moving, and always the same temperature, and no one thinks this is remarkable. The metal that exists — the grate, a few door hinges, the blade of a single knife kept in a household — is dark and heavy and does not rust, which is also unremarked upon.

Wood is used where wood cannot be avoided, and avoided where it cannot. The tools are simple. The clothes are woven from some local fiber, dense and oily, almost waterproof, in the colors of the place. The light, at evening, comes from small oil lamps, and the lamps are made of stone, and the wicks are a moss that has been prepared in some way no outsider has ever been shown.

There is no writing. Not because writing is forbidden, but because no one has needed it. The accounts are kept by other means. The dead are remembered by other means. The agreement between the settlement and the spring is renewed, annually, by a method that involves no documents and no words, only a particular pattern of footsteps around the basin and a particular cup made of a particular wood.

This is not mysticism. It is administration. The people here are not mystics. They are simply very old at being here, and they have learned, over that long time, what works.

---

And so the place holds. It does not change. The buildings do not change. The channels do not change. The vines grow a little each year and the moss creeps a little further up the carved figure and the stones underfoot sink a little deeper into the ground and the water finds new small ways to move and the small deer continue to pass through the lanes as if the buildings were only a particular kind of forest, and the birds continue their two-note questions into the damp air, and the cold continues, and the damp continues, and the light, when it comes, continues to make everything briefly, gently bright.

Nothing is decaying. Nothing is being built. The place has found its shape, and the shape is holding.

You could live here a long time and never know you were anywhere.

That, perhaps, is the point.
