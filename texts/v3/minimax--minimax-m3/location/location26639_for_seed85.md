# The Spire

You descend. The dark begins long before the place itself does — kilometers of it, water so cold it is no longer a temperature you feel but a substance you move through. It presses. Not painfully. Steadily. The way a hand rests on a shoulder without intent.

Then the basalt.

It is young. You know this the way you know a face is young — the features have not been worn into patience. The rock arrives in ridges sharp as broken bottles, in glassy cliffs where the cooling was too fast for crystals to organize, in scalloped hollows the size of rooms where dissolved gas once boiled out of the molten throat. Below you, the spire continues down into a deeper dark, part of a ridge that surfaced here and there across an ocean floor that elsewhere is flat and tired. Here it punched upward. The rock is dark grey, almost blue, and where the faint light touches it — which is seldom — it answers with a dull sheen, the wet gleam of a thing that has never been dry.

The spire's peak rises to a depth of perhaps six hundred meters. Not a summit exactly. More a shoulder — a place where the geometry narrows and the cliff-faces close in, and there is, between them, a kind of platform of cooled flow, broad enough to stand on, broad enough to build on. Cool, even this far down: the water carries four degrees of itself with no seasonal sympathy, and the basalt holds that coolness as a stone holds the shade of a tree.

The settlement is here. Small. Cathedral-scaled, in the way a single sanctuary can swallow a street. Several structures — three or four, you can never be sure because the angles keep disagreeing with you — clustered against the western face where the rock curves inward. Sparse. The water between them is not built over. The negative space is as deliberate as the construction. There is room to drift through and see the far cliff opposite, and the far cliff opposite is a thing worth seeing because it has been left alone.

The structures themselves are not quite *of* the basalt, and not quite *on* it. They seem to have arrived by some negotiation with the rock rather than by placement. Their surfaces are matte and dark, but a dark that does not behave like a dark should. You look at a wall and your eye slides. You look at an edge and the edge is not where you left it. The material absorbs the faint light and returns it in a register that suggests, but does not confirm, a color — somewhere between a deep indigo and the inside of an oyster. The geometry of the buildings is rectilinear in some places and in other places refuses to be rectilinear; there are corners that open into themselves, doorways whose thresholds you cross without remembering the crossing, surfaces that appear to be inside and outside at once.

There is no visible machinery. There is no visible source. The settlement does not appear to *do* anything. But it is not inert. Something in the materials — the same thing that makes your eye slide, the same thing that puts a corner inside itself — is at work in the walls. A faint, even luminescence, not from any single point but as if the matter itself is tired of being dark and is, on its own schedule, becoming slightly less so. The light does not travel. It rests. You cannot see where it ends.

The water around the settlement is almost still. There are currents, somewhere higher up, but they do not reach this depth in any form you would recognize as motion. What moves here moves because it is alive, and almost nothing here is alive. The settlement has been here long enough that the basalt beneath it has not been further eroded by its presence — a fact that would surprise you, except that you suspect the basalt beneath it has not been further eroded by *anything*, and that time has, in this small region, agreed to be still.

There is fauna. Rare. You might, in a long watch, see one — a pale thing, slow, with the loose architecture of a jelly but the patient drift of something older. Its bell pulses once every several seconds. It does not acknowledge the settlement. It passes through the negative space between the buildings the way light passes through a window it has not chosen. There may be others. There may not. You do not have the language to ask.

The sound is mostly the absence of sound. The water is too deep for waves to matter. The rock does not settle, does not groan, does not tickle itself with thermal contraction. The settlement, if it makes a sound, makes one below the register you can parse, or in a register your ear has not been built to receive. What you hear, if you strain, is your own blood, and the faintest clickings of shrimp from somewhere far above, and once, just once in the hour you are here, a low tone that is not a tone but the inside of your skull making peace with the pressure. The place does not need sound. It has been completed.

There are no plants. None. Not a film, not a spore, not a single photosynthetic cell leaning into what light is offered. The surfaces of the basalt are bare glass. The surfaces of the buildings are bare something-else. The absence is not hostile. It is the absence of a category, the way a library does not contain weather. Plants, here, would be anachronistic. Plants belong to a project this place is not part of.

Above you, far above, the ocean. Below you, deeper still, the ridge continues its long geological argument with the mantle, but the argument is silent and slow and you are not here to witness it. You are here, suspended, in this small dark pocket of architecture and cold water and rock that has not yet learned to be old. The settlement does not greet you. The light in its walls does not change as you approach. The rare creature does not turn. Nothing adjusts to your presence, because nothing here is the kind of thing that adjusts.

You are not observed. You are not unobserved. You are within the attention of something that does not need to move its attention to see you.

The spire will be here tomorrow. The settlement will be here tomorrow. The water will be cool tomorrow. Nothing, in any way you can detect, is going to change. The equilibrium is so complete it does not need to be maintained. It simply *is*, the way a theorem is, once written.

You surface eventually, or you do not. Either way, the place will not have noticed, and the place will not have changed, and the dark will have kept the rock's secret without effort and without end.
