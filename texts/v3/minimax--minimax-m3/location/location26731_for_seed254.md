# The Engine Cathedral

You come down a service stair, iron-grated, mossy in the treads, and at the bottom the sound meets you before the air does. It is a low, constant, layered sound — the sound of something that has been running for a very long time and has forgotten how to stop. It rises through the floor grating and enters your chest before you've taken the third step.

The stair opens onto a catwalk, and the place unfolds beneath you.

It is smaller than you expected. That is the first surprise. From above, with only a faint grumble leaking through the access hatch, you had imagined something vaster — a tunnel, perhaps, or a buried hall. Instead the chamber is contained. Cathedral-sized, yes, but in the way a single large room is cathedral-sized: bounded, knowable, with walls you can sight across in the dim. Perhaps sixty meters long, thirty wide, and tall — perhaps fifteen meters to the vaulted ceiling of riveted iron and glazed brick. Big enough that your voice would carry. Small enough that the man on the far catwalk, were there a man, could be recognized. A single room. A single machine, more or less.

The second surprise is the green.

There is no surface that is not dressed in it. The catwalk railing under your hand has a velvet fur of moss so old it has gone black at the core. The I-beams that frame the ceiling are sheathed in a thick, lumpy hide of climbing ferns and creepers, and where the iron shows through, it shows through as dull plum-colored stains — the slow patina of decades of condensation. Every pipe that runs the length of the chamber, and there are hundreds of pipes, has become the trunk of a hanging garden: each one trails a curtain of aerial roots two, three meters long, fine as hair, beaded with water that does not fall but hangs, deciding, in the warm saturated air. Where a pipe meets a flange, a fountainhead of broad leaves has erupted, oriented always toward the faint warmth of the joint. Bolthead ferns have colonized the bolts themselves; there is no head of iron left that does not wear a small green crown.

The air is wet. Wet is too dry a word. The air is *full*. You take a breath and your lungs feel rinsed. Every metal surface weeps. Condensation moves across the glazed bricks in dark wandering tracks that look almost deliberate, like the paths of snails. The catwalk under your boots has a thin film of water on it that your soles squeak against. Somewhere below, the lower platforms stand in an actual shallow sheet, ankle-deep in some places, where the floor drains have long since been claimed by roots and can no longer carry their load; the water sits, patient, reflecting the dim.

The dim is itself a thing to notice. There are no windows — you are below grade — but there are four grated skylights set into the ceiling far above, where the brick vault meets whatever earth lies over it. Through them a weak grey-green light filters down, further reduced by the dense canopy of hanging vines that has colonized the upper beams. It arrives at the floor as a kind of underwater glow, soft enough that it does not cast real shadows, only suggestions. The place has the visibility of a forest at dusk. You can see across it. You cannot see *into* it; corners, the undersides of platforms, the interiors of the larger machines, remain pools of darker green-black.

But there is another light. Amber. From the machines. The great brass gauges set into the flanks of the boilers and the steam drums still glow — not brightly, but with the patient pilot-flame warmth of instruments that have not been switched off in living memory. Tiny service lamps clipped to the railings throw their yellow circles onto nearby leaves. A few indicator panels along the central gallery, the cracked glass of their faces half-covered in moss, still manage a faint internal luminescence, a suggestion of green or red that you only catch from certain angles. The combined effect is the light of a harbor at night, or of an aquarium: dim, amber and green, the world made of suggestion rather than sight.

Now: the machines.

The chamber is a pump hall, or was once — a kind of cathedral of moving water built into a sunken industrial complex, the kind of place that in another century would have been manned by a dozen stokers and tenders going about their shifts under hanging chains and the smell of oil. That is what the bones say. A central aisle runs the length of the room, and down its spine stand three enormous steam-driven pumping engines, each one a cathedral unto itself: cast-iron cylinders the size of small rooms, flywheels three meters across, pistons thick as tree trunks. Around them, packed tight as a forest, is the apparatus — pipework of every diameter from a finger's width to a body's, valve-wheels red with rust or green with growth, pressure gauges, expansion joints, condenser coils, feedwater heaters, economizers, the great latticed iron staircase that climbs up the wall to a service gallery running at half-height. Above the gallery, more pipes. Below, more pipes. The ceiling is a thicket of them. Every horizontal surface that is not iron is water; every vertical surface that is not iron is plant; and the iron itself, where it shows, is only the skeleton of the place, its bones.

They are still running.

This is the third surprise, and the strangest, though once you have stood in the chamber for ten minutes you stop being surprised by it and begin to wonder if there was ever a moment when they were not. The pistons move. Slowly — one stroke every four or five seconds — but unmistakably. The flywheels turn, ponderous, their rims rimed with moss that has somehow survived the rotation, growing always just faster than the wear. Valves open and close on their schedules. Steam hisses from a dozen joints in slow white plumes that are caught by the hanging roots and turned, somehow, into water again. Somewhere, deep in the lower workings, water is being moved: you can hear it moving, a continuous liquid sound that underlies everything else, the place's true bass note.

The place is loud. The sound does not fade into background. It is *the* background. The pistons give a slow heavy *thunk-ssshh-thunk* that you feel in the soles of your boots. Above that, the higher clatter and patter of valve gear and linkages, a precise mechanical chatter that sounds almost articulate, like a language being spoken in another room. Above *that*, the constant hiss of steam and the near-constant drip of condensation — not the drip of a single leaky faucet, but the drip of an entire forest after rain, every leaf contributing, a vast delicate percussion that fills the spaces between the machinery's syllables. From time to time a larger clank rings out — a feedwater pump somewhere swallowing, a relief valve lifting for a moment before settling — and it rolls around the chamber and is swallowed by the green. The ceiling of leaves and roots absorbs the high sounds; the iron floor reflects them back. The result is a room that does not echo exactly, but *holds* its sounds, layering them, so that what you hear at any given moment is the composite of the last several seconds, a chord.

There are no people.

You notice this after a while. There are no people because there are no people anywhere — there is no chatter of footsteps on the catwalk above you, no one tending the gauges, no one shoveling coal into the boiler mouth you can see gaping from the central engine's flank. The fire door is open, and inside the firebox there is no flame, only a low pulsing orange glow from a bed of clinker and ash that someone, sometime, allowed to bank itself down to its lowest possible combustion, and that has held there ever since, a coal-bed on the edge of going out, fed by the slow trickle of unburned fuel from a hopper that has rusted open above it. The fire breathes. That is all. The steam pressure reads, on the great brass dial by the stair, at a number that has not varied by more than a needle's width in all the time you have stood there. The system is stable. The system has *settled*.

There are no animals either. No birds, of course — you are below the earth. But also no rats in the rafters, no cockroaches skating across the lower platforms, no spiders in the corners of the gauge panels. The ferns and mosses have no company. You think, at first, that this is the absence of fauna typical of any machine room treated with poison; then you look closer and understand it is something else. Look at the catwalk rail where you rest your hand: the moss there is not grazed, not nibbled, not even disturbed. Look at the broad fleshy leaves of the creeper that has climbed up to the skylight grates: no slug trails, no chew-marks. The place is *occupied* — every surface has its green tenant — but it is unvisited. Whatever runs these machines, the plants and the iron between them have come to an understanding that excludes the rest of the living world. This cathedral is closed to everything that breathes air in lungs.

And yet it breathes. The whole chamber breathes. The pistons lift and fall like a heart. The steam hisses out and condenses and runs down and pools and is drawn up again by the feedwater pump and returned to the boilers in a closed circuit that has been cycling, almost certainly, for longer than you have been alive. The plants transpire and the drips fall and the roots drink from the shallow standing water and the leaves orient toward the faint heat of the engine bearings and the whole system — iron, water, green — turns over and over, patient, in its dim warm wet dark.

It changes slowly. You cannot, watching for an hour, see it change at all. But if you came back in a season you would see that the creeper on the north wall had advanced another half-meter along the ceiling beam. If you came back in a decade you would find that the great boiler's brick housing had been broken open, somewhere on its upper curve, by the steady pry of a root, and a young fig — or its descendant — had taken root inside the firebox, its pale leaves unfurling in the banked heat. The machines are turning. The plants are growing. The water is moving. The fire is breathing. None of them is in any hurry. None of them has anywhere to be. They have been here a long time. They will be here longer.

You stand on the catwalk in the warm wet dim, with the slow heartbeat of the engines under your feet and the perpetual fine rain of condensation drifting down around you, and you listen to the place. After a while you stop hearing it as a sound. It becomes the weather. It is simply what is here, the way the wind is what is here on a hilltop. The chamber is not empty. It is not abandoned. It is *inhabited*, by a community that does not notice you and does not need you, and that has arranged itself, over many patient years, into a kind of equilibrium that is not quite machine and not quite forest and not quite any word you have.

You go back up the mossy stairs. The hatch closes behind you. Above, the ordinary world continues. Below, in the warm wet green dark, the pistons lift and fall, and the water moves, and nothing at all is waiting for you to come back.
