# The Drowned Plateau

You come up over the last ridge of the outer range and the sound reaches you before anything else. It reaches you as a single thing at first — a low, continuous pressure against the eardrums, the kind of hum that lives more in the chest than in the ear. Then it opens, as you listen, into its component layers: the metallic groan of the long steam-lines strung along the cliff shoulders; the slower pulse of the great hammers somewhere down in the basin, each stroke rolling across the plateau like a struck bell that has not yet finished ringing; the higher, more articulate whine of the turbines mounted along the spine of the range; the rhythm of pumps; and beneath it all, never stopping, the rain.

The rain is the constant. It falls as a warm mist on the Drowned Plateau, in threads you can almost count when you stand still and let your eyes unfocus, in sheets that come and go, in sudden heavier curtains that drum on metal roofs and on the backs of animals and on the broad leaves of the dark flora. It is never storm and never fair. It is the weather, and it has been the weather for longer than the foundries have been working, longer than the rail has been strung between them, longer than the watchtowers were manned or the cableways strung. The basalt weeps where the rain does not reach — every joint and fracture in the dark rock beading with condensation, every scree slope running with small rills that braid together and vanish into the soil of the higher meadows. The mountains sweat. The plateau sweats. The air is so wet it condenses on the teeth.

You cannot see the sky. There is no sky to see. Above the plateau is a ceiling of cloud so thick and so old that it has become, in effect, a kind of second ground — a soft grey-black roof from which the rain descends and into which the smoke of the foundries rises and is lost. The cloud is so low in places that the smokestacks of the larger installations disappear into it, and you can watch, sometimes, the orange glow of an active smelter bloom across the underside of the cloud and slowly fade, as if something enormous were turning a lamp toward you and then turning it away. From a distance the whole plateau looks faintly lit from below, the way the floor of a forest looks lit by glow-worms — an uneasy, reddish underglow that has no source you can point at and that moves, very slowly, as the work shifts from one valley to the next.

The light that is on the plateau is not daylight. It never has been. What light there is comes from two sources only, and they have come to resemble one another in colour over the long centuries: the fires of the industrial works, and the cooler, dimmer, more patient lights of the living things that share the rock with them. A foundry's mouth glows a fierce sodium-orange; the blossoms that open on the cliffs after a heavier rain glow a faint blue-green or a deep, almost red, amber. By either light the rest of the world is reduced to silhouette and suggestion — a tree trunk; the curve of a hill; the moving bulk of something large and many-legged crossing a slope in the middle distance, picking its way with care among the dark ferns. Eyes appear and disappear in the ferns. The ferns themselves are not green; they are a black-green, almost blue, leathery and heavy with the water they cannot stop drinking.

The terrain is high but not sharp. The Drowned Plateau is a piece of continent lifted and then worn down — a slow, ancient basalt platform that has been receiving rain and giving back rivers for so long that the rivers have stopped cutting and started wandering. The valleys are broad and shallow; the ridges between them are rounded and softened; the cliffs that ring the plateau on its eastern edge have been combed by weather into fluted columns and hanging curtains of stone that look, in the industrial half-light, almost like the folds of fabric. Up close the basalt is full of vesicles and seams, and the seams run with warm water — heated from below, where the planet's interior still turns and pushes, where the geothermal fields lie in their long belts along the roots of the mountains. The warm water rises through the rock and joins the cold rain, and the two meet in every streambed and puddle on the plateau, and the streams themselves are tepid, and the air above them steams faintly when the temperature of the air drops even a little.

The vegetation is profligate. It has had no reason not to be. With no sun to ration and no frost to fear, and with the rock continually feeding it warm mineral water, the plateau has overgrown itself many times over. There are forests of the dark fern that stand eight metres tall and never flower. There are mosses that have built their own hills, low domes a hundred metres across, soft underfoot, breathing out their own warm mist. There are hanging gardens on every cliff — curtains of trailing root and leaf that fall two hundred metres to the valley floor and are still attached, somehow, to the stone above. There are pale, slow fungi that grow along the rail-lines in pale fans, digesting the sleepers where they touch the ballast. There are pitcher-plants the size of barrels, half full of captured rain, half full of captured smaller pitcher-plants. The smell of it, when the wind shifts, is green and dark and faintly mineral, with an undertone of warm stone. Things move inside it constantly.

The fauna is abundant, and it is strange, and it is loud. It is loud in the way that a forest at night is loud when you actually stop and listen: a layered, ongoing, unending conversation of calls and responses, of feeding sounds, of warning sounds, of the great shag-rush of many bodies moving through the undergrowth in the same direction for reasons of their own. The larger herbivores — low, heavy, many-legged things with hides like wet rubber and faces like nothing you have a name for — move across the plateau in herds that can take a whole day to pass a single point, the adults on the outside with their young protected in the moving centre, their feet leaving warm prints in the moss that steam gently after they have gone. Smaller things swarm in the canopy and under the rocks: things that click, things that sing in registers you feel in your sinuses, things that glow when they are alarmed. Predators follow the herds at a distance — lithe, low, silent things with too many eyes that reflect the foundry-light from ridges and are gone. The fauna does not sleep the way you expect it to sleep. It works in shifts, one species giving way to the next as the dimmer hours shift from cool to warm and back, and there is always something moving, somewhere, on the plateau, and almost always something calling.

The built environment is scattered across all of this, and is, in its way, just another kind of growth. There is a foundry here, in the crook of a river, its three great stacks breathing into the cloud. There is a row of worker barracks there, low and windowless, painted a colour that was once bright and is now a kind of mineral grey. There is a watchtower on a high point, with a searchlight that is supposed to be used for surveying the herds and is mostly used, in the long watches, for signalling across the cloud-blurred distances to the next watchtower. There is a pumping station tucked into a cliff; a cableway strung between two ridges; a stack of cooling vats that has become, over time, a habitat for something that builds nests in the spaces between the pipes. A rail-line runs along the floor of one of the broader valleys and then turns and climbs, by means of a series of long inclines, up to the higher workings on the shoulder of the central range. The rails are kept clear of vegetation by constant work; everywhere else the vegetation has won, and where the rails end they disappear into the fern within a season.

The industry has been here a long time. It has settled into the landscape the way the rivers have settled into the landscape — slowly, by accretion, finding the paths of least resistance, growing around the obstructions rather than over them. The erosion of the rock and the erosion of the human occupation have reached a kind of truce. The drainage ditches from the smelters run down into the streams, and the streams carry the runoff into the soil, and the soil feeds the mosses and the ferns, and the ferns grow over the abandoned buildings, and the buildings hold the heat of the deeper rock and warm the small things that live in their walls. There is no place on the plateau where the industrial and the biological are not mixed. Even the strange blooms that open on the cliffs after a rain have come, over generations, to track the chemical composition of the runoff; the blue-green ones cluster around the older workings, the red-amber ones around the newer.

It is a vast place. The plateau runs for a thousand kilometres in one direction and is wider in the other, and at its edges it falls away into other country — into the lower, hotter, wetter lowlands where the rain comes down in columns and the ferns grow to the height of the smaller buildings, and into the higher ranges beyond, where the rain becomes snow in spite of the warm air and the snowmelt feeds the great rivers that run, eventually, off the edge of the continent. The weather on the plateau is its own weather. The cloud-deck above it is not the cloud-deck of the surrounding lands; it is older, lower, thicker, fed by the warm exhalations of the geothermal fields and the smoke of the foundries, and it moves with a slowness that has its own rhythm, thickening and thinning across the decades, dropping lower in some eras and higher in others, but never, in any living memory, lifting. The animals have learned to read the thickening and thinning. The foundry-men have learned to read it. The rail schedules bend around it.

There is no horizon in the usual sense. There is only the curving away of the nearer ridges into the murk, and beyond them the suggestion of more ridges, and beyond those the slow orange bloom of another valley's industry moving across the underside of the cloud. When you climb to the highest point on the central range and the murk lifts, briefly, you can see the whole of the plateau laid out below you in a wash of dim firelight: the rivers, the fern-forests, the rounded hills, the strings of lights that mark the inhabited valleys, the slow, pale scatter of the herds moving through it all. Then the murk closes again, and you are back inside the cloud, and the rain finds you, and the warm air presses against your face, and the distant sound of the hammers picks up where it left off, and continues.
