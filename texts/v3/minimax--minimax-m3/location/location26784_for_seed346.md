The cold is a texture before it's a temperature.

It lives in the throat, in the wrist-bones, in the small soft places behind the ears. The damp is everywhere — in the air, on the walls, underfoot, the whole settlement weeping slightly, holding its moisture close. The quiet isn't silence so much as the muffled presence of sound: rain on a roof somewhere, a distant door, a footstep that becomes a footstep that becomes nothing. The cold damp air swallows sound the way it swallows heat.

You can cross the place in ten minutes if you don't stop, which most people don't, because there isn't anywhere to stop that isn't someone's room. It is the size of a hamlet built to the density of a beehive. There are perhaps sixty structures, perhaps fewer. Counting is uncertain — the boundaries of a structure here are not given. Some rooms have become rooms of rooms. The accretion has layered things until what's a wall and what's a building is a question of where you start looking.

The ground is the most uncertain thing.

It is a salt-flat, or it was, or it is becoming one. The substrate is dark with brine, glistening in the lower places, soft underfoot, never quite solid. It is not yet geology. It is a thing in the process of becoming stone, or mud, or something else without a name. Buildings rest on it lightly. The residents know the ground is not finished and they build as if it isn't — shallow foundations, light structures, things that could be lifted and set elsewhere if the substrate decides, in some slow season, to be something else. Nobody has lifted anything. The accretion has been gentle. But the possibility sits in the architecture like sediment.

The buildings look built — they have doors, windows, walls, eaves — but they feel grown, or deposited, or arrived. Each is the sum of many layers. The first layer might be a hundred years old, or three hundred. The most recent is last winter, perhaps. They thicken like slow coral. Each new stratum is laid against the old in a patient conversation, the new wall easing into place beside the older wall, the new roof joining the older roof, the structure growing by the slow addition of itself to itself.

Inside, each room is its own private physics.

This is the technology nobody remarks on, the most ordinary strangeness. In one room gravity sits a little heavier than it should; in the next, light bends; in the room above the bakery, momentum is something the residents have learned to negotiate. The walls are made of something that isn't quite wood and isn't quite stone and isn't quite anything known — a dense composite, perhaps, or a cultivated material, or a thing that has been taught to behave like both wall and computational substrate. Somewhere in the dense body of each wall, a small region of matter implements a small set of physical laws. The laws are local. They are local with great precision.

Where the rooms meet, the laws meet.

A wall between two rooms is where two slightly different gravities lean against each other. A door between two rooms is where the speed of light on one side is not quite the speed of light on the other. Reality at the seams is a kind of stitching — a small quiet patchwork, the local physics knit together into the larger settlement. The seams flex. The seams have learned to flex. The settlement holds because the seams have learned to hold.

Outside, in the rare open spaces — a courtyard here, a narrow alley there — the air is the physics of outside, which is mostly normal, mostly standard. The sky is the sky of a low cold place. The light is the light of a damp afternoon. But even outside, the seams leak. The sky above one courtyard is not exactly the sky above the next. The light falling on one wall doesn't quite match the light falling on the wall beside it. The settlement is lit by a patchwork of suns, none of them wrong exactly, none of them quite right.

Vegetation is sparse and uncertain.

Lichens on the lowest walls, in colors that haven't decided — orange, gray, a sickly green that might be hope. A few mosses in the dampest corners, tufted, thin. Stag-headed things with stems that might be plants, or might be the substrate being impatient and reaching upward. Nothing blooms. Nothing sets fruit. The cold doesn't permit it. The damp doesn't permit it. The patchworked light doesn't permit it.

Fauna is rarer still.

There are things in the interstitial — small, dark, many-legged or no-legged, living in the wet places between the substrate and the lowest walls. There is a thing in the sky sometimes, low, dark, briefly seen — it might be a bird; it might be the residue of a bird; it makes a thin reedy sound that nobody tracks to its source. Most fauna has either not arrived or has declined to a kind of half-presence, an almost-thing, a thing at the seam of biology and not-biology. The settlement doesn't quite support life. It supports the suggestion of life. It supports the way life behaves when physics is uncertain.

The sound is mostly the absence of sound.

Rain on a roof. The muffled clatter of a kitchen through three walls and a corridor. A voice somewhere, distant, made small by the damp air. The creak of a structure adjusting itself — though *adjusting* is a polite word; the structures settle, the seams flex, the physics negotiates. A rare footstep. A rarer laugh. The settlement is a quiet place. The cold and the damp and the patchwork keep it quiet. Most of what happens here happens in the small private physics of individual rooms, in the small private time of individual lives.
