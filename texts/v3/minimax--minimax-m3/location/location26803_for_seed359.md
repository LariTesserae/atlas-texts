The interior is the size of a temple. The ceiling is held up by columns that were not always there, or were always there, depending on which column you ask. Every surface has been worked. Not a single inch of raw material remains in the visible world of this place — even the dust between the tiles is a kind of felt, a slow accretion of worked fiber that has been walked and re-walked and trodden into something finer than felt, something almost granular, almost alive.

The air is warm. Not the warmth of a hearth — there is no hearth — but the warmth of a body, of breath held too long in a closed room, of stone that has been warmed for so long by so many bodies that it has forgotten what cold is. The air smells of cedar and of something sweeter, a little rotten, a little resinous, the way a fig leaf smells when you crush it between your fingers and don't quite wash your hands.

There is no water. None has ever been here. You can tell because the vessels that line the walls — hundreds of them, thousands, ceramic bowls set into niches — are dry, and have been dry for longer than the memory of the builders. Some are glazed in pale green, some in iron-red, some are unglazed and rough. They are for catching, but nothing falls into them anymore. The lids are on. The lids were always on.

Yet the place is green.

The growth is everywhere — a fur of dark, wet-looking filaments that climbs the columns and hangs from the arches in long curtains that brush the floor. It is not moss, because moss needs water. It is not lichen, because there is no stone for lichen to feed on, only worked stone, only tile and brick and polished surfaces that have been sealed with something harder than themselves. The growth is something else. It drinks the light. You can watch it on a slow afternoon — the filaments lift, slightly, toward the high windows, and where the light touches them they deepen in color, and where the light withdraws they pale, and somewhere inside them something moves, slowly, like sap, like blood, like attention. The growth is dense. Where two curtains of it touch, they knit, and you cannot find the join. Where a curtain meets the floor it puddles outward in a dark pool of itself, and the dark pool is not a stain — it is the same thing, only thicker, only older, only having forgotten that it was ever a thing at all.

The place is young.

You can tell because the edges are sharp. The corners have not been worn down. The tiles, though they fit snugly, still show the chisel-marks where they were cut to size. The fiber that makes up the felted dust was clearly spun, clearly woven, before it was walked into powder. The builders were here not long ago — or they are here now, still working, somewhere in the slow places. The growth is young too. A vine that crosses an arch in the time you watch it is a fast vine. A bowl that loses its glaze over a year is a quick bowl. You have the feeling, standing in the warm light, that the place is finishing itself around you as you stand.

The builders are not here. Or — they are here, but they have been woven into the work.

You can see them if you know how to look. A column that is shaped a little too deliberately to be a column, and at its base a tile pattern that resolves, when you stare, into the careful toes of a foot. A niche that holds a bowl that holds, if you look very long, the shape of a hand curled around nothing. A patch of growth in the corner of a high gallery that hangs in the silhouette of someone kneeling, very still. The builders are slow now. They are part of the tilework, part of the felted dust, part of the fibrous green. They are still working. They are still building. The place is still becoming. A new pattern appears, faintly, in the floor where the small ones cross most often — laid by no one, laid by the place itself.

The fauna are small. Mostly. There are things that skitter in the walls — you hear them, a fine papery scratching, and sometimes a tile will shift in its setting and then shift back as if remembering where it belongs. There are things that move in the growth itself, slow shapes that bulk against the filaments and then disappear, leaving the green to sway a long time afterward. There are larger things, less often seen — a long pale shape that crosses an upper gallery and is gone, leaving a wake in the hanging green. They are made of the same substance as the place, in some sense. Their hides are tiled, scaled in small plates that fit the architecture. Their eyes are dark and round and look at you without alarm. They have built nothing. They live in what was built. They drink, perhaps, the same thing the growth drinks.

The light comes from above. There are openings in the ceiling — skylights, oculi, slots between stones — but the light that comes through is not quite the same as the light outside. It falls in slabs. It pools on the tile floor in shapes that do not match the openings. Sometimes a column stands in light that should fall on empty floor. Sometimes the floor is lit where no opening is. The light knows the place better than the place knows itself.

The quiet is not silence.

There is a hum, low, that you feel in your teeth before you hear it — the place is settling, or breathing, or being dreamed. There are small sounds: the papery scratch in the walls, the soft pad of a creature on the felted floor, the rare creak of something overhead shifting on its perch. There are also sounds that don't quite happen: a step that almost lands, a breath that almost leaves a mouth, a word that almost forms in the warm air and dissolves. The place is full of almost-sounds. They gather at the edges of the real sounds and make the real sounds feel like islands in a wider sea.

You can stay here. The place will let you. The growth will lean toward you, gently, and the creatures will come to watch you from the columns, and the builders-in-the-tilework will almost-acknowledge you as you pass. The bowls will remain dry. The light will fall in its wrong shapes. The almost-sounds will gather at your heels.

You will begin, after a while, to notice that the place has begun to remember you in its tilework. A new pattern, very faint, where your feet have walked most. A new shape, in the growth, where your breath has been warmest. You will begin, after a while, to forget whether you came in through a door. You will begin, after a while, to wonder if the place is the size of a temple, or the size of a room, or the size of a body.

The place will not answer. The place is mostly quiet. The place is mostly patient.

A vine moves, somewhere far above, an inch in an hour.

A bowl, very slowly, lifts its lid.
