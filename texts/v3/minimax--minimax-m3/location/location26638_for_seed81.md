# The Plateau of Condensing Light

You arrive at the rim on foot, the road ending where the engineers ended it three centuries ago. Beyond the last iron marker, the plateau opens like the cupped palm of a giant who has been holding something hot. The city spreads across the stone — but you cannot call the stone *stone*. It is glass, mostly, volcanic glass weathered into long low swells, black-purple and green where it catches what light there is. The light is dim because there is almost always a ceiling. Not a sky ceiling — a vapor ceiling, a low stratus of condensed air that the heat pushes up against itself. It hangs about two hundred meters above the highest tower, gray-white, rarely broken.

The city is old in a settled way. It has not collapsed. It has *settled*, the way silt settles, the way a thing that no longer has a strong opinion about itself comes to rest in its own shape. Buildings are layered into buildings. The lower stories of the central tower are basalt — squared blocks, hand-cut, no mortar visible because the moisture has welded them together with a kind of mineral sweat. The upper stories are poured concrete from the industrial age, and above those the final two floors are something else: a pale alloy that has been there for eighty years and is still slowly oxidizing in patterns that look almost deliberate. No one is making them.

The streets are wide for a city with almost no one in it. There is no one in it. The census — when census was still a word anyone used here — recorded thirty-one permanent residents across the whole basin, and that was a generation ago. They are not here now. But the streets were built for hundreds of thousands, and they remain.

Heat comes from below. You can feel it through the soles of your boots within minutes of leaving the railhead. The plateau is riddled with vents — hundreds of them, gridded across the urban plan in patterns that obey some industrial logic no one remembers the reason for. Some are capped with iron. Some are open. From the open ones, steam rises in a slow continuous column. The steam meets the vapor ceiling and condenses into a fine rain that drifts back down across the whole basin in almost imperceptible amounts. There is no weather here in the way weather works elsewhere. There is only this: heat up, moisture down, heat up again. A circulatory system for a place that used to be someone else's project.

The moisture collects on everything. On the alloy towers. On the basalt. On the glass. There are weeping walls along the eastern edge of the central concourse — basalt that sweats so constantly that the lower courses have grown a kind of pale rind, mineral accretion, layered like coral but mineral only, no biology in it, just chemistry willing itself into form over centuries. The rind is warm to the touch. It is not alive. It is not dead. It is the city continuing to construct itself in the absence of any constructor.

The structures are industrial in origin but have become something stranger in their obsolescence. Pipes run along the facades, capped or broken, sometimes rerouted into nothing. There are turbines in three of the towers — the largest ones — and they still turn, very slowly, because the steam finds them. They do not produce anything that anyone collects. They have been turning for longer than anyone now alive has been alive. The sound is faint, a low cyclic exhale, almost below the threshold of hearing. *Hnh. Hnh. Hnh.* A pulse.

The sound of the place, when you stop moving, is mostly water and mostly quiet. Condensation dripping from a thousand eaves into a thousand small basins. The turbines. The vents themselves, sighing. Occasionally a larger collapse — not dramatic, never dramatic, just a section of pipe somewhere giving up its hold on a bracket and falling one floor onto the next floor below, where it will lie until the moisture welds it down too.

You can walk for an hour and see no animal. Then you will see one. They are small. They are pale. They live in the steam vents, in the hottest part of the basin, where the air is unbreathable to anything without a peculiar chemistry. You do not know what they eat because you cannot get close enough to find out. They have no shape you can reliably name. Some are long and thin. Some are round and almost transparent. They move through the steam columns as if the steam were water, and sometimes you catch them suspended in it, motionless, drifting, watching with whatever they watch with. There are not many. There are not few. There is enough of them that you understand this place is not empty in every sense. It is only empty of you.

The buildings — and this is the strangest part — the buildings have a kind of density that is hard to reconcile with how few things they contain. Layer after layer of construction, one stratum of purpose built on top of another, each one absorbed into the one above. A residential block becomes the foundation for a refinery becomes the foundation for a temple to whatever this culture worshipped, and the temple becomes the foundation for a water cistern, and the cistern becomes the foundation for an alloy tower, and on top of that tower someone installed a beacon that no longer beacons. The strata are visible from the eastern cliff where the city has been cut open by ancient erosion. You can read the city like tree rings. Each ring is a century.

The basin is open and wide. From the rim you can see the whole of it — perhaps two kilometers across, perhaps three, you cannot tell because the haze softens edges. Across the floor, the grid of streets, the layered towers, the vent columns, the slow rain. It is a city that has been left to its own devices for longer than any individual who built it is now remembered as themselves. It is continuing. The vents are venting. The turbines are turning. The condensation is condensing. The pale shapes are drifting in the steam. The accretion is accreting. Nothing is waiting for anything.

It is hot. It is wet. It is mostly quiet. It is strange in a way that you cannot put your finger on, because the strangeness is not in any one feature of it but in the way all the features continue to operate in the absence of any need for them to operate. The city is not a ruin. A ruin is a thing that has stopped. This is a thing that has not decided whether to stop.

You walk further in. The basalt is slick. The steam from a nearby vent passes over you in a slow warm breath. Somewhere ahead, a pale shape moves in the column and you almost see it and then you do not see it.

You keep walking.
