There is a way in. You wouldn't know it from outside. The vegetation closes behind you like a curtain of wet felt. But if you push through — and you have to push, the stems are thick, the fronds are slick, the air between them is heavier than air should be — you come out into a chamber that shouldn't be here.

The chamber is maybe the size of a small chapel. Vaulted, but the vault is grown, not built. Ribs of woody vine arch up from a wet floor and meet overhead in a knot so dense it looks woven. The ribs are not separate from the walls; the walls are not separate from the ribs; nothing here is separate from anything else. The whole interior is one organism breathing in slow time. The surface of every surface is alive — wet, slightly pulsing if you watch, furred with a thin pelt of moss or root-hair or something that has no name. It is dark green and black-green and the green of a bruise. Water weeps from it constantly, in sheets, in threads, in single heavy drops that find the floor and become part of the floor. The floor is shallow water, three inches deep, warm, faintly the color of weak tea, covering a substrate that you can feel underfoot but not see. The light is the light of an overcast afternoon — a flat gray-green glow filtering down through the curtain of vegetation above, enough to see the whole chamber, not enough to be certain of any of it.

The sound is the first thing you feel. The sound and the warmth, and they come from the same place.

In the center of the chamber, at the convergence of the ribs, the plant has built around a thing. Or the thing has built itself into the plant. It is a pump. A pump made of cast iron and copper and something that was once paint, now flaking away in green-black curls. It is the size of a person curled fetal. It has a flywheel. It has a piston-rod that comes out of its body and goes up into the woven knot of the ceiling and back down again. It has pipes — and these are the pipes that are hardest to look at, because the pipes are roots, or the roots are pipes, and you cannot tell where the metal ends and the cellulose begins. The plant has grown into the pump the way a fig grows around a wasp, slowly, with patience, until there is no line.

The pump is running. The pump has been running for a very long time. The flywheel turns at a constant rate — you can time your breathing to it if you want. The piston rises and falls. Water is drawn up from somewhere below, from a deep place you cannot see, and pushed up into the ribs of the ceiling, and from there it weeps out through every surface of the chamber and falls back to the floor and is drawn down again. The whole room is a single closed circuit of water and pressure. The plant is the radiator. The pump is the heart. The chamber is the lung.

The sound is the sound of a small factory that has been running for a hundred years without anyone listening. The piston clunks. The flywheel hisses at its axle. Vapor — warm, smelling of iron and chlorophyll — escapes from a joint at the top of the pump and rises into the vault and condenses on the ceiling and falls as warm rain onto your shoulders. There is a low hum underneath, too low to hear as a hum and more felt as a pressure in the chest, in the teeth. The water on the floor is in constant motion, rippling outward from where the downspouts hit, and these ripples meet and cancel and begin again. It is loud. After a minute you stop noticing that you are hearing it. After ten minutes you stop noticing that you are not hearing anything else.

There are things that live here. More of them than you first think, because the chamber is generous in niches even if it is sparse in numbers. A beetle the length of your thumb, blue-black, with a shell that looks stamped from sheet metal, walks a path along one of the lower ribs in a continuous loop. It has worn a groove. The groove is dark and wet. The beetle has been walking this loop for your entire life and will walk it for longer. A second beetle, smaller, copper-colored, is doing the same thing on a higher rib, in the opposite direction. A pale frog sits on a knob of root at the edge of the floor and does not move and does not need to move; its throat pulses with the rhythm of the pump. In the shallow water, thin pale worms inch through the silt in paths that branch and merge. A snail the size of a coin clings to a vertical surface and turns its chamber slowly, scraping, scraping. Moths — three of them, soft gray, dusty — rest in the folds of the ceiling with wings folded, and you only know they are alive because sometimes one flies down to the water and back up again, and the flight is silent against the sound of the pump, and silent against everything.

A bird — and you do not know how a bird got in here, and then you stop wondering because the answer is that the bird was born here and has never left, and its wings are vestigial, soft down, and its song is timed to the flywheel and is part of the sound — sits in the woven knot above the pump and sings a single three-note phrase over and over in the gaps between clunks. The song has no beginning and no end. The song has been going for longer than you.

None of this changes.

The water level on the floor is the water level on the floor. The piston is at the top of its stroke and will be at the top of its stroke again. The first beetle will pass the knot of root at the wall and the groove will be no darker and no wetter when it returns. The frog will still be sitting there. The bird will still be singing. The chamber has been in this state since before the rust formed on the pump, since before the moss furred the walls, since before the bird's ancestors lost the use of their wings. If you leave — and you should leave, because nothing in here needs you, and your presence is a small disruption that the system will absorb and forget — and if you come back in a year, it will be exactly as you left it. The water will be the same depth. The song will be at the same point in its phrase. The beetle will be at the same point in its loop. Nothing here has anywhere else to be. Nothing here is going anywhere. The pump will run. The water will fall. The chamber will breathe.

You stand in three inches of warm tea-water. The pump clunks. The beetle turns the corner of its groove. The bird opens its beak. The chamber breathes.
