The basin is older than names. Older than the languages that would try to name it. It is what remains when the patient work of weather is finally, almost done.

Stand at the rim of something vast — a saucer in the rock, miles across, with low ridges of darker stone still rising from the worn-down floor like the ribs of something buried. These are not mountains. They are the memory of mountains. They are the parts of the original rock that were hardest, slowest to give. Around them the rest has gone — down to silt, down to clay, down to dissolved mineral that the slow rivers carry out toward whatever sea is left. The basin is essentially a vast, slow, wet dish. The plain inside it is so flat in places that the water cannot decide which way to flow.

The sky here is the color of wet wool. It never quite lifts. The clouds are a permanent ceiling, low enough that the mist from them and the mist rising from the marshes are nearly the same thing, and from them a fine rain or near-rain descends almost always. The light is dim — not dark, but never bright. The dimness has the quality of late afternoon held indefinitely. You can see well enough, but shadows are soft, colors are muted, and the eye never quite has to contract against any glare. People who come here from elsewhere sometimes weep for the first few days, and weep again at the thought of leaving.

Water is the dominant thing. It is in everything. It is the air itself, condensing on every surface, beading on stone, making a dark shine on every outcrop, every leaf that is not there. It is in the ground — water just below the surface, water weeping from cracks, water gathering in every low place. There are pools everywhere: shallow, clear, dark-bottomed, ringing with insect noise. There are slow rivers — so slow they barely deserve the name — that braid across the silt flats and rejoin and braid again. There are marshes that have no particular boundary, just places where the water is shallower than elsewhere and the ground less firm. In some seasons these marshes swell; in others they recede, leaving a white crust of something mineral behind.

The sound is the second thing you will notice, after the wet. You cannot escape it. The basin is not silent. The basin roars.

It is a layered roar, and it never stops. At the base of it is the wind — a permanent wind that moves through the basin, around the ridges, across the open flats, never strong enough to push you over but never absent. It carries the mist. It carries the smell of rot and water. It moans in the rock crevices with a low note that has no pause in it. Over the wind sits the chorus. Insects — and they are insects of a kind, though not all of them have names elsewhere — rise from the marshes and pools in clouds so dense they cast a faint, shifting shadow in the dim light. Their hum is a physical thing. You feel it on your skin. Frog-things, or near-frogs, call from every watercourse in a rolling, overlapping trill that has no gap. Something larger — something with a voice like wet leather being stretched — answers from the ridges. There are birds, or near-birds, calling thinly above the louder noise. There is always the sound of water. There is always the sound of the wind. There is always the sound of the small lives in the mud, dragging, hopping, eating, being eaten. The basin does not sleep. It has not slept in a long age.

Vegetation, in the way you might mean it, does not exist here. There is no green. There is no forest. There is no grass. What grows here grows in a different mode. Lichen crusts the stones in patches of grey, bone-white, dull ochre. There are fungal threads that vein through the wet soil and emerge as small pale domes or as flat rubbery shelves on the damp rock faces. There are algae — green-brown, hair-fine — that slip in the water and stain the shallows. There are mosses, though they are the humblest of mosses, growing only where the mist is most constant, and they never form more than a thin nap on the stone. Nothing has roots that grip. Nothing has wood. Nothing stands tall. The basin is essentially a mineral landscape that has been wet for so long that a thin biological scum has accumulated on its surfaces, and that scum is all that distinguishes it from a wet stone desert.

And yet fauna is abundant. This is the strangeness of the basin. Without plants to convert sunlight into the bulk of living matter, the food web runs in other directions. The algae and the fungal threads and the bacterial mats at the bottom of the pools — these are the foundation. They feed the worms, the larvae, the small crustaceans, the snails, the things with too many legs that no one has named. These in turn feed the fish — small pale fish, sluggish in the cool water — and the amphibians, which are the true rulers of the basin. The amphibians are everywhere, scattered one or two to a pool, a hollow, a stretch of mud, but in their aggregate number overwhelming. Some are small enough to sit on your thumb. Some are the size of dogs. There are reports of things larger, in the deeper marshes, that move in a way that does not quite fit the shape you first see. They have soft, damp skins, and their eyes, when you catch them in the dim light, are wet and round and very still.

The amphibians eat the insects, the snails, the worms, the small fish. They eat each other. The larger ones eat the smaller ones. The smaller ones, in turn, eat the eggs and young of the larger ones. There are predator amphibians, scavenger amphibians, amphibian-things that filter-feed through their skin. The niche of the grazer, the niche of the browser, the niche of the burrower — all of these are filled by creatures that never had a backbone, or only barely have one. The basin is a place where the role of grass is played by algae and the role of antelope by something that lays eggs in a damp hollow and watches you without blinking.

Above all of this, in a sense, are the things that live on the rest. Raptor-things — long-legged, narrow-winged, hunched on the ridge crests — wait in the dim air for a frog to miscalculate. There are snakes, though fewer than you would expect, and what snakes there are move slowly. There are the things-with-leather-voices, which no one has gotten close enough to describe well. There are small things, rat-sized, that dart between the rocks and seem to be wearing damp fur. Nothing is furry here in the way you mean it. Everything is a little damp. Everything is a little off — the proportions of the legs, the spacing of the eyes, the way the small ones watch you when you are still. You cannot quite put your finger on what is wrong with the picture, but you do not feel you have seen it before.

The fauna is the loudness. The fauna is the wet. The fauna is the abundance, and the basin is in constant, patient motion because of them — the predator and the prey, the scavenger and the carrion, the eggs laid tonight and eaten by morning, the slow tread of the larger things through the shallows at dusk, the rise and fall of the chorus with the hour, with the season, with the weather.

And scattered through the basin — sparsely, a long walk from one to the next — there are marks of people.

The marks are small. They are primitive. They are made of what the basin offers, which is mostly stone, and the hides of the larger amphibians, and the dried fibrous stems of fungal towers that rise in some of the deeper marshes. There are cairns. There are low walls built from the harder rocks, not walls exactly but arrangements — windbreaks, perhaps, or markers, or the foundations of shelters long abandoned. There are hide tents stretched between outcrops, so dark and so low that you might walk past one in the mist and not see it. There are fish-traps — weirs of woven reed and weighted stone — set in the slow rivers. There are drying racks on which pale strips of amphibian meat hang in the wind, curing slowly in the damp. There are, in a few places, standing stones. These are taller, single, set up with an effort that suggests they matter. They are not decorated. They are not arranged in any pattern that can be read. They are simply there, and the people who set them up are thin, careful, and few.

The tools of these people are simple. A hand-axe of river stone. A digging stick of harder wood — and where they get wood, no one asks, because wood is rare here and the answer would be a long story. A knife of sharpened amphibian bone. A needle of bone and a sinew of gut. A woven basket, watertight with pitch from the fungal towers. They have no metal, or only traces of it — a bead of native copper, perhaps, prized past any reasonable value. They trade these things among themselves slowly, by walking, across distances that would discourage anyone in a hurry.

You can live in the basin. People do. But the basin does not love you for it. The basin is patient. The basin is older than you. It has outlasted everything soft. It will outlast the cairns, the hide tents, the drying racks, the standing stones. It will outlast the raptor-things on the ridges and the amphibians in the pools and the faint mist that falls on all of them. It will go on wearing itself down, so slowly that you cannot watch it, until what remains is something even more reduced, more final — a perfectly flat wet stone, perhaps, with water on it, and the small cold lives still going on, and the sound still going on, in the dimness, under the low ceiling of cloud.

For now, the basin is what it is. Wet. Loud. Cool. Dim. Old. Full of small moving things and a few larger ones and a few people, who have learned to listen past the roar to hear the silence underneath — and to understand, eventually, that there is no silence underneath, only a quieter layer of the same sound, all the way down.
