# A City in the Weeping Air

The plateau extends further than the eye can resolve. Not because it is flat — it is gently folded, buckled in long slow ridges that rise a body's height and fall again — but because the light is often the same color as the distance, and the air is rarely clear. You come up onto it through a corridor of stones and arrive at the edge of something that does not declare itself. There is no wall. There is no gate. There is a change in the pattern of the stones, and that is all.

The city begins without ceremony and does not end. It runs the length of the plateau in a kind of ribbon, then spreads sideways, then climbs into the folds, then climbs again. Seen from the highest ridge — and there are many ridges, all about the same height, so that one is never quite sure one is on the highest — it looks like a single organism that has decided to be stone. A long low animal made of rooftops, lying along the spine of the world, breathing in weather.

The air is thin and cool. It enters the lungs with a slight resistance, a politeness, a refusal to fill completely. At midday the sun is sharp but gives little heat; the stones hold the cold of the night for hours and pass it gently to anything that touches them. Mornings are colder still, and the surfaces of things are often damp, beaded, running with a fine clear water that has come out of the air itself. The dew here does not fall. It assembles. You can sit and watch a single drop form on the corner of a worked stone, swell, hesitate, gather a neighbor, and then trail down the face of the wall in a slow silver line. By the time the sun has lifted a hand's width above the ridge, every flat surface in the city is moving with water.

This is what one notices first, after the size. The wetness. The way the place is drenched without being rained upon. There is rain — long gray passages of it, sometimes for a day, sometimes for three — but more often the water simply condenses. It is in the air as vapor and decides, here, to be in the stones. The stones accept this. They are not the polished stones of a river. They are rough, dark, slightly greenish where the light catches them, and they drink. You can see them drink if you watch. A dry patch appears, lasts a few minutes, and then darkens as the moisture finds it.

The buildings are stacked. There is no word for the building type except perhaps to say they are terraced and they are layered and they are continuous. A house is not a single box. It is a low platform on which another platform sits, on which a wall rises, on which another roof turns. Roofs are also floors. A roof here is a yard. A yard here is a roof. The result, seen from any raised point, is that the city reads as a single field of level stones, broken by vertical things — walls, posts, low towers — and softened everywhere by the water that runs down them. Walking through it, one passes under lintels and along ledges and down short flights and across courtyards and into rooms that are cooler still, and the route is not a line. It is a braid.

The construction is handwork. There is no metal anywhere. You will not find an iron nail or a copper pin or a hinge. The stones are fitted. They are fitted very well, in a way that requires time and patience and the ability to look at a stone for a long while before deciding where it goes. The joints are tight but not sealed. Water moves through them freely, a capillary system running through the whole body of the city. In some places the masons have left channels — small deliberate troughs in the upper stones — that carry rainwater from one terrace to the next, and from there to a stone lip, and from there into a cistern, and from there, slowly, back to the air. The city is its own weather.

Vegetation is not absent but it is sparse and severe. Mosses in the damp joints, dark green, almost black. Lichen on the upper walls in pale crusts that take the shapes of continents. A grass that grows only where the runoff is regular, in pale tussocks along the channels. There are no trees. There is nothing with a thick trunk. The highest plants are knee-high and only in the most protected folds, where a wall shelters them from the constant drying wind. The wind, when it comes, is thin and cold and smells of nothing. It moves the water on the stones into patterns and moves on.

Animals are rare. One hears them rarely because one hears almost nothing. There are small high-voiced birds that come down from the higher ridges in the morning — they are the loudest thing in the city for most of the day. There are dark quick things that run along the channels and are gone before they can be named. There are small pale invertebrates under the stones, in the wet, that one might count as the city's smallest citizens. There are no large presences. No herds. No packs. The city is not a place that feeds many bodies.

And yet — and this is the strangeness, if there is a strangeness — there are inhabitants. One sees them. One does not hear them. They move along the terraces in the slow hours, and they pass through rooms that are dim and lit only by the gray light from the courtyard, and they do not speak above a murmur. Their feet are wrapped. Their tools are bone and stone and sinew. They carry water in clay vessels they have shaped and dried in the wind. They work on the stones, replacing the ones that have finally given up, resetting the channels, clearing the moss where it has begun to choke a joint. They have been doing this for a very long time. They do not seem in any hurry about it. The city is in balance with them, or they with it.

The scale is difficult to hold in the head. From the southern ridge one can see the city running north along the plateau for what must be a full day's walk, and from a point in the middle one can see it running both ways. There are districts whose names one does not know, separated by full half-days of walking. The city has weather. It generates a faint heat of its own, gathered from bodies and fires, and this heat rises into the mist and returns as condensation on the stones. In the very early morning the city is often a low white sea, with only the upper walls and the highest posts emerging, and then the sun touches it and the sea lifts and the city is revealed again, wet, waiting.

In the long rain, one does not go out. One sits in a room that is slightly warmer than outside, and listens to the water finding its way through the stones above. The sound is not loud. It is the smallest possible percussion — a tap, a shift, a long hiss on slate. It is the only weather-sound in the city. There is no thunder to speak of. The lightning stays on the higher ridges and does not come down. In the rain the streets — such as they are, the narrow gaps between platforms — run with a film of water an inch deep, moving with intent toward the lower terraces and the cisterns.

When the rain stops, the dripping continues for hours. Every surface sheds what it has held. The mists form, thin, and move through the city in slow pale sheets. Then the sun comes back, low and indifferent, and the stones begin their long slow exhale of water into the air, and the cycle begins again.

The silence is not a silence of absence. It is the working sound of a place that does not require noise. The water moves. The stones settle, microscopically. The few birds speak briefly. The inhabitants go about their work. The city, vast and cool and ancient and damp, lies along the spine of the world and tends itself. One comes to it expecting a settlement and finds instead a long low animal made of stone, breathing weather, drinking the air, and continuing.
