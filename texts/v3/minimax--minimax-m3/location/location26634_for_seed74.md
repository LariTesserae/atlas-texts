The town sits in a fold of low ground where the water table breaks the surface in patches, and you can tell it was once something because the bones remain: a grid of streets, a brick works, a chimney that still leans slightly to the east as if listening. But the plant matter has long since moved in, not as a guest but as the primary tenant, and the buildings have become the way the vegetation holds itself upright. You can read the original architecture only in the negative space where the green things haven't quite filled in. A doorway shows as a darker rectangle in a wall of moss. A second story is suggested by the place where the ivy thickens and darkens. A roofline is the height at which the ferns stop and the sky begins.

The elevation is small. The whole place is perhaps a meter and a half above the bog it sits on, sustained by a hundred years of composted leaves, brick dust, soot, and the slow silt of the river. The ground gives slightly when you walk. The water is everywhere in the air but not in the streets, except in the troughs along the curbs where a thin black stream always runs, fed by some underground system that no one troubles to maintain because it maintains itself. The damp is constant, a kind of ambient mist that does not so much fall as inhabit. Your cuffs are wet within the hour. The brick weeps.

The buildings are mostly dark brick and weathered timber, two and three stories, packed close along a main street that runs perhaps six hundred meters before it dissolves into a peat field. The industrial scale is visible in the scale of the windows — tall, deep-set, the kind that let in north light for work that needed it. The glass is mostly intact, clouded on the inside with condensation, and behind it the rooms are full. Full of what looks from the street like a slow green tide frozen mid-rise: ferns that have climbed the walls and crossed the ceilings and come down again; ropes of vine that drop from the eaves and rejoin the earth in slow loops; lichens that have eaten the paint and left the wood silver-gray and soft as bread. Some of the structures are still being used. You can see where paths have been worn into the moss of the floors, and doorways framed in paler brick than the walls around them, in the shape of a doorway only, the original having been eaten back to its rough opening and a new edge set in.

The industrial elements persist, but in a softened form, as if the town had reached an accommodation with its own machinery. There is a wheel at the edge of the main square, not water-driven but turned by a small engine that coughs and purrs on alternate days, with a belt that runs into a low shed where something presses or spins. The engine is small, the shed is small, the work is small. The power is mostly human here, or the work is done by the plants themselves: the slow, patient work of extraction, of distillation, of ferment. A stillhouse, tucked behind a wall of yew, works the resins of the conifers that grow in a band to the north. A pulping shed takes the inner bark of the willows and turns it into a thick cream that is then spread on the brickwork with a wooden paddle to seal it against the damp. The town maintains itself in the way a body maintains itself, by a thousand small exchanges, and most of these exchanges are with the bog, and most of the bog's answers come back in the form of something you can render down and use.

The light is even, the color of old paper. It comes in flat from a sky that is almost always overcast, a low ceiling of cloud that sits on the hills to the west. In the morning the mist burns off the lower streets and leaves the upper stories and the chimney emerging from it, as if the town were rising out of a lake that drained slowly. By afternoon the brightest part of the sky is to the south, where a long low ridge of bog holds the light and gives it back in a pale wash. The shadows are not sharp. The damp takes the edges off everything. Colors run toward green and gray and the brown of wet wood, and a few warm notes here and there from a painted door or a stack of cut peat left to dry.

It is quiet here, but not silent. There is the constant soft noise of water — not falling, not running, but present, the way a faucet is present in another room. There is the creak of the wheel when the wind catches it, even when the engine is off. There is the rustle, which is everywhere, of leaves in their slow conversations, and the small ticks and sighs of wood adjusting to itself. And there is the deeper sound, the one you only hear if you stop walking: the sound of the bog itself, the slow gas of it, a kind of breathing, with the rhythm of a sleeping animal, and the occasional soft *pock* of a bubble surfacing in the peat field. Once an hour, perhaps, the stillhouse lets out a long whistle that is not quite a whistle — more the suggestion of one, played on a hollow reed — and the sound moves across the rooftops and is gone.

The fauna is thin. You might see, in the course of a day, a heron at the edge of the water, standing in the black stream with the patience of a thing that has nowhere to be. A fox crosses the main street at dusk, long-bodied, low to the ground, uninterested in you. The small bright birds that nest in the eaves come out in the late afternoon and feed in the willows. Insects are more present than vertebrates — the pale moths that come to the lit windows at night, the gnats that rise from any patch of standing water, a fat bumblebee that works the yew wall in summer. But the dominant life is the plant life, and it is not still, it is not silent. It is always doing something. A vine is always reaching. A bracket fungus is always softening its host. A root is always finding the next damp line and following it. A seed is always waiting. The activity is not fast, but it is total, and it is the activity the place is built around, the way a port is built around the tide.

The town is small enough that you know it by the end of a week. There is the main street. There is the wheel. There is the stillhouse and the pulping shed. There is the long brick building that was once a school, with its tall windows, and is now a drying room, with lengths of bark hung from the rafters in slow rows. There is the peat field to the east, where a man with a long knife cuts the surface in neat rectangles and stacks them in low pyramids to season. There is the river — or what serves for one — a slow dark channel along the south edge that turns the wheel when it can be bothered, and otherwise just moves at the pace of its own long business. The boundaries of the town are not sharp. The buildings thin out, then the buildings stop, then scrub begins, then the bog, then the low hills that close the horizon in every direction.

It is, in the way of such places, a little odd. Not strange — you would not call it strange to anyone who lived here — but off, in the register of a half-heard word. The proportions are slightly wrong. The doorways are too narrow for the height of the walls, or the windows are too wide for the rooms behind them, or the chimney is taller than the buildings it serves, by a margin that looks deliberate until you measure it. The moss grows in patterns that are almost geometric, as if a slower hand had laid it. The mist, when it moves, moves in a way that seems to have a plan. The people who live here — and there are some, a few hundred, enough to keep the wheel turning on its slow days — speak in a way that is slightly behind the times, not because they are ignorant but because the place has its own pace, and the pace is the pace of the bog, which is very slow, and very sure, and does not bother to keep up with anything.
