The brightness is the first thing, and it does not so much fall on the place as inhabit it. The light is everywhere at once, a white that has no source, only presence. The sky is the color of old bone. The horizon, where the country runs out into a haze of itself, is a paler bone still. Nothing casts a shadow of any depth. The heat that lives in this light is not the heat of an open sun, but a settled heat, the warmth of a body held in a closed hand.

Everything is built.

This is the second thing, and it is the one that takes longest to accept, because the building is so thoroughly entered by what would once have been called weather that the place reads first as land. There is a ground. There is a low country, hardly varied in elevation, that extends in every direction further than walking could exhaust. The ground is real to the foot. It gives under the step. It is warm and slightly damp. Moss grows on it. Small ferns push up through seams. Snails leave their silver scars across its surface in the cool hour before the cool hour ends.

But none of it is substrate. Under the moss, the ground is tile. Under the ferns, the ground is a hand-laid pavement of dark brick, fired from a clay that is not quite of this place, the bricks fitted so close that only a thin line of mortar — or something that has become mortar over the long interval — remains between them. Under the moss on the slopes of the long, shallow rise that might be mistaken for a hill, the ground is dressed stone, fitted without pegs, the way some far older people fitted stone, the seams traced with a thin grey lichen that looks almost deliberate, and may be. The hill is a hill because it was built as one. The plain is a plain because it was built as one. The shallow valley that runs through it, and the slow water that moves through the valley, were placed there.

The water is everywhere. Not as a sea, not as a flooding, but as a presence. It sits in basins. It moves in narrow channels that have been carved — patiently, by hand, with tools that would be called simple — through the brick and the stone and the older, rougher construction beneath. The channels are perfectly level. The water in them moves at the rate a body moves in sleep. The basins are shallow, with clay bottoms, and they are filled to a consistent mark. The water is warm. It is full of small things: pale fish, the length of a finger; pale frogs at the margins; a kind of slow shrimp that walks along the bottom on feet that are too many. Their movements are nearly imperceptible. If you watch the basin long enough, the mark of the water does not change. The water is held at a height that is the same height in every basin, even the basins that are set on the higher slopes, which should not be possible, and which is.

The vegetation is in pockets. Across the wider country, the planted areas are distributed at intervals, separated by stretches of bare pavement that are hot, white, and empty. The pockets are not wild. They are cultivated, but the cultivation has reached a state in which the gardener and the garden have become the same thing, and it is no longer clear which is which. Each pocket is a small biome in itself: a grove of fruit trees with their roots in hand-dug pits filled with dark compost, the trees spaced so that their canopies just touch. Beneath the trees, in the half-shade, things grow that the bare pavement will not support — vines, broad-leafed herbs, a green density of small plants that have moved in on their own and have been permitted to stay, and have stayed. Between the groves, the open pavement runs for a hundred paces or more before the next grove begins. The causeways that link them are paved in a different color of brick, a paler brick, almost the color of the bone-sky.

The fauna is everywhere, in the sense that the place is full of it, and nowhere, in the sense that it is rarely seen. Lizards run along the seams of the brick, their colors close to the brick's color, as if the brick were a thing they had agreed to match. The pale fish are in the channels, rising to the surface for a small insect and falling again without breaking the surface. Insects of a great number of kinds move in the air, which is thick with them, and which remains, somehow, quiet. There are birds. They are small, and colored in ways that do not quite repeat from one to the next, and they are not many. The lizard on the brick does not click. The frog at the edge of the basin does not call. The insects do not hum. The place is full of life, and the life is silent. Sound is made, and is taken up by the surfaces as soon as it is made. The brick drinks it. The water takes what is left. The air holds nothing. The place is the quietest place that has ever been, and the quiet is not a quiet of absence. It is a quiet of absorption, a quiet of a thing that has been laid down on top of every other thing.

The construction of the place is in primitive materials. There is no metal visible. The tools that made this place have left traces that suggest stone, bone, fiber, fired clay, the kinds of materials that can be worked by hand. The bricks are handmade, and you can see, in certain lights, the print of a thumb in the corner of one, a curl of fiber caught in another, the maker's small accidents preserved in the surface. The channels are handmade. The seams are handmade. The size of the place is incompatible with this. The place is days across on foot. The labor to lay even a fraction of its surface would be the labor of a people for a hundred generations, and there is no sign of a people. There is no sign of a maker. There is only the made thing, and the made thing continues.

The weirdness of the place is not in any one feature. It is in the totality, and in the way the totality refuses to vary. The place is too complete. The place is finished. The place has the quality of an image that has been resolved, a thought that has been thought to its end. Nothing here is in transition. Nothing is being added. The moss is the moss that will always be moss, and the moss in the next grove is the same moss at the same stage. The water in the basin is at the mark it has always been at. The fish are the length they will always be. The trees are the trees they will be. The shadow of a tree is the shadow of a tree, fixed in place, the same shape at every hour, even though no hour can be distinguished from any other. The sun, if there is a sun, is at the same height. The place is held in itself. The place has been thought, and the thought is over, and the thought is still.

In the haze of the far distance, where the country loses itself, there are structures. They are not tall. They are wide. They are the same brick as the pavement, the same stone as the dressed slopes, and they have the same integration of moss and fern and small animal. They are buildings, or they were. Their roofs are gone, or were never there. Their walls are open to the air in long, deliberate gaps. They contain the same groves, the same basins, the same channels, the same causeways, the same pale fish, the same silent frogs, the same insects that do not hum. They are not separate from the country. They are a denser version of it. The place, when you arrive at one of these structures, does not change. It thickens. It becomes the same thing, more so, and the more-so continues.

The world was made, and the making is over, and the made thing continues.
