# The Cold Sump

The place is bounded. You can feel the edges of it even when you cannot see them — a wall of rock to one side, a curving sweep of piled rubble to the other, and overhead a ceiling of black basalt that sags downward like the underside of a held breath. The whole settlement fits within a single vault, a chamber the size of a cathedral congregation hall, hollowed out of wet stone and then filled, centuries ago, with the things people thought to put inside it. Now those things have come to rest. The Sump has been here longer in its quiet form than it ever was in its working one, and the working one is mostly forgotten.

The light is low and comes from a few stubborn places. A row of glass-shelled lamps still hangs from the crossbeams of the largest workshop, and three of them still burn — a sullen amber, the filaments barely visible inside their clouded bulbs. The current that feeds them travels through copper wires that have gone almost black with verdigris but have not, somehow, broken. Beyond the lamps there is the glow of the Engine itself, which seeps from its seams and from the joints of its pipes in a faint bluish wash, like the light trapped under ice on a winter lake. And beneath everything there is the phosphorescence of the rock — pale veins of mineral, slow to release what they took in during the day from somewhere far above, casting the deeper corners in a greenish suggestion of light that never quite reaches the eye in any direct way.

The water is the dominant fact. It comes down through the ceiling in threads and in sheets — the ceiling weeps, every square foot of it, from a thousand hairline cracks and a few open rents. It gathers on the lip of a stone cornice that runs around the chamber at the height of a man's head, and from there it falls in a permanent shallow rain into the central floor. The floor was once flat and paved. Now it is dished, scooped, channeled by the patient work of dripping. There is a grid of brick gutters set into it, half-buried, and a series of basins — some built, some carved by the water itself — where the runoff collects before it slides off through a culvert in the back wall into a darkness no lamp has reached in living memory. The air is so damp that it condenses on everything. To stand here for a quarter of an hour is to be filmed over. The stone sweats. The iron weeps. The brick blooms with efflorescence like frost.

The buildings stand around the central floor in a loose semicircle, as if they were placed rather than grown. They are low, long, peaked at the roof — workshops, storehouses, the housing for whatever the Engine once served. Their walls are a layering: riveted iron plate at the bottom, brick above, then more iron where the roof meets the wall. They were built to last and they have lasted, but the lasting has changed them. The iron has rusted through in long streaks, and where the rust has eaten completely, the bricks behind have crumbled outward, and the spaces have been filled in turn by moss and fern and the small, pale lichens that thrive in this dimness. Some of the workshops are more plant than iron now — green and rust-colored furrows running up the walls, the rivets still bright where they held, the seams now thick with growth. Other workshops, facing away from the wet, are bare metal and slowly flaking paint, the original color of which is no longer recoverable.

Between the buildings, narrow alleys of broken paving lead back to the walls. Nothing moves in them quickly. There is a catwalk overhead on one side — a thin iron gallery that runs the length of two of the workshops, with a railing that has bent downward under its own weight and now hangs in long curves like vines. From the catwalk, ladders descend, and the ladders end at doors that no longer close. There is a sense of design here that has gone slack, like a sentence that has lost its verbs.

The Engine is at the center of the Sump. It is large enough to be entered through a hatch in its side, though no one does that anymore. It is built of iron plates riveted into a cylinder taller than a man and half as wide, mounted on a poured concrete pad that has cracked and been recolored by the water a hundred times. A forest of pipes emerges from its top and sides — some as thick as a thigh, some no wider than a finger — and these pipes climb the walls, branch along the ceiling, dive into the rock, re-emerge as iron, become stone again, become calcite, become a vein of ore that no longer looks quite like a pipe at all. The Engine was made to push something through those pipes, but what and where, the geometry no longer declares. The pipes simply continue, in iron or in stone or in some in-between material that has the color and weight of neither, until they vanish into the rock. And the Engine hums. A note so low it is more a pressure than a sound, felt in the teeth and in the lungs, varying almost imperceptibly across the day as if it were breathing in its sleep.

The fauna is what makes the place feel inhabited, and what makes the inhabitation strange. Moths are everywhere — fat-bodied, paper-winged, the color of old newsprint — and they cover the lamps in shivering tiers, and when a moth falls, three lizards are there to take it. The lizards are pale, with a faint bluish tint to their bellies, and they have large black eyes that catch any available light and throw it back as a small wet flash. They move along the walls and across the catwalks, and they have learned the geometry of the gutters and can be seen at certain hours picking along the brick edges to drink. Larger things live in the deeper workshops and in the channels of water in the floor. There are rats, certainly — long, pale-furred, with their tails bare of hair — and there are things that are not rats but live in the same places, with too many joints and a way of moving that is not quite walking. The water itself hosts a layered ecology: pale worms in the silt of the basins; soft-bodied things that cling to the underside of the brick with their feet in the current; small pale fish, eyeless, that nudge through the gutters in loose shoals and never come into the light; things like frogs, but with too much webbing between the toes, that sit on the rim of the deeper basins and sing a single note, very softly, in a chorus that does not synchronize.

The slow processes are visible if you watch long enough. A stalactite of rust grows down from the lip of a broken pipe over the course of months. A fern unfurls against a windowpane. The water lays another fraction of a millimeter of calcium on the brick of the floor. The Engine hums. The moths hatch from eggs laid in the seams of the workshops and die on the lamps and their bodies fall into the gutters and are carried away. The whole place cycles without progress. It is in balance the way a forest is in balance — not because nothing is happening, but because what happens is taken up by what comes next, and what comes next, and what comes next, in a chain that has been running long enough that the running itself is the place.

The strangeness is not in any one thing. It is in the way the iron has learned to be stone, the way the stone has learned to be moss, the way the moss has learned to be the wall. It is in the way the Engine still hums a note whose purpose is no longer answerable. It is in the way the eyeless fish drift through brick channels that someone built to carry something else. The Sump was made by people who are not here, and it is now inhabited by things that have never been people, and the two populations have made a single system of each other in the long slow time they have shared. To stand in it is to feel the place consider you briefly, in the patient and uninterested way that an old forest considers a footfall, and then to return to its long, damp, lit-from-within dreaming.
