# The Bason Floor

You are below the reach of any surface light. This is the deep — not the deepest place imaginable, but deep enough that the water has forgotten the sun. The cold here is not a temperature so much as a substance: a slow, mineral chill that enters through the skin and keeps going. It has been cold for so long that the rock itself seems to have absorbed it, so that when currents shift, what moves through the water is a memory of cold that was cold before.

The light, such as it is, comes from nowhere identifiable. A faint, sourceless gray — bioluminescence from creatures too far away to see, or the last dregs of sunlight filtered through a mile of black water. Your eyes, even if you had eyes here, would find nothing to fix on. The gray is uniform. It has no direction. It feels less like a color and more like a suspension, a fluid stain in the air.

But there is a floor. You can feel it rising to meet the turbulence, somewhere far below. The floor has been here longer than the water has been cold. It is a primordial plain — once, perhaps, a mountain range; once, perhaps, a seabed of younger stone; now, after a billion years of patient wear, it is almost flat. *Almost.* There are still rises: low, whale-backed humps of harder rock that resisted the long grinding. They rise from the plain like sleeping animals, their flanks polished so smooth that the currents slide over them without purchase. They are the only landmarks. The few primitive tools scattered across the floor — a hand-axe of black chert, a wedge of volcanic glass, a bone needle still threaded with a length of dried sinew that has not dried because nothing here is dry — lie against these rises as if placed there deliberately, though no one placed them. They are survivors. They are the only things in this place that were ever made.

The current is the only real weather. It does not arrive; it is always here, moving in slow, immense gyres that shift their direction every few hours without warning. When two currents meet, the water does not so much churn as *shear* — you can feel the layers sliding past each other, a horizontal violence, sediment plumes lifting and falling like the breath of something vast beneath. The turbulence is constant, ancient, and without mercy. Anything not anchored is rolled. Anything anchored is buried, slowly, in a drift of fine silt. The silt is everywhere. It hangs in the water as a permanent, low haze, a thickness you could almost drink.

And yet the floor is dense. Packed. There is no bare rock to be seen. The humps and the plain between them are buried under a layer — a *packed* layer — of something. At first it looks like stones. Then, as your perception adjusts, you realize it is not stones. It is shells. Billions of them. Tens of billions. A continuous pavement of calcium carbonate plates and chambers and spirals, each one the former home of a small soft thing that lived and died in the upper water, then drifted down, down, down, and settled, and was buried by the next one, and the next, and the next. They are of every kind — some with the geometry of ammonites, some like flat coins, some spiraling tight as a watch-spring. Many are so old they have fused to each other, becoming the floor itself. A few, near the rises, are recent. The recent ones still hold a faint, mother-of-pearl shine in the sourceless gray. The ancient ones have gone matte, gray, indistinguishable from the stone.

It is the bones of the sea, but it is not morbid. It is just *floor*. It is what the bottom is made of.

Vegetation here is a generous word. There are things that grow — things rooted to the shell-pavement in the lee of the humps, where the turbulence is fractionally less. Long, kinked stalks of something black and brittle, rising perhaps three feet before the current whips them sideways and holds them there, frozen in a permanent lean. They do not sway. They are held. They are a kind of cold-water weed, or coral, or fungus — it is hard to tell. Sparse. You could walk a long way and see only a few. They have no color you would call a color. They are the gray of the water, the gray of the shells, the gray of the stone. If you pulled one up, you would find it had no roots, only a holdfast, a black crusty grip on the shell beneath, and the shell would come up with it, fused.

Fauna is rarer still. In the time you have been here, you have seen — perhaps — a pale thing the size of your hand, moving in quick darts between the humps, its body transparent, its single black eye rolling. You have seen — perhaps — a long, slow shape at the very edge of perception, a shadow that does not move like a shadow should, that suggests mass. That is all. The place is not dead. It is not even hostile to life. It is simply that life here is, by long evolutionary negotiation, very small, very patient, and very few. The water gives almost nothing. What little it gives, the few that live here take, slowly, over decades.

The sound is what surprises you, if you stay long enough to notice. There is almost none. The turbulence is silent — the water moves too slowly, too massively, to make a sound your ear can catch. The current passing over the humps produces a low, sub-audible moan, a pressure you feel in the chest more than hear. Occasionally — once an hour, once a day — a shell far away will crack, the weight of the silt finally exceeding its strength, and the sound will travel through the packed floor and arrive as a faint, sharp *tick*, like a fingernail on porcelain. That is all. That is the only clock the place has.

A primitive thing made the tools. You know this because they are not shaped by water — the edges are still sharp, the flake-scars still visible. A thing held them. A hand closed around them. A wrist swung them down. The hand is long gone. The tools remain, patient as the floor, waiting for a hand that will not return.

This is the basin. This is the deep ancient cold packed darkness, the turbulence and the silence, the shells and the few hand-axes. It is not beautiful in any way that has a name. It is only itself. It will be here, essentially unchanged, long after anything that could describe it has also become a shell in the pavement. The current moves. The stalks lean. The pale thing darts. The shadow, far off, does not move.

The gray persists. The cold persists. The tools lie against the humps and do not corrode, because there is no oxygen here to corrode them. They are preserved, which is not the same as being kept. They have been forgotten by everyone. Including the sea.
