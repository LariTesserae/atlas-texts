The sound reaches you before anything else reaches you. A low, constant, percussive hum that lives in your chest and behind your eyes, the kind of sound that has weight — that you can feel settling into your molars. It is the sound of a thing running. Running continuously, unendingly, as if it has been running for longer than anyone has thought to listen, and will continue running whether anyone listens or not.

You are underground. Not deep — not the crushing, geological dark of a mine shaft or a subway tunnel. More like the underside of a low ceiling. Cathedral height, perhaps, if cathedrals were squat and sweating. The air is dense with moisture that does not quite become rain; it hangs, suspended, breathing against the skin, condensing on every metal surface in lazy beads that swell and fall and swell again. The heat is not a temperature you feel so much as a presence you inhabit. It presses. It pools in the low places. It makes the air shimmer even where there is no light to shimmer through.

The light, when it comes, comes in pieces. Sodium-orange. Mercury-blue. The sickly amber of old bulbs behind clouded glass. It falls in cones from fixtures bolted to the ceiling at irregular intervals, leaving most of the space in a kind of working twilight — enough to see by, not enough to be sure by. The lamps themselves sweat. Their casings are furred with condensation, and where the moisture gathers thickest, it ferments into a pale orange rust that crawls down the fixtures in slow dendritic patterns. Some bulbs have burned out and no one has replaced them. Some have been replaced with the wrong bulbs. Some flicker in a rhythm that is not quite regular, and the flickering throws the shadows into a state of slow agitation, like water barely disturbed.

The ceiling is a ribbed vault of poured concrete, gone dark with decades of humidity. Water tracks follow the seams between forms. In places the concrete has begun to surrender — hairline cracks, spalled patches, exposed rebar that has thickened into orange coral and drips rust-water into shallow troughs carved below. Stalactites of mineral deposit, pale and chalky, have begun to grow downward from the worst of the leaks. The stalactites are wrong. They grow too fast. They grow with a kind of intention. Some of them have begun to curve, as if reaching.

The floor is the more interesting thing. The floor is where the world has happened.

It is a forest.

Not a forest of trees. A forest of pipes.

They rise from the ground in clustered thickets — copper, iron, steel, some of them alloyed with metals you cannot name because they have not yet been invented above. Some are thick as thighs. Some are thin as fingers. They ascend at every angle, bend at every conceivable joint, branch and rebranch and rebranch again, until the upper canopy of the pipe-forest disappears into the dimness overhead and you cannot tell where any individual line goes. The thickets are close together. You can stand inside one and reach out and touch another on every side. Between them, the floor is a shallow, persistent marsh — not standing water, exactly, but a state of ground that is never fully dry. Condensation drips from every pipe. The marsh catches it. The pipes root in it. Small ferns have taken hold in the wet crevices at the base of the thicker lines, and moss — that deep, aggressive, jungle green — climbs every pipe to a height of about a meter before the metal grows too hot for it, and then it stops, a tideline of green against the orange-and-black of corroded steel. Above the moss line, the pipes continue up bare, sweating, gleaming in the lamplight.

The pipes are not all the same kind of pipe. Some of them carry something. You can hear it. You can feel it — a vibration that is different from the hum, a localized tremor that runs through individual stems like a pulse. When you put your hand against a carrying-pipe, you can feel the thing inside moving. It is hot. It is moving fast. Some of the pipes are warm but quiet. Some are cold and silent, abandoned, furred with a different kind of growth — pale, fibrous, fungus-like, hanging in long skeins from the joints like Spanish moss in a place that has forgotten what Spain is. Some of the pipes are singing. A few of them, in particular — tall, vertical, isolated in clearings between the thickets — produce a tone when the fluid inside them passes a constriction. It is a low, organ-note, almost musical. There are perhaps a dozen of them across the visible space, and they are not in tune with one another, but their dissonance is itself a kind of harmony, a chord that shifts and re-forms as pressures somewhere upstream change. This is part of the sound. This is most of the sound.

Structures have been built into the pipe-forest. Not many. Not grandly. They have the quality of things that grew up against the pipes rather than being planned. A catwalk — narrow, grated, rusted through in places — runs along one wall at about head-height, bolted to concrete stanchions that have begun to lean as the marsh has softened the ground beneath them. A small shelter squats at the base of a particularly dense thicket: corrugated steel, a hatch-door, a single bulb behind a wire cage. The bulb is on. The shelter is empty. A second shelter, higher up, is lashed to a junction of four large pipes with cable and what looks like baling wire. It lists slightly. Its hatch is ajar. Inside, you can see, even from the ground, that something has been arranged with attention — a shelf, a folded cloth, a vessel of some kind. Whoever tended this place has not been here in a while, or has not been here in a while in the way that counts.

There are machines.

The machines are the loud part.

The machines are also the still part, which is the strange part.

They run, but they do not move. They are installed in clearings that have been cleared with effort — pipes rerouted around them, marsh drained into shallow channels, ground tamped down and paved with brick or poured with a darker concrete. The machines are squat, heavy, bolted to their own foundations. Some of them are familiar in shape — pumps, compressors, the silhouettes of generators, the bell-curve of a centrifuge. Some of them are not familiar at all. They have geometries that do not parse. They have housings that seem designed to contain something specific that you cannot see, and the housings are warm to the touch, and they vibrate, and from inside them comes a sound that is more felt than heard: a subsonic pressure, a tectonic patience.

All of them are running.

This is important. This is the thing the place keeps telling you, in its low, humming way. They are all running. None of them appear to be doing anything. Pipes feed them and pipes leave them and the pipes carry things away and the machines continue their consumption. The pipes are hot. The machines are hot. The air around them is hot enough to feel like a wall. And yet — no product emerges. No exhaust. No outlet. No byproduct that you can see. The matter that enters the machines leaves by the pipes, and the pipes carry it onward into the dimness, and somewhere — somewhere in the forest, somewhere past where the lamps reach — it joins the larger system, the great circulatory thing that the whole place is a chamber of.

The machines are older than the lamps. The lamps are older than the catwalks. The catwalks are older than the shelters. You can read this in the layering. Each generation has come in, found what was already running, added its own small accommodation, and then either left or become part of the deeper silence. The earliest machines are nearly buried in moss and mineral deposit and the pale fungal drape of the cold pipes. Only their housings show. Only the hum betrays them.

Nothing moves that is not mechanical.

This is also important.

There are no rats. There are no insects. There are no birds, obviously — no birds underground — but you notice their absence the way you notice a missing instrument in an orchestra. The moss grows without being grazed. The fungus spreads without check. The marsh does not ripple except where a pipe lets loose a breath of heated vapor and the surface dimples for a moment. There is no fauna. There has been no fauna for a long time. Whatever lived here once has been cooked out, or driven out, or has become something else. The fungi are patient. The moss is patient. The machines are patient. The pipes grow their mineral stalactites toward the floor at a rate that suggests centuries. The whole place has settled into a kind of equilibrium — a hot, wet, humming, half-lit equilibrium in which the only living things are the ones that do not require other living things, and the only work being done is work whose purpose has been forgotten by everyone except, possibly, the machines themselves.

Time passes strangely here.

You feel this. You feel that an hour has gone by and you have not moved, and also that you have been here for days. The lights flicker in their not-quite-regular rhythm. The pipes sing their not-quite-tuned chord. A stalactite somewhere above you grows by a millimeter. A joint somewhere below you thickens by a millimeter of mineral scale. A bulb somewhere ahead of you burns out, and the dimness deepens by one small degree, and the shadows of the pipe-forest shift, and the moss-line on the nearest pipe is, when you next look at it, perhaps a centimeter higher than it was.

This is the slow part.

The slow part is the part that is doing all the work.

A pipe near you — a small one, copper, green at the joints — has been leaking for a long time. The leak has carved a channel in the brick paving of a clearing. The channel is colonized by a single species of fern, dark green, glossy, growing in a precise line. The fern follows the leak. The fern has followed it for years, perhaps. The leading edge of the fern is moving, now, away from the leak, into drier ground, as if it has begun to suspect that the water is not the point. The water is just the way it got here.

Somewhere, very far off — or perhaps not far, the acoustics lie — a machine restarts. You had not noticed it had stopped. It had been silent for some interval you cannot measure, and now it is not. The chord in the pipes shifts up by a quarter-tone. The lamps in that direction brighten by some imperceptible fraction, as if a current has been redistributed.

The place adjusts itself.

It has been adjusting itself for a long time.

It will continue to adjust itself whether you are here or not.

You stand in the clearing, in the heat, in the dim light, in the wet air, in the sound that has weight, and the pipe-forest breathes around you, and the machines do their work, and the moss climbs, and the stalactites reach, and nothing — nothing at all — looks back at you, because there is nothing here to look.

There has not been, for a long time.

The place is not waiting for you.

The place is not waiting.
