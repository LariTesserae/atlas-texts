A low country. Almost low enough to be sea, though there is no sea visible from anywhere in it. The elevation is the kind you feel in your sinuses when you walk uphill, the inverse — a subtle pressure at the eardrums, a sense that the air above you is heavier than the air below. The whole place rests in a shallow bowl, or perhaps the bowl rests around the place. The distinction is not always clear.

The ground is young. This is the first thing to notice, and the thing that takes longest to believe. Streets have not yet learned the weight they are carrying. Paving slabs sit a half-centimeter above their bedding in places where the bedding is still drying, still finding its level, still settling into itself the way sand settles after a tide. The roots of the larger trees — and there are many larger trees, more every month — have already begun to lift curb stones in quiet, slow rebellion. Engineers have stopped trying to fight this. Some of them, the older ones who came here when the settlement was just being laid down, speak of the ground as if it were still deciding what it wants to be.

Rain here does not fall so much as negotiate. The sky thickens for an hour or two in the afternoon, the air goes heavy with something that smells like iron and warm leaves, and then the moisture arrives in slanted sheets that vanish almost before you register them. After: a stillness that is wetter than the rain was. Condensation pearls on every surface. The pavement shines for an hour and then forgets. You never feel dry here, exactly. You feel less damp. The water table is not far below your feet — three meters, four, depending on which part of the district you cross — and the buildings have been raised on pads partly to keep them above it and partly, the contractors say, because the ground likes to give back what you bury in it.

The vegetation is what the place is, more than the buildings are. This was perhaps inevitable. Lush does not begin to describe it, but lush is the closest word. Ferns have colonized the north faces of every inhabited structure. Moss covers the south faces with a fur so thick it can be peeled back like a pelt. Vines have made architectural decisions of their own, threading between balustrades, sealing themselves into walls that they have begun, in some places, to become part of. There are gardens on the roofs. There are gardens under the floors, where root systems have found the warm cavities of utility tunnels and refused to leave. The trees are old. The trees are also young. Both of these can be true here, and are.

The buildings are few and far between, for a city. Wide gaps open between them — fields, really, or what would be called fields elsewhere, though the grass here is not grass. Tall stands of a sedge that has a purple seam along each blade. A ground cover of something flowering in cream. Between the structures, the green has been permitted, even encouraged, to do as it likes. Avenues here are not avenues; they are clearings with buildings at the edges of them. You can stand in one and not see another inhabited structure for some distance. The density is the density of a small village scattered across the footprint of a small city.

The buildings themselves: this is where the strangeness begins, and where it does not end.

They are tall, some of them. They are tall in a way that does not match their footprint. A tower visible from the central clearing is, when you walk to it, only a few stories high; from a quarter mile away it cuts the sky in a way that suggests a dozen more. The closer you get, the smaller it becomes, until at its base it is a building that is large but ordinary, and you cannot quite remember why it had seemed larger before. This is true of several of them. There is one — pale, ribbed, with no windows on its visible faces — that you can see from almost anywhere in the district, and it is the size of a warehouse. It is always the size of a warehouse, no matter how far away you stand from it. People who work inside it do not speak about what is inside it. They speak about the commute.

The construction is layered. A history of building is visible in the walls of some structures — older stonework visible behind newer cladding, behind newer cladding still, as if the buildings have been re-skinned several times and have decided to keep all the skins. Where the layers disagree, they have begun to buckle in patterns that have a strange regularity, almost like handwriting. Some of the buildings are joined to each other at the upper floors by bridges that look as if they were grown rather than built — bores of a darker material in the walls, sealed at their ends, smooth as river-stone where they enter the parent structures.

Light here is dim. Not dark — never dark, not really, since there is always the soft amber glow from beneath the eaves of the inhabited buildings and the fainter cold-green from the drainage channels and the occasional violet pulse that travels through certain walls at a rate that suggests, but does not confirm, intention. The dimness is the dimness of a room with curtains drawn in late afternoon. Everything is legible. Nothing is bright. The sky is overcast all the time, here, in a way that you eventually realize is not a property of weather but a property of the place — the sky has the color of the place, and the place has a sky to match.

You get used to the dimness. After a week, your pupils widen and stay widened. After a month, you can read by the light from a wall.

There is not much to read. The settlement is not heavily populated, and those who live here move through it at a pace that makes their scarcity feel deliberate. You will see no one for an hour. You will see three people in an afternoon, and they will be moving from one building to another with the unhurried gait of people who do not have to be anywhere in particular. They do not speak loudly. They do not, often, speak at all. When they pass each other, they nod. When they pass you, they look at you in a way that suggests they are not sure whether you are a visitor or a thing that the city has produced.

There are no vehicles on the avenues. There are no vehicles, period, as far as you can tell. There are wide, low channels in some of the roads that look as if they were intended for vehicles but have been filled in with a dark, glassy substance that gives off the violet pulse. The channels terminate in shallow basins at intersections. There is something moving in the basins, slowly, but when you approach it retreats below the surface.

The fauna is rare. This is strange, given how lush the vegetation is, how damp the air, how many shelters the buildings offer. You would expect more. A bird, occasionally — large, dark, with a beak that seems too heavy for its head — crossing the sky at a height you cannot estimate. A lizard on a wall, of a green that is almost fluorescent, holding itself still with a stillness that has intention in it. Insects that hover near the glowing walls and are themselves faintly luminous, in colors that do not occur in insects elsewhere. Something that lives in the tall sedge and moves when you are not looking at it. The dogs that some of the residents keep are visible, but the dogs do not behave like dogs; they sit and watch the buildings, and they do not bark, and they follow their owners at a distance that suggests they have not entirely decided what owners are.

The sound of the place is low. It is the sound of a machine running at idle, except that you cannot find the machine. There is a hum that you feel in your teeth first and then in your hearing, a hum at a frequency that does not match the electrical grid or any other grid you have known. There is the drip of condensation from eaves. There is, at certain hours, a faint and rhythmic breath from somewhere beneath the pavement — in, out, in — that the residents have stopped noticing. There is, occasionally, a sound that might be a vehicle somewhere in another part of the district, or might be a structural settling, or might be the sound the buildings make when they are listening to each other. It is not a loud sound. It is not a sound that requires you to raise your voice to be heard over.

The activity of the place is steady and slow. Vegetation grows. Sediment compacts, millimeter by millimeter. The water table rises in the morning and falls in the afternoon, and the smell of the city changes with it: a heavier iron smell at low tide, a sweeter vegetal smell at high. The buildings are settling, still, into the young ground, and you can see the evidence in the way certain door frames have begun to lean, in the way certain floorboards have begun to sigh underfoot. The residents have learned to expect this. They have learned, also, that the settlement is changing faster than they can document it — that the floor plan they walked through this week will have a corridor next week that wasn't there this week, and that the corridor will lead somewhere that is, somehow, the same place they were already going.

There are rules here, though the residents do not always articulate them. The vegetation is not to be cut. The buildings are not to be entered at night — not because anything has happened to anyone who has, but because of the way the residents look at you when you say that you want to. The violet pulse is not to be interfered with; the dark glass in the channels is not to be touched. The hum is not to be traced to its source. Nobody will stop you. There is no enforcement. There is only the way they do not look at you when you ask about it.

The place is strange. You arrive thinking you will find a settlement, a low city on damp ground, with odd architecture and few people and not much going on. You find all of this. You also find that the settlement is older than the buildings in it, though the buildings are the only structures here. You find that the sky is the same gray from every vantage point, and that the gray is not a color. You find that the residents speak of the city in a tense that does not have a name in your language — neither past nor present nor future, but something that contains all three at once, with the emphasis shifting.

You find, eventually, that you do not want to leave. Not because you are stuck. Because the place has begun to make sense to you in a way that you cannot articulate and that, when you try, comes out sounding like a smell, or a color, or a name you have not yet learned.

You stay.

The dim light holds. The hum holds. The vegetation continues its slow, lush, unhurried claim on the buildings, the avenues, the channels, the gaps between the structures that no one seems to be in a hurry to fill. The buildings settle. The ground takes the weight and gives a little back. The weather arrives and departs and arrives again.

It is a quiet place. It is an active place. It is a young place.

It is a place that has only just begun.
