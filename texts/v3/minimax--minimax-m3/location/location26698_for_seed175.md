The mountain has a dimple in its flank, and the dimple is where the water goes.

You arrive into the dimple out of weather, the way you would step out of a rain and into a soaked room. The air here is so full of water that the wetness reaches your skin before the temperature does, and the temperature is warm — not tropical, not lowland, just warm the way a held hand is warm, the way a stone is warm after a long morning, the way a body is warm, as if the place were keeping a small fire in itself that you cannot see. The wetness is a veil, then a membrane, then simply the medium. You do not enter this hollow so much as pass into it through a skin of vapor.

The hollow is small. It is the size of a small cathedral, or a meeting hall, or a generous room. Its walls are dark stone, sloped and stepped into ledges and shallow seats and the kind of low benches a body might rest on. The walls curve around to almost close on the fourth side, and from the floor you cannot see the opening, because of the curve and because of the plants in the way. You are high. You can tell because the light here has the thinned, slightly-too-clean quality of light that has come through less air to reach you, and because the clouds, when there are clouds, are at your level, walking through the hollow as slow white animals, and sometimes you are inside them and sometimes above them, and the hollow itself does not seem to mind.

The walls weep. Every face, every ledge, every stone is sheeted in a hair-thin film of water that gathers itself, without hurry, into other films, into small black channels, into the dark pools that collect at the base of each step. The weeping is so constant and so total that the walls seem to be melting downward at a pace no one could ever see. In a thousand years they will be lower. In ten thousand they will be mounds. The process is patient, and the process is silent, and the process has been going on for a long time, and the walls have the smoothness of long wetness, the way a river stone has the smoothness of a river.

The floor is two things. Half of it is a moss so deep and so soft that the ground is uncertain underfoot, a foot and more of dark green that drinks from below, rooted in saturation, in a water table so high that the moss is never dry, that the moss is permanently wrung. It is the green of a wet leaf in shadow, the green of an old bruise, and where the sun finds it the moss gives back a brighter emerald that seems, in certain light, faintly lit from inside, as if the moss were running a small cooler process than the rest of the world. The other half of the floor is exposed stone, the same dark stone as the walls, smoothed into a long low shape that suggests a tongue, or a bench, or a hearth depending on where you stand. The stones are warmer than the moss. They hold the day. When the sun comes in at a low slant, in the morning or the late afternoon, the stones give it back as a long pale stripe across the floor, and the moss beside the stripe is lit to a green that is almost a color you have a name for but cannot quite reach.

In the center of the hollow there is a pool. It is the heart of the place, the only thing that gives the place a center. It is not large. A person could walk its margin in a hundred unhurried steps. The water is so still that the surface has the texture of a glass placed carefully down, and so clear that the bottom, dark stone, looks like a continuation downward into a further depth that is not really there. The pool is fed from below. It does not fill and overflow; it rises to a level, and the level does not change, and the pool loses only by the slow creep of evaporation, which becomes the vapor that keeps the walls weeping. The pool is the engine of the weather in the hollow. The hollow is a small machine of climate, and the pool is its fire, and the fire is made of water.

Nothing lives in the pool. There are no insects skating its surface, no fish, no frogs, no worms in the silt at its edge. The pool is water alone. The pool has been water alone for a long time.

The plants are few. The hollow is lush, but the lushness is in the forms of what is here, not in the number. A single great fern stands near the western wall, its fronds as long as a person is tall, every pinna edged with droplets that hang and do not fall, that grow, slowly, to a size at which the leaf can no longer hold them and lets one go, and the drop falls the long way down to the moss, and the moss takes it in. A small tree, almost a sapling still, leans at the eastern edge, with broad soft leaves that are never quite dry and that turn, in the slightest way, when the sun moves, so that the light grazes them differently through the day. Between the tree and the fern the moss extends unbroken, except for the pool and the stone tongue. There are also smaller things, lower things: a kind of ground-orchid with thick leaves and a pale bloom that never quite opens, a bloom that has the shape and the faint, sweet, almost-meat smell of something that is waiting to be pollinated, and that has been waiting, patiently, in the same half-open posture, for as long as the moss at the base of the wall has been growing. There are clusters of cup-shaped growths at the foot of the weeping stone, dark green outside and a paler, iridescent green within, each one holding a finger's worth of water and never spilling it, even when the fern above is touched, even when something passes close. The cups are bait-cups. They are the shape of a thing that would catch a thing. Nothing comes to be caught.

The hollow is silent in a way that is not the absence of sound but the presence of a particular kind of quiet, a quiet that has weight and almost visibility, a quiet you could put your hand into. The only sounds are water sounds, and they are so soft and so constant that they have become the texture of the silence rather than a break in it. The weeping of the walls is too quiet to hear. You feel it as a kind of breathing in the stone. A drop falls from a fern-frond into the pool, every several minutes, and the small plop of it spreads as a ring on the still surface, and the ring reaches the margin and is gone, and the pool is again still, and the drop does not fall again for a while. Sometimes two drops fall at once from different leaves, and the two rings meet on the water and make a small lattice of light, and then the lattice is gone. The hollow is a place where the loudest sound is two drops of water meeting on a pool.

The light is normal. It is the light of a place that is under open sky, filtered through the vapor of the place, and at certain hours the sun reaches the floor in a long shaft and the moss in the shaft becomes a glowing thing, a lit thing, and the drops on the fern are points of light, and the pool takes the sun in and gives it back doubled, and for a while the hollow is brighter than the world outside it, as if the hollow were a bell struck briefly by the day. At other times the light is even and soft, and the hollow is in a kind of permanent overcast glow, the sun behind its own veil.

There is a strangeness here, and it is the strangeness of the whole more than of any one thing in it. Part of it is the absence of animals. There is no life in the hollow that walks or flies or burrows or sings. The place is unbroken by any quick movement except the very slow movement of water and the very slow movement of plants. The plants have, over a long time, taken up the work of animals. The cups hold water for creatures that do not come. The orchid opens for a pollinator that does not come, and yet the orchid continues, year after year, in its half-open posture, as if it had not yet been told, or as if it did not believe. Part of the strangeness is in the way the plants lean, very slightly, all of them, toward the pool. The lean is so small that you would have to look for a long time, comparing against the vertical of the walls, to see it. But it is there. The fern leans. The tree leans. The orchid leans. The cups lean, each one oriented a little toward the center, as if the pool were a thing they could drink with their whole bodies, as if the water in the air were a message and the pool were the source of the message, and they were turning, slowly, to face it.

There is also, on a ledge of the eastern wall, at about the height of a hand resting on a hip, a stone. It is dark, almost black, and it is shaped — not by water, not by weather, but by someone, with patience and a simpler tool — into a wedge, a hand-axe, a thing that fits a closed hand. It is placed, not fallen. It sits in a small hollow of the ledge as if it had been set there on purpose and then left, and the weeping of the wall has kept it wet, and a tiny moss has begun at its base where it meets the rock, a moss so small you would not see it unless you were looking. No one has come to take it. It is the only artifact in the place. The hollow does not use it. The hollow is unaware of it. The hollow continues its slow thing regardless, as it has for a long time, and the stone sits in its small hollow of the ledge, and the moss at its base grows a little in a year, and the wall weeps onto it, and the wall will weep onto it for a long time still.

The hollow is old. It has the feel of a place that has been settling into its own shape for longer than the tree, longer than the moss, longer than the orchid has been opening. The rock has the smoothness of long wetness. The pool has the clearness of long stilling. The moss has the depth of long growing. The hollow is in balance. What falls is taken up. What weeps is recondensed. What is shaped is mossed over. The hollow is a system, a small engine, a cathedral of water and growth, and it has been running so long that it has worn smooth the line between itself and what happens in it, and now there is no difference between the place and the weather of the place, and the hollow is only itself, patient, wet, warm, a little strange, and continuing.

You would not stay long in the hollow. There is nothing to do there. There is no one to meet. The wet air would press on you, and the silence would press on you, and the slow lean of the plants would press on you, and the wet stone on its ledge would press on you, because it is a sign that someone came here once, a long time ago, and sat on this ledge, and held this stone, and shaped it with another stone, and set it down in the place where the wall weeps most, and then went away, and did not come back, and no one has come since. The hollow asks nothing of you. The hollow is not waiting. The hollow has finished waiting. The hollow is only itself, and the only thing it will give you, if you stand still long enough, is the slow recognition that there are places in the world that have stopped being for anyone, and have become, instead, simply ongoing.
