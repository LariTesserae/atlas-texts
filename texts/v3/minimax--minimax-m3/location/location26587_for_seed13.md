The valley opens like a held breath. You come over the ridge expecting nothing — the maps end three days back, and the cartographer's notes say *unreliable, possibly unfinished* — and instead there is the town.

It is built on a shelf in the mountainside, a stone-and-timber shelf that should not exist. The mountains around it are sharp. Young sharp. The kind of sharp that makes you think of teeth, or fresh fractures, where the rock is still pale where the snow has only just finished pulling back, and where the streams run off the cliffs in pale threads that look as if they have only just begun to learn how to fall. The peaks around the town have not yet softened. They are waiting to. They have not.

The town sits on its shelf in layers. A lower terrace, cut into the mountain itself, where the cellars and the byres and the workshops sit back from the weather. A middle band of houses, timber-framed, the walls packed with moss and lichen, the roofs weighted with stone to keep them from lifting in the wind that comes up the gorge. An upper terrace, the thinnest, where the council hall — or temple, or whatever it is — leans against the cliff face and looks down over all of it. The buildings are not crowded. There is a great deal of air between them: paths, ledges, vegetable plots, little meadows of unmown grass where the goats are tethered and forgotten. From above, the town looks like a sentence with too many spaces between the words.

It is damp. The air is damp. The walls are damp. The moss is so wet it drips when you touch it, even on days when it has not rained, and it often has not rained. The damp comes from somewhere else — from the snowmelt up the high gorges, from the river that passes below the shelf in a sound you can hear but not always see, from the clouds that come down and sit in the valley like cats in laps and stay for hours without deciding whether to rain or not. There is fog here in a way that feels less like weather and more like the mountain breathing.

The vegetation is heavy for the altitude. The conifers come up close to the houses — fir, spruce, and a darker, nameless conifer that the locals use for roof beams and that no outsider has ever been able to identify from the wood alone. There are ferns in the gullies the size of your arm. There are mosses everywhere — red, green, and the color of wet iron. The vegetable plots on the terraces are fat; the cool air and the constant moisture seem to do something the sun alone cannot, and the cabbages are enormous, and there is a kind of barley that grows here that no one outside the valley has ever been able to coax through a season. It is a green town. A dark green town.

The animals are many, and they move through the settlement as if it were a forest. Goats, of course. A small grey deer that you see in twos and threes at the edges of vision, stepping delicately across the rooftops in a way that does not quite seem possible, though nothing stops them. Birds everywhere — thrushes, and a fat black bird with a red throat that sings only at dusk. Larger things in the high passes, that you only see when you look up: shapes against the snow that are too big to be goats and too still to be anything you would want to ask about.

It is quiet here, mostly. The wind makes a sound against the eaves, and the river makes a sound below, and the birds make their sounds, and the people make sounds inside their houses, but there is a hush over all of it, as if the sounds were passing through linen. You can stand in the middle of the market square — there is a market square — and hear someone speak a quarter mile away, and the sound arrives softly, as if wrapped. The quiet is not the quiet of an empty place. It is the quiet of a place that is full and holding still.

The light is normal. Midday, even, for half the year. The sun moves through the sky in its ordinary arc and the shadows fall in their ordinary way. It is only when you try to remember, later, that you notice you cannot remember the sun being in a different place than it was. It was always where it was. The shadows were always where they were. The dampness was always the dampness it was. The mountain had always been this young. The town had always been here, in this configuration, with these people, in these clothes, in this light.

You don't notice it while you're there. You notice it after.

There is one more thing. The town is built with hand tools. You can see it in the joints. The timbers are adzed, not sawn. The stone is hammered, not dressed. The nails, where there are nails, are hand-forged. The pots are wheel-thrown. The cloth is hand-woven. There is no machine in the town — no waterwheel that turns more than a grindstone, no bellows that does not have a person standing at it. This is consistent. This is everywhere.

And yet the roads are in good repair, and the cellars are dry, and the walls have not shifted in a way that suggests any of this is getting older. The moss on the roof beams is the same moss that was on the roof beams when the first cartographer passed through, three hundred years ago, and wrote in his ledger: *small settlement, of no great note.* The moss has not thickened. The timbers have not silvered. The people are the same people. The children in the lanes are the same children who were children three hundred years ago, and they have not grown.

The cartographer did not come back to correct the note. No one has come back to correct the note.

The town goes on. The damp is the damp. The mountain is the mountain. The birds sing at dusk. The deer step across the rooftops. The fog comes and sits. The sun is where it is.

That is the place.
