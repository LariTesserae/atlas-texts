# The High Terrace of the Still Engines

The plateau is new. That is the first thing to understand about it. The edges have not yet decided how to fall, and so they stand where they were cast — angular, exact, the way a thing is before it has learned to weather. Everywhere you look, the angles hold. Ridges run for kilometers without softening. Buttresses leap from one terrace to the next in taut diagonals, as though drawn rather than poured. You can stand on a shelf the width of a road and see the next shelf a hundred meters below, and the geometry between them is the geometry of blueprints, not geology.

It is not stone. It is a poured substance the color of dull bone, veined with darker seams where the forms joined. Some of the seams have already begun to delaminate, thin curls of pale material lifting from the parent face like the lips of drying paint. Beneath those curls, if you kneel and pry, you can see the metal armature inside — a weave of copper-bright rods, surprisingly warm to the touch. The whole terrace hums, faintly, in those wires.

---

The light is extraordinary and incorrect.

It arrives from a sky that is not quite a sky. There is a sun — there is, after a fashion, a sun — but it does not behave the way a sun should. It sits at one altitude and does not move, or moves with the patience of seasons rather than hours. The daylight therefore has the quality of noon locked in place: a glaring, level, white illumination that finds every surface equally and refuses to grade into shadow. The buttresses cast no shadows, or they cast shadows that fall in directions that have nothing to do with the light source. Objects in this place are visible from all sides at once, as though the air were a developing fluid.

The result is that nothing here recedes. The eye keeps trying to put something in the middle distance and failing. Every detail is at the surface. Every rivet, every seam, every drop of condensation on a flange is presented with the same insistent clarity. After a while this produces a kind of visual fatigue — the world feels overexposed, a photograph left in the fixer.

The light is also warm, despite the cold. The metal surfaces feel it. You can put your hand against a riser in the morning and sense, through the chill, that something has been heating the air, and the air has been giving that warmth back to the metal without giving any to you.

---

It is cool, here, in the way that high places are cool even in summer. The cold does not arrive on a wind — there is no wind, or only the faintest, which is why this place is so quiet. The cold arrives by contact. You sit on a step and the step takes your heat and goes on being cold. The dampness is the same. It assembles on surfaces in the form of condensation: a fine, even rime of water that gathers overnight on the upturned faces of things and lingers into the afternoon. The poured substance sweats. The metal bones beneath the poured skin sweat. You wake to find the bench you slept on beaded with moisture as if the bench had dreamed.

Rain does come. It comes rarely, and when it comes it is a vertical gray sheet that lasts an hour and then stops, and afterward the terrace gleams. During those hours the sound of it is the loudest sound the place can produce — a hush of impact, a hiss at every seam — and then it is over, and the quiet returns.

There are channels. The rain, when it falls, does not soak into anything, because there is no nothing to soak into. It runs along the engineered grades of the terrace and gathers in long, shallow troughs that have been cut into the upper surface of the plateau. These troughs are glazed, the color of weak tea, and the water sits in them in thin sheets that reflect the too-bright sky. From a distance the troughs look like a system of small rivers. If you follow them you find that they end, always, in vertical intakes: round mouths set into the terrace floor, ringed with brass, down which the water disappears without a sound. Where the water goes, no one who lives here knows. It does not return as a river. It returns as condensation, mostly.

---

There is vegetation.

Or rather — there are plants.

The terrace permits them in designated places. Along the long eastern shelf, where the light pools in a particular way, there are beds of low moss and lichen that have been encouraged to root in the cracks between poured panels. The moss is the green of tarnished copper, and the lichen is the gray-orange of a heating element. They cling with the patience of things that have been told they will be removed. There are also, in a few sheltered corners, stunted woody things — not trees, exactly, more like the memory of a tree, a stick with a tuft — planted in cylinders of imported soil that sit on the terrace like clay pots on a windowsill. The soil is dark and damp and smells of rot, which is a smell you would not otherwise smell here, because rot requires death and death requires life and life on this terrace is, by policy, a thing of edges.

The sparse vegetation gives the place its only soft colors. Without it, everything would be the off-white of bone and the warm brown of the metal beneath and the dull gold of the trough-water and the white of the sky. With it, you have green and gray-green and the occasional smear of russet where a lichen has fruited. From a high vantage the green is visible as a kind of mottling, the way moss appears on a roof.

---

The fauna are abundant, and they are the thing that is wrong.

There are animals here. Many animals. They live in the gaps and on the surfaces and inside the channels. They are not the animals of any wilderness you have read about. They are small, mostly. They are the size of mice, or the size of beetles, or the size of a hand with the fingers tucked in. They have too many legs, or too few. Some of them have legs at the front and not at the back, and they move in a slow looping gait that is not walking and is not crawling. Some of them appear to be made of the same poured substance as the terrace, the same dull bone, with darker seams — and these you cannot at first distinguish from the architecture. It takes motion to find them. A seam that has moved, overnight, two centimeters to the left. A flange that is breathing. A rivet head that turns, slowly, to watch you pass.

They are quiet. That is part of how they have survived here. Whatever sound they make, if they make any, is below the threshold of the place — beneath the hum of the wires, beneath the hiss of the trough-water, beneath the soft tick of cooling metal. You become aware of them by the faint drag of their bodies across a glazed surface. You become aware of them by the small bright toothmarks that appear overnight on the imported soil of the potted plants. You become aware of them by the fact that, if you sit still for long enough on a step, they begin to ignore you.

The larger ones — and there are larger ones, you come to realize, because you find their droppings, which are pale and dry and contain fragments of metal — the larger ones you almost never see. They live deeper. They live in the seams that have delaminated, in the spaces between the poured skin and the wire armature, in the dark. You hear them, sometimes, when you put your ear to a riser: a slow scrabbling, far away, as of something rearranging itself in a cavity that exactly fits it.

---

The technology is total and unobtrusive.

It is not that there are no machines. There are machines in every structural element. The buttresses are engines, or contain engines, or are the cooled housings of engines that once ran and are now, gently, still running. The troughs are pumps, or contain pumps, or are the visible portions of pumps whose bodies are buried in the terrace floor. The light itself is partly engineered — you begin to suspect this when you notice that the brightness varies by surface, that the eastern shelf is always a little warmer-lit than the western, that a riser you remember as dull bone has, on some days, a faint internal glow, like a banked coal.

But none of it announces itself. There are no smokestacks. There are no whistles. There is no clatter of a factory floor. The terrace is, by appearance, silent infrastructure. It has the air of a place where the work has already been done, or where the work is being done at a frequency below your perception, or where the work consists of slow rearrangements too vast to perceive from inside.

You find evidence of the work in small signs. A panel that is warm. A flange that hums when you lean against it. A trough that is flowing slightly uphill. The wires in the walls, glowing faintly, in patterns that shift when you are not looking directly at them.

The terrace is dense with these signs. You cannot take a step without standing on something that is doing something. The density is not a density of objects but a density of *activity* — every surface has a small life, every seam is in some kind of conversation with its neighbors. The place is saturated with function. It is, you come to understand, *running*. It has been running for a long time, and it intends to keep running.

---

The reality is thin here.

You notice this first as a feeling of incorrectness in the inner ear, as if the gravity were a hair too strong or a hair too weak. You notice it as a tendency for things you set down to roll slightly toward walls, or away from walls, in patterns that do not match the slope. You notice it as a way the light behaves around corners — a glare that arrives before you turn, as if it knew.

It gets worse the higher you go. The topmost tier, the one that is just a thin shelf with no rail, behaves oddly. If you drop a chip of the poured substance from up there, it falls, but it falls slowly, and it does not strike the terrace below where you would expect. It strikes several meters off. The trajectory bends. You cannot make yourself believe the wind is doing it, because there is no wind.

Time, also, is unreliable. You sit on a step and an hour passes. You sit on another step and an afternoon passes, and you are not sure, afterward, that the afternoon was longer than the hour, or shorter, or whether the difference between them is a meaningful distinction in this place. The animals seem to know. They move on a different clock. If you watch a beetle-like thing on a riser, it travels for what feels like a long time and crosses perhaps a handspan. Then it pauses for what feels like a moment and is suddenly on the other side of the riser. You did not see it move. It did not, perhaps, move in the way you understand moving.

The strangest thing is the sky. There is, sometimes, a sky. A blue one, with a sun in it, that behaves correctly. There is, other times, no sky at all — only a continuation of the white glare, an infinite up, a sense that if you climbed high enough you would arrive at a ceiling. Once, late, you looked up and saw the silhouette of another terrace above yours, only inverted, hanging downward from the glare like a stalactite. It had its own moss, its own troughs, its own small fauna moving on its undersurface. It did not fall.

You did not go up to look at it more closely. There was no up.

---

It is mostly quiet.

The hum of the wires is below hearing, mostly. The trickle of the troughs is below hearing, mostly. The breathing of the animals is below hearing, mostly. The rain, when it comes, is the loudest thing, and even the rain is hushed.

What you hear, when you hear anything, is your own footsteps. They are too loud. They echo off the poured surface and return to you changed, as if the echo has traveled a longer distance than you have. You hear, sometimes, a slow creak from a buttress — the sound of a thing settling, or of a thing rearranging itself over the timescale of stones. You hear, very rarely, a sound from below the terrace: a distant, wet, percussive note, as of something large shifting in a cavity full of water. It does not repeat. It is not answered.

When the condensation falls from a flange in the morning, in a slow fat drop onto the step below, the sound of that drop is a small clear bell, and it carries across the whole terrace, and every animal pauses to listen, and then continues what it was doing.

---

The terrace is slow.

This is the final thing to understand. All the strangeness, all the apparent activity, all the small wet lights in the seams — they are not for you. They are not on your timescale. The terrace is doing something that takes a very long time, and it is doing it patiently, and it does not need you to witness it. The moss will grow a centimeter in a year. The animals will migrate a meter in a season. The poured skin will delaminate another millimeter by the next time the rain comes. The light will shift, very slowly, by an amount that you cannot see in a single day, toward a configuration you cannot yet imagine. The gravity will, perhaps, become more correct, or less correct, in the direction of some eventual state. The inverted terrace above will, perhaps, descend. The troughs will, perhaps, fill with something other than rainwater.

Or none of that will happen. The terrace will simply continue to be what it is: cool, bright, damp, dense, total, humming quietly in its wires, breathing through its sweating seams, populated by its small and patient fauna, waiting out its own slow becoming under a sun that does not move and a sky that may or may not be there.

You sit on a step. The step is cold. The damp gathers on your sleeve. A beetle-shaped thing, the color of dull bone, crosses the riser in front of you at a pace that might be a centimeter an hour, and pauses, and is somewhere else.

You do not hear it move.
