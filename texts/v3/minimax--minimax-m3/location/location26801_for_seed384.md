# The Inhalation

The water is everywhere. It hangs in the air as a fog so thick it has texture, so dense it can be carved. It lies in thin sheets across every surface, beading on rivets, pooling in the seams between plates of iron and plates of copper. It drips from the overhead — always overhead, the ceiling of pressed-steel decking — in slow, deliberate intervals, each drop striking the flooded floor with a sound like a finger tapping once on a door. The floor is two feet underwater. The water is black and warm on the surface, colder the deeper you reach, and it tastes of salt and tin.

This is a place that was built to be dry, and lost the argument.

The structure is a long hall, perhaps two hundred meters end to end, perhaps longer. The scale says small but the smallness is absolute — a cathedral is small if the cathedral is all you know. Here, the whole world is the hall. There is no outside. There is a wall, and beyond the wall another wall, and beyond that the bulkhead of a ship or a station or something that no longer needs its name. The steel is riveted. The rivets are the size of fists. The plates curve with the patience of something made to withstand pressure, and the pressure is the only weather this place has.

It is cold. The cold is not the cold of open air, not the wind's cold. It is the cold of a refrigerator that has been unplugged for a long time, the cold of metal that has soaked itself through and through and has forgotten how to be warm. Your breath does not steam. The breath goes in, and the water in the air goes into the lungs with it, and the line between breathing and drowning has become a polite suggestion.

The light is the color of a bruise. There are fixtures overhead — hexagonal cages of glass and corroded brass, each one holding a bulb that has been dead for so long that the filament inside is a small black spider — but a few of them still work. Three, maybe four, in this whole drowned hall. They give off a thing that is not quite light, more like a region where the darkness has been asked to step aside. The beams fall through the fog and the falling water and they land on the floor in cones of dull amber, and the cones do not reach each other. The rest is the dark gray of a place that has been wet for a hundred years.

In the cones that work, you can see the machinery.

It is dense. The machinery is everywhere, packed in, layered, built with the greed of a civilization that had not yet learned the word *enough*. It is the scale of factories but the density of a tumor. Pipes run along the ceiling in bundles, some of them as thick as a man can wrap his arms around, and from them smaller pipes descend, and from those smaller pipes still, until the architecture of conduction is a curtain of metal hanging over the hall. Some of the pipes are hot. You can see the steam rising from a joint, a pale slow flower blooming against the black. Some are cold, sweating condensation. Some are empty, and when you tap them with a knuckle they ring with the hollow note of a thing that once had a purpose and has not yet been told.

Below the pipes, the machines themselves. Pump housings the size of rooms, their flywheels still, their inspection plates removed, their interiors visible as dark mouths. A row of compressors, each one a cylinder of cast iron the height of a standing man, banded with stiff steel rings, their valves missing. A long bank of something — boilers? vats? — squat and rounded, set into a raised platform of iron grating, and out of one of them a steady stream of dark water pours over the lip and into the flood below, so the flood is always being fed, always being renewed, and the water is never quite still but always moving with the patience of a long exhale.

The floor is grated. Because the floor is grated, the water is not the surface. Below the grate there is more water, and below that more, and if you knelt and looked down through the iron lattice you could see the water going down into a further darkness, a darkness below the floor, a darkness that has its own fixtures and its own pipes and its own machines, faintly, reduced to silhouettes by the depth. The whole place is a sandwich of industry. You are in the layer that has been chosen, somehow, to be the inhabited one. The others are still working, or pretending to.

There is vegetation. There is almost not vegetation. There is the green of algae, in long curtains hanging from the lower pipes, in slick black sheets across the walls, in tufts pushing up through the grating where the grate has rusted through. There is something that might be a fern, growing out of a crack in a load-bearing column, three fronds, each one the color of a week-old bruise. There is a smell when you pass the column — a smell like wet stone and old paper and the inside of a shoe. Below the waterline, the green is brighter. Things grow where the eye cannot easily follow. Things have made their home in the drowned machinery of the previous owners and are not interested in giving it back.

The animals are few. A fish, once, in the grating — a pale blink of a thing, gone. A crab, also once, clinging to the side of a pump housing, watching with an eye on a stalk. Something in the ceiling that makes a sound like a small motor, and then doesn't. The place is not empty in the way a desert is empty. It is empty in the way a church is empty when the congregation has gone home. There is the feeling of absence. There is the feeling of a population that has not yet arrived, or has not yet noticed they are expected.

The sound is the dripping. The sound is the slow splash of the overflow into the flood. The sound is a deep, subsonic groan, every few minutes, that comes from the metal itself — the structure flexing, the pressure equalizing, the whole hall breathing in its sleep. The sound is the tick of a valve somewhere that has not fully closed. Nothing roars. Nothing hisses. The machines are not running. The machines are just *being*, in the water, in the dark, in the cold, and being is enough for them, and they do it very quietly.

It is strange here. The strangeness is not in any single thing. It is in the compounding. The grates are too perfect. The pipes are too uniform. The light fixtures are too evenly spaced, still, all these years after whoever installed them died. Someone *built* this. The order is too much for chaos. The order is too much for sense. And yet the water has come in, and the green has come in, and the dripping has come in, and the machines wait, and the lights in their cages burn on, three or four of them, amber in the fog, and you stand in the knee-deep dark and you listen to the hall breathe and you cannot tell whether you have arrived at a ruin or a beginning.

The temperature is whatever the metal is. The metal is cold. The metal has always been cold. You stop noticing after a while, and that is when you have been here too long.

Above you, hidden in the fog, the ceiling goes up into a darkness that the lights do not reach. Somewhere in that darkness, more pipes. Somewhere in that darkness, the suggestion of a second deck, a third, a tenth. The water is on this level only. The water has chosen this level, or been chosen for it. Below and above, the world is dry, or drier, or differently wet. You do not go there. You are in the hall. The hall is enough. The hall is all.

A drop falls. It hits the water. The small ring spreads and is gone.
