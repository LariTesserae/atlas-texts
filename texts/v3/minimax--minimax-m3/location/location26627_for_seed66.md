The plateau holds a city that no botanist would recognize. From the rim road, what you can see is mostly geometry: long stepped terraces of black stone and slag-concrete climbing the inland slope, smoke-diffusers rising like organ pipes, rail-beds and pipe-beds running together in parallel scars toward the horizon. It is warm here. Even in the small hours before dawn the air is the temperature of a held breath, and on most mornings a fine condensation beads on every vertical surface — on the basalt walls of the warehouses, on the shoulders of the smokestacks, on the rust-flared rails — so that when the first light comes the whole city appears to be sweating.

There is no grass. There are no trees. There is, at the edge of the rail yards, a low violet crust of something that is not moss and not lichen and not fungus so much as a memory of all three: a film of biology that lives directly on stone and gets its water from the air. In places this crust thickens into shallow hummocks, and in a few favored spots — under the eaves of the cooling sheds, between the cobbles of the older streets — it rises in soft fruiting bodies the size of a child's fist, which the workers call breathing-stones because of the way they exhale a fine spore when stepped on. Nothing here is green. The dominant colors are basalt-black, oxide-red, the pale rust of weathered steel, and the wet sheen of condensation on everything.

Above this mineral ground the fauna is the thing that overwhelms. At any given moment you can look up and see the sky is not there. It is replaced by a layer of bodies. The smallest are gnats the size of pinheads, dense as fog, drifting in slow columns around the warm outflow vents. Above them, in lazy circling tiers, are the things the city calls the sails: flat triangular creatures, perhaps a hand-span across, membrane and cartilage, riding the thermals that pour off the heated stone. They feed, in some way no one fully agrees on, on the spore-puffs of the breathing-stones and on each other's shed skins. Higher still, occasionally, you can see a deeper shape — a slow-flapping bulk against the grey sky, something the size of a small room, that the sailors on the inland sea would call a ketch and that no one here has named. It is rare. The sky between these layers is a haze of wings and slow drifting.

The sounds are constant and they layer. At ground level you hear the city: the long drone of the sulfur lines, the clatter of ore-carts on the basalt tracks, the bass complaint of the compressors in the gas-house, the whistled signals of the yard-shifts. Above this industrial bed the fauna adds its own music — a high thin hush of insect-scale wings, the soft percussive clack of larger creatures landing on the eaves, and now and then the deep resonant moo of the big ones overhead, a sound felt more in the chest than heard with the ears. There is no quiet hour. The compressors run through the night. The fauna does not sleep. The condensation at dawn, and the slow drumming of the moisture falling from the eaves, provide the city's only punctuation.

You can walk for an hour along the inner terraces and not see a single empty surface. The walls are built of stacked and mortared basalt blocks, but in many places the mortar is older than the city's memory, and the masonry has been colonized by layers of life. There are tube-worms — they are not worms, but the name persists — that cement themselves into the gaps between blocks and pulse slowly with the day's warmth. There are sessile feeders that look like wet tongues pressed flat to the stone. There are mobile forms the size of rats with too many legs that graze the violet crust in careful strips, and larger forms, the size of dogs, armored in mineral plates, that prey on the smaller ones. Between the streets and the rail yards the population density of these creatures is such that at any moment you can see dozens moving in your peripheral vision, none of them alarmed, all of them feeding, mating, being eaten, dying, growing.

The wetness is what holds the system together. This is a high plateau, but it sits in the path of the ocean's moisture — a long ridge of land two thousand meters above the inland sea, where the warm air off the coast meets the colder air pooling down from the glaciated interior and dumps its load continuously, not as rain so much as a hanging mist. The city's engineers have learned to harvest it. Every roof is angled. Every gutter is oversized. The runoff feeds shallow condensation-channels that run between the terraces, black with mineral sediment, alive with small flatworms and the larvae of the larger armored forms. The city, in this sense, drinks the sky.

What it eats, in turn, is itself. The ore comes up from the deep workings below the plateau — basalt, copper, sulfur, iron — and is processed in the long low factories along the rim. The slag is piled in great warm heaps that radiate heat for decades. These slag heaps are the city within the city: the breathing-stones grow thickest there, and the fauna is densest, and at night, when the industrial sounds drop by a fraction, you can hear the heaps themselves moving — the soft continuous rustle of a thousand small bodies working the mineral for the trace organics they need. Imported food exists — there are caravans of ore-carts returning from the lowlands with crates of dried something — but it is a luxury. The base of the food chain is mineral, atmospheric water, and time.

It is an old place. The terraces have been built, abandoned, rebuilt, and re-eroded so many times that the lower levels now read as geology rather than construction: a long stratified slope of mortared stone, slag, compacted fauna-bone, and re-grown mineral crust, layer upon layer, the older work visible only where later construction has cut into it. The current city is built on top of at least three earlier ones, and the cycle is still ongoing. A street that was paved in the reign of some previous administration will, in a working life, begin to lift and crack under the root-like holdfasts of the tube-worms; the cracks will widen; the violet crust will spread; the small fauna will follow; the mortar will fail; and within two or three generations the street will be a soft terraced ditch of biological rubble, indistinguishable from the natural slope beside it. The city is being eaten, very slowly, by the lives it holds.

Because of this, the city has a particular quality of light. The basalt is dark, the slag is dark, the fauna is mostly dark, and the sky, when you can see it, is the bright hazy grey of a marine-cloud inversion. At midday the place is luminous in a flat way: shadows are short and soft, colors are muted, and the eye keeps being drawn to the moving layers of fauna overhead. In the warm months the inversion lifts for an hour or two in the afternoon and the inland mountains show — distant, pale, glaciated — and this is the only time the city feels like it has a horizon.

People live here. You see them along the rail-beds in their stained coats and their face-masks of woven fiber, walking with the unhurried pace of people who know that the fauna will part around them and that the wet stone is not, most of the time, dangerous to step on. They speak a language of shouted warnings and short whistles, partly because the compressors are loud and partly because the fauna's wing-noise eats the consonants. Their children grow up knowing the names of the local fauna before they know the names of their own grandparents. Their funerals are, by long custom, conducted on the slag heaps at night, in the rustle, with the great shapes drifting overhead unseen. There is no graveyard. The dead are laid out on the warm stone, and in the morning the breathing-stones are thicker where they lay.

This is what the plateau looks like from the rim road. It looks like a city. It sounds like a city. It smells, in a way I have not yet described, of warm mineral dust, of ozone from the compressors, of the sweet almost-rotten breath of the breathing-stones, and of the faint copper undertone of so many small bodies living so close together. It is a place that has settled into itself. The rain — and it is always raining, gently, somewhere — will fall tonight again, and in the morning the condensation will be on every wall, and the fauna will be moving, and the city will continue to be very slowly eaten by the lives it holds.
