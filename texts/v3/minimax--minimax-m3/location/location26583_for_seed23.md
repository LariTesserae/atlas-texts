# The Stone Confluence at the Tindal Pass

## I. Arrival

You come to it through three miles of scree. The path — if it can be called a path, more a suggestion left by the hooves of things that refused to fall — threads between tilted slabs of dark stone that ring faintly underfoot, as though the mountain is humming below hearing. The air is thin and very cold, and it has a quality you can almost chew: wet, mineral, with a faint sweetness underneath, like wet wool left near a stove. Every breath you take is also a small effort.

Then the scree gives way, and you are standing on the lip of the Tindal Pass, and the city is in front of you.

It does not look like a city. From here, on the high shoulder, it looks like something the mountain forgot to push up. A crowding of low, dark shapes pressed into a saddle of rock between two ridgelines, every roof and wall beaded with moisture, every surface moving with reflected sky. You can see steam.

## II. The Confluence Itself

The town — *Korr-Véd* the traders call it, though no one who lives here uses that name — is built on a *spring line*. The mountain is cracked here, fissured by some ancient sideways shove, and water comes up out of the rock along a band that runs, roughly, east to west, right through the middle of the settlement. The ground is never dry. The streets are channels. The doorsteps are the tops of stairs you cannot see, descending into dark water.

There are three principal springs. They have been given names in a language none of the present inhabitants speak; the names have been kept anyway, as one keeps a tooth.

The *Coldwell* is the eastern one. It emerges from a vertical cleft in the cliff behind the uppermost houses, a thick silver-white rope of water falling six meters into a stone basin that has been carved, over generations, into something between a cistern and a small lake. The water is not freezing but it is not warm; if you sit on the basin's edge for an hour, you stop being sure you have feet. Children are not allowed here alone. They come anyway.

The *Hollowspring* is the western one. It does not fall; it rises. A slow, deliberate dome of water that pushes up through a flat stone in the market square and stands, trembling, half a meter above the floor before it spills. The stone around it has been worn into a perfect bowl by centuries of this. At certain hours of the day — no one has ever figured out which, or pretends not to — the dome pulses. The whole square goes quiet when it does. Then the water breaks and falls in a brief, deliberate rain, and people go back to what they were doing.

Between them, threading through the town like the seam in a closed hand, runs the *spine-water*: a shallow, fast, very clear stream that emerges from a dozen cracks along the fault and joins itself into a single current by the time it reaches the lower gate. From above, looking down, you can see every stone of its bed.

## III. The Buildings

The town is small. Two thousand people, perhaps, if you count the herders who come down for the cold months. It is dense: the houses lean against one another the way tired people lean. You can walk the entire settlement in fifteen minutes, and in that walk you will cross the spine-water nine times on stepping stones, pass under seven overhanging upper stories, and be unable to see the sky for more than a few seconds at a stretch.

The buildings are stone, and they are *wet*. This is the first thing anyone remarks on. Every wall, every roof-slab, every doorframe is dark with moisture, and where the light catches them they throw it back in long vertical streaks, like rain that has paused mid-fall. Moss grows on every horizontal surface that gets less than full sun, and that is most of them; the moss is the color of tarnished copper, almost black in the shade, a deep wet green where the sun finds it between buildings for the half hour before it sets.

The roofs are flat. They have to be; the snow is heavy in winter and a peaked roof would shed a person before it shed a flake. The roof-stones are pinned down with river rock, and the pins are crusted with a yellow lichen that has a faintly sulphurous smell. The houses are two, sometimes three stories, with the upper stories cantilevered out over the lanes on stone brackets. The brackets are carved. Most of them are worn almost smooth; some still show, in the angles, the old motifs: spirals, paired lines, a thing that might be a wave or might be a hand.

There is no mortar that anyone uses now. The stones fit because they were chosen to fit, and because the people who laid them understood this stone in this place. Where a wall has been repaired, the repair is visible: newer rock, lighter, drier-looking, mortared with a pale paste. The repairs do not last as long as the original work. Nobody talks about this.

There is one building that is not stone. The long dye-house by the Coldwell is built of peat-block, cut from the high bogs and hauled down on sledges in the brief dry weeks of late summer. It is black and fibrous and smells of tannin and iron. Smoke comes from it in winter; in summer, when the dyes are not working, it stands empty and dark and sweating, and the herders say the peat-house dreams in summer, though they will not say of what.

## IV. The Air and the Light

The light is the second thing people remark on.

You are high. The sun, when it is out, is a thing that has opinions. It comes in low and hard, sideways, and finds every wet surface and every metal fitting and turns the whole town into a dazzle. For an hour after dawn, and again for an hour before the sun goes behind the western ridge, the place is unbearable to look at: water flashing, mica in the stone flaring, the brass fittings on doors throwing long horizontal bars of light across the lanes. In between — from mid-morning to late afternoon — the town is in shade, because the cliff to the north is taller than the town is wide, and the sun simply does not reach.

So the rhythm of the day is set by light. The morning is for the lanes and the open squares, where the sun has touched the stones dry. The middle of the day is for the covered ways, the colonnades, the dark interior courts. The evening is again for outside, and at evening the long streaks of water on the walls catch the low sun and the whole town seems to be crying light.

The air is always wet. The coldwell throws a constant fine mist; the spine-water, running fast, throws more; the rising of the Hollowspring throws a soft, slow, almost warm breath over the market square; and through all of it the mountain itself is breathing, the rock sweating in the sun, the cracks running with condensation. You arrive dry and you are never dry again. Your clothes grow heavy. Your boots darken. The metal things you carry begin, within hours, to show a fine crust of verdigris, and the leather things to grow a faint white bloom.

It rains often. When it rains, you cannot tell. The rain and the mist and the falling water of the springs become one event, and the town simply becomes louder, and the stepping stones in the spine-water disappear under a fast brown skin, and people go indoors and the peat-house begins to smoke.

## V. The Sounds, and the Quiet

For all this water and all this life, the town is *quiet*.

This surprises people. They expect, with so much falling and running and splashing, a great noise. There is noise — the Coldwell has a voice, a steady low hiss and deeper thump as the column of water strikes the basin; the spine-water chatters; the Hollowspring, when it pulses, makes a sound like a man clearing his throat. But the water sounds do not add; they cancel. After a day in the town you stop hearing them. They become the floor of the silence, the way a clock you have lived with for years becomes silence.

What you hear, in the town, is:

- A bell, struck once, at a particular hour no one can keep track of, from a stone building near the upper gate. The bell is cracked; it sounds like a stone dropped into deep water.
- Voices, low, in a language with many gutterals and almost no vowels, spoken fast and not raised.
- The creak of the wool-press in the dye-house, when it is working.
- A small, distant, continuous sound from the direction of the upper scree, which is the wind in the rock, and which the townspeople say is the mountain *thinking*.
- At night, a single note, held for a long time, from somewhere inside the cliff above the Coldwell. A horn, perhaps. No one says.

You do not hear: birdsong, mostly. There are birds; the high scree is full of them. But the town itself, in its damp and dark, is a poor place for birds, and they stay on the slopes. What you hear instead, in the long evenings, is a low piping from the moss on the walls, which is not, the herders insist, the wind; the moss itself, breathing.

You do not hear wheels. There is no wheeled vehicle in the town. Everything is carried, or pulled on low sledges. The lanes are too narrow and too wet and too stepped for anything else. The sledges, when they pass, make a long, slow, wooden complaint, like a chest being dragged across a floor.

## VI. The Inhabitants

The people of the town are not the people who built the stone walls. The carvings on the brackets are not theirs. The names of the springs are not theirs. They came, two or three centuries ago, from somewhere downslope, and they found the town already here, in its balance, and they settled into it the way moss settles into a crack: by being willing to be small.

They are short, broad, dark-skinned people, used to the cold, used to the thin air. They speak a language that sounds like stones being knocked together. They dye wool in the peat-house and trade it, on foot, over the lower pass, for grain and salt. They are not numerous. They are not rich. They are very competent. They know every spring, every leak, every slow bulge in every wall. They know which stones to walk on and which to step over. They know, without being told, when the Hollowspring is going to pulse.

They do not smile at strangers. They are not unfriendly; they are simply not yet sure of you. By the second day, someone offers you a bowl of something hot and bitter. By the third, you are being asked, in their unvoweled language, which side of the fault you sleep on at home.

The children are different. The children are everywhere, and they are loud, and they are curious, and they climb the walls and the roofs and the cliffs above the Coldwell with a confidence that makes the herders cross themselves. They have a game, in summer, of trying to stand in the dome of the Hollowspring while it pulses, and being thrown into the market square, and laughing.

## VII. The Other Lives

The town is not alone on the mountain.

The high scree above it is the territory of the *white grazers*: a species — or a thing that functions as a species — of pale, thick-coated, four-legged animal, the size of a small horse, with two sets of horns, one above the other. They come down in the cold months to the edges of the town and stand in the spine-water, in rows, staring at the houses. They are not afraid of people. People are not afraid of them. They have an agreement that no one has ever written down. The grazers do not enter the lanes. The people do not hunt them. In the worst winters, when the snow is up to the door lintels, the grazers come further in, and stand in the doorways, breathing, and are not turned away.

On the cliffs to the north, in summer, nest the *splitwings*: large, dark, very silent birds with feathers that are not quite feathers, more like overlapping plates of thin bone. They hunt the high meadows, where a kind of small, very fast, almost legless rodent lives in burrows under the snow. The splitwings do not cry. The herders say this is because they have no mouths, which is not true; they have beaks, long and pale, and they open them only to eat. When a splitwing flies low over the town — which it does, sometimes, once a season — everyone stops and watches and the children are quiet, and the bird goes away, and the town exhales.

In the Coldwell basin, if you sit long enough, you begin to see the *eel-things*. They are not eels. They are long, pale, and they move in the water with a slow, deliberate undulation, and they have, at the head end, a cluster of fine white filaments like the roots of a plant. They feed on the minerals in the water, or on the light, or on something else; no one is sure. They are eaten, in the early spring, when the Coldwell runs higher and they come up out of the deep places. They are good. They taste, the herders say, like the memory of a vegetable you ate as a child, though the herders are not always to be trusted about what they ate as children.

## VIII. The Weather of the World

The mountain is not a stable place.

The wind comes up, in the afternoons, with a violence that has intent. It does not start; it is there. The herders will be standing in a square, talking, and the next moment they are leaning, and the next they are walking, fast, to the lee side of something. Stones the size of a fist move down the lanes. The wool hung out to dry is taken, instantly, into the air, and is not seen again. Children are pulled, by the hand, into doorways. The spine-water, which had been running west, runs, for the duration of the wind, east.

When the wind stops, which it does after an hour or two, the town is the same, and the people step out, and look up at the sky, and begin again.

In winter, the snow comes early and stays late. The springs do not freeze; the water is too fast, and too deep, and too full of minerals, to freeze. The town becomes a place of steam and dark stone and moving white. The long evenings are lit by the brass lamps, and the long mornings by the pale, level, almost apologetic light of a sun that is always just about to come over the ridge and never quite does. The people go about in felt and fur and heavy wet wool, and they are patient, and the peat-house smokes, and the eel-things are eaten, and the grazers stand in the doorways, and the bell is struck once, at the hour no one can name.

## IX. The Strange

And then there is the thing that is not spoken of.

The mountain is cracked. The town is built on the crack. The water comes up through the crack. And sometimes, in the evenings, when the light is at its long horizontal best, and the whole town is crying light, the crack *breathes*.

You feel it before you see it. A small drop in the pressure of the air. A sense of the ground having shifted, by a millimeter, in a direction that is not down. Then, along the line of the spring, in the stone of the street, in the walls of the houses, a fine, slow, almost silent *opening*. Hairline cracks, half a millimeter wide, running for meters, opening along the grain of the stone. The water in the spine-water brightens. The Coldwell hisses higher. The Hollowspring, which was not going to pulse, pulses.

It lasts, usually, less than a minute. Then the cracks close, with a sound like a whispered word repeated many times, and the town exhales, and the herders, who had stopped, go on.

No one has ever been hurt. Stones do not fall. Walls do not crack. The cracks, when they open, do not go all the way through; they open in the surface only, a centimeter or two, and close again. But things move. A door that used to open inward now opens outward. A stone step, sure for generations, becomes a trap for a day, until the foot learns its new depth. A house, in the morning, has a new, thin, bright wet line running down its inner wall, where none was before.

The herders say the mountain is *settling*. The dyers say the mountain is *stretching*, the way a cat stretches. The children say the mountain is *talking to itself*. The herders do not contradict the children.

Once, the herders say, in the time of their grandmothers' grandmothers, the crack opened and did not close for a whole night, and in the morning the spine-water was running in a new channel, and two houses had moved a hand's breadth downslope, and the bracket above the tanner's door, which had always shown a wave, now showed, unmistakably, a wave with something in it. The crack closed, the next day, at noon. The bracket is still there. The thing in the wave is still there. It looks, if you look long enough, like an eye.

No one looks long.

## X. Leaving

You go back up the scree, the way you came. The wind is in the rock. The light is on the wet walls behind you. The Coldwell hisses, and the spine-water runs, and the Hollowspring, in its square, stands in its trembling dome.

You do not look back.

You will, though, all the way down the pass, hear the faint, cracked bell, struck once, at an hour you cannot name, from a stone building you cannot see, in a town you cannot quite describe, on a mountain that is, very slowly, very quietly, very deliberately, *settling into itself*.

---

*The town is in the spring line. The spring line is in the crack. The crack is in the mountain. The mountain is in the weather, and the weather is in the light, and the light, on the wet walls, is in your eyes all the way home.*
