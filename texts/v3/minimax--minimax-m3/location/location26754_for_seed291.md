# The Foundry of Low Cresting

It begins with the land.

A low shelf of country, hardly a hill — more the suggestion of one, an upward drift in the terrain that shouldn't be there and is still, technically, arriving. The ground rose first. That is the part everyone agrees on, the part the older maps cannot account for: a smooth plain, drained by a sluggish river, that over a span of maybe eight or nine years pressed itself upward by thirty feet at its highest point, fracturing as it came. The river did not vanish; it was simply persuaded to flow around the new ground, which is now a long, blunt-backed ridge running east-west, like a sleeping creature just beneath the skin of the world. The ridge is not done. Sounding rods driven into it ring differently from one season to the next. In places it has risen another inch since spring. The engineers from the city have stopped pretending this is finished.

So the land is raw. The strata that the uplift exposed are young to the air — crumbly grey clays, a pale stone that cracks into cubes when it dries and goes slick and dark when wet, seams of something black and vitreous that the mineralogists have not yet finished naming. Slump scars run down the flanks where the rise outpaced its own coherence. A lip of newer ground on the eastern end is mostly scree held together by roots, and even the roots are unsure of their purchase. When the wind comes hard off the river, you can see the loose material breathe.

It is cold. The kind of cold that lives in the lungs and stays there. The river fog, drawn in from the west, never quite clears; it lies along the lowest streets in the morning and burns off only in patches by afternoon, when the chimneys of the foundry district replace it with their own. Breath hangs. Windowpanes weep on the inside. The warmth that does exist is mostly the warmth of industry — the radiant heat of kilns and steam mains, the small thermal gift of the vents that the earth itself puts up through the new ground, breathing warm mineral air from somewhere deep and still.

---

The town grew on the ridge because the ridge is where the heat is.

They called it Low Cresting at first, and then just the Cresting, and now mostly nothing — the postmark reads *Foundry*, which is the only word anyone needs. It is a single town, continuous, packed the way packed things are packed: no margins, no breathing room, every square foot claimed. From the river side it looks like a green-grey cliff of buildings and pipes and chimneys, and from above it looks like the earth has come up in a rash of masonry. The oldest part of town is at the eastern foot of the rise, where the land first pushed through and the first shelters went up — half-buried timber sheds, bricked in later, their roofs patched with newer tile and then with corrugated iron and then with more tile again. From there the town climbed. Each generation of buildings is stacked somewhat on the last, and the streets are not so much laid out as negotiated: narrow, crooked, rising with the ground, occasionally disappearing into a courtyard or a covered way where the slope grew too steep. The newest construction is concrete and steel plate, and there is a lot of it.

Density here is not a metaphor. The buildings touch. They share walls; they share downspouts; they share, in many cases, the same chimney. Where two structures meet, the gap between them has been made useful — there is almost always a pipe in it, or a ladder, or a strip of moss-fed vegetable garden on a jutting sill. The alleys are not alleys so much as interior rooms of the town, with ceilings of sagging timber and floors of wet cobble. You can walk for ten minutes under the same roofline and pass, in order, a tanner's yard, a schoolroom, a chapel, the back door of a public house, a coop of chickens, and someone's kitchen. Rain comes in only where the roof has failed, and the failures have been partially bricked over and become skylights.

The industry is the point of the place. There is a foundry — the real one, the first one — somewhere in the middle of all this, and around it has accreted everything the foundry needs and everything its workers need and everything their children need, until the whole packed settlement is, in effect, a foundry's body. The metal comes up from below: iron, mostly, but also a strange dark alloy no one has refined before, that the forge men work at night and that does not behave quite as metal should. It is taken from shafts sunk into the ridge itself, where the uplift brought deep rocks within reach for the first time in human memory. The smelting is done in long brick halls whose chimneys are taller than any steeple. The stamping and the rolling and the drawing are done in sheds that tremble when the presses run. The waste goes back into the ground, into settling ponds at the town's edge, where it cools into slag that the children collect and pile into small cairns.

Power here is steam, but not the clean steam of textbook engines. Steam is everywhere because the vents are everywhere. The earth itself is a boiler, somewhere far down, and its product comes up through cracks and bored wells and is collected in cisterns at the corners of buildings. There is a faint wheeze to the whole town, an infrastructure of hiss. In the cold, the steam is visible from every joint and seam, and the foundry district in winter is a city seen through gauze. When the temperature drops far enough, the steam freezes on whatever it touches — a rime of white on iron, on brick, on the shoulders of any man walking home late. There are mornings when the railings are feathered with frost that grew from the inside out, as if the town is gently molting.

The electricity arrived a generation ago, in thin wires that run along the eaves and disappear into walls. It is not reliable. The dynamos are driven by the same uncertain steam, and on days when the vents heave and shift, the lights brown and brighten and the trolleys in the foundry yard shudder to a stop. People carry small oil lamps anyway, out of habit, and the lamps give the night streets a warmer and more local kind of glow than the public lighting ever does.

---

Vegetation, against the odds, is lavish.

The cold slows it but does not stop it; the mineral steam feeds it; and the density of human settlement provides an endless architecture of surfaces for it to climb. Ferns grow from the cracks between cobbles, deep green and improbably large for the latitude. Moss is everywhere — on every north-facing wall, on every iron fitting, on every roof tile that has gone soft with age. There are willows along the river, of course, but the willows are not the point; the point is the ivy, the ivy, the ivy. It has eaten the western quarter of the town. Half the buildings there are visible only as shapes under a heavy green hide, and in summer the flowering vines come down the facades like beards. There are birches somehow, birches in a place no birch should be, their white bark stained dark by the constant moisture, their roots splitting the pavement around the chapel square. A single enormous rhododendron, planted by no one remembers whom, has climbed the back of the smelting hall and is now erupting from the chimney in a kind of botanical seizure; every spring it blooms magenta against the soot.

The vegetation is not always welcome. The foundry men fight it in the working sheds, scraping it from the gears, prying it from between bricks. It is hopeless. The plants are too well-fed by what the ground is putting up. They have learned the rhythm of the vents and grow fastest in the hours after a fumarole opens. The parts of the town that have been abandoned — and there are parts, mostly the lowest, where the slope is steepest and the ground moves most — are no longer buildings at all but mounds of green, with a stovepipe or a chimney emerging from each one like a periscope.

The fauna is correspondingly busy. Crows — dark, heavy, opportunistic — own the rooftops in flocks. They sit on the smokestacks in rows and watch the foundry yard. There are pigeons in the old parts, and starlings that roost under the eaves in such numbers that the droppings have whitened whole streets. Rats are present as rats are always present; they are large here, well-fed, and unafraid. Bats in the chimneys. Moths in the lamplight — pale, heavy-bodied things, drawn in through every opened door. In the margins, where the slag heaps meet the scrub, there are pigs that have gone feral, and something like a goat, and once in a while, at dusk, a dog with no collar that moves through the alleys like it knows where it is going.

There are also, occasionally, things that do not quite fit.

The strangeness of the place is not in any one feature but in the way all the features lean against each other. It is in the fact that the smoke from the foundry chimneys, when the air is very still, does not rise but hangs in shaped bands, as if it has weight. It is in the fact that the slag cairns the children build sometimes have, by morning, small crystals on them that the morning-shift forgemen pocket and say nothing about. It is in the fact that a street that was a street last autumn is now a shallow valley with a dry streambed down its middle, and the buildings on either side have their first floors sunk into the new ground, and the people inside have simply begun using what used to be their parlors as cellars. It is in the fact that the new concrete of the newest mill has set at an angle, because the ground beneath it continued to rise for weeks after the pour, and the foreman has ordered that the windows be cut to match the lean and no one has questioned it.

The mineralogists come twice a year and leave confused. The dark alloy will not melt at the temperatures they expect; it rings when struck; once, in a sample, a piece of it was found to have a seam of something that looked like wood embedded in its surface, and the wood had grain that did not match any known timber. The vents do not always vent the same gases; a vent that is sulfurous in March may be sweet in August. A well that was drilled for steam came up one morning with a small fountain of clear water that, when tasted, was not quite water. The man who tasted it said nothing for an hour and then went home and did not come back to work.

Travelers find the town difficult. It is on no two maps in the same place. A person can be shown the way twice and reach it only once. The locals do not find this strange. They say the ground shifts; they say the fog is thick; they say you have to want to arrive. They smile when they say it, in the way of people who have decided not to think about something.

---

The sound of Low Cresting is, against all expectation, mostly quiet.

The damp eats noise. The fog eats noise. The density eats noise — sounds bounce once off a wall and are smothered by ivy before they bounce twice. The industrial clamor that one might expect from such a place arrives at the ear as a low, far hum, felt more in the chest than heard. There is a continual soft percussion underneath everything: the working of the steam in its pipes, the slow groan of timber settling in the cold, the lap and drip of condensation running off every iron surface. Hooves on wet cobble sound like hooves wrapped in cloth. Voices carry less than they should; conversations happen close. A shout on one street is a murmur on the next.

What sound there is has a particular character. There is the hiss of the vents, which is everywhere and is, after a while, indistinguishable from silence. There is the rhythmic clang of the foundry stamp, which comes in waves — a pause, a clang, a pause — that one stops hearing. There is the occasional cry of the crows, which is startling precisely because everything else is so muffled. There is, in winter, the sound of the river ice creaking at the edges of town, a sound like a ship in a bottle. There is, sometimes at night, a long low note from somewhere under the ground that is not quite a note, that rises and falls over the course of an hour and then is gone.

The dynamic of the place — and it is, beneath its hush, a deeply dynamic place — is the dynamic of a thing in the middle of becoming. The ground is moving. The vents shift. The buildings settle, lean, are reinforced, are abandoned. The foundry men work through the night and the slag heaps grow and the children climb them. The ivy puts out a foot of growth in a week when the conditions are right and a year when they are not. The fog rolls in and the fog burns off and the fog rolls in again. People are born in the upper stories and grow up walking down the stairs that used to be the front walk, and they think nothing of it. The dark alloy is forged into things whose purpose is unclear and shipped down the river to places whose names the foundry men forget as soon as the crates are loaded.

The town is not finished. That is the strangest thing about it, and the truest: that it is being made, still, by the same forces that made the ridge, and that whatever is making it has not yet stopped, and that the people who live in it have learned to live in a place that is not yet a place, that is still in the act of arriving. They build their floors, and the floors tilt. They lay their pipes, and the pipes kink. They plant their gardens, and the gardens come up in the wrong place. They wake in the morning and the mountain has grown another inch under them, and they say *ah*, and they go to work.

The fog comes in. The steam goes up. The crows sit on the chimneys and wait. Somewhere underneath it all, the earth is still pressing itself into shape, and the town is rising with it, slowly, packed and quiet and strange.
