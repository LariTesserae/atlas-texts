The valley sits in a saddle between two ridgelines, neither of them high enough to be called mountains. From the rim you look down into a place that doesn't quite look like land. It looks like something is being made there — something that has been being made for a long time — and the making is what the place is.

The first thing you register is the green. Not the bright green of new growth but the deep, almost-black green of plant matter so old and so layered that the chlorophyll has been doing its work continuously for centuries. There is no soil visible. There is no rock. There is no bare earth. From edge to edge the valley floor is a continuous surface of living and dead plant, woven so tight that a foot placed upon it would still be on plant, only deeper. The place is not growing on top of something. The place is itself the substrate.

The cold is the second thing. It comes off the basin like something exhaled. The air sits at a temperature that is not freezing but doesn't want to be far from it. There is frost on the western rim where the morning sun hasn't reached. The plants themselves hold rime in their folds and cups. The cold is part of the engine of this place — it slows things just enough that nothing rots fast, but it doesn't stop the cycle. The cycle has been running too long to stop.

Then the movement registers. The surface of the place is not still. In the middle distance, a slow wave moves through — not water but growth. A section of plants in the northwest quadrant is taller, glossier, more saturated than its neighbors. As you watch, it spreads. Tendrils or runners — at this scale it is hard to tell what is one plant and what is many — push outward into the duller zones around them. The greener patch thickens, lifts, fills out. And on its trailing edge, something else is happening: where the patch has grown densest, it is collapsing. The thick mass has gotten too tall for the cold to support it; the inner layers are dying, falling back into dark mulch. The wave of growth is followed, a meter behind, by a wave of rot.

The waves are not random. Stand on the rim long enough and you see that the patches form a pattern — long parallel bands running roughly northwest to southeast, each band maybe forty meters wide, advancing across the valley at a speed you couldn't call fast but couldn't call still. Seen from above, the valley would look like a series of conveyor belts, or like the surface of a slow millstone, or like the grooves of a record. There is a rhythm. There is a regularity.

The regularity is what makes the place feel industrial. There is no factory. There are no smokestacks. There is no engine you can see or hear. But the pattern — the bands, the advancing fronts, the wave of growth followed at a precise interval by the wave of decay — is the pattern of a process that has been optimized. Not by anyone. By time. The plants that survived here, the configuration that didn't die out, has settled into a kind of efficiency: it grows, it falls, it grows, it falls, and it does this in stripes that march across the basin in a steady rotation. It is a biome that has been running so long that it has found its rhythm, and the rhythm is the only industry here.

The water is what makes it run. The valley is damp the way a basement is damp — not from a source you can point to, but from condensation, from seepage, from the slow exhale of moisture that the cold precipitates out of the air every night and that the sun doesn't quite evaporate. There are pockets. In the troughs between the growth-bands, where the rotted matter has slumped lowest, water gathers in flat black pools so still they look poured. They are small. They are everywhere. There is no river. There is no runoff. Whatever water falls onto this valley, whether from the sky or as condensation, is taken up almost immediately by the dense plant matter, processed through the cycle, exhaled back into the air as vapor, condensed again by the cold, taken up again. The valley is its own water system, a closed loop that has been running for longer than anyone could date.

The cold is what keeps the place quiet. Not quiet — silent. The dense plant matter absorbs everything. The air is too heavy with moisture and too still from the valley's enclosure to move. The growth happens without sound. The decay happens without sound. The water in the black pools doesn't ripple because nothing disturbs it. If a bird — and there are a few, small dark shapes that lift and circle and descend without calling — passes overhead, you don't hear it. The place is acoustically dead. Whatever churns here churns in absolute quiet.

The animals are few. The valley cannot support much. There is a small dark bird, low-flying, that nests in the upper layers of the growth-bands. There are things like insects, but they are hard to see and not numerous. Whatever is here is here because it can use the plant matter directly, or because it can hide in it. There are no mammals. There are no large things. The valley belongs to the plants, and what is not plant is incidental.

There is a slight wrongness to the place, though it takes a while to name. The plants, when you look closely at them, are mosses and lichens and low herbaceous things, but they are not arranged the way such plants arrange themselves in other places. The bands are too regular. The growth waves are too coherent. The way the dead matter collapses into black pools that don't drain is too consistent. It feels as if the whole valley is a single organism, or a single process, and what you are calling plants are just the visible parts of it — the leaves of a tree whose trunk is the valley itself. The place has a coherence that living things are not supposed to have. It is the slight wrongness of something that has been working at its task for so long that it has worn a groove in the world, and now runs in that groove without deviation.

The cold will not let you stay long at the rim. The place gives you what it has — the green-black expanse, the slow advance of the bands, the absolute quiet, the sense of a process so old and so efficient that it has become its own kind of machinery — and then the chill of the rim sinks in and you have to move. Behind you, the valley continues. It doesn't notice you were there. It is doing what it has always done. The conveyor belts of growth and rot keep advancing. The water keeps cycling. The cold keeps the silence in place. The place is not for you. It is for itself.
