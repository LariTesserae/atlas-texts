The cirque holds everything in its bowl.

You arrive — if you arrive — through a narrow cut between two ridges of fresh granite, the stone still pale and splintered at its edges where some force sheared it clean only recently, geologically speaking. The ridges have not yet softened. Rain has not yet rounded them. They stand like knife backs, dark with moisture but still sharp, still crisp in the way that newly-broken things are crisp. The cirque behind them is small — you could shout across it and be heard on the far wall, and the far wall would shout back in the language of wet stone. The walls themselves are cathedral-tall, three of them in a horseshoe, the fourth open only as a gap where you came in, and where the sky pours down.

The sky here is not the source of light. There is sky, true — a pale disc above, sometimes almost white, sometimes almost grey, sometimes almost the color of skimmed milk — but the light in the cirque seems to rise from everywhere at once. It is dim. It does not cast hard shadows. It simply *is*, the way air is, the way damp is. You cannot tell where one illumination ends and another begins. The granite walls are visible in detail but do not glitter. The moss on them — and there is moss on them, every surface, every crack, every overhang — has a green so dark it is almost black, and in the dim light the green reads as a kind of shadow itself, a positive shadow, a darkness that grows.

Water comes from everywhere. That is the first thing. Not in flood, not in torrent, but in *countless small offerings*. From each crack in each wall, from under each overhang, from between each block of talus at the base, a thread of water descends or wells or slips. Some are threads of silk. Some are the width of a finger. Some are wider than your spread hand and pour in slow curtains. Where the walls meet the floor, the water has built terraces of travertine and peat and living moss, and over these it spreads in a thin film that catches the sourceless light and returns it as a kind of gleam. The floor is a marsh but not a stagnant marsh — it is moving, gently, everywhere, every molecule of it on its way downward to some unseen throat in the cirque's center.

The throat is there. A round dark pool, almost perfectly circular, perhaps ten meters across, set in the lowest part of the bowl. The water enters it from all sides and does not, so far as you can ever tell, leave it. The pool is still. Not glassy — there is no wind to ruffle it, and the surface has a faint pulse, slow, regular, like the surface of something breathing. If you watch long enough you can see the pulse. If you press your hand into the water above the pool, you can feel it — a vibration, deep, almost at the edge of perception, that travels up through your bones and hums there. The pool is not cold. It is not warm. It is exactly the temperature of the air, and the air is exactly the temperature of the water, and the rock is exactly the temperature of both, and this is one of the first things that tells you something is wrong with the place, or right with it, or different.

The trees are few. Twenty, maybe twenty-five, scattered across the floor and on the lower slopes of the walls, each alone or in clusters of two or three. They are old, though the cirque is young. They are old in the way of things that have grown very slowly for a long time — stunted, gnarled, low, with trunks twisted into shapes that suggest deliberation. Their bark is furred with moss a foot thick in places. From their branches hang mosses of other kinds, lichens, ferns, and small orchids that have no business flowering where they flower but do. Each tree is a world. Between the trees is open ground — wet peat, low sedges, the slow water moving through. The trees are spaced as if placed, with intention, as if the cirque were a grove someone had arranged — and yet no one arranged them, and the eye refuses to believe this, and keeps returning to them.

The fauna is the second thing.

The creatures do not appear all at once. They appear in pieces. A call from somewhere in the upper wall — a long downward slur, musical, repeated — and you look and there is nothing. Then a movement in a tree — a slow swing of a moss-laden branch — and you look and there is nothing. Then a shape on a rock — a still thing, watching — and you look and it is gone. Then, slowly, you realize the cirque is full. They are simply everywhere and nowhere. They are quiet when you move and loud when you stop. Their calls cross the bowl and answer each other in patterns that feel almost, but never quite, like language. They are wet, all of them. Their bodies seem to share the temperature of the water. Their eyes, when you catch one, are very dark and very calm.

The drone is the third thing, and once you hear it you cannot unhear it.

It comes from everywhere. From the walls, from the floor, from the pool, from the trees, from the creatures, from your own chest. It is a low frequency, almost subsonic, felt more than heard, and layered over it are higher tones — the constant hiss of water, the deeper pulse of the pool, the chime and clatter of water on travertine, the calls of the creatures, and underneath all of it a hum, a steady unvarying mechanical hum, like a great engine running at its design speed forever. The cirque is loud. It has always been loud. It will always be loud. The loudness does not change. The pulse does not quicken or slow. The hum does not rise or fall. The creatures call but the pattern of their calling is the same. The water falls but the rate of its falling is the same. The pool breathes but its breath is the same breath it has breathed since before you arrived and will breathe after you leave, if you leave, which you may not, because the narrow cut between the ridges is no longer where you remember it being.

The cirque is stable. This is the most unsettling thing.

Nothing here is in transition. Nothing is decaying. Nothing is being born. The water that falls is not new water — it is the same water, returning, having always returned. The moss does not grow. It does not die. It is the length it is. The creatures do not age. The trees do not thicken. The pool's pulse is the pulse it has always pulsed. The hum is the hum it has always hummed. The cirque is a closed system, sealed in its own time, repeating itself with the precision of a flywheel, and yet it is not a machine, and yet it behaves like a machine, and you cannot find the boundary between the two.

Look at the far wall. There is a pattern in the granite. Dark lines, regular, running across the face of the stone. They could be natural — they could be the way the rock fractured, the way the water has traced its paths over centuries. But they are too regular. They run parallel for meters and then turn at angles that are not the angles of cracks. They meet at junctions. They form, if you trace them with your eye, a pattern that resembles — but is not — a circuit. You cannot decide whether you are seeing this or imagining it. Other visitors — if there have been other visitors, if there could be other visitors — would not agree on what the lines mean.

Look at the trees. Each one, if you approach it carefully and wait, will eventually show you that its trunk is not entirely wood. There are seams in it. Ridges. The texture changes near the base, becomes smoother, almost metallic, almost warm. If you were to press your ear to one of these ridges you would hear the hum more clearly, and you would notice that the hum has a rhythm to it, a slow tick or throb that syncs with the pool, with the creatures' calls, with your own pulse if you let it. The trees are not machines. They are trees. They grow from the peat and drink the water and bear, in season — but is there season here? — small pale flowers that smell of nothing and attract the creatures. And yet. And yet. The seams.

Look at the creatures. They are not quite animals. They move like animals. They feed like animals. They have young, perhaps, though you have never seen a young one — only adults, always adults, always the same adults, watching with the same calm dark eyes. Their bodies are warm and wet and their hearts beat and their breath comes. But they do not startle. They do not flee. They watch you the way an engineer watches a gauge — with attention, without alarm. When they call, the calls are not random. When they move, the movements are not random. The cirque is full of small coordinated behaviors, all of them cycling, all of them the same, all of them contributing to the hum, to the pulse, to the steady state of the place. You cannot tell if they are maintaining the cirque or being maintained by it. You cannot tell if the trees are part of the system or the system is part of the trees. You cannot tell if the pool is the heart of the engine or the engine is the heart of the pool.

The light does not change.

It is dim when you arrive and dim when you stay and dim if you stay for what feels like a long time. There is no day here. There is no night. There is only the sourceless gleam on the water and the moss and the granite and the slow pulse of the pool. Your body insists on a day. Your body insists on a night. Your body begins to disagree with itself. You sleep, perhaps, or you do not sleep, or you enter a state that resembles both. The creatures call in the same pattern whether your eyes are open or closed. The water falls in the same pattern. The hum does not pause for you.

The narrow cut between the ridges is still there. You can see it from where you stand, across the bowl, a dark V in the bright rim of the sky. It is exactly where it was. It is not exactly where it was. The proportions of the cirque feel different now — the walls feel closer, or the floor feels smaller, or the pool has widened, or the trees have moved an inch closer together. You cannot prove any of this. The place is stable. Nothing is changing. And yet you are not where you were, and the cirque is not what it was, and neither of you has moved.

You begin to suspect that the cirque is not a place at all.

You begin to suspect that the cirque is an event, repeating. You begin to suspect that the water falling is the same water falling, that the creatures calling are the same creatures calling, that the hum is the hum of the event itself, the sound of something that was set in motion a long time ago and has been repeating ever since with no input and no output, sealed against time, sealed against change, sealed against you. You begin to suspect that the lines on the granite are not lines on the granite but a description of what the cirque is doing to itself, written in a language that is also stone, in a medium that is also a place.

You begin to suspect, finally, that the cirque is running.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. *Running*. As an engine runs. As a heart runs. As a system runs, in the engineering sense, with its components cycling, with its outputs balanced by its inputs, with no waste, no loss, no entropy. The water cycles. The creatures cycle. The trees cycle. The hum cycles. The pool pulses. The moss breathes — does moss breathe? it does here. Everything is a part. Everything is in phase. The cirque has achieved a state that should not be achievable in a place like this, a place of wet and cool and stone and growth, and it has achieved it anyway, and it holds it, and it will hold it.

And it does not care that you are here.

The creatures pass you without curiosity. The water runs over your feet without pause. The hum passes through you without acknowledgment. You are in the system. You have always been in the system. You are a feature of the stable state — a small perturbation, a slight asymmetry, a thing that the system has absorbed and incorporated and will continue to incorporate, indefinitely, in the dim sourceless light, in the steady loud drone, in the cool wet air of the cirque that does not change.
