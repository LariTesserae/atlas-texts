The first thing to understand is that you are inside something, though whether you are inside a building or inside an organism is the question the place keeps asking, and the place keeps answering both.

The air is wet. Not raining — there is no sky here — but wet, the kind of wet that has weight. It hangs in long gray veils between the verticals, condenses on every surface, drips in slow metronome from the high places into the low. The temperature is cool the way cellars are cool, the way the inside of a throat is cool. You can taste iron in the moisture, and something sweeter underneath, vegetal, like the smell of a cut stem held too long to the face.

The verticals are stalks. That's the closest word. They rise from a floor you can't quite see — a floor of old brick, perhaps, or old brick becoming something else — and they go up into a darkness that has texture. They are dark. They are fibrous. They are packed tight enough in some places to read as wall, sparse enough in others to read as grove. Between the nearest stalk and the next there might be three feet of empty air. That emptiness is part of the architecture. That emptiness is a kind of room.

There is metal here. Rusted pipes follow illogical trajectories through the stalks — sometimes disappearing into the mass of them, sometimes protruding like exposed ribs. There are stacked forms that might be kilns or might be chimneys or might be the negative space of kilns and chimneys, hollow brick shapes with throats at the top that exhale the damp air in slow periodic breaths. You feel the breath more than hear it. A shift in pressure. A tide in the stillness. The whole cathedral inhales and the condensation trembles on every surface. The whole cathedral exhales and a fine rain begins to fall only in the upper volumes, where it accumulates on the underside of the tallest stalks until the drops are fat enough to fall, and then they fall into the standing water in the troughs and channels below.

The troughs and channels. That is the second clue that there was a settlement here, or is one. Brick-lined channels run along what was once a floor, still carrying slow dark water toward some lower point you can't see. There are platforms. There are the remains of staircases. There are doorways that open onto more stalks, more dark, more of the same improbable interior. The architecture is layered — built and built upon, then grown into, then built upon again. Industrial in its bones. Brick and iron and rivet. But the rivets are soft now. The brick is soft. The iron is being slowly translated into something green-black and fibrous, something that pulses if you watch long enough.

Light is scarce. What light there is seems to come up from below — a faint greenish phosphorescence in the standing water, in the slick on the brick, in the eyes of things that move. Bioluminescent. Or chemiluminescent. Or some other luminescent that doesn't have a name because the chemistry isn't chemistry anymore. The light is enough to see by if you let your eyes adjust for an hour. It is not enough to feel safe by.

The fauna. You hear them before you see them, because they are quiet, because the place is mostly quiet, because the silence here has a pulse and the fauna live inside the pulse and have learned to move with it. A small wet click from somewhere in the stalks. A long sliding sound, like fabric being dragged over wet stone, but there is no fabric and there is no stone. Something with too many legs — or with legs that aren't quite legs, that ripple rather than step — crossing a brick channel. Something larger higher up, a slow heavy unfolding, a door opening where there is no door. A small darting luminescence hunted by a slow heavy darkness. The hunt takes perhaps four seconds. There is a small wet scream. Then quiet again. Then the pulse of the breath resumes, as if nothing happened. The place is active. The place is a working ecology. The place just doesn't care that you can hear it working.

And here is the breakdown.

The stalks are not separate plants. When you look closely — when the phosphorescence is right, when your eyes have given up on the idea of normal scale — you can see it. The stalks share a circulation. There is a darker fluid moving through translucent vessels in their walls, a slow pulse that is the same pulse as the breath of the place. They share a skin. Where two stalks press together they have fused, become one wall of one body. The whole vertical forest is one organism, or one colony so integrated that the distinction has lost its meaning. A single dark fibrous cathedral-body that is still in the process of becoming itself.

The brick is not brick. The brick is scab. The iron is bone. The pipes are veins. The kilns are hearts, or hearts are kilns, the distinction has gone soft. The whole settlement — the layered urban infrastructure, the industrial bones of engines and stacks and riveted plate — was never a settlement at all. It was scaffold. It was the chrysalis. It was the thing the body grew inside of while it was becoming. Or the body dreamed itself out of the settlement, the way a forest dreams itself out of a fallen log, the way a city dreams itself out of the bones of the city that came before. Either reading holds. Both readings are true at the same time. That is part of the breakdown.

The place is not finished. The erosion is going the wrong direction — things are forming, not wearing away. Stalks are still thickening. Bricks are still being absorbed. The fauna are still being invented, generation by generation, in the bodies of their mothers, new morphologies emerging to fill new niches in the body of the body. The chimneys exhale their slow breaths. The water accumulates and falls. The phosphorescence brightens slightly when a fauna dies, then dims as its chemistry is reclaimed. The place is becoming. It has perhaps been becoming for a long time. Or it became all at once, just now, the moment you stepped into it. There is no way to know. Time here is not what time is out there, where out there is a thing you can be sure of.

What you can be sure of:

It is cool.
It is damp.
It is dark.
It is quiet, except for the breath, and the drip, and the small wet sounds of things eating each other.
It is small. Cathedral-scale. Grove-scale. A hamlet of a place, with walls you cannot see the top of and a floor you cannot quite trust.
It is empty in the way that cathedrals are empty — vast hollow volumes with the presences few and far between and the spaces between them enormous.
It is dense in the way that forests are dense — every surface alive, every cubic foot a working negotiation between organism and substrate.
It is built. It is industrial. The grammar of its construction is rivet and stack and channel and the patient accretion of layered infrastructure.
It is real, except where it isn't, and the where-it-isn't is widening.

You are inside something. The something is becoming. The something is not yet finished. The something breathes. The something will, when it is ready, do something. You do not know what. The something does not know what. The something is still inventing the verb.

You are very quiet. The thing is mostly quiet too. The thing notices you, perhaps, in the slow blind way that bodies notice a splinter. The thing is not yet sure what to do about you.

The drip falls. The phosphorescence pulses. A stalk somewhere in the dark lengthens by a quarter inch. The breath of the place continues.

This is where you are.
