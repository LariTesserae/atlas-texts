# The Hush at Beryl Bend

The place fits in a cupped hand.

A low dome of black basalt rises from the marsh, perhaps forty meters across, no taller than a chest. Fresh rock — sharp-edged, the fractures still bright where nothing has yet had time to weather them dull. You can read the place where it was born: a seam of upthrust stone, cooled too fast, full of glassy inclusions that catch the light and wink like flies' eyes. Below, the water table is uncomfortably close. The dome is an island in everything but name, though the name is the only thing it lacks.

Heat. The air shimmers at the rock's surface even in the open shade, and the few clouds that pass overhead seem to slow down and lose substance as they cross. Condensation gathers in the deeper fissures and runs in slow, patient threads down the stone, never quite dripping, just glazing each face a darker grey before evaporating somewhere partway down. The rock *weeps*. It is the loudest thing, and it is not loud at all. It is the constant companion.

The marsh wraps the dome in a quilt of browns. Reeds, the only concession to vegetation, stand in ragged tufts, spaced wide enough that you can see the cracked crust of pale mud between them. Sparse is the right word — this is not a hostile absence, only a thin and brittle presence. A heron's feather caught on a reed, the bleached curve of a fish skull, a thousand tiny failures of growth that read as the land being tired rather than dead. The smell is warm, vegetal, slightly sweet: standing water in the process of becoming more interesting than it was.

At the dome's center stands a single engine.

It is the only building. A squat brick housing, knee-high, with a rusted iron cap and a thin steel chimney that points up like a finger. Pipework — three runs of it, the size of your wrist — comes out of the housing and dives straight down into the rock, sealed where it meets the stone with a flange of lead that has gone powdery and grey-green with verdigris and lichen, both. The pipes do not, from this distance, appear to be doing anything. The chimney does not smoke. There is no obvious power source. The whole assembly has the quality of a thing that has been *here* longer than anything else has, and is not in any hurry.

But listen.

A sound, low and patient, comes up through the rock. You feel it in your ankles before you hear it. A breath, a sigh, a hum. The pipes throb. Somewhere under the basalt, somewhere the boreholes must have gone, something is breathing, and the air inside the pipes is being moved — in, out, in, out — and that is the heartbeat of the place. It is loud the way a heart is loud: not in your ears but in the whole field of your attention. It rises and falls, and the surface of the marsh answers it, the water level seeming to lift and resettle in sympathy. You could time your breath to it. You find yourself trying.

A concrete path, cracked into three wandering slabs, runs from the dome's lower flank down to the water. It was once a proper approach, but the marsh has been eating it back for a long time, patient, slow, and the slabs end one by one in tilted wade-depths. At the foot of the path, a cast-iron mooring ring sticks up out of the water on a stub of post, furred with algae, holding nothing. Beside it, a sign: enamel, white, the black letters still sharp but the surface a constellation of rust blossoms. **BERYL BEND BORE — INSTRUMENT HOUSING 4 — DO NOT INTERFERE WITH VENT.** A second line, smaller: *Schedule subject to review at Main. Inquiries to 14, Subbasin Office, by appointment only.* A third line, hand-painted, the paint a different blue and clearly older: *its listening don't shout near the pipes* — no period, no signature.

There is no wind. There are no birds. There are no fish breaking the surface, no insects, no little scuttlings at the margin. The dome and the marsh hold no fauna. You notice this after a few minutes, and then you cannot stop noticing it. The only motion in the world is the breathing, the weeping, the slow swing of the light as the sun crosses a sky that is clouding and unclouding in long, indecisive drifts. The sound fills the empty slots where animal presence would be, and is not the same. The sound is older. The sound will be here when you are not.

A strange thing happens, on the second hour.

The weeping on the eastern face of the dome thickens. The water does not run faster — it has no hurry — but it runs *more of it*, a dozen threads becoming twenty, and where the threads cross the old fracture lines they turn briefly bright with something dissolved in them, a faint copper-green, the color of the verdigris on the lead. The pipes, at the moment the weeping thickens, change their song: a quarter-tone higher, then back, then a half-tone lower, then back, the way a sleeper shifts in dreaming. The mooring ring's stub tilts, very slightly, in its mud. The reeds nearest the dome lean, very slightly, *away*.

You are not, you understand, being threatened. You are being *recorded*. The instrument housing, the bore, the four redundant housings you have not been shown and will not be shown, are listening to something, and the thing they are listening to is the rock and the water and the heat and the slow negotiation between them, and you are a perturbation. A small one. A noted one. Somewhere, on a paper that has not been written yet, a small angular deflection has begun to be recorded. The pipes will hum a little differently for the next several hours, accounting for you.

The place holds you gently and pays you no further attention.

---

The light is the kind of light that has not decided to be anything yet: the mid-morning of a place that does not really have mornings, only a long brightening and a long dimming and a long dim brightness between. Your shadow on the rock is sharp but short. The brick of the engine housing is the same temperature as the basalt by a margin you cannot feel with your hand — only by lifting, putting your palm down, and then, after a moment, not being able to tell which surface is warmer. The heat of the rock is not a heat you suffer; it is a heat that surrounds you, a heat that is *you* if you stay long enough.

You could leave, on the broken path, wading back toward wherever the marsh thins into firmer ground. You could stay. If you stay, the place will continue not to mind. The pipes will throb. The weeping will wax. The heron's feather will not move. The sign will remain unilluminated in the bright indifferent light.

You are at Beryl Bend. Nothing has happened for a long time. Nothing is about to happen. The instrument is patient. You should be too.
