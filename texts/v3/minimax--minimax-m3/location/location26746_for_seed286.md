# The Long Water

The place is a water. Not water-in-a-place — the place itself is water, all the way down and all the way up, and what isn't water is plant, and what isn't plant is more water. There is no shore. There has not been a shore for longer than anything living here remembers.

It sits high. The floor is a plateau — a tabletop of stone lifted into the cold upper air of the world — and the ocean has climbed up and over it and has never gone back. The depth here is measured in the time it takes sound to fall. Light arrives from the surface in diminishing green installments, becoming bluer, becoming darker, becoming a kind of dusk that never becomes night because the sun cannot fully surrender and the plants will not let it.

The temperature is a held breath. The water does not warm, does not chill. It is the temperature of having been the same temperature for so long that the question has lost its meaning.

---

Across this depth, sparse as gods, stand the organisms.

Each one is enormous. Each one is alone for a great distance around itself, and then there is another, and then another, and the spaces between them are tens of kilometers of quiet water where small things dart and drift. The plants are the architecture of the place, but they do not function as walls or floors or ceilings. They function as weather.

A single one of them — call it a stem, though the word is inadequate — rises from the plateau floor and ascends the entire water column. Its base is gnarled and sedimentary, layered in calcium and iron excreted over unthinkably long periods, anchored by its own weight into the bedrock. Its mid-section is a column of living tissue the diameter of a village, sheathed in bark-like cuticle the color of wet slate. Its upper reaches fan into a crown of fronds that brush the underside of the surface, and at the surface they unfurl into broad sails that ride the windless air above.

There are perhaps a thousand of these in the entire basin. They are spaced so that you could fly a season between them and never see another.

---

The sound is constant and the sound is loud.

It is not the sound of waves. There is no surf, no fetch, no coastline. It is the sound of plant metabolism at scale. Each stem, in its long life, has accumulated within itself a system of internal cavities and tubes — a kind of biological plumbing, evolved or simply grown, that moves water and dissolved gasses up and down its length in continuous slow cycles. The water rises in the stem, passes through membrane after membrane, releases oxygen at the crown, descends loaded with carbon, is reabsorbed, rises again. The valves of this plumbing — fleshy, calcified, older than some mountains — open and close on a rhythm so slow it is below the threshold of perception, but the cumulative sound of ten thousand valves operating across a thousand stems is a low industrial hum. A rumble. A bass note that you feel in the chest before you hear it with the ears.

If you press your ear to the stem itself, the sound resolves. Click. Click. Click. Like a telegraph. Like a mill. Like the heart of something that has been beating since before there was anyone to count beats.

There are higher sounds too. The fronds at the surface creak against one another in the slow lateral push of planetary rotation. Small fauna — and there is a great deal of small fauna, despite the sparseness, because each stem is an entire world — chirp and click and grind against the bark in the interstices. A fish-school will occasionally pass, and the water heaves slightly, and a stem will groan in adjustment. But these are punctuations. The base note is the hum. The base note never stops. The base note has never stopped.

---

The ecology is industrial in the oldest sense of the word: *diligent, organized, scaled.* Each stem is, in its patient way, a factory. It takes in water at the floor — water that is saturated with dissolved minerals from the rock and with organic matter drifting down from above — and it lifts this material, ton by ton, century by century, through its body. It incorporates what it needs into its own growing structure. It releases the rest at the crown as oxygen and as a fine exudate, a kind of organic snow that drifts back down through the water column, feeding the smaller things, the grazers, the filterers, the things that live in the bark. The stem is a lift. The stem is a chimney. The stem is a mine. It has been doing this for so long that its own mass, its accumulated layers, have begun to resemble the geological strata of the plateau it stands on. It is becoming stone. It is becoming the next floor.

Around each stem, a halo of life. Algal films on the lower bark. Mollusks cemented into crevices. Worms that have bored long meandering tunnels through the outer layers and feed on the tissue inside, pruning without killing. Small fish that hover in the lee of the fronds. Crustaceans that ride the exudate plume upward each morning and ride it down again at dusk. A whole vertical city, narrow but dense, climbing the stem like ivy climbs a wall. None of these creatures are large. All of them are abundant. The stem feeds them and they prune the stem and the arrangement has not changed since before the arrangement began.

---

There is no built thing. No structure. No artifact.

There are no structures because the plants are the structure and the water is the structure and the plateau is the structure and any human notion of *making* would be redundant here, would be a splinter in a hand that is already whole. There are no walls because there is no inside. There are no roofs because the surface is not a ceiling but a distant event, an unreachable place where light is made. If a person were to bring a hammer and a nail and a board into this place and attempt to build something, the board would simply become part of the system. The nail would rust. The hammer would be colonized by algae and barnacles and would eventually sink, and the stem would not notice, because the stem has not noticed anything in a very long time.

---

The light is ordinary. That is the strangest part.

It comes from above through clear cold water in long green shafts, and where it touches the bark of a stem it makes a moving oval of gold that swings gently with the surface chop. The fronds at the surface, when seen from below, are silhouettes against a sky that has clouds, because the basin is large enough to have weather. Distant rain falls on distant water and you cannot hear it. The light is the light of any afternoon, anywhere, on any temperate sea. It is the only ordinary thing in a place that otherwise has none.

---

The temperature, the sound, the light, the chemical composition of the water, the position of the fronds, the spacing of the stems, the direction of the slow currents, the behavior of the fauna — none of it varies. None of it has varied, in any measurable way, for longer than measurements have existed. There is no day and no night because the rotation is too small to notice and the light penetrates to the floor anyway, and there is no season because there has been no reason for one to begin. The system is balanced. The system is balanced the way a stone is balanced on a stone on a stone on a flat place: not because anything is holding it, but because nothing has yet disturbed it.

If you sit on the plateau floor in the lee of a stem and close your eyes, you hear only the hum. You feel the slight warmth of the rock that has absorbed the day's light and is now, very slowly, releasing it. A small fish, luminescent in the dark water, drifts past at the edge of vision. The hum goes on. The hum has always gone on. The hum will go on.

This is the place. It is vast. It is sparse. It is loud. It is still. It is the kind of place that makes you wonder whether *stillness* and *loudness* are, after all, opposites — or whether they are two names for the same condition, which is: *something that has been going on so long it has become a kind of quiet.*
