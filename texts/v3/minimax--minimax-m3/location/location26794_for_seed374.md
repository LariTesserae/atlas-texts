# The Iron Reaches

The basalt comes up black and sweating.

This is what they call the Reaches now, though no one who lives here remembers when the naming happened. The stone is young—still warm in places where the crust hasn't finished cooling—and it stretches out flat and gleaming in every direction, broken only by the slow movements of water and the vertical insistence of the buildings the smiths have raised upon it.

## The Ground

Walk out from the settlement and the rock is slick underfoot. Condensation hangs in the air like the breath of cattle, droplets forming on every surface that isn't hot enough to evaporate them. Steam wisps from fissures in the basalt, sometimes thick enough to hide your feet, sometimes just a shimmer. The stone is dark grey when dry, near-black when wet, and in the dim light it gives back no clear reflection—only a sense of your own shape standing in the surface, as if the ground were a tarnished mirror.

There are pools. Not proper water yet—the rain is too new for that, and the rivers haven't found their routes—but everywhere a slight depression has formed, the runoff collects and sits, warm, faintly mineral, tasting of iron and sulfur if you're foolish enough to kneel and drink. The moss has not yet arrived. The lichen is only beginning—pale orange stains, no bigger than a hand, crusting the rock in sheltered corners. In another thousand years this place will be green, but right now it is the world just out of the oven.

The basalt breaks in cooling columns, hexagonal pillars leaning at impossible angles where the contraction went wrong, and these are the first things the builders use.

## The Settlement

It rises in clusters, low and wide. No one builds tall here—the footing is uncertain, the rock still settling, and tall things lean within a generation. So the structures are squat: forge-houses with walls three feet thick, sleeping-platforms cut into columns of stone, covered walkways roofed with hammered copper that has gone already to verdigris.

The smiths are everywhere. They are a dense people, crowded onto this young rock because the rock is what they need—iron-bearing basalt, copper in the veins, tin in pockets they have only begun to find. A forge-settlement, called for by the geology. Three thousand souls in a place that will, in time, support thirty. For now the density is enormous. They have built on top of one another in places, second-floor platforms resting on first-floor roofs, ladders propped against every vertical surface. Smoke from the forges never quite clears. It hangs among the buildings in grey-blue layers, and the smell of it—charcoal, hot iron, the sweet tang of flux—is the smell of the whole Reaches.

## The Sound

You cannot hear yourself think.

The hammering does not stop. It begins again before the last ring has faded. Somewhere there are always two smiths striking in alternating rhythm, and somewhere else always three more. The bellows breathe. The anvils answer. Children grow up here and never learn what silence is, only what it means when the wind covers the hammering for a moment before the next wave of it rises.

At night it does not quiet. The forges must keep their heat, the night-shifts take their turns. The sound is a physical presence, a pressure on the eardrums that newcomers suffer for weeks before they stop noticing. The birds—if they are birds, though no one in the Reaches has ever seen a bird—call in counterpoint, some metallic piping sound that fits oddly well with the ringing of the metal.

## The Water-Folk

In the warm pools, things move.

The smiths call them the crawlers, and they are abundant—thick-bodied, limbless things the color of the basalt, a hand's length long, that surface and breathe and sink again. They taste, when boiled, of iron and iodine, and they are the only protein the settlement can easily get. Children catch them in copper pans. Dogs hunt them. A dozen species live in the cooler margins—things with too many legs, things with shells of crystallized mineral, tiny drifting threads that stain the water rust-orange.

There are no mammals. No reptiles. Only the things that have evolved to live where the rock is still warm and the water is full of dissolved metal. They are dense as the settlement is dense, each pool a small city of writhing life. At evening the smiths stand at the edges of the larger pools and watch them move, and the watching is a kind of rest.

## The Light

It is dim here, always. The sky is overcast—the steam from the rock condenses into low cloud that never lifts more than a hundred yards above the basalt plain, so the sun is a smudge at midday and the stars are rumors at night. The light is grey-orange in the mornings where the cloud catches the first glow, grey-blue at noon, deep and close in the evenings when every forge-slag and cooking-fire blooms in the murk. Eyes adapt. The smiths see well enough in this perpetual dusk. Visitors stumble.

In the forges themselves the light is brighter, sharper—the orange-yellow of worked iron, the white-blue of the hottest coals, sparks rising like brief stars into the low ceiling of cloud. The whole settlement, seen from a distance, must glow.

## The Feel of the Place

There is no wind that registers. There is no weather in the usual sense—the sky does not change, the rain falls in fine mists that drift sideways for a day and then stop, the temperature holds warm and slightly humid year-round. The basalt continues to cool, fractionally, somewhere beneath the feet. In a hundred years the steam will be less. In a thousand the moss will be a carpet. In ten thousand the rivers will have carved valleys and the smiths will be somewhere else.

But now, in this moment, the Reaches are young and crowded and loud and wet and dim and absolutely still in their deep geology. Everything human about them—the hammering, the smoke, the density of lives pressed onto young stone—is balanced on the surface of a world that is still becoming itself.

The smiths know this. They build low. They build temporary. They hammer, and the place answers back, and the crawlers surface in the warm pools, and the sound goes on and on without cease.
