# The Vent

---

You come up through the crack sideways. The crack is not a path. It is a misunderstanding in the rock — a place where the mountain failed to commit to being one thing, and so became a place where two things could meet. The stone on either side is sharp, young, the kind of edge that has only just remembered it exists. You tear your sleeve against it. The cloth comes away damp. The dampness is hot.

The pocket beyond is not large. You could lie across it. You could not stand in it. The ceiling is low and the floor is a shallow basin of something that is not stone and not water but a slow negotiation between them — a skin of mineral that has been weeping for a long time and has forgotten how to stop. Condensation runs in threads down the walls. The walls themselves are not quite stable. They are too new. They are ringed with growths that could be crystals or could be something else — branching, hair-fine, faintly catching the light that doesn't seem to have a source. The light is normal. The light is ordinary. The light has nothing to confess.

The air is thick enough to feel with your mouth. Hot. Wet. Taste of iron. Taste of something older than iron — a mineral that has no name in any language you would use to ask for water. You breathe and the breath seems to fall back on you.

The first thing you notice, after the heat and the wet, is that there are no plants. There should be. The conditions are not the conditions of a place that refuses plants. There is water. There is warmth. There is a kind of light. And yet nothing grows that knows it is growing. The branching things on the walls do not know they are branching. The mineral skin on the floor does not know it is a skin. There are thin pale fans of something against the wetter rocks that might be fungal, might be lichen, might be the way stone dreams when it has been left alone long enough — but if they are alive they are alive in a language that doesn't have a word for alive.

The second thing you notice is that there is no sound.

There is the faint ticking of condensation gathering into a drop heavy enough to fall. There is the soft, near-silent glide of water moving down an incline slower than you can watch. There is, when you put your ear to the rock, a deep hum that could be the mountain breathing or could be your own pulse played back at you from a different angle. But the absence of sound is so total, so dense, that you become aware of your own joints as if they were a disturbance.

The third thing you notice is that you are not alone.

They are everywhere. There is no inch of wall, no curl of mineral, no thread of standing water that does not contain one. They are small. Most of them are smaller than your smallest fingernail. A few are larger — the size of a closed eye, the size of a stopped heart. They are packed so densely that the surfaces of the pocket seem to have a texture of their own, a moving grain, a slow shimmer that is not quite shimmering. They are silent. Some of them are shaped like insects. Some of them are shaped like the ideas of insects — a leg that is also a vein, a wing that is also a crack, a body that is also a fold in the rock. Some of them are not shaped at all. They occupy shape. They are the color of milk, of bone, of wet ash, of a thing seen too long.

They do not move when you move. They do not flee. They do not approach. They go on with whatever they are doing — and there are too many of them going on with too many things for you to follow. You glimpse one, and the next moment it is somewhere else, and you are not sure it moved or you did. You see a pair of them locked together in a way that could be feeding or could be courtship or could be a third thing that the place has invented because it could. You see a long pale shape that has no limbs and too many and seems to be made of the smaller ones, the way a river is made of its own water. You reach a finger toward one and it does not flinch. You touch it. It is cool. It is the only cool thing in the pocket. You withdraw your hand and the place where you touched it is smooth, and you are not sure if the creature was there before you touched it.

There is no building. There is no tool. There is no sign that anything with hands has ever been here — and yet, in a crack along the floor, you find a sliver of obsidian. It has been knapped. The edge is deliberate. The shape is not made by any hand you would recognize. It is too small for a human hand. It is the right size for something that has only just learned what an edge is. Beside it, in the wet, there is a mark in the mineral skin — a spiral, a half-circle, a series of dots that could be a notation of something that happened here, or a warning, or a song. You cannot tell. You are not the one it was made for.

The pocket is not the whole world. You can feel, beyond the crack you came through, the larger shape of the mountain — the cold, the wind, the thin air of a place this high. But the pocket is its own place. The rules are different here. The heat is not the heat of a sun. It is the heat of something beneath the skin of the rock, something that has been heating this small space for a long time and will continue to heat it after the mountain has forgotten its name. The water is not the water of a sky. It is the water of the deep, brought up through cracks, tasting of metal and memory. The creatures are not the creatures of any ecology you have studied. They are the creatures of a place where biology has been left to improvise with too much heat and too little time and not quite enough reality to fully commit to being alive.

You stay longer than you mean to. The wet heat softens the edges of things. The creatures move around you without acknowledging you. The mark in the floor catches the light and then doesn't. The obsidian sliver sits in its crack, patient, waiting for whatever it is waiting for. You realize, at some point, that you cannot remember exactly how you got here. The crack is behind you. The crack is ahead of you. The pocket is the same size it has always been. The creatures are the same number, which is too many to count. The spiral is older than you are. The mineral skin is younger than your breath.

You will leave. You will climb back through the crack. You will emerge into cold air and thin light and the world will be the world again, and you will tell someone about the place, or you will not, and either way the place will go on. The heat will continue. The water will continue to weep. The creatures will continue their long, silent negotiation with the idea of being. The obsidian sliver will wait. The spiral will mean what it means, to whatever it is.

The mountain is new. The pocket is older than the mountain. You are not sure how this is possible. You are not sure you need to know.

You climb back through the crack. You tear your sleeve again. The dampness is hot.
