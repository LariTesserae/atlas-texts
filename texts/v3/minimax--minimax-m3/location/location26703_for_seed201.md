The High Bowl

A small cirque, then — a closed amphitheater cut into the upper country, two hundred paces across at its widest. The walls rise sudden and pale. The rock is fresh-looking: light granite, sharp-cornered, the kind of stone that has not yet learned to round itself, the kind that remembers ice only just let go of it. It throws back the sky. From the floor, looking up, the rim is a jagged horizon and beyond it the blue is the blue of the upper air — thin, hard, almost violet at the zenith. The bowl is roofless. The bowl is also, somehow, a room.

The floor of the bowl is not what the rock would predict. Below the cliffs the ground is green. Tight alpine grass, sedge, moss deep as a fingertip where the seeps run, the humped and pin-cushion shapes of plants that have agreed to grow low and not be moved. Dwarf willow creeps at the edges. There are flowers here that should not be at this altitude — small pale orchids the color of skimmed milk, low and shy, blooming in the brief season. The grass is springy underfoot and gives a faint sweet smell when crushed. The ground is not wet, exactly. It is damp. It has been damp for as long as anyone would care to remember — held in by the walls, fed by the melt that comes down the cliffs in dark lines on slow summer days, by the rain that arrives in three o'clock slaps and is gone, by the condensation that beads on every stone once the sun passes the rim. The plants have learned to drink what falls and what hangs in the air.

Light here is a thing to be endured. The air is thin and the sun comes through it barely filtered; the pale walls of the cirque catch the light and throw it back again, so the whole bowl at midday is double-lit, every surface giving it off. Shadows are knife-edged and short. Snow patches — small, persistent, in the shadowed lee of the high rim — give the light a third turn. Things in the bowl cast two shadows, one black, one white, and at certain hours the eye has to look away. The light, after a storm, is almost cruel: clean, sharp, rinsed. The grass under it is the green of something newly invented.

The place is not empty. It is only empty of one kind of thing.

Movement in the meadow, all the time, low and continuous. A red fox crosses the far side on its long legs, stopping to listen with one ear up. Pikas — small fat cousin-rabbits — call from among the rocks at the base of the cliff, a sharp single note that travels around the bowl and comes back as a kind of round; the note goes out when something moves. A marmot stands tall on a boulder, dark against pale grass, and whistles once and drops. Two ravens on the rim work the wind without flapping, tipping, tipping, their shadows sweeping the meadow below in long arcs. On the cliffs, where ledges look like they were drawn by a pen, mountain goats move — pale shapes on pale stone, hooves placed with the seriousness of long practice, climbing to where the green is greener. Insects are in the grass. Small burrowing things are under the grass. The place is a web of appetite and evasion, and the eye cannot hold it all.

Scattered, in this abundance, the faint remains of people. A cairn at the western foot of the cliff, knee-high, built of flat stones chosen and stacked with patience. Another, smaller, near a wall. A windbreak — three stones leaned into a triangle, no roof, just a place to put your back against. A shelter, low, rough, of stacked stone without mortar; the roof if it had one is gone, and a small animal lives in it now and does not mind visitors. A fire ring, blackened stones, in the lee of a boulder the size of a room. All hand-work, all hand-carried. No metal here — or only a little. Whoever came, came up on foot, and not often. The grass has grown through the fire ring. The shelter has been claimed by other lives.

The bowl is quieter than the country around it. Wind, when it comes, has to climb the rim and falls into the cirque already tired, so it arrives as a hush, a passing breath, a slow eddy around the floor, and then it is gone. Sounds inside are small and intermittent and far between: the tinkle of meltwater running somewhere down the cliff; the clip and tap of a goat's hoof on stone far above; the pip of the pika; a raven's hollow call turning over; the wind, when it is there; the wind, when it isn't. Long stretches of the day the only sound is the body — its own blood, its own breathing, the small working of its own ears.

The place is busy, though, in its quiet way. The weather moves through it like a wheel. Mornings begin with mist pooled in the bowl, a flat white lake that comes up to your shins and dissolves by the time the sun clears the rim; the grass comes out of it strung with water. Afternoons, the clouds build on the western wall and sometimes a storm comes — hail, a brief horizontal rain, lightning that strikes the cliffs and the sound returns from the opposite wall and meets itself — and then it is gone and the light comes back harder. The snow patches retreat at the rate a man can pace out. The plants grow an inch a week in the season, and the insects come up the mountain to be with them, and the fox hunts them, and the hawk hunts the pika, and the ravens eat what is left. The seasons turn fast here. A week at this altitude is a month in the valley.

The strange thing, the slight thing that is off — and it is slight, only slight, so that you notice it the third day and not the first — is that the meadow is too much. A botanist, if one came, would call it an anomaly: the south-facing bowl, the heat held by the walls, the constant melt, the centuries since the ice retreated — these add up to a place that should not be this soft at this altitude. The plants should be sparse. They are not. The flowers in particular are out of place; they are too lush, too generous, blooming when they should be sleeping. And there is a stone in the middle of the meadow, larger than a person, that does not belong to the cirque's walls. It is darker, denser, fine-grained, and where the weather has worn it, lines are visible — regular, parallel, almost like the edge of a tool. The cairns are built pointing toward it. The shelter has its back to it. The fire ring is between the boulder and the stone, as if the stone were a kind of back wall for what was happening there. The meadow has grown around it and incorporated it, but the stone has not been forgotten. Something is being tended to, here, still.
