A great room at the bottom of warm water. Or rather — a small cathedral, sunken, carved into the top of a mountain that has almost stopped being a mountain.

The mountain is older than the mountains that are visible from shore. It is basalt, or something like basalt — a spine that pushed up out of the abyssal plain in some unimaginably distant time when the tectonics of the seafloor were different. It has been worn down. Centuries, millennia, the patient dissolving work of warm water and mollusk and boring sponge, until what was once a sharp peak is now a low tableland, a hundred meters across, far below the photic zone. The tableland is near-flat. The tableland is at the end of its erosion. It will subside, in time, into the plain below — but slowly.

On this tableland, a room has been built.

The room is what the divers who have been here call the Hall, though none of them has ever spoken aloud in it. The word is used afterward, on boats, in daylight. It is forty meters across, perhaps sixty tall — a single chamber, walled and floored with worked stone. The proportions are unusual. They are the proportions of a temple, or of a tholos, or of some interior that was meant to feel larger than the building containing it. The walls rise. The ceiling lifts. One cannot see the ceiling clearly in the dim, but one feels it there, holding the warm water up.

The walls were not poured. They were fitted. Basalt against basalt, in courses, without mortar. There are bronze-green fishhooks still wedged in some of the joints — not as fasteners, but as markers, as tally-strokes, perhaps, or as offerings. The joints are tight. They have held for an age. The patina on the bronze is the patina of something that has not been polished in a very long time.

There are no windows. There are no doors, exactly — there are openings, low and wide, in the lower walls, through which the warm water moves with its slow currents, and through which larger fauna come and go. The openings were not broken. They were carved, by patient hands, with tools the imagination supplies as chisel and rasp and the long grinding work of one stone against another. They are smooth. They have the look of having been used for a very long time.

The fauna is what the place is made of, now.

It is impossible to overstate this. The Hall is full of life. The Hall is dense with life in a way that has nothing to do with crowding and everything to do with strata — life in layers, life in arrangements, life packed into every available surface and channel and ledge. There are ledges everywhere. Carved into the walls in horizontal courses, hundreds of them, sometimes ten to a vertical meter, each one wide enough for a creature to lie along, narrow enough to require a body to fit itself there. And on every ledge, something lives.

The ledges near the ceiling are the narrowest. There, in the darkest part of the dim, certain pale fish hang in almost stationary columns. They are nearly transparent. Their bellies carry a faint bluish glow, and their eyes are wide — wider than the eyes of any fish that lives where light is plentiful. They do not move. They breathe, slowly, and they eat what drifts past them, and they wait. There are thousands of them. They fill the upper part of the Hall like a slow precipitation.

Lower, the ledges widen, and the creatures change. There are eel-shaped things that coil in the wider grooves, armored isopods the size of serving plates that grip the stone with too many legs, snails whose shells have grown over the carved edges in long, slow spirals until it is impossible to tell where the carved stone ends and the creature begins. Some of these creatures are known to surface ichthyologists. Most are not. Many seem to have evolved specifically for these ledges, in this Hall, with no analogue anywhere else on the seamount or in the water column above.

At the floor, in the broader aisles that the carved ledges leave open, larger fauna move. Slowly. They have to. The water here is warm and dense and almost motionless, and any creature that wants to move through it must do so with great patience. Shapes with long lateral fins drift from one wall to the other. Shapes with broad heads and almost no eyes feel their way along the floor, looking for things to eat. There is a creature, sometimes seen, that is as long as a tall man, but flat, and ridged along its back like a young mountain range, and it moves through the aisles with a kind of ceremonial slowness that makes the divers who have seen it keep very still for a long time afterward.

The fauna has, over time, become the place. Their shells, their cast-off molts, their calcium, their slow deaths — all of this has accumulated in the channels, on the ledges, in the aisles. The original worked stone is now visible only in glimpses, here and there, where a wall has been kept clear by some larger creature's habitual passage, or where the slow current happens to scour. Most of the Hall, to the eye, is fauna. It is a living reef built on the armature of an older construction.

There are no plants. There have never been plants. The depth is wrong. The light is wrong. Nothing rooted could grip the carved stone. Nothing photosynthetic could survive here. The base of the food, in this Hall, is what falls from above — the slow rain of organic matter that descends through all the world's oceans, the falling of bodies, the sinking of fecal plumes from creatures that live near the surface. It drifts down through the openings and settles on every horizontal surface, and the creatures eat it, or eat each other, and the system has reached its stable age. Nothing grows here. Nothing really decays. Things persist, in their layered strata, almost indefinitely.

At the center of the floor, there is a basin.

The basin is round. The basin was cut, by hand, into the basalt floor. It is perhaps four meters across. It is perhaps a meter deep. The walls of the basin are smooth — smoother than the walls of the Hall, smoother than anything else in the place. It is older than the visible fauna. It is older, perhaps, than the Hall itself, or it is the oldest part of the Hall, the first thing cut into the tableland before the walls went up around it. The divers who have seen it have a hard time describing what it is. They use words like "well" and "platter" and "offering-bowl," but none of these is quite right.

The basin is empty, mostly. It contains water — the same warm water as everywhere else — but the water in it is sometimes stiller, clearer, as if the currents of the Hall part around it. There is something at the bottom. There is always something at the bottom, though the eye cannot quite resolve what it is. Sediment, perhaps. Or an arrangement of small objects. Or nothing at all — the eye, expecting something, sees the play of faint light on the smooth stone and invents.

Around the rim of the basin, sometimes, a creature works.

It is small, by the standards of the floor fauna. It is hunched. It has forelimbs that almost look like hands — long, jointed, capable of gripping. It holds something in those forelimbs that might be a tool — a small stone, a wedge, a length of worked bone — and it works at the rim of the basin, slowly, with what looks like intent. It has been seen only a few times. It does not look up when light strikes it. It does not stop when sound reaches it. It continues. The divers who have observed it longest have written, in their reports, that it does not seem to be making anything. It seems to be maintaining something. The slow erosion of the rim, perhaps. The slow accumulation of sediment. The slow disappearance, into time, of a feature that someone wanted to keep.

It is the only suggestion, in the entire Hall, of a builder.

The light in the Hall is low. It is not the low light of cloudy days or deep caves. It is the low light of water that has filtered out almost every photon, and that receives, in compensation, a faint drifting glow from the creatures themselves. The bioluminescent fish in the upper columns. The luminous organs on the undersides of the larger floor fauna. The pale, almost imperceptible phosphorescence of certain worms in the deeper channels. None of this light is strong. None of it travels far. The Hall is lit, as one might say, in patches. There are regions of relative brightness — a corner where the columns of translucent fish are densest, the rim of the basin when a larger creature passes and casts a faint shadow. There are regions of near-total dark — the upper ceiling, the deep aisles at the edges of the floor, the far side of any wall that does not directly face a glowing column. To move through the Hall is to move in and out of these regions, and the eye adjusts, and the brain accommodates, until the dark is not an absence but a presence, a kind of weather.

There is almost no sound.

The water moves slowly. The fauna make their small sounds — the soft pop of a snapping shrimp, the distant creak of a grouper's swim bladder, the occasional long hiss of a creature exhaling through gills — but these are so infrequent, so muted, so absorbed by the dense warm water, that the overall impression is one of profound quiet. To speak aloud here would feel like a kind of vandalism. To clear one's throat, almost violent. The Hall does not ask for silence, exactly. It is just that anything louder than a whisper is, in the place's terms, an interruption. It is easier, when one is here, to simply stop talking. Easier to listen. Easier to find, after a while, that one can hear the slow creak of the basalt itself, the almost inaudible grinding of the seamount into the abyssal plain below. It is easier to find that one can hear one's own pulse in one's ears.

Nothing changes quickly.

A ledge fills with calcium over decades. A channel silts up. A creature dies, and its shell remains, and is colonized by smaller creatures, and is incorporated into the architecture of fauna. A new generation replaces the old, with no visible transition. The hands at the basin work, perhaps, on something the eye cannot quite resolve, and the work is patient, and the work does not finish, and no one knows what it is for. The seamount, beneath the Hall, continues its extremely slow subsidence — a centimeter a century, perhaps — and will, in geologic time, take the Hall with it. But this is not the Hall's concern. The Hall's concern is the moment, and the moment here is very long.

Everything in this place was made.

The walls were fitted, by hand. The basin was carved, by hand. The ledges were arranged, by hand — widened where the creatures needed width, narrowed where the creatures needed narrow, roughened where the creatures needed grip, smoothed where the currents needed flow. A civilization did this. A civilization with bronze-green fishhooks, with woven fiber that does not rot, with patience enough to spend a hundred years on a single carved line. That civilization is gone, perhaps. Or it is not gone. It is at the basin. It is the figure with hands, working at the rim
