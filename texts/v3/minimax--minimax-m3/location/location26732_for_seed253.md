# The Dark Garden of the Long Floor

You are standing on a shelf of black basalt, three thousand meters below a sea you have never seen. The water is cool — not cold in the way of polar deeps, but cool the way a cellar is cool, the way stone stays cool long after the sun has gone, the way your own blood is cool in a still room. The temperature does not change. It has not changed in a longer time than you can imagine changing.

Above you, an ocean. The whole ocean. Twelve kilometers of it, in places, pressing down with a weight that has made the basalt itself a different mineral than the basalt you know. The pressure is not a thing you suffer here; it is the medium. It is the air of this country.

Light is rumor. Some thread of it, somewhere far above, has been filtered through kilometers of water and come down blue, then dim, then dusk, then a faint grey-green memory that does not quite reach the floor. It touches the tops of things and goes out. By the time you look down, by the time you look out, the world is a deep bruised indigo that fades, in every direction, to a black that is not quite black but is darker than the dark you have words for. Things are not seen so much as guessed.

The plants glow.

This is the first thing to understand about the place, and also the last. There is no other architecture here. There is no wood, no stone-cut, no metal, no fired brick, no woven thatch. There is no *made* except what has been *grown*, and the growing is so old that the line between grown and built has been forgotten by everything, including the growing. What you are standing among are trees. They are also towers. They are also cities. They are also the bones of the country.

The trunks are pale — bone-white, ivory, the color of peeled willow — and they rise. They rise for hundreds of meters. They are not the dense, bushy trees of the surface world; they are the other thing trees can become, the way coral is the other thing animals can become. Each one is a single column, a pillar grown slowly upward through the centuries by accretion of calcium and cellulose and something that is neither, something that only exists at this pressure and has no name in surface languages. Their girth is enormous at the base — the base of one tree, here, is wider than a city block — and they taper, slowly, gracefully, to a thin pale finger pointing at the memory of light. They do not branch. They have not branched in ages. Branching was a younger thing, an experiment tried by their ancestors in shallower water, abandoned, the way a language abandons a case.

There are not many of them.

This is the second thing. Across the whole long floor of the basin, scattered with the deliberate spacing of stones in a Zen garden, there are perhaps a few thousand. You could walk between any two of them in less than a day. The space between is not empty — it is full of the dark water, of silt, of the slow drift of sediment that has been falling forever from above — but it is unbuilt. Ungrown. Each tree is its own dominion. Each tree has the room around it to be what it is, which is mostly: an ancient, quiet thing, older than the mountains on the surface, which themselves are not young.

The trunks are not smooth. They are ribbed, fluted, scalloped, the way old pillars are scalloped by weather, the way cliffs are scalloped by waves. The water has been at them. The water has had all the time in the world. They are the final form of themselves. Nothing more will happen to them; they have arrived. The fluting is regular, almost musical, a vertical pattern of ridges and troughs that catches the faint glow of their own soft light and makes a kind of moving architecture of shadow even when nothing moves. The water flows past them. The shadows flow with it.

At the top, where the column begins to thin, the structure changes. There, very high above you, lost in the gloom, the trunks begin to throw out — not branches — *fronds*. Long, streaming, dark-green and dark-purple pennants of living tissue, each one as long as a city street, that hang in the slow current like the hair of drowned queens. Some of them, the oldest, are not green at all but a deep blue-black that drinks the faint light and gives back, in exchange, a faint phosphor glow. This is what the trees give the country: light. They are not the only light. But they are the principal one.

Between the trunks, in the negative space of the forest, life is dense.

This is the part that surprises, when one first comes down. You expected, perhaps, a desert. The sparseness of the trees, the gloom, the coolness, all of it suggests a place of little. But the place is loud. It is loud with life. The darkness is full of it.

There are things here that move.

Long pale shapes, thicker than a man, slower than a thought, drift between the trunks at the height of the middle air. You cannot always see them. You hear them — a vast, low, tonal humming that comes from the whole front of one of them, sustained for a minute, two minutes, then a silence, then the next one. The sound crosses the basin. It is not song, exactly. It is not language. It is the way the air leaves the spiracle of a creature the size of a train, modulated by the resonant cavities of its skull, bouncing off the fluted trunks, arriving at you from a direction that may or may not be the direction of its source. The trunks sing it back, in a different key. The silt on the floor absorbs the lowest parts and returns a felt vibration that comes up through your feet.

Smaller things flicker. The glow of the fronds above, falling dimly down, is not the only light. There are creatures — wheel-shapes, ribbon-shapes, small darting shapes — that carry their own cold blue and green fires with them, brief punctuation marks in the long sentences of the larger hums. They go past you in a current, or against it, in ones and twos and short bright commas of many. They leave trails. The trails fade in a few seconds but the eye remembers them and stitches them into constellations that aren't there.

On the floor itself, in the silt, things burrow. You do not see them but you know they are there by the small plumes of sediment that occasionally rise, very slowly, very gently, and disperse in the current like cigarette smoke in a still room. Sometimes one of them — a long, segmented, many-legged slow-shape — comes up to the surface of the silt and rests there, half-emerged, its many small eyes (or the equivalents) reflecting the phosphor light as a row of tiny green lamps. It does not move. Nothing moves, for a long time. Then it is gone, and the silt closes over it, and the only evidence is the absence of the row of lamps.

The sound of the place is not one sound but a layer-cake of them. At the bottom, infrasonic, the deep hum of the creatures and the deeper hum of the water itself moving across the basin, a frequency below hearing that you feel in the molars and the soft palate. Above that, the sustained tones of the large pale drifters, calling and answering across miles. Above that, a constant brittle sound: a clicking, a popping, a soft crackling like fat in a pan, which is the sound of small creatures talking to each other in pulses of pressure, the language of the deep, the only language that works across kilometers of black water. Above that, the constant faint creak of the trees themselves, the slow growth of their tissues, the tiny tectonic shifts of calcite being laid down or absorbed, the patient architecture of the trunks adjusting to a pressure that has not varied in a hundred thousand years. The trees, you realize after a while, are not silent. They are the noisiest thing here, if you know how to listen. They are talking to each other, or to themselves, or to the water, in a register so low and so slow that it is almost a kind of weather.

Nothing changes.

This is the rule of the place, and you learn it by the second hour, and by the second day you have stopped fighting it. The current flows, but it has always flowed in this direction, at this speed. The silt falls, but it has always fallen at this rate. The creatures hum, and answer, and are answered, in cycles that have been refined until there is no variation left. The trees grow, but so slowly that a year of growth is the width of a fingernail, and the difference between one year and the next is something only a tree would notice, and the trees are not talking to you about it. The whole basin is an equilibrium so old it has forgotten it ever had to find itself. Nothing is in the process of becoming anything else. Everything has arrived.

It is not a dead place. That is the surprise of it, and the strangeness. It is the opposite of dead. It is so alive that it has stopped needing to prove it. The fluting on the trunks is not the fluting of decay. The glow of the fronds is not the glow of a last effort. The humming of the drifters is not the humming of distress. It is the sound a system makes when it has no more use for change. It is the sound a thing makes when it is fully what it is.

You stand on the basalt, on the silt, between the pale trunks, and the dark water moves past you very slowly, and the glow falls down from the fronds you cannot quite see, and the deep voices call and answer across miles of black, and you understand, after a time, that you are in a place that will still be here, in exactly this form, long after the things that made you have all gone quiet. The trees were here before the drifters. The drifters will be here after you. The silt is very patient. The water is very patient. The basin is so old that the word *old* is a child's word for it.

The trunks rise into a darkness that is their own and not yours. The creatures move through it in their slow geometries. The sound is a held chord, the longest chord ever sounded, that began before the first of them learned to hum and will not resolve for as long as there is water to carry it.

You are a small warm event in a cold, dark, ancient, settled, singing, glowing, patient country. It does not mind you. It has room for you the way the sea has room for a stone you drop in — entirely, and without comment.
