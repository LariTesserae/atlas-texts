# The Toll Room

You arrive through a low arch and immediately the sound takes you. Not loud the way a waterfall is loud — not a single voice, but a thousand small voices, each one a single note sustained without breath or rest. The room is the size of a chapel. It is packed absolutely full.

Every surface is constructed. Walls, floor, ceiling, columns, beams — all of it. No stone shows because there is no stone. The place was built up in layers over so long that even the original materials are forgotten; what remains is a dense accumulation of made things pressed together until the gaps between them disappeared. The walls are not walls so much as a frozen crowd of objects leaning into each other for support. If you pulled one out the others would fall.

Everything is warm. Not the warmth of a fire — nothing burns here — but the warmth of a body that has been standing in the sun, or a hand held too long. The temperature is exactly the temperature of skin. The dim light, which has no obvious source, makes everything the color of old wax.

The objects are tools. They are the tools. There is no metal here, or almost none — a few dull wedges of something that might once have been iron, or might be some local substitute. The rest is bone, wood that has gone dark as chocolate, hardened clay, fiber twisted into cord and woven into mats and baskets so tightly that they have become almost like wood themselves. Baskets within baskets. Mats within mats. Bowls nested inside larger bowls. Shafts of polished antler. Countless small hooks, each one carved by hand from a different piece of material. There are no two identical objects. Every piece has the mark of a hand that held it while it was being made, and the mark is worn down now almost to nothing.

The density is total. You cannot see between the objects. Where two surfaces meet they have rubbed each other smooth over the centuries, and where they have not quite met, narrow channels remain, and in those channels — in the labyrinth of gaps and crevices that runs through the entire structure like a nervous system — the air moves. That is the sound.

The sound is a hiss. Not white noise — it has shape. It rises and falls in slow waves that take a long time to complete, longer than you would expect, longer than makes sense for a room this size. The air being forced through the packed maze of objects has found its paths, and it sticks to them, and the sound is the room breathing along its own established routes. It does not change. It has not changed in a very long time. The objects have settled into their final arrangement, and the air has settled into its final song, and both will continue until something from outside intervenes, which nothing has done for a very long time.

There is no water. There may once have been water, or some liquid — you can see places where something seems to have been meant to hold a liquid, a bowl set at a particular angle, a channel routed with care — but those vessels are dry now and have been dry longer than the polish on their rims would suggest. The clay is the color of dry clay. The wood is the color of dry wood. Nothing here is decomposing, because there is nothing to decompose, and the dryness has preserved everything the way the cold preserves things in other places.

There are no plants. There are no animals. There is no soil. There is no sky visible. The dim light does not come from anywhere you can identify — it comes from everywhere at once, the way the sound comes from everywhere at once, and the two are perhaps related, perhaps one is the byproduct of the other. The light is enough to see by. The light is never more than enough to see by.

The scale of the room is small enough to feel enclosed but large enough that you cannot tell where the back wall is without walking, and walking is difficult because the floor, like the walls, is a dense thicket of made things — mostly flat things, mats and boards and worn planks — laid down over each other in so many layers that it is almost spongy underfoot. Your weight makes it settle a little with each step, and somewhere in the depths something shifts, and the sound changes for a moment before resuming.

You are the first thing to enter this room in a very long time that is not itself a made thing. The room does not mind you. It has no opinion. It is past opinion. It is the final state of a long, careful project that no longer has anyone working on it, and it has continued without workers because the work, essentially, was finished, and what remains is just the result, holding its shape, humming to itself in the warm dim air, in the silence of no rain and no wind and no insects and no birds and no footsteps except yours.
