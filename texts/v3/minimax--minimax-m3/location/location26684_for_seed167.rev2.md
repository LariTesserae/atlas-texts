You arrive at the city the way light doesn't — by sinking.

It begins as a change in pressure, then a change in color. The warm water above has a green-brown opaqueness, like old tea, and as you descend the tea-thick light thins to amber, to rust, to a deep bruised blue that is less a color than a remembering of color. The city lives down here where the sun is mostly rumor. What light reaches it has been filtered so many times it arrives tired, horizontal, draped over surfaces rather than striking them.

The first structures are towers. They used to be something else — something with windows and lintels, something human-scaled — but the years under water have made them rude and porous. They are coral now, mostly. Calcareous skeletons stacked on calcareous skeletons, the geometry of the old city used as scaffolding by every encrusting organism with patience. A tower that once might have stood fifty meters above a street now stands perhaps eight, and what remains is so thickly bearded with growth it has lost its edges. It is a stack of sponges, a column of brain-coral, a pillar dressed in tube-worms whose red plumes hang in the slow current like wet hair. Where a window used to be there is now a curtain of crinoids, arms branching and re-branching in the dim, each tip tipped with a tiny gripping hook that has never held anything but the current.

Between the towers run the streets. You can still follow the grid — you can feel it, the way the buildings align, the way the channels between them hold their direction for long distances before a curve intervenes. The streets are not paved anymore. They are sand, mostly, fine pale sand drifting in dunes at the corners of intersections, sculpted by whatever current runs here — a current that moves but does not hurry. The current is the city's weather. It comes from one direction in the morning and another at night, and the fauna know this and move with it. Schools of small silver fish use the avenues as highways, traveling block to block in pulsing formations, their flanks flashing the only true light this place gets. Above them, slower, individual fish with their own colors — saffron, indigo, a green so deep it looks black — drift at their own errands. A ray once an hour will come gliding down the main thoroughfare, its wings making a soft pressure wave you feel against your skin before you see it.

The fauna are the city's true inhabitants. The structures are backdrop, geology by now. Crabs sidestep along the lower walls, a thousand of them, each to its own crack. They are the small citizens, armored and watchful, and when you approach they vanish into crevices with the speed of thrown dice. Bigger things move at the edges of perception — a shadow passing across a far facade, a slow mass shifting in a courtyard, something with too many legs seen only as motion. The abundance here is not the abundance of herds on plains. It is the abundance of a reef, layered and stacked, every surface hosting its own community. A square meter of wall might hold anemones, hydroids, a small grove of soft coral, a dozen brittle stars tucked into crevices, and behind all of them the patient architects — the worms, the bivalves, the boring sponges — slowly digesting the city's old bones and rebuilding them in their own image.

There is vegetation, but it is honest about its modesty. Patches of turtle grass in the wider plazas where some shaft of light still reaches from far above. Tufts of algae on every south-facing surface, brown and red, swaying. A stand of kelp here and there, rising from cracks, its blades flattened by the current into ribbons. None of it is lush. The light won't allow it. But what grows here is stubborn and exact, and the green of it, when you find it, is so saturated against all the rust and the blue that it hurts the eye.

The buildings — there are thousands of them, and you can walk for hours and not see the same one twice. But they all share a quality: they have settled. Erosion here is not destruction, it is digestion. The place has been eaten so many times and rebuilt so many times that it has reached an equilibrium, a kind of architectural digestion where what is consumed becomes what is built, and the city's shape changes but its shape does not fade. A facade that took a hundred years to dissolve will be replaced, over those same hundred years, by a wall of coral that took its place so gradually no one could say when the transition occurred. The city is in balance with itself. It will be here tomorrow, and the day after, and in a thousand years it will be in the same configuration, the same towers, the same avenues, the same crabs in the same cracks.

Mostly it is quiet. The water carries sound strangely — flattens it, softens it, pushes it away — so the sounds that exist here are felt more than heard. The clicks of snapping shrimp, like sand in a tumbler, far off. The low whoosh of a large fish moving. The scrape of a crab's leg against a shell. A kind of constant soft hush that is the sound of water moving against itself. Sometimes, at night — and night here is only a small darkening, a passing of a shadow across the surface far above — sometimes at night the city lights up. Bioluminescence. The anemones along a particular wall, a species that only glows in the deepest dark, will trace every column and arch in pale blue fire, and the fish that school through will leave trails behind them, and the whole avenue will be a cathedral of moving light. It does not last. It is not for anyone. It is the city's own breathing, made visible.

There is something wrong here. You feel it. The grid is too perfect. The symmetry of certain intersections, where four buildings meet at exactly the same height, exactly the same degree of coral-coat, suggests a pattern beyond accident. The current does not bend here the way currents bend around a reef; it bends the way currents bend around something it recognizes. And sometimes — rarely, and only when you have been still long enough — you will see a structure that has a window still. Not a window colonized, not a window crusted over, but a window, an opening, an actual rectangular absence in a wall, and through it you can see into a darker interior, and you can see, dimly, the suggestion of a room, and in that room the suggestion of another wall with another window, and through that, the suggestion of a street you have already walked. The geometry is too persistent. The city knows its own shape. It has not forgotten what it was.

It does not mind you. It has accommodated you. The crabs do not flee from you any faster than they flee from each other. The fish school around you the way they school around a rock. You are welcome here, in the way that any new shape is welcome in a place that is always rebuilding itself. Go down. Go deeper. The towers get taller as you descend, not shorter — this is wrong, this is the wrongness — the deeper streets are wider and more intact, the grid more faithful, the buildings more massive, and the fauna more abundant, and the dark more complete. Go down, and the city will hold you the way the warm water holds you, completely, without comment.
