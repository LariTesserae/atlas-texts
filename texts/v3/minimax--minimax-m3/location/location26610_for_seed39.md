# The High Extraction

The altitude here is almost structural — not so much a place you stand in as a condition you endure. Eleven thousand feet of cold air pressing down from above, eleven thousand feet of cold rock pressing up from below, and you, if you were here, somewhere in the middle, with your lungs making change for what they used to need.

The light comes sideways. It always comes sideways at this latitude and this height — a low, slate-colored wash out of cloud that doesn't so much illuminate the ground as refuse to let it be entirely dark. The sun, when it exists, is a rumor behind two layers of cloud and a thin ice mist, and the shadows under every rock are the same color as the rocks. You can stand in a valley here at midday and not be able to tell which way is north from the light alone. You have to use the wind, which always knows.

The wind is the dominant feature of the place. It comes from the west, funneled between two ranges that look, from above, like a pair of jaws that have been pried slightly open. By the time it reaches the plateau it has been accelerated, compressed, dried, and re-wetted half a dozen times, and it carries everything: snow pellets in winter, dust in spring, the smell of distant iron, the faint mechanical hum that lives at the edge of hearing and never quite leaves.

---

## The Ground

The geology is recent. That is the first thing a person would notice, even if they couldn't name it — the sense that the ground hasn't *finished* deciding what it is. There are no rounded shoulders here, no patient weathering, no soil that has had time to forget where it came from. The rocks are sharp. The rocks are almost rude. They were either pushed up so fast they shattered, or uncovered so recently by retreating ice that they still hold the bite of the glacier that let them go. A person walking here would twist an ankle twice in an hour. The scree is loose and travels underfoot in small, dry whispers.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. There is no river. There is no visible lake. But the rocks weep — every flat surface that faces the sky wears a thin dark sheen of condensation by midmorning, and by mid-afternoon a shallow pan no bigger than a dinner plate might hold a finger's depth of perfectly clear water that wasn't there an hour ago. The air itself is wet. You taste it. It condenses on eyelashes, on metal, on the feathers of the birds that come down to drink from a place that looks, from a distance, as dry as bone. Springs emerge at the base of cliffs and are gone again within fifty meters, sinking into the rubble of broken shale. In late summer, when the snowmelt runs highest, a temporary stream appears in the eastern gully — brown, loud, gone in three weeks.

Vegetation is what clings. Cushion plants the size of teacups, their hairs so dense they look like small animals. A lichen that has spent a hundred years spreading across a single boulder. A grass that grows only on the lee side of rocks, where the wind has buried it just enough to keep it alive. Nothing taller than your ankle. Nothing that would cast a shadow if the light were stronger, which it isn't.

## The Activity

And then there is the machine.

There is no town. There are no houses, no sheds, no anchored walls, no foundations that look like they mean to stay. But there is equipment — an enormous amount of equipment — distributed across the plateau in a pattern that suggests a process rather than a place. Conveyor structures on steel legs stride across the broken ground like long-legged insects. A primary crusher sits at the center of a worked-out scar, ringed by a berm that wasn't there two years ago. Cables run between capped posts in clean vectors. Diesel generators in weatherproof housings exhale a constant thin plume of pale exhaust that the wind peels off and lays across the rocks in greasy streaks. Storage tanks the size of houses, painted gray, unmarked, set on raised platforms so the permafrost under them doesn't melt and tip them in.

This is the contradiction at the heart of the place, and you would feel it as soon as you saw it: all this *machinery* and yet not a single thing that a person is supposed to live inside. The workforce, when it comes, comes in shifts. The machines are not built here; they were brought here, in pieces, by a road that only exists for nine weeks of the year and was engineered to *leave* no trace afterward, though it has left plenty. The infrastructure is mobile, or wants to be. It moves with the ore body. It moves with the glacier's edge, which is retreating faster than the planners expected. It moves because nothing here has been here long enough yet to stay.

There is a kind of beauty to it, if you can bear the beauty — the long lines of the conveyors vanishing into the haze, the slow rotation of the crusher visible from two kilometers away, the way the lights on the equipment cut yellow wedges into the dim and throw moving shadows of birds across the rock. The whole system runs on a rhythm that is not the rhythm of the plateau but has been threaded into it: ore extracted, crushed, concentrated, loaded, hauled. A heartbeat that belongs to the machines and not to the place, and yet the place is where the heartbeat lives.

## The Sound

Sound does not carry well at this altitude and this temperature. The air is thin and the wind is constant and the ground is broken into a thousand small absorbing surfaces, so the noise of the operation, which from inside it would be deafening, arrives at a distance as a low, persistent wash — the hum of generators, the slow drag of conveyor belts, the periodic shudder of the crusher. From a kilometer away it sounds like distant surf. From five kilometers it sounds like a held breath.

The wind is louder than any of it. The wind is the only true voice of the place. It sings in the cables. It plays the conveyor structures like enormous flue pipes. It is the sound that you stop hearing after a day and only notice again when it dies down, which it rarely does. When it dies down, what fills the silence is the birds.

## The Life

The birds are why the place is not what it looks like.

From a distance the plateau reads as dead — gray rock, gray light, gray absence. Up close, and especially in summer, the air is full of moving black-and-white shapes. Andean condors ride the thermals off the heated rock faces in the early afternoon, so close to the conveyors sometimes that a wing tip brushes a guy wire and the bird adjusts without seeming to notice, the way a person steps over a curb. There are geese — upland geese, in their absurd white-and-rust plumage — that nest on the ground in scrapes among the scree and lead their goslings down to the temporary stream in single file, the parents hissing like kettle spouts at anything that comes near. There are caracaras, opportunistic and intelligent, that walk among the equipment on their own business and ignore it completely. There are foxes, small and gray, that live under the crusher pad and eat the rats that live under the kitchen modules, and so on, down through every level of the local food web, all of it busy and loud and completely unconcerned with the philosophical question of whether they belong here.

The fauna is what gives the place its motion. Without the animals it would be a still photograph — a postcard of industrial geology under a dim sky. With them it is a working system, a real place, one in which the machine is an unwelcome guest and the wildlife is the host and the negotiation between them is the daily business of the landscape. The condors nest on the cliffs that have not been worked. The geese nest on the scree that has not been scraped. The foxes den under the structures that are already there because the structures have made dens possible. Everything is finding its place. The place is being made.

## The Weather

What passes for weather here is mostly a question of which way the wind is lying. There is snow nine months of the year. The snow falls sideways, in horizontal sheets, and accumulates in the lee of every obstruction — including the machinery, which gathers strange white caps on its upper surfaces and occasionally collapses under the weight of them. There are storms that come up so fast the equipment has to be shut down on emergency protocols and the on-site crews retreat to the heated modules, where they watch the wind shear the loose snow off the plateau in great streaming banners. There are days of clear, brutal cold when the sky goes a darker, harder blue and the shadows sharpen and you can hear individual rocks cracking in the permafrost a hundred meters away, and the sound of the crushers carries for miles and miles because the air has stopped carrying anything else.

In summer — and summer here is a generous word for a six-week reprieve — the snow retreats up the slopes and reveals the new ground underneath, ground that no one, neither human nor animal, has ever seen before. Every summer the working face is a little further back, the haul road a little longer, and the permafrost a little shallower, and the exposed rock weathers a little further under rain and frost and the slow patient teeth of lichen. The geologists call this *emerging*. Everyone else calls it *new*.

---

## The Feel

To stand here is to feel small in a particular way — not the smallness of being beneath something grand, but the smallness of being a temporary thing in a place that is itself still temporary. The rock is new. The ice is leaving. The mine is here now and will not be here later. The birds are seasonal. Even the equipment has the look of the provisional, of the thing that will be dismantled and loaded onto trucks and driven down a road that will itself be erased, leaving behind a worked scar that will, over the next thousand years, slowly fill with meltwater and become a tarn, and then a meadow, and then a place the future birds nest without remembering why the rocks are shaped the way they are.

The dim light and the cold and the constant wind and the hum of the machines and the calls of the geese and the slow rotation of the crusher and the thin mist condensing on every surface and the occasional condor drifting past at eye level, closer than you'd like, with a wing span wider than you are tall — these are the place. It is not beautiful in the way that old places are beautiful. It is beautiful in the way that new places are beautiful, which is to say it is beautiful in the way that *becoming* is beautiful, which is also to say it is a beauty that will not wait for you, and you should pay attention now or it will be something else by the time you look again.
