# A Flooded Chamber

You arrive at the place from above, but you don't really *arrive* — you are already at its level when you find it, as though the world has quietly let you down a step. A square opening in something harder than earth — limestone, perhaps, or the pale, almost-bone color of a stone that hasn't decided what it wants to be — and through the opening, the sun pours in. Not filtered. Not refracted through clouds. Direct, white, slanted, the kind of light that knows exactly what it's doing. It lands on water, and the water does what water does when you give it this much attention: it becomes a second ceiling, moving, the underside of a sky installed six feet below the real one.

The chamber is small. You can stand at one edge and see all the other edges without turning your head. The walls are vertical and they are *cut* — every one of them hand-cut, the chisel-marks still readable in the stone, parallel little gouges that catch the light and throw it back in short, sharp flecks. The floor is not a floor. The floor is water, warm, the warmth rising off it in a faint, visible shimmer that you only notice when the shaft of sunlight passes through it on its way down. The water is clear, but not the way a glass of water is clear. It is the clear of a shallow tropical sea — greenish at the edges where it thins against stone, a deep, lit teal in the middle where the shaft of light has been working on it for some time. You can see the bottom. The bottom is also cut. Steps. Three of them, descending into the pool from the side where you are standing, each one carved from the same parent stone as the walls, each one with its own crisp edge, the corners still square.

The sound is the first thing, really, if you came in through the opening. It is loud here, and it is all water. A sheet of water comes in from somewhere above your sightline, off to the left, and falls the last foot or so into the pool with a flat, decisive impact — *tok, tok, tok* — a pulse rather than a roar, because the fall is short but the chamber catches it and holds it, amplifies it the way a cupped hand amplifies a whisper. From the impact, the water radiates outward in small, persistent rings that reach the cut walls, climb a quarter inch, and return. Other sounds layer over this. A higher, thinner one: a stream somewhere, a channel, a notch in the wall where the water is being let out as fast as it is being let in. The overflow falls past your shoulder, down some unseen continuation, and that fall has a different sound — thinner, swifter, the sound of water that has somewhere to be. And underneath all of it, almost felt rather than heard, the deep bass hum of the basin itself, the resonance of warm water held in close stone, a low *mmm* that the chamber makes without quite meaning to.

Everything is built. This is the thing you notice, after the light and the sound. There is no natural substrate in here. No earth. No sand. No pebble bed. The rock underfoot, the rock around you, the rock above — all worked. The pool is not a puddle that collected in a hole; it is a hole that was made in order to hold a puddle, and the puddle was persuaded to come. The channel that feeds it was cut to a specific width. The overflow channel was cut to a specific fall. The little ledge running around the chamber at water-height — for sitting, for setting things down, for resting a foot on as you stand in the shallows — is regular, deliberate, the same height on all four sides, its inner edge rounded by use but its outer edge still sharp enough to catch a thread. This place was made by hands. The hands had tools. The tools were simple — points, edges, a thing to pound with — but they were certain, and they were recent, or at least the work is. There is no centuries-deep patina here. No moss-softened lip. The stone is the color of fresh-cut things. The chisel-marks are crisp enough to cast their own tiny shadows when the sun moves.

The sun *does* move. The shaft of light walks across the surface of the pool as the hours pass, and where it walks, the water answers. Bright spots migrate along the cut walls. The teal deepens and shifts toward gold in the lit zone and back toward green in the shadow. Things that were invisible an hour ago become visible — a string of small bubbles stuck to a vertical face under the water, a faint tracery on the floor where the original tool-marks were deeper, a small carved basin in one corner, filled with its own warmer inch of water, the heat-source or the feed-source, you can't tell which, but it is the reason the pool is warm. Around this little basin, a faint steam rises and dissolves into the shaft of light, and for a moment, the chamber looks like it has a breath.

Vegetation, what there is of it, lives in the cracks. A single fern has found a fissure in the lip of the opening above — the only place in the chamber that is not regularly submerged — and hangs down into the light, its fronds trembling slightly when the falling sheet of water on the left sends a breath of moving air across the pool. A bright green moss has colonized the back of one of the steps where the waterline stays constant. A few strands of something threadlike and dark grow out of a single crack in the wall at the waterline, sway gently with the returning rings from the falling sheet, and that is all. The rest is water and stone. There are no animals. No fish, even — though the water would clearly support them, you can tell because of the clarity, the warmth, the patience of it. The pool is not waiting to become an ecosystem. It is content to be a pool. Something about the regularity of the flow, the steady pulse of the falling sheet, the absence of any input from outside except water and light, has made this small system self-sufficient in a quiet, mineral way. Things grow where they can. Nothing moves under its own power.

The temperature is felt mostly in the air above the water, which is warmer than the air you came in from, and which holds the steam and the fine mist thrown up by the falling sheet. You stand on the ledge, or on the top step, and you can feel the warmth coming up around your ankles before you have even put a foot in. The water itself, when you do step down into it, is bath-warm. Not hot. Not the heat of a spring. The heat of water that has been sitting in a sun-warmed stone basin in a sun-warmed chamber, being added to and overflowing in steady, even amounts, for as long as the chamber has been here, which is not very long, in the life of stone, and which feels like a long time, in the life of a person.

What you feel, standing in the pool with the water at mid-calf, is the whole thing working. The overflow at your shoulder. The feed on the left. The bubbles rising in the corner. The fern trembling in its crack. The sun traveling. The light walking. The chamber, with nothing in it but itself and you, doing its small, complete, well-made thing. It is loud here, and bright here, and warm here, and there is no creature in it but you, and everything you are touching was cut to be touched, and the water is always moving, and the stone remembers the chisel, and the chisel was yesterday, or the day before, or a year ago, or ten — but not long ago, and not carelessly, and not by anything other than a hand.
