The chimney is a vertical concrete throat, a hundred and forty feet across at the base and rising straight for five hundred feet before the open mouth narrows against the cloud cover. You are at the bottom of it. The chimney goes up. There is no other direction.

The interior is hollow. The interior has been hollow for a long time, long enough that the walls have become terrain.

The walls are poured in stacked rings, each ring ten feet tall, with cold seams between them where the formwork joined. The seams caught dust. The seams caught spores. The seams caught the first rains that fell through the open top before the canopy closed over it, and the water sat in the seams and didn't dry, and from the seams things began to grow. Ferns now uncurl downward from the upper rings, their fronds hanging in long green tassels, six feet, ten feet, their tips brushing the rings below. Mosses fill the seams in dense cushions the color of wet slate. A climbing fig has rooted somewhere far above and sent a single woody stem all the way down the wall, holding on with small adhesive pads, putting out leaves the size of dinner plates wherever a seam gives it a foothold. At the base of the wall, where the concrete meets the steel floor, the growth is a thicket — a low jungle of ferns and aroids and bracket fungi the color of bone, fed by everything that has dripped down over the decades.

The walls are built. Everything here is built. There is no soil. The earth is somewhere outside and above, and the chimney is a hollow needle embedded in it, and what lives in the chimney lives on the chimney, in its dust and its seams and the bodies of things that died and were caught in the holdfasts of other things. The growth is a skin, two inches thick in places, over the industrial bone beneath.

The drips fall.

The chimney breathes. The interior surface is cool, the air inside is warm and saturated, and the moisture condenses on the concrete in a continuous slow film, gathers into threads, gathers into drops, gathers into the long vertical stains you can see on the wall like the tide-marks of a slow rain. The drops fall from the rim of every fern. The drops fall from the underside of every bracket. The drops fall from the rim of the open mouth five hundred feet above, where the actual rain enters and hangs in the humid air for a long time before letting go. They fall onto a floor that was once riveted steel plate and is now a foot of black sediment and rotting frond, and they fall on the sound.

The sound is the turbine.

Somewhere under the floor, in a sub-chamber that was sealed when the plant was decommissioned and the doors were bolted and the jungle was permitted to come back over everything, a turbine still turns. No one came to turn it off. No one could, perhaps, or the decision was made long ago and forgotten, or it runs on a feed from something that doesn't know how to stop. It runs. It has been running for longer than the moss has been growing. It will run.

The sound is below hearing. It is not a roar. A roar has peaks; this has no peaks. It is a single sustained low frequency that you stop hearing in your ears after a few minutes and begin to feel in the architecture — in the soles of your feet, in the bridge of your nose, in the small of your back. The chimney resonates. The rebar inside the concrete has learned the frequency and sings it back, a sympathetic wobble that wanders by a fraction of a hertz as the temperature changes, as the load on the turbine shifts imperceptibly, as another section of rebar rusts through and the weight of the wall redistributes. The pitch is not constant. The pitch is very, very slowly changing. You would have to stand here for a year to notice.

The volume inside the chimney is mostly empty. The walls hold the growth, the floor holds the sediment and the things that live in it, and the rest — the center, the vast vertical space that rises above you like the nave of a cathedral whose roof has been removed — is air and falling water and dim light. Standing at the center, you can see the wall as a continuous cylinder receding, and the cylinder recedes, and recedes, and the eye gives up before it reaches the top. The light at the top is a circle, far away, the color of a clouded sky, and it falls down the inside of the stack in a long pale column. The light is normal. It is the same light that falls on any forest clearing at midday, but it has been filtered through five hundred feet of humid air and through the bodies of the things that fly in the upper third of the chimney, and by the time it reaches the floor it is gray-green and moving slightly, the way light moves under deep water.

In the upper third, the fauna.

Bats, mostly, but not only bats. There are bats in the thousands, hanging from the underside of ledges and from the woody stem of the climbing fig, folded into themselves, silent during the day, a dense living wallpaper of small dark shapes pressed into the concrete. They are not the only thing. There are other shapes roosted among them, longer in the limb, with wings that are not membrane but a translucent stretched skin, pale, veined, that catches the down-falling light when they shift. They do not move during the day. At dusk the mouth of the chimney opens onto a dusk the same color as the inside, and the things that roost there pour upward in a long dark river, ascending through the column of light and out into the canopy, and they return at dawn, and the sound of their returning is a second sound beneath the turbine — a continuous soft rustling, silk on stone, a sound that comes from above and never stops.

On the floor, in the sediment, things move. A pale elongated thing the size of a forearm, with too many legs and no eyes, surfaces when a drop lands near it and then is gone. Small bright frogs, the color of a cut leaf, sit on real leaves and do not move. There are beetles, lacquered black, traveling in lines along the seams of the steel plate where the sediment is thinnest. A snake, possibly — you see a length of it once, drawn across a gap between two ferns, and then it is not there, and you do not see it again. The floor is alive in a thin layer, a film of motion over the hum of the turbine, and the film is dense with the things that feed on the things that feed on the drips.

The air is warm and wet. The smell is wet concrete and the inside of a machine. The smell is ferrous. The smell is sweet, in the way that overripe fruit is sweet, in the way that a wound is sweet. The smell has been the same for a long time.

The chimney was built. The growth came after, and the fauna came after the growth, and the drips came after the fauna, and the cracks came after the drips — slow cracks in the concrete, following the rusting rebar inside, widening by fractions of a millimeter a year. The cracks catch more spores. The cracks hold more water. The cracks will eventually, in a time longer than you will stand here, bring the chimney down, but the balance is held. The turbine runs, the breath runs, the drips run, the vines climb, the bats sleep, the snake moves, the moss thickens, the rebar rusts. The place is in its own state. It is not a ruin — a ruin has stopped. This has not stopped. It is a slow machine, and you are standing in it, and a drop falls on the back of your hand, warm.
