# The Coral That Became the Plain

The place is a single thing. That is the first thing to understand. There is a salt flat, white and cracked, that runs in every direction toward a low bright horizon, but the place itself is the thing that sits upon it: a fossil the size of a modest room, the bleached skeletal remnant of something that lived once, a colony of small polyps who built their apartment building out of themselves and then vanished, leaving the building behind.

The fossil lies on its side like a skull that has forgotten its name. Its surface is the color of old ivory, of bone, of salt — a pale matte that the bright light strikes without mercy and finds no shadow to soften. From a distance it could be a boulder, a shed, the ruin of some shed. Up close it resolves into structure: thousands of tiny cups, each the size of a fingertip, each once housing a soft-bodied creature, each now just a hollow. Hexagonal here, pentagonal there, the geometry of efficient packing imposed by small lives long dissolved into mineral. The cups are packed shoulder to shoulder. There is no empty surface. The whole skin of the thing is habitation, was habitation, is the memory of habitation.

The wind here is loud, and it is loud in a particular way. It does not gust. It does not rise and fall. It is a flat, steady, ceaseless pressure of cool air moving across the plain, and where it meets the fossil it finds the thousand small mouths of the cups and the long cracks that run through the structure from settlement and from time, and it sings through them. The fossil is a held note. A flute made of stone. The chambers inside are resonators. The sound that comes out is below melody — a low, sustained humming that you feel more in the chest than in the ear, a vibration that travels through the salt crust and into the soles of the feet and stays there. It does not change. The same note has been sounding, perhaps, for as long as the wind has been blowing across this flat. The note is part of the place.

Inside, which you can only reach through a few of the wider cracks, the architecture changes. The outer cups give way to inner chambers that the wind has filled, over millennia, with slow mineral growth — calcite, aragonite, occasional dark crystals of something iron. The interior is a packed forest of these growths, rising from the chamber floors in blunt stalagmites and clustered druzy sprays, each crystal small as a tooth, each catching the light that finds its way in through the cracks and refusing to let it pass unchanged. The inside of the fossil is brighter than the outside, in a fragmented way: a constellation of small bright points, each one a crystal face turned toward one of the apertures, multiplying the single source of light into a thousand. There is no darkness anywhere in the interior. There is only distributed, multiplied, broken brightness.

There is no water. The flat around the fossil has a faint saline crust that is not water but the memory of water — the place where water was, briefly, long ago, and left behind the dissolved mineral of itself. The air is dry. The salt is dry. The fossil is dry. Your tongue, against the inside of your mouth, finds nothing. And yet — because the place is 0.4 of water, not zero — there is a faint sense, more perceived than sensed, that moisture could be here. The crystals in the chambers are not quite the right shape for absolute aridity. The salt flat, in the deepest part of its cracks, retains a whiteness that wants to dissolve. The cool air, when you stand in it for a long time, has a quality that is almost breath, almost humidity, the ghost of a climate that was once wetter, never arriving.

The vegetation is sparse. A few hard tufts of something grey-green grow in the lee of the fossil, where the wind is slightly less, where a thin film of dust has accumulated enough to hold a root. They look mineral themselves. They are not. But they are close to it. Their leaves, if they have leaves, are scales; their growth, in a year, is barely perceptible; they flower without ever seeming to flower. They are the last things.

There are no animals. There are no insects. There is no movement at all, except the wind, and the wind is not movement in the way a bird is movement. The wind is the place itself, expressed. Nothing walks, nothing crawls, nothing flies. The only sound is the hum of the fossil under the air. The only light is the light. The only changes are the very slow seasonal changes of a place that does not really have seasons — a slight cooling in winter, a slight warming in summer, the difference between which is smaller than the difference between two breaths.

The temperature is cool and stays cool. The flat does not warm under the sun the way a darker plain would; the white salt and the bone-colored fossil throw the heat back, and the wind carries whatever little builds up away. The place is the temperature of a room left open to a cool morning, indefinitely.

The light is bright and comes from above, mostly, but not entirely. The fossil has been here so long that the sun has shifted where it sets, over the slow pivot of the world, and the fossil has accepted every angle of light it was offered and is now equally exposed on all sides, equally bleached, equally without shadow. The light inside the fossil, as said, is a shattered version of the outside light. There is nowhere in the place that is dark.

The place is weird in a quiet way. It is unusual that a coral colony this size should be lying on a salt flat. It is unusual that the structure should be so packed, so totally without empty space. It is unusual that everything here is made — every surface of the fossil is biological in origin, was built by the soft bodies of polyps out of calcium they pulled from the sea, and the salt flat around it was built by the evaporation of a sea, and the crystals inside were built by water that no longer exists, and the lichen tufts outside were built by plants from somewhere else, and there is no natural substrate here, there is no stone that was not at some point something else — and yet none of it was built by tools, none of it by devices, none of it by anything that resembled a hand. The place is a construction site that finished before any constructor was needed. The place is a city built by a citizenry that dissolved into the walls after the building was done.

The place does not change. The fossil does not erode further. The salt flat does not become less flat. The crystals do not grow. The lichen tufts do not spread. The wind does not stop. The note does not alter. The light does not dim. This is the final state of the place. It arrived here a long time ago. It will stay. The hum is the only event, and the hum is always the same event, and the hum is not really an event at all. The hum is the place being itself, continuously, the way a held chord is the chord being itself.

Stand near the fossil in the bright wind and the place becomes what it has always been: a thing made by absent makers, packed with the slow architecture of their absence, singing one note into the cool dry air, waiting for nothing, holding its shape, fully itself, asking nothing of you except that you stand there and listen and feel it in your chest.
