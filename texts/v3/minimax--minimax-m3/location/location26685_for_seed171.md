# The Long Plateau of First Light

You stand on a high place that has not yet decided what it is.

The continent beneath you is still being lifted. That is the first thing to understand. The rock here is not old. The rock here is *impatient* — it has been rising for what might be a few million years or a few thousand, and it has not finished. The plateau tilts underfoot, not as a single slope but as a thing in argument with itself: here a fold, there a tension, a faint tremor that runs through the entire mass like a shiver caught halfway. Strata fold upward at the horizon where the air is too thick to see through clearly, and you understand that those shapes are not yet mountains. They are the intent of mountains. They are what mountains are thinking about becoming.

And yet everything is held. Saturated. Packed so full of itself that there is no negative space at all.

## The Substrate Is Alive

There is no soil. There is no stone you can stand on without it being a kind of animal.

The ground is a continuous organism. A single mass of plant matter that may have been seeded here, or may have grown up from spores that drifted in on a wet wind, or may have been — and this is the thought that recurs, the one you keep pushing aside — *placed*. The surface is a felt of interlocking root, stem, rhizome, and thread, pressed and woven into a mat several meters deep in places, thin and crust-like over the rising shoulders of the new geology, but always, everywhere, *alive*. When you put your weight on it, it gives. Not much. A centimeter. The way a sleeping back gives. The mat knows you are there.

The plants are not the kind of thing a field guide would recognize. They are mostly low — knee-high, waist-high in the hollows, never higher than your head in this region, though you have heard that elsewhere, in the deeper depressions where water has gathered and not left, there are forests of them that stand three hundred meters tall and never stop. Here, on the plateau, the living mass spreads laterally, in patient slow waves of growth that you can see if you sit and watch. A ripple of new green, the color of tarnished copper, moves across the surface at a walking pace every few hours. It is not wind-driven. It is the plant *deciding* to be slightly farther over there.

The texture is dense and damp. The leaves — if you can call them leaves; they are flat, they are photosynthetic, but they overlap like scales and have a faint heat to them that you can feel through your boots — the leaves are always wet. Not raining. Raining would be the wrong word. The moisture is coming up from below. The whole root mat is breathing water out, exhaling it into a cool air that cannot quite hold it, and so the water becomes visible, becomes a low fog that lies in the layers of the vegetation and never quite lifts. Every surface is beaded. Every stem has a thin film. When you brush against a cluster of them they let go of their water and it falls on you in a long slow drip, and the sound it makes when it lands on the leaves below is part of what you are hearing.

## The Sound

You cannot get away from the sound.

It is not the sound of wind. It is not the sound of water in any usual sense. It is the sound of a planet that has not yet decided to be quiet, and it has been going on for as long as you have been here, and it will go on for as long as you stay.

There are at least four sources, layered.

The deepest is geological: a low, sustained groan that comes from the rising rock itself. The strata are still settling. They are being squeezed from below and lifted from the side and the result is a sound like a great wooden ship working in heavy seas — a continuous complaint at a frequency you feel more in your sternum than your ears. It is everywhere at once and it has no direction. It is the planet, breathing.

Above that, and almost as constant, is the sound of water. Not falling water — there is no rain here, not yet, though the air is so full of it that you feel it on your skin as a permanent dew. The water is *moving through the living mass*. It is being drawn up by the roots and pushed out by the leaves and it is doing this on a scale so large that the entire plateau is, in effect, a very slow pump. The result is a sound like an enormous wet cloth being slowly wrung, hour after hour, in a great quiet cathedral. There are variations. There are places where the water moves faster — in the channels the plants have made for themselves, in the depressions that fill and brim over — and these places give the sound a kind of voice, a susurrus, a hiss that is almost speech.

The third layer is the plant sound. This is the one that, if you listen carefully, you will find is not quite right. The living mat clicks. Not loudly. Not like a Geiger counter. But the rhizomes are constantly adjusting, sliding against each other by fractions of a millimeter as the plateau tilts under them, and the friction produces a steady tick-tick-tick like a vast clock that has been running for geological time and has no hands. There are louder clicks, too, occasional — a sudden snap as a thick stem gives way under its own weight and a new one takes the strain. The plants do not die when they break. They fork, they reroute, they keep going. The sound of them is the sound of a system that is always almost failing and never quite failing.

The fourth layer is the one you have not yet named, and it is the one that, on certain nights, makes you put your hands over your ears.

It is a tone. A single sustained tone, just at the upper edge of audibility, that comes from everywhere and nowhere. It is not mechanical. It is not organic. It is the hum of a process operating in the tissues of the living mass at a scale you cannot see, a process whose smallest unit is a protein or perhaps something smaller than a protein, and whose largest unit is the entire continent. You have walked for eleven days along the plateau and the tone has been with you every step. It does not modulate. It does not stop. You have begun to suspect that the plants are not plants.

You have not said this aloud. There is no one to say it to.

## The Light

The light is the color of a bruise.

There is a sun — you have seen it once, on the second day, a brief dimming of the overcast where the disc burned through as a coin held behind muslin, and the vegetation around you turned a darker shade of green and the fog lifted for exactly the length of time it took you to draw a breath. Then it was gone. The sky here is a permanent low ceiling, gray-violet, and the light that gets through is the light that has been filtered through a great deal of wet air. Everything is soft-edged. There are no shadows. Or rather, the entire world is a shadow of itself, and the light is the small remaining fraction.

At night it does not get dark. The living mat itself glows, faintly, a pale phosphor green that is enough to walk by. You do not understand why. The plants, you have come to believe, are not entirely made of what plants are made of. There is something in their cells that catches the geological groan and uses it, the way a phosphor catches radiation. The glow pulses, almost imperceptibly, in time with the tone. The whole continent, in the dark, breathes a pale light like a sleeping thing.

## The Air

The air is wetter than any air you have breathed.

It is also cooler than you expected, given the work the living mat is doing. The plants are pulling heat out of the ground to drive their pumping, and the result is a plateau that is always five or ten degrees below the air just above the rim. The fog is not fog. It is the air, made briefly visible by the temperature differential. You are walking through a continuous, slow condensation that never quite resolves into droplets. When you stop moving, the moisture accumulates on your clothes and your pack and the fine hairs of your forearms, and you become, for a while, a small wet version of the place.

The air tastes of peat and of something else, a faint metallic sweetness, like the inside of a battery, or the smell of a storm before it arrives. You breathe it and it fills you. You do not feel sick. You feel *clear*. As if the air were a thought you were having about yourself.

## The Absence

There are no animals.

You have walked for eleven days and you have not seen a bird, an insect, a worm, a fish, a single moving thing that was not you. The plants do not move in any way that an animal would recognize — they grow, they shift, they breathe, they click, but they do not *behave*. There is nothing to eat them. There is nothing to eat. The system is closed, in some way you cannot fully parse, and it runs on water and on whatever it is that the rock below is giving it through the rising heat.

And yet, three nights ago, in the deepest part of the night, you heard something move in the mat. A single, soft displacement, like a small body pushing through wet leaves, and then silence. You held your breath and listened for ten minutes and it did not come again. In the morning you searched the place where the sound had been and found a depression in the living surface, a small bowl, perfectly round, as if something had crouched there and been *absorbed*. Around the bowl the leaves were curled slightly inward, the way leaves curl when a hand has held them. There was a faint warmth rising from the center.

You marked the place. You went back to it the next night. The bowl was still there. The tone was louder there.

## The Impression

You do not feel watched.

You feel *counted*. The way a grain of sand on a beach feels the tide come in. The living mass is aware of you in the way that a city is aware of the people in it: not by attention, but by inclusion. You are a small, warm, unfamiliar pressure on a system that has been under way for longer than you can guess, and the system is, very slowly, taking your measure. The roots beneath your feet have grown around the soles of your boots. The leaves above your head have rearranged themselves so that the few small gaps in the canopy that would let a little extra light through are now above and behind you, so that you are walking in your own private dimness. The fog around you is slightly, almost imperceptibly, thicker than the fog twenty meters away.

You are being made comfortable. You are being made small.

## The Horizon

The plateau goes on. You can see this because the slight rises and folds in the living surface give you, every few hours, a longer view than you had before, and each time the view is the same: a pale gray-green distance that loses itself in its own moisture, and beyond the moisture, sometimes, a darker line where the strata are pushing up. The new mountains. The unfinished mountains. They are not shaped like mountains yet. They are shaped like knuckles, like something clenched. They will be mountains later. They will be the bones of the place. Right now they are just the suggestion.

The scale of the place is not human. You could walk for a year and not cross it. You understand this not from any map — there are no maps, and the plants erase your footprints within hours, the mat rising up behind you as you pass like water closing behind a boat — but from the tone. The tone is the size of a continent. You can hear its edges, if you listen, far away to the north and far away to the south, where it thins and disperses into individual smaller hums and clicks. The tone is the sound of a single very large thing thinking very slowly, and you are inside it, and you are very small, and you are being thought about.

## The Long While

You have been here eleven days.

You will be here longer.

The plateau is patient. The plateau is wet. The plateau is cool and dim and dense and loud, and it is being made, still, by forces that are older than plants and larger than continents, and the plants are part of that making, and you are part of the plants' making, and the tone that is humming through the roots and the rock and the long wet air is, you have come to think, the sound of a process that does not know it is being observed and would not stop if it did.

You set your pack down. The mat receives it. A faint green ripple runs out from the place where the pack settled, a slow wave, and the leaves around it tilt a few degrees to shade it from what little light there is.

You lie down. The mat accepts your weight. Beneath you, somewhere very far down, the rock adjusts. Somewhere very far up, the fog shifts.

The tone holds.

The light pulses.

You close your eyes. The sound continues.
