The basin does not announce itself. You do not come upon it from above or below; you enter it laterally, through a narrows in the ridge where the wind cannot quite reach, and then the world changes all at once.

The first thing is the ceiling. A mass of cloud sits low over the valley, perhaps two thousand feet up, sometimes lower when the weather presses down, a uniform gray that absorbs whatever light might come from above and diffuses it back as a kind of muted everywhere. There is no sun here, not in any meaningful sense. The light has no source. It is simply present — a flat illumination that erases shadows, flattens relief, and makes distances hard to judge. Walking the floor of this valley you cannot tell if the next ridge is two hundred yards or two miles away. Everything reads at the same depth.

The elevation is severe. The ridges around the basin rise steeply, and beyond them the peaks continue climbing until they bury themselves in the same cloud-ceiling that sits over the floor. You cannot see the summits. The basin itself sits at the elevation where the air begins to mean something — where every breath is thinner and colder than the one before, where the body notices it is borrowing against its reserves.

The cold is a fact. It does not sting; it just persists. A temperature the body stops trying to warm itself against and instead learns to occupy. The cold is in the rock, in the moss, in the slow dark water of the rock basins. Even when the sun should be highest, if you could see it, the temperature would not rise above what it is now.

The dampness is a different fact. The air is saturated. Water does not fall here so much as it accumulates. Condensation gathers on every surface as a permanent thin film — on the basalt, on the moss, on the long slow-moving forms of the larger growths. The lichens glisten. The stone sweats. When there is rain, it is rare and heavy and brief, and afterwards the air is washed clean and the whole basin gleams for an hour before the moisture returns and the film re-forms.

The geology reads like an industrial site, although no one built it.

The rock is layered with a regularity the eye keeps wanting to call construction. Horizontal strata of basalt and rhyolite, each band perhaps six inches to two feet thick, stacked for hundreds of vertical feet, weathering out into ribs along the valley walls that look like the floors of an unfinished building, like cooling stacks, like the pages of a geological ledger. Erosion has worked the rock for so long that the layers have settled into their final form — balanced, no longer in transition. Long tongues of scree run down from the ribs in pale gray fans that look poured, that look placed. The mineral veins that cut through the rock are regular in a way that veins should not be — straight lines, repeating distances, parallel courses. They glitter faintly in the dim light: pyrite, chalcopyrite, something darker. They look like wiring.

The basalt dykes that cut vertically across the horizontal strata are hexagonal columns in places — perfect hexagons, fitted together without gaps, looking like furnace stacks or organ pipes. They are not built. They are the result of a slow contraction as the rock cooled, perhaps a billion years ago, perhaps much more recently; the timescales here are uncertain. But the regularity is total. The eye reads them as a built thing because the eye expects regularity to be a sign of intent.

And there are fissures in the rock from which steam emerges, in pulses. Long exhales. The hiss of pressure released, the slow white cloud of vapor drifting out and being absorbed by the wet air. The pulses are regular — thirty seconds, perhaps forty-five, between each one. Some of the fissures have been doing this for so long that the rock around them is stained in concentric rings, like the tide-marks around a fumarole, like the patina on a machine part. The place breathes through these openings.

The vegetation is absurd.

This is where the lushness lives, in defiance of every reason it should. The cold should forbid it. The light should forbid it. The thin air should forbid it. But it is here, in impossible density, colonizing every surface that is not actively steaming.

The floor of the basin is a continuous carpet — not a meadow, not a forest floor, but a carpet, a woven thing, dense and deep, composed of mosses and lichens and the gelatinous leaves of plants that have no good name. The mosses are dark. They are not the bright green of lowland mosses; they are deep green-black, almost the color of the wet rock beneath them, and they hold water in their tissues the way sponges hold water. When you step on them they compress and release a small quantity of dark liquid, and the print you leave is filled in within minutes as the moss slowly re-inflates. There is no walking here without leaving and erasing footprints.

The larger growths — and they are larger, much larger — are not trees. They are tall, fibrous, branching structures that rise along the ridges and the broken cliff faces, perhaps forty feet tall. Their trunks are bundles of vertical fibers, dark and wet, that look almost muscular in their bundled arrangement, and they sway without wind because of slow contractions that run through the fibers in waves. The branching is regular. Each fork happens at the same angle. The result is a uniform geometry across the whole population, as if they were all growing to the same plan.

The leaves are gelatinous and they glisten. They are large, sometimes a foot across, and they hang downward under their own weight, the color of the mosses and the rock, dark green-black, almost translucent at the edges, and when the ambient light catches them they shine briefly and then go dull. The leaves are how these growths drink. They condense moisture on their surfaces and let it run down into the bundle-fibers of the trunk, and the trunk carries it down to the root mass at the base — a tangled dark knot that grips the rock and refuses to let go.

There is no wind here, but the growths sway. The sway is peristaltic — a wave that starts at the tip of a branch and runs down toward the trunk, then up another branch, then down again. It is slow. You can watch it for minutes and not see the wave move. But if you look away and look back, the branches have shifted. The whole forest is engaged in a slow coordinated movement that is somewhere between respiration and circulation.

These growths do not flower. They do not seed visibly. They appear in regular intervals along the ridges, almost as if planted, almost as if the rock has decided where they should be and they have agreed. The pattern is too regular. The intervals are too even. It reads as cultivation. It reads as agriculture. It reads, somehow, as industry.

The fauna is here, but you have to know how to see it.

The abundance is real. The place is full of life. But the life is quiet, slow, embedded, and dark, and it does not call attention to itself through sound.

In the moss carpet there are things. Small things, the size of a thumb, sometimes larger, that move when you are not watching and freeze when you are. They are dark. They are soft-bodied. They look almost like slugs but they are not slugs; their bodies have a regularity of segmentation that slugs do not have, a kind of jointed articulation, and they leave trails that are not slime but something finer, a thin dark line the moss absorbs within an hour. They are everywhere. Step anywhere and within a few minutes the moss around you is moving, slowly, in many directions. The whole carpet is shifting.

In the rock pools — and there are rock pools, shallow basins carved in the basalt where the infrequent rains collect and then sit for weeks before evaporating — there are other things. They are translucent. They have the consistency of thick jelly. They pulse, very faintly, with a rhythm that is not quite the rhythm of the steam fissures and not quite the rhythm of the swaying growths, but close enough to suggest that all these rhythms are related. They drift in the dark water. They feed on the moss that grows down into the pools from the edges. They are abundant. They are silent. In the dim light they have a faint internal luminescence that you only see if you are very close and very still.

Higher up, on the ridges, in the broken cliff faces, there are larger things. They are dark and they move in the fissures and the caves. You do not see them whole. You see parts of them — a slow motion in the corner of the eye, a wet gleam in a shadow, the brief appearance of an angular dark shape against the gray rock. They are perhaps the size of a large dog, perhaps larger. They are quiet. They are numerous. They are not in a hurry. They are the predators of this place, or the scavengers, or both. They live in the cracks. They emerge at intervals you cannot predict and they do things you cannot see.

The dynamic of this place is the dynamic of a slow engine.

The steam pulses. The growths sway. The moss shifts. The things in the pools pulse. The things in the rocks emerge and recede. The water condenses and drips. The lichens grow. Everything is happening. Nothing is loud. The sound of the place is the sound of an industrial site heard from a great distance, or heard from inside a closed room — a hiss, a slow creak, the faint tick of things settling and resettling, the distant rumble of something deep and large shifting in its sleep. You have to listen for a long time to hear any of it, and what you hear is never quite enough to confirm that you heard it.

The light is the light of a place where light has been filtered through cloud and through time and through the bodies of many things. It is dim. It is gray. It is even. It has no source and no direction. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. In this light, colors are muted and shapes are softened, and the world looks like a photograph that has been slightly overexposed and then slightly underexposed at the same time.
