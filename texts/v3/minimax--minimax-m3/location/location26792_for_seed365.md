The cleft opens like a wound that has not decided whether to heal. Its walls are sharp and pale — basalt, granite, the local stone — freshly broken, the kind of edges that cut anyone who leans against them. They rise on either side into a sky that is not really a sky but a brighter grey. From floor to where the walls taper together, perhaps forty meters; along the length of the place, perhaps ninety. A cathedral of broken rock, recently made.

This is a high place. The air is thin and cool and damp — the kind of damp that settles on skin and does not leave. There is no real weather here, only the slow exhalation of mist that drifts in over the broken upper edge and pools along the floor in pale drifts that vanish by midmorning and return by midafternoon. It rains sometimes. The locals will tell you when, if you can find one awake and willing. Most of the moisture arrives by condensation, by the breath of the cleft itself, which sweats in the cool air.

The stones are young. Some slow uplift, perhaps, or a glacial retreat. The edges have not been rounded. Where moss has begun to colonize, it has done so in thin pale sheets that follow the fracture lines, like maps of rivers that have not yet learned to be rivers. Ferns push out of cracks at improbable angles — ladder ferns, sword ferns, their fronds reaching across the stonework as if tasting the air. The vegetation is the loudest thing here, if loud has any meaning in a place so quiet. Liverworts and selaginella crowd the wet shelves. A pale lichen hangs in long strands from the higher rocks, swaying without wind.

In the floor of the cleft, a settlement.

Three buildings, perhaps four, depending on how you count the long low one that is half buried in the moss. They are built of the same broken stone as the walls, dry-stacked into walls that look grown rather than made. Thatch of something soft and grey — not straw, not reed, something that grows here and nowhere else. The doorways are low and dark; a person enters bent at the waist. Smoke comes from one chimney, but the fire is small. The people of this place live small. They live low. They live among things larger than themselves.

Between the buildings, emptiness. The cleft is mostly stone and moss and air and the things that live in it. The buildings stand among the stones like stones.

The fauna is the strangeness, and the fauna is overwhelming.

There are no birds. There is no birdsong in the morning. There are no wings overhead. Instead — and at first you do not see them, because they are made for not being seen — there are many small soft bodies pressed against the stone. They are the color of lichen, the color of wet basalt, the color of thatch. They are flatter than they should be, with limbs that grip and do not release. They do not chirp. They do not call. They shift in the moss and you hear nothing. When something moves among them, you hear that — the disturbance — but it is small.

There are a great many of them.

The walls breathe with them. The roof of the thatched building breathes. When a person walks the path between two houses, the path moves underfoot, lightly, like a rug of slow things. They are not dangerous, perhaps. They are not pests, perhaps. The locals do not speak of them. They step carefully and do not speak of them. They leave certain stones unturned.

There are larger ones too, deeper in the cleft, where the light fails further. You can see their shapes only as absences in the moss — places where the moss does not quite grow. They breathe. That is the only sound: a slow respiration, in and out, from the deepest shadow. Their eyes, when the eyes are visible, do not reflect. They are warm. The stones beneath them are warm, in a place where the stones are not warm anywhere else.

The settlement is built on top of them. The locals know this. They put their hearths in the warmest places.

What the larger ones eat, no one has explained, or no one has asked. The small ones eat the moss. The moss eats the stone. The stone, sharp and young, is being unmade — slowly, patiently — by the very things that live on it. In a hundred years the cleft will be rounder. In a thousand it will be a meadow. The locals know this too, and so each spring they repair their walls with fresh-broken stone hauled down from higher up, before the frost loosens it. Their buildings are always being rebuilt. Their buildings are always being eaten. They have made a kind of peace with this.

The light is dim. It is mid-morning in the world outside, perhaps; here, the sun never reaches the floor directly. It arrives as a glow, as a milkiness, as a suggestion. The eye adjusts. The eye begins to see in shades of grey and green and the wet black of stone. Time moves differently here. Things are watched that have not yet decided what they are.

It is quiet.

Even the wind, when it comes in over the broken upper edge, does not speak. It enters as mist. It enters and disappears. You stand in the cleft and you feel the cool damp air against your face and you do not hear anything. You see the moss. You see the lichen swaying. You see the buildings with their small smoke. You see the path move underfoot as you walk it, and you do not speak of it, because there is nothing to say, and because saying it would break the quiet, and the quiet is the only thing here that is not strange.
