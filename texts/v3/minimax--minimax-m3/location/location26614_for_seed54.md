The plateau sits at the bottom of a sky that no one has ever seen the top of. The air thins here until it stops pretending to be air at all, and what moves across the surface of things is closer to a slow liquid — something the cold has squeezed out of it, all the noise and rush taken away, leaving only weight and a grey pearl-light that comes from nowhere and goes nowhere. The light is not light so much as the absence of darker. It rests on the structures like soot.

The structures are everywhere. They do not stop at the horizon because the plateau does not have a horizon — it has only more plateau, curving away under the low sky, layered with construction after construction, stacked and sloped and tunnelled and bridged until the geography of the place has become a kind of argument between the rock and what has been built on top of it. You cannot tell which is older. The rock weeps — long pale seams of condensation running down its face in the manner of an old building's pipes sweating — and where it weeps, things are growing. Not plants. Nothing rooted, nothing green, nothing that asks for sun. Things more like the rock itself, mineral and patient, building themselves out of the water that the cold leaves behind. They crust the walls in overlapping plates. They drip from the undersides of bridges in curtains of pale papery material that rustles when anything moves past.

The fauna is what you hear first, before you understand you are hearing it.

It is in the walls. It is under the platforms. It moves through every joint and seam of the construction the way water moves through a sponge — except that it is loud. A constant low industrial hiss, like the inside of a generator room heard from a mile off, but broken by sharper sounds: the click of small hard bodies against hard surfaces, the scrape of chitin (if it is chitin; it has the sound of chitin), the occasional high trill that rises and dies and is answered by another trill three structures away and then by seventeen more, so that for a moment the air above the plateau is strung with cross-talk that has no human shape to it. The fauna is the size of a fist, mostly, or the size of a thumb, mostly. It is dark and it is pale. It is so numerous that the buildings seem to breathe. When you stand on a platform and look down into the lower strata you see the surfaces move with a fine continuous shimmer, as if the city has been wrapped in a black cloth that is twitching. They are not afraid of you. They have never heard of you. They were here when the city was finished, or the city was finished around them, or they are what the city was finished *for* — the distinction is not clear and not important to them.

The cold is the kind of cold that does not bite. It sits. It is patient. It is a cold that has been cold for a long time and intends to keep being cold. Your breath does not plume here because the air is too thin for that kind of display; instead it condenses against the nearest surface in a thin film that the fauna come to drink. You can watch them — if you stand very still and let your eyes adjust to the pearl-grey dark — a slow tide of small dark shapes flowing up a wall to a wet patch and flowing back down. The wall is glistening with them. They go about their business in the same unhurried way the water goes about its dripping, in the same unhurried way the erosion goes about its slow patient cutting of new channels down the sides of the platforms into the plain below. Nothing here is in a hurry. The place is being made. You are watching it be made.

The technology of the construction is not a thing you can read. The surfaces are not stone and not metal and not any polymer you have a name for. They are smooth in places, faceted in others, in some places scarred by long pale lines that might be tool marks or might be something else entirely — channels scored into the material by something that knew exactly what shape it wanted the rock to be in ten thousand years. The platforms are fitted together with a precision that has nothing to do with rivets or welds. The seams are not seams. If you press your hand to one you can feel something running through it, a faint warmth that is not warmth, a vibration that is not vibration — something the rock is doing on its own, the way a heart does. Some of the structures hum. Some of them do not. The ones that hum are louder, and the fauna is thicker on them, and the condensation on them runs in patterns that almost resolve into writing if you watch long enough.

There is no road. There is no street. There are walkways — narrow pale strips between the constructions that are not paved but worn, the rock beneath them polished to a soft gleam by the passage of the fauna and the slow patient trickle of meltwater and the long slow shuffling of whatever larger things also move through here, of which there are fewer but which you can sometimes hear at night — and the walkways branch and rebranch and join again the way capillaries do, and if you follow one for long enough you will eventually arrive at a place where the constructions thin out and the rock is more itself, where the erosion is winning, where new channels are being cut by meltwater into raw stone that has not yet been built upon, where the fauna is sparser and the silence between the sounds of the fauna is wider. These places are cold in a different way. They are unfinished. They are the leading edge of the place — the place is still being made, here, you can watch it. A channel a metre wide one season will be two metres wide the next time you come back, if you come back in ten years. The meltwater is patient. The cold is patient. The fauna will follow the water in, eventually, and the construction will follow the fauna. You are watching the edge of a city that is still being written onto the rock, slowly, in water and in the small dark shapes that drink the water and in the technology that no one now alive built and that no one now alive can read.

You have been standing here a long time. Your eyes have adjusted. You can see, in the pearl-grey dark, that the sky is full of slow things — specks drifting across the upper air, too high to be fauna, too low to be stars. The wind, where there is wind, carries them along. They make no sound. They are very cold. You do not know what they are.

You will not stay long. Nothing stays long. The fauna will be here. The meltwater will be here. The construction will still be patient in the dark, humming in places, silent in others, being added to in the places where the rock is still raw. The cold will keep its seat. The place will go on making itself, slowly, in the dark, for as long as the dark lasts.
