A small hollow beneath the skin of somewhere. The opening you came through — a crack, a root-channel, a place where the surface has not yet committed to being surface — admits you into a temperature that drops by degrees as you descend, and a light that is already failing, and a wetness that is not a wetness you recognize.

The hollow is the size of a room. A small room. The ceiling is low where the plant matter has thickened, higher in places where it has slumped or not yet grown. The floor is not a floor. It is a mass of the same pale, fibrous substance that makes the walls and the ceiling, layered and felted, damp, breathing faintly under your weight if you put weight on it. The geometry is the geometry of growth. There are no straight lines. There are curves that are almost architectural, almost deliberate, and then they soften, they waver, they become the shape of slow accumulation. The hollow has not been here long. It has been here a long time. Both of these are true.

The walls are made of plants. This is the first thing to understand, and the last. Not plants in a room. Not plants growing in a room. The room is the plants. The hollow is a space held open inside a living tissue that has, for centuries or for millennia, been laying itself down in pale strata, each layer a fraction of a millimeter thicker than the last, each layer the memory of a slow season. The material is something between root and mycelium and a thing you have no name for. It is the color of bone, of wax, of the underside of certain shelf fungi when they are very young. It is fibrous, layered, perforated with galleries and channels that go nowhere, that go everywhere, that connect to the air and to other channels and to the spaces where the fauna live. It holds water the way a sponge holds water, but more — the water is not just in the pores. The water is in the fibers themselves, in the chemistry of them, in the long slow climb from somewhere far below, drawn up by capillary and by something else, something that is not quite capillary, that moves the water against the gradient, that puts the wetness into the upper walls and the ceiling and the highest galleries.

The cold is total. It is the kind of cold that is not a temperature so much as a substance, a thing you move through. The air in the lower parts of the hollow is below the freezing point of water but the water has not frozen. It hangs in beads on the fibers, it trembles at the edge of phase change, it waits. Above, where the plant-walls are thinnest, where the breath of the place is most active, the temperature is just above the threshold, and a fine mist drifts through the hollow, slow, almost motionless, a fog that you can push aside with the back of your hand. Your breath, when you exhale, makes a small cloud that is reluctant to disperse. The cold is a wet cold. It condenses on your skin. It makes a sheen on every surface. It is in the fibers and in the air and in you, if you stay long enough.

The light is dim and has so many sources that it seems to have none. The walls glow. A faint, sourceless luminescence runs through the plant matter, a pale green-blue, the color of deep-sea organisms, of radium-paint, of the gills of fungi that grow in the dark. It is not enough to see by in the way you are used to seeing. It is enough to see the shapes of things, the sheen of water on every surface, the slow movement of what moves. Some of the fauna carry brighter lights in their bodies — points of yellow, points of violet, points of a color you cannot name — and these move through the felt of the walls, through the mist, in long, patient trajectories. The light in the hollow shifts as you move your head, but it also shifts when you do not move your head. The walls pulse. Faintly. Almost imperceptibly. A rhythm that is not quite a rhythm.

The fauna is abundant. The walls are not solid. They are a city, threaded with galleries made and remade by the things that live inside them. Pale worms, the thickness of a thumb, move through the substrate in slow peristaltic waves, surfacing sometimes into the air of the hollow, regarding nothing, descending again. There are things with too many legs and no eyes that traverse the walls in careful, deliberate lines, leaving trails. There are things that look like insects and are not — translucent wings, folded, unfolded, that catch the glow and throw it back. The air is full of small things. Gnats. Mites. Almost-invisible motes that drift and pivot and form brief eddies in the mist. They do not flee from you. They continue. A pale worm surfaces near your knee. It has no face. It waits, or it does not wait, and then it descends into the felt.

The sound is the first thing you understand, even before the light. The hollow is loud, but the loudness is not a loudness of volume. It is a loudness of density, of layering, of many small sources summing into a single sustained texture. There is the constant drip-drip-drip of water from a hundred points on the ceiling, each drop a small bell with its own pitch, each landing a small percussion in a small pool or on a small leaf. There is the wet hum of the plants themselves — not breathing exactly, but something metabolic, a low tone you feel in your teeth before you hear it, that varies as you move through the space, louder in some galleries than others, almost silent in others. There is the chewing. A sound like a thousand small jaws working in the fibers. There is the clicking. A sound like seeds in a dry gourd, but not dry, never dry. There is a soft persistent static that might be the sound of countless tiny bodies moving against countless tiny fibers, the sound of friction at a scale you are not built to perceive but that is, in the aggregate, a roar.

And beneath all of it, if you stay long enough, you begin to hear the slow sound. The one that has no easy name. The sound of the place doing what the place does. The sound of a process, or a calculation, or a long thought being thought at a speed and a scale that has nothing to do with the speed of your life. The technology of the hollow is not built. It is grown. It is a technology of fiber and water and cold and patient time. It does things that the physics you carry in your body does not describe. The water moves uphill, in the fibers, in the walls, in defiance of the gradient. The light falls in directions that light should not fall. The fauna pause, sometimes, all of them at once, in a unison that is not coordination, that is not communication in any sense you would recognize, and then they resume. The mist moves in patterns that are not quite random and not quite ordered. The geometry of the hollow is wrong, but only by a little, the way a dream is wrong. Nothing is broken. Nothing is wrong. The place is simply operating on a set of rules that is adjacent to yours but not identical, and the difference is everywhere, in every detail, and you cannot point to any one of them and say there, that is the impossible thing, because the impossible thing is the whole.

The hollow is not finished. It will not be finished in any timeframe you would recognize as a timeframe. The walls will lay down another stratum in the next season, in the next century, in the next span of time that is too long for you to hold in your head. The fauna will establish another gallery, will move through the felt in a new pattern, will pause and unpause in a new unison. The water will find another path. The long calculation will take another input, will produce, at some distant and barely-imagined horizon, an output that the place has been working toward since before you arrived, and will continue working toward after you leave. The place is emerging. It is in the long, slow middle act of becoming, the part that takes so long that it looks, to anything in a hurry, like a kind of permanence. It is not permanence. It is patience. The patience of fiber. The patience of water. The patience of a cold that has nowhere to be.

You sit in the hollow, if there is room to sit. The plant-walls give under you. They are softer than you expect. Water seeps from them where you press, and the fauna do not flee. They continue. A small flying thing lands on your hand and waits, luminous, patient, and after a long time flies away. The hollow is loud. The hollow is dim. The hollow is wet and cold and alive with a slowness that has nothing to do with death. It is being made. It is being thought. It is being, in a way that you can almost understand, if you stay long enough, and that you will never understand, even if you stay forever.

You leave, or you do not. Either way, the hollow continues. The crack you came through seals itself, in time, with new growth. The entrance is forgotten. The light continues to fall in directions that light should not fall. The water continues to climb. The walls continue to thicken. The sound continues, the loud, layered, sustained sound of a small place in the middle of becoming something that is not, and will not be, and is.
