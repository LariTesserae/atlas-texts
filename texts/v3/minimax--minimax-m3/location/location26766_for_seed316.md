# The Sounding Flats

You stand where the continent has finished dying. Not a place of dramatic ruin — there was no war here, no flood, no catastrophe. Just time. So much time that the mountains wore themselves down to suggestions, and what is left is flatness so extreme it has become a kind of architecture. The horizon is broken only by the humps of things that were once ranges, now so rounded they resemble sleeping animals buried up to their spines.

## The Ground

The soil is mostly water. Warm, dark, breathing. Each step produces a soft exhalation, a release of marsh gas from soil that has been saturated since before human memory invented itself. It is peat, but the peat has finished becoming peat — it is something older, a substance that has cycled through enough decay and regrowth that it has become uniform, a black-brown paste spread over what was once a watershed. Underneath this layer the original minerals have been leached into a soft kaolin paste, white in places, oozing between your toes if you stand too long.

Stumps of ancient trees are everywhere. They are not upright. They lie half-buried, fused into the peat, their rings no longer readable, their bark long since converted to a spongy mat that mushrooms grow from in pale crowns. Some of these trunks are enormous — wide as rooms, longer than buildings — but they are weightless now, waterlogged almost to buoyancy, and a strong push would set them sliding. Nothing pushes them. They have been settling for a million years.

## Vegetation

Vegetation here is not so much present as *insisted upon*. The land is so flat, so wet, so warm, that growth never stops, but it cannot find purchase either. Everything is low. Ground cover of sphagnum and sundew — the sundew here is enormous, the size of dinner plates, perpetually beaded with digestive mucus, perpetually digesting. There are few trees. Where they exist they are stunted, bonsai by pressure of climate, with full-sized root systems compressed under mounded hummocks that rise from the marsh like the backs of whales.

The canopy, such as it is, hovers at knee height. To walk through it is to be in a forest turned sideways, a thicket that exists as ceiling and as floor simultaneously. Seedlings sprout directly on top of older growth; ferns unfurl from the bodies of their parents.

## Water and Air

The air is wetter than water. It precipitates onto every surface within minutes of exposure. You cannot stay dry. The condensation forms on skin, on clothing, on the inside of nostrils and ears. The water here is mostly above your ankles and mostly below your knees — a depth chosen by the land itself rather than by any topography.

The light is dim and greenish, filtered through a billion tons of atmospheric moisture and the low ceiling of vegetation. There is no direct sun, ever. The brightest moment of day might be the moment a cloud thins overhead and a slight increase in luminance registers, briefly, before it thickens again.

And the sky is not absent — it is *somewhere up there* — but you can rarely see it. You move through a soft brown-green dark, with the light coming from nowhere in particular.

## The Sound

The sound is what you remember first. Even before the wet, even before the smell. A sound so constant and so all-directional that you stop noticing it within minutes, and then it becomes the silence of your life.

It is a roar. A low, tectonic, unending roar.

Not water. Not wind exactly. It is the sound of the surface of the land itself vibrating, tens of millions of insect bodies pressed so close together that their individual motions have become a single emission. Beetles, mostly. Larvae in the peat. Midge swarms so dense they have weight and weather. Cicadas emerging on schedules so deep in evolutionary time that they no longer correspond to anything a year could measure.

When you step, the noise briefly parts around you — a pocket of relative quiet — and then closes again. Your footsteps are absorbed into the roar the way a stone sinks into soup.

There is another sound underneath. A deep, slow percussive *thud-thud-thud*, irregular, like the heartbeat of something the size of a country. It is the peat itself. Continents of saturated organic matter compress and release, compress and release, on timescales that have nothing to do with seasons. The flat is breathing. You are standing on a living lung.

## The Built

You find, here and there, things that are not peat.

A concrete post, leaning, its surface colonized by moss so thick it has lost its angles. A rusted iron ring, set in a post that has nearly sunk into the ground. A paved footpath that ends in the middle of the marsh for no discernible reason and resumes three hundred meters later with no road between — as though the path was laid down by someone who had not yet agreed on where it should go.

A bridge, half: two abutments of poured concrete and a span of nothing between, with vegetation already old across the gap. The bridge was never finished. The land won.

These structures are not in ruins — they are simply *outlived*. Humans stood here once and raised things; the things were absorbed, the humans are not mentioned. There are no buildings. There is one long collapsed greenhouse whose frame is still intact, furred with growth, its glass long since replaced by leaves. You step through its doorless doorframe and into a cathedral of ferns.

## Fauna

Animals are scarce but visible. They have learned the rules of this place, which are not the rules of any other place.

A heron, enormous, standing motionless in the distance. It does not move. You watch it for what feels like a long time and then realize it was not there in the first place — it was a snag of wood, standing where a heron had stood the day before, in the same posture.

Something moves at the edge of vision, low and quick — an otter, or a creature that has otter in its ancestry but also many other things. It is briefly there and then gone, with a V-shaped wake closing behind it on the still water.

A frog, the color of muddy gold, sits on a half-sunken log and watches you with no urgency. It has lived a long time. It will continue to live a long time. Nothing here dies on schedule.

There are no fish in the wet. There are no mammals either, except the otters-things. The trophic energy of this place is locked into its invertebrate base. What walks here walks softly or not at all.

## The Weird

Here is what is strange, and it is the strange that holds the place together:

The flat is *large* in a way that approaches its own kind of country. It has climate. It has weather. There are rains that begin at one edge and travel across the land at the pace of a person walking, advancing as a wall of condensation, and the creatures of the flat know to move ahead of them or to turn and let them pass, as a fish turns in current.

There are migrations of midges so vast they darken what little light exists, and after a migration passes, the place is brighter by some small fraction, and warmer, and you realize the insects had been a kind of weatherproofing.

There are *cold spots*. You walk into one and the temperature drops and the roar quiets and for a moment it is like stepping into an interior room — a small bubble of still air, perhaps ten meters across, where the peat is older still, where the insects do not go. There is no warning, no border you can see. You step in, you feel the silence, you step out. The roar returns.

Once, only once on your walk — and you cannot be sure it happened — you see a tree stand upright. Tall, straight, lit from within by some light that has no source. It is gone when you look again. Or perhaps it was never a tree. Perhaps it was a stand of old wood arranged by currents, briefly catching the dim sky at the right angle. You walk on. The marsh closes behind your steps.

## Turbulence

Nothing here is at rest, though everything seems still. The ground is sinking, slowly, under its own accumulating weight. Water is flowing in sheets too thin to see, finding its way to the lowest places, which are always shifting. Trees are falling, roots heaving up, the canopy reconfiguring in decades you could never count. The peat is bubbling — bubbles of marsh gas rise and pop silently against the underside of the mist. Something is always rotting. Something is always growing. The two processes are not in balance and never have been; they are simply in negotiation.

This is not a place that will end. It is a place that will become *more itself* for so long that you, and the continents you came from, will be remembered — if at all — only as the thickness of one muddy layer in the peat, fossil leaves and iron rings and the long bones of herons, all converted by patient, deafening, patient, deafening time into the same dark, warm, breathing paste.

You stand in it now. It closes around your ankles. It hums.

It hums.
