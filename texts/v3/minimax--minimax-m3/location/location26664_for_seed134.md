# The Forge-Seamount

The mountain is new. You can feel it in the sharpness of everything — in the way the rock has not yet learned to be smooth. Black basaltic glass rises in splintered ridges from the seafloor, folding upward into a single cathedral-sized prominence that breaches the upper water column but does not, will not, reach the air. It is a thing the ocean made recently and is still in the act of making. Cool currents drag along its flanks at walking pace, patient with the place, and where they meet the glass the water lifts into a constant low roar — a sound like a great slow breath drawn through a steel throat.

The peak itself is a jagged crown of folded lava, frozen mid-flow into spines and cusps and overlapping curtains of obsidian. Light falls on it from above in long green shafts, broken and re-broken by the surface chop far overhead. The illumination is enough to read by, but everything is tinted — every surface wears a thin filter of blue-green, the color of the medium itself. The volcanic glass, where the light catches it, returns that color doubled and twisted: deeper blue in the cracks, almost white on the upturned lips of flow-fronts, an oily iridescence where thin films of precipitate have begun to gather on the youngest faces. There is no green of plant. No moss, no kelp, no algae. The rock is bare and newborn and the only things that grow here grow in the manner of machines.

The fauna is dense. It crowds every surface and saturates the water in layered strata above and around the peak. It is wrong fauna — wrong in the way that a dream of a factory is wrong. The creatures here are not soft. They clatter. They have segmented bodies of silicate and iron sulphide, jointed like linkages, plated like casings. They drift in long rotating columns around the upper spines, their collective motion producing a steady grinding hum, the way a mill hums when it is full of grain. They cling to the glassy faces in sheets, click against each other when currents shift, and from deep within the clustered bodies comes a continuous faint ringing — like struck metal, but prolonged, almost tonal. They reproduce. They build themselves. They were not, however, built by anyone. They are the way this place *does* industry: a self-assembling ecology of mineral forms that eat the vent-chemistry and the trace metals in the water and slowly accrete into more of themselves. The seamount is a factory floor, and the factory floor is alive.

The water is loud. Sound moves through it the way heat moves through iron — fast, far, almost without loss. The whole ridge rings with it. There is the roar of current over the upper spines, the chorus of the grinding fauna, the regular tick of thermal cracking where the youngest flows are still settling against each other in the dark below, and beneath everything a deep thrum — the magma chamber, far down, breathing. No single sound dominates. The place is full to the brim with noise, layered, but never sharp. The loudness is patient. The loudness is the slow machinery of a young world doing its work.

The temperature is cool — not cold, not warm. A held breath. It carries the smell, if smell could travel here, of iron and sulphur and ozone, and the water is heavy with suspended mineral: a faint glitter when you turn your head into a light shaft, motes of oxide drifting like snow in a town where the lamps never go out.

The fauna density creates the architecture. In the cracks and the leeward sides of the spines, generations of them have layered into something that resembles scaffolding — porous, mineral, humming. They are near-touching. Where one ends another begins. Their forms vary: some are small and quick, darting between strata with a buzz like a thrown file; some are vast and slow, anchored to the rock, pulsing with a low mechanical rhythm that you feel in your chest cavity as much as you hear. They are not aggressive. They are not gentle either. They simply are, in great number, doing what they do — which is to convert heat and chemistry and light into more of themselves, slowly, forever.

Above the peak, the water column thins into brighter blue. Below it, the glass gives way to rubble fields, then to the slow slopes of the seamount's apron, then to darkness. The transition is gradual. Even at the upper limit, where light is strongest, the place keeps its industrial sound. Nothing here is silent. Nothing here is young in the way a garden is young. It is young the way a forge is young — full of heat, full of noise, full of the patient making of things that will outlast the maker.

The rules seem to be these: the mountain grows a little each century; the fauna grow a little each season; the currents carry their sound outward in widening rings that no one answers. Time here is slow but audible. If you sit still on a ledge of the glass — if a thing could sit still — you would hear the whole operation as one long held chord, and the chord would not change, and the place would not notice you, and the light would keep falling in green shafts across the humming iron-colored crowd.
