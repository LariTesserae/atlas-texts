# The Alchemical City of Veth Khariv

I begin with what I cannot see: a sound. A low, breathing hum, the way a transformer station sounds to a child sleeping three streets away — except here it is the city's pulse, never stopping, never quite resolving into silence. It rises through every wall and pier. When the wind shifts in the long corridors between buildings, the hum tilts, almost a note, almost music, and then settles again.

## The Setting

Veth Khariv is built into the south face of a mountain that should not, by rights, be habitable. The elevation is a thing of permanent headache and thinner air; newcomers arrive gasping and red-faced, and the locals have a word for them — *the flushers* — which they use with affection. The mountain itself is a stack of pale, almost luminous limestone, stratified like the pages of a book left out in the weather, and the city has been carved into and onto it for so long that the two are no longer distinguishable. There is no ground. There is no substrate. Every surface you stand on, walk along, lean against, was placed there by some hand, some dynasty, some committee, some desperate individual with a chisel and a vision.

The city extends in a single curving shelf — a half-moon of habitation that wraps the peak at roughly the 3,400-meter mark. From a distance, against the bright high sky, it looks like a honeycomb pressed into the rock, or like a great barnacle colony: layered, dense, gleaming. The shelf is not flat. It steps. It shelves again. It corkscrews. Buildings rise on buildings, bridges span nothing and land on something, stairways lead to rooms that lead to other stairways. There is no obvious street plan because there was no obvious street plan to begin with — the city grew where the rock allowed growth, and where the rock did not allow it, the rock was persuaded.

## The Material World

Everything is made. The stone is dressed but not dressed-up — chisel marks remain, sometimes deliberate, sometimes just the way the work stopped. The mortar between the older blocks is a dark reddish compound that the locals call *mother-set*, and no one can quite reproduce it, though the masons try. Newer construction uses a pale cement that almost glows in the afternoon light, so the city shows its age in palette as well as shape. The oldest quarters are rust-colored and seem to drink the sun; the newest quarter, the one that grew up around the cable station in the last sixty years, is the color of cream and almost too bright to look at.

Wood appears only in shutters and in the floors of the uppermost residences, where the air is dry enough to keep it. Metal is everywhere — in pipes, in brackets, in the riveted seams of the great cistern-towers. The metal is mostly copper, gone green and black with patina, and the patina is treated as ornament. Some houses wear their pipework on the outside like a nervous system, the joints wrapped in cloth and tar, the vertical runs strapped to walls with iron bands. From a balcony, looking down into a courtyard, you see a forest of pipes and ducts in dark green, threaded between windows, climbing walls, diving through arches.

## The Water

There is not a great deal of water here, but the city is obsessed with it, has always been, will always be. Moisture comes in three forms: the heavy seasonal rains that arrive like a held breath finally released, the year-round condensation that gathers on every cold surface and drips, drips, drips into the gutters and the cistern-mouths, and the meltwater from the small glacier that still clings to the very top of the peak above the city. The glacier is sacred and dwindling. The city's industrial fortunes depend on it. The two facts are not discussed together.

You hear the water before you see it. A constant, layered trickling runs through the whole city — from gutter-spouts into clay channels, from channels into the cistern-towers, from the cistern-towers through the metered mains into the houses, where it sings in the pipes when someone opens a tap. The public basins in the squares are always full, always slightly overfull, and the overflow runs in shallow carved ruts along the paving stones, so that to walk through the lower squares is to walk through a thin sheet of moving water, ankle-deep in some places, that your foot splashes quietly as you go. Children play in it. Cats drink from it. Old people sit with their chairs pulled just out of its reach and watch it go by.

The smell of the place, especially in the early morning before the sun has cleared the eastern ridge, is of wet stone and of something sweeter, almost floral — the lichen that grows on the north-facing walls, fed by the condensation, releases a faint perfume when it is damp. By midday the stone is dry and the perfume fades, and the air smells of copper, of cooking oil, of the distant chemical sharpness of the refineries in the lower quarter.

## The Vegetation

Because the elevation is extreme and the natural soil is, again, a thing that does not exist here, the plants are all brought plants, all tended plants, all of them part of the city's project of asserting itself against the rock. They are everywhere. They are not naturalized. They are *kept*.

The rooftops are gardens. Almost every flat surface that catches sun and rain has been planted. You walk through the city looking up, not down, because the views are all in the upper registers: fig trees trained against walls, their pale leaves trembling; grapevines on pergolas that throw shifting lace-shadows on the paving; pots of basil and rosemary lined up on the sills of even the most cramped workers' rooms. There is a cultivar of dwarf pomegranate, pink-flowered, that has become the unofficial emblem of the city, and it appears in window-boxes, in courtyard planters, trained up the risers of stairs. In late summer the pomegranates actually fruit, small and sour, and the children eat them with salt.

The public gardens are another matter. These are the showpieces: terraced beds cut into the rock, watered by the channeled meltwater, planted with roses and oleander and a fierce magenta bougainvillea that the climate, against all reason, has allowed to thrive. In the evening, when the light is low, the bougainvillea appears to glow from within. The terraces are connected by stone stairs with worn treads, and the stairs are the places where the city comes to see and be seen — old men walking slowly down for their dinner, lovers walking slowly up for no reason at all, a child in a too-large coat chasing a chicken that has escaped from somewhere.

## The Fauna

The city does not lack for creatures, but they are almost all commensal — the pigeons, the cats, the small brown lizards that live in the walls and eat the insects that come for the moisture. The pigeons are a particular feature: they are kept, in a sense, though no one admits to keeping them. The rooftops have their own pigeon-coops, and the pigeon-keepers, who are an entire recognized profession, communicate with one another across the city in a complicated semaphore of flags and shouts. Pigeon-racing is the second religion.

The cats are more mysterious. They are not fed so much as they simply *are*, a baseline population that fluctuates but never disappears. They sit on windowsills and judge you. They appear on stair landings when you are troubled and seem to know. The story is that the cats belong to the glacier, and the glacier sent them down to keep the city company, and this is, of course, not true, but it is not not-true either.

The larger fauna — there are a few — are kept in the upper pastures above the city, where the snow line has retreated to. Goats, mostly, and a small number of the shaggy mountain ponies that the courier companies use. You can sometimes hear the bells of the goats from the upper terraces, a faint and melancholy tinkling that is part of the city's soundtrack as much as the hum of the refineries.

There is also the thing in the lower cistern. No one speaks of it. It is large. It moves at night. The water in the morning is slightly disturbed.

## The Industry

Veth Khariv is an industrial city, though it does not look like the ones in the picturebooks. There are no smokestacks, or rather, there are no smokestacks taller than the highest building, because the city has an ordinance. The refineries — and there are several, for the processing of the rare-earth ore that comes down from the mines even higher on the peak — are built into the rock itself, their flues venting through bored tunnels that emerge far above the city, in the thin clean air near the glacier. From below you see only the intake towers, which are tall and cylindrical and made of the same cream-colored stone as the houses, so they do not look industrial at all. They look like minarets, or like the watchtowers of a vanished kingdom, which in a sense they are.

The work of the city goes on. Ore arrives by aerial tram from the upper mines — you can see the cables, thin and dark against the bright sky, the buckets gliding up and down at all hours. The refineries process it. The slag is dumped, still warm, into the shaping-yard at the city's lowest point, where it cools in great slabs that are later cut and sold. The finished product — ingots of something that glows faintly and is heavier than it should be — is taken down the mountain by the cable system, or by the long zigzagging road that descends to the lowlands. The road is barely visible, a pale scratch on the mountainside. The cable system is visible from anywhere: the thick suspension cables, the slow movement of the cars, the periodic *clank* of a braking mechanism that is never fully silenced.

The industrial hum, then, is the constant note. The cable system, the refinery pumps, the ore-crushers, the ventilation of the smelting works, the small motors that drive the water-pumps in the cistern-towers — all of it hums, at slightly different frequencies, so that the city is a chord rather than a single tone. To live here for a long time is to lose the ability to hear the hum, and then to hear it again, suddenly, when you go away and come back, and to be briefly stunned by the loudness of the place you have made your home.

## The Light

The light here is the kind of light that the lowlanders write poems about, except that the poems never quite capture it. The elevation means that the air is thin and dry, and the sun, when it comes, comes without the softening that humidity provides. Shadows are sharp-edged and very dark. Colors are saturated almost to the point of vulgarity. The bougainvillea is not merely magenta; it is the *idea* of magenta, the platonic form. The white stone of the newer buildings is not merely white; it is luminous, almost a light-source itself, and on a clear afternoon you have to squint to look at the western terraces.

The light changes through the day in ways that feel almost choreographed. There is the long, slow dawn in which the eastern ridge becomes a knife-edge of fire and the city below it is still in blue shadow. There is the sudden flood of morning when the sun crests the ridge and pours down the face of the mountain and into every courtyard and alley, and for a few minutes the whole city gasps into brilliance. There is the high, white, slightly merciless light of midday. There is the long slow afternoon in which the shadows lengthen westward and the eastern walls of the buildings begin to glow. And there is the dusk, the best time, when the light is amber and horizontal and the whole city turns the color of honey, and the pigeons come home to their coops in slow circles, and the bells of the goats drift down from the upper pastures, and the steam from the lower refinery plumes rise into a sky that is turning, slowly, the deep saturated blue of the high altitude evening.

## The People and the Pace

I will not say much about the people, because they are not really the subject, but I will say this: they move slowly here. The elevation makes exertion unpleasant, and so a kind of grace has been built into the gait of the place. People walk as if they are listening to music no one else can hear. They pause in doorways. They lean on parapets. They watch the water go by. The slowness is not laziness; it is adaptation, and also a kind of aesthetic choice, the pace at which the city reveals itself.

The pace of the city itself is slow, in another sense. The buildings have been here for centuries; they will be here for centuries more. The glacier is retreating, but the retreat is measured in decades. The ore will last. The water, if it is managed, will last. The bougainvillea, against all reason, will last. Nothing here is in a hurry. The slow processes — the patina on the copper, the wear on the stair treads, the slow shift of the light through the day — are the city's real time, and the human time, the hurried time of work and worry, is the interloper.

And yet the city is loud. The hum of the refineries, the splash of the water in the gutters, the bells of the goats, the cries of the pigeon-keepers, the occasional sharp whistle of a refinery pressure-valve releasing, the distant clatter of a cable car passing through a station, the calls of children in the courtyards, the sound of a hammer on stone from somewhere you cannot see — all of it layers into a continuous wall of sound that is not unpleasant but is also not ignorable. You live inside the sound. It is your climate, as much as the air you breathe.

## The Slight Uncanniness

The city is not quite right. I do not mean that it is frightening, or wrong, or threatening. I mean that there is something about it that does not fully resolve. The way the light falls in certain alleys is too even, too orange, as if it has been arranged. The way the cats watch you from windows is too knowing, as if they have been briefed. The way the water in the public basins sometimes runs the wrong way, against the slight downward grade of the paving, before resuming its proper course. The way, on certain nights, the hum of the city changes pitch, very slightly, and you cannot tell if you have imagined it. The way the pomegranates, when you bite into them, are always sweeter than they should be.

These things are not discussed. Or rather, they are discussed only in the registers of the city that are not really language — in the slight hesitation before a stranger is invited into a home, in the way the older residents glance at the new moon, in the offerings of small coins and salt that are left, sometimes, on the parapets of the upper cistern-towers.

The city is a real city. It works. The ore is processed. The water flows. The gardens bloom. The people live and die and are mourned and remembered. The slight uncanniness is the city's secret, and it is not mine to tell, and I will not try.

I will only say: if you go, go in the late summer, when the pomegranates are fruiting and the light is at its most amber. Find a stairway and sit on it. Listen to the hum. Watch the water go by. Do not be in a hurry. The city will not be in a hurry to reveal itself. It has been here for a long time. It intends to be here for a long time more.
