# The Green City That Has No Citizens

You feel it before you see it.

A pressure in the air, like the moment before a thunderstorm breaks but never does. The wind is coming from somewhere ahead, somewhere low, somewhere that shouldn't be this loud. Even from the lip of the valley, half a kilometer out, you can hear it as a kind of *white roar* — but wet. Closer to the sound a forest makes in a hard rain, except the rain is permanent and the forest is bigger than forests are.

## The Approach

The valley sits between two low ridges of young stone, fresh and sharp-edged, the kind of rock that has not yet learned to round itself. The ridges are the color of split cucumber, mineral-wet, lichen-scarred at the base, but only at the base. Above a certain line they are clean. Nothing has eaten them yet. They are not old enough to be eaten.

The valley floor is not visible. It has been filled.

From the rim you see a continuous canopy, but the word *canopy* is too tame for it. This is a *topology*. Ridges and valleys of green tissue ripple outward toward you in long parallel waves, each one taller than a house, each one dense enough to look solid. Between the ridges run darker stripes — the *streets*, or what passes for streets here, which are actually channels carved through the living mass by centuries of runoff, by the weight of pooled water, by some older geometry that the growth has respected without anyone telling it to. They wind. They branch. They form loops. From above, the whole thing reads as a *city*, unmistakably a city, with avenues and cul-de-sacs and what must be plazas, and yet nothing in it was ever built. Nothing in it was ever designed. It has organized itself, the way coral organizes itself, the way a slime mold pretends to be Tokyo when no one is looking.

There is no smoke. There are no chimneys, no wheels, no glass, no signal-fires, no footprints in any dust. No animals. You knew this from somewhere, you knew it before you arrived, and now you confirm it: there are no footprints because there are no feet, no animals, no machines, no hands. The only citizen is the city itself, and the city is one organism, or many organisms pretending to be one, or many organisms that have forgotten which of them is which.

## The Sound

The valley is *loud*.

Not loud the way a market is loud, with distinguishable voices. Loud the way a furnace is loud — a single body of sound with internal gradations. You can hear the sap moving. Not metaphorically. The vascular tissue of these plants carries fluid under pressure, and when the pressure shifts — when a leaf unfurls somewhere, when a root pushes against a stone, when a load-bearing trunk swells with the next hour's growth — the sound is audible as a thick wooden *tick*, or a wet hollow *bonk*, or a slow sliding hiss like a thumb drawn across a melon. These are the consonants. The vowels are the canopy itself: a million leaves in constant, restless motion, chattering against each other in a wind that never stops because the wind is *made* here. The plants transpire so heavily they have built their own weather. Vapor rises, condenses, falls, swirls, rises again. There is always moisture in the air. There is always a breeze, and the breeze is always talking.

Underneath it, so low you feel it more than hear it, is the sound of growth. The slow cracking of fibers. The pop of a seed case. The deep groan of a trunk thickening by another centimeter in an afternoon. It is the sound of a thing that is being assembled continuously, that never finishes being assembled, that is also being disassembled at the same time — older trunks hollowing out from within while new layers of bark add themselves to the outside, walls becoming scaffolds becoming walls.

You step down into it.

## The Streets

The first thing you notice is that the streets *breathe*.

They are not paved. They are channels worn into the soft mass of root and stem and mossy underlayer, sometimes a meter deep, sometimes three, sometimes shallow enough that you could step over them. Their floors are wet. Not flooded, but *sweating*. Water seeps up from below through root membranes; water drips down from the overarching growth; water gathers in shallow bowls at every intersection and sits there, still and brown-green and alive with small floating flowers. The water is warm. Everything here is warm. The air sits on your skin like a held breath.

The walls of the streets are *built*. There is no other word. Stems the thickness of thighs have been braided together by no one and have held each other up for what looks like a hundred years. They form smooth surfaces, almost plaster-smooth in places, where the outer bark has been worn to a polish by the rub of passing vapor. Above them, where a human city would have eaves and shutters, there are *foliations* — fans of leaf-tissue that interlock across the gap and form a roof, a green ceiling moving with light. Where the streets are wider the ceiling opens into skylights — gaps where a single trunk has shot upward and burst into a fountain of fronds, each frond the size of a parasol, and the sunlight falls through these gaps in coins of gold that move as the canopy moves. The light is alive. The shadows run.

You try to walk a straight line. You cannot. The streets curve. They curve because the oldest root systems are the ones the city grew around, and the oldest root systems follow the water, and the water follows the lowest contour of the young valley floor, and the lowest contour is never straight. The streets curve and they curve and then, suddenly, *they do not curve correctly*. You turn a corner expecting the curve to continue, and the street goes *sideways*, into a direction the valley geometry did not permit. You stop. You look behind you. The street you came from is also there, also sideways, and now you can see both at once, and the air between them tastes like rain and smells like cut grass and your inner ear has quietly stopped trusting your eyes.

You keep walking. There is no other option.

## The Plaza

There is a plaza.

It is round, or it is many-sided, or it is a circle that has been folded through itself so that looking at it from two different positions gives you two different shapes. The plaza is the size of a small village green. The floor of it is packed root-mass, walked-upon by no feet but compacted by its own weight, and from this floor rise — *buildings*.

The buildings are trees. They are also not trees. They are columns of vascular plant tissue that have, through long cooperation with their neighbors, formed vertical surfaces, plinths, capitals, pediments. They are *mimicking*. They are mimicking a kind of architecture you half-recognize — pillars that swell at the top like bundled reeds, lintels that span from column to column in single living arches, facades of woven cane that hold the shape of walls. They are mimicry with no audience. No animal, no mind, no eye in this place requires them to look like this. They look like this because the *growth pattern* that produced them was the growth pattern that a city's growth pattern would have been, if cities grew.

Between the pillars hang curtains of vine. They move without wind. They move when you look at them, and they move when you don't, and you cannot tell which it is, because every time you look away and look back the curtain has shifted. The vine curtains are the deepest green in the place. Behind them, faintly, you can hear *water*.

## The Water

Water is everywhere and nowhere abundant. It does not rain here, not really, but it is always *almost raining*. Vapor condenses on every leaf, on every pillar, on your skin, on your clothes. It forms beads and the beads run together and the runnels feed into the shallow channels at the base of every wall and the channels meet the streets and the streets meet the deeper channels at the edge of the plaza and the deep channels flow — *out*. There is a river somewhere at the bottom of this. You haven't seen it yet. You hear it. The whole city is hollowed above it like a green church built over a fast stream.

The river is older than the city. It carved the valley when the stone was new. The plants came after, colonizing its banks, then its floodplain, then climbing its lower slopes, then — in the absence of anything to stop them — continuing up. They have built a green kilometer of themselves above the river's path. The river runs under them. You hear it underfoot at all times, a dark bass note beneath the canopy's treble chatter.

In the plaza there is a pool. It is round and shallow and absolutely still. The water in it is black and clear at once. Leaves float on it. A single flower floats on it — purple, twice the size of your hand, pulsing faintly open and closed as if breathing. Around the pool, the floor is mosaic. You bend down. The mosaic is made of *seeds*. Thousands of them, inlaid into the living root by some patient process you cannot imagine, arranged in a pattern that is geometric and is also slightly wrong. The pattern looks like a wave. The pattern looks like an eye. The pattern looks like a sentence you almost know.

## The Reality

Here is where the place stops being a place.

The light does not behave. In one alley it falls correctly, in stripes and pools. In the next alley, the *same* sun, the *same* hour, produces light that is *denser*, thicker, as if the photons have a slight weight. Your shadow on a wall is your shadow, but it lags by a quarter-second. It arrives after you do. In some of the plazas you find your shadow is the right shape but *twice*. One shadow on each wall, both of them moving, neither of them yours in quite the way yours is yours. You wave. Both shadows wave. They wave at slightly different speeds.

The geometry bends. You walk down a street that is, you are certain, twenty meters long. You emerge after what feels like a minute into a plaza you passed three streets ago. You walk down another street and emerge somewhere *new*, somewhere that should not exist relative to the position you entered from, but is unmistakable once you arrive. There is a fountain there. The fountain is alive. Water rises from a stone basin through the center of a flowering stem and falls back through a spiral of petals. You watch it for a while. When you look up, the sun has not moved. You check. The sun has moved *two hours*.

The growth is wrong in ways you keep catching out of the corner of your eye. A stem growing *downward* into the soil and then *up again* through the same path. A flower whose petals open in the wrong order. A leaf that is the green of healthy chlorophyll but the *shape* of a hand, fingers spread, palm out, and you swear you can see the lines of the palm if you look too long. A tree whose bark is smooth on one side and deeply furrowed on the other, and the boundary between the two is a single vertical line, straight as a plumb-cut, as if the tree were aware of left and right.

There are places where the city has *failed to remember itself*. A street that ends in a wall of compacted green that looks like the *back* of a building. You walk around it and the building is a building, it has rooms, it has an interior — but the interior is smaller than the exterior. The walls curve inward as you go in. The ceiling lowers. By the time you are at the back of the room, you are crouching in a space the size of a cupboard, and through a window-shaped gap in the wall you can see, impossibly, the plaza you were standing in ten minutes ago. The light coming through the gap is *evening* light. The light falling on you where you crouch is *midday*.

You do not panic. There is nothing here to panic about. The place is not hostile. It is not a trap. It is simply a place where the rules have softened, where the agreement between geometry and growth has become a *suggestion*, and the suggestion has been mostly followed but not always.

## The Motion

Nothing sits still.

Even the stone moves — the stone is young, it weeps mineral salts, it is being slowly lifted by roots that pry at its joints. A boulder the size of a room tilts three millimeters a year, and you can hear it ticking in its cradle of root as the roots heave. The moss that creeps across its face advances visibly while you watch. A handspan of green in an hour.

The plants move faster. You can see growth if you stand still long enough — a bud swelling, a tendril uncoiling, a seed pod darkening from green to brown in the time it takes to drink water. The canopy overhead is never the same canopy twice. Leaves rotate toward light sources that shift as the plants themselves shift the light. Branches sway in the wind they make. A whole avenue of trees leans six degrees to the east, then corrects itself, then leans again — a slow breathing, the city inhaling.

And at ground level, things *move*. Not animals. There are no animals. But roots slide beneath your feet with a faint pressurized whisper, redistributing weight, finding water, finding stone to pry. Tendrils whip slowly past, exploring. A vine drops from a balcony thirty meters up, descends with the speed of a clock hand, touches the floor, recoils. A flower opens. A flower closes. A flower that was open a moment ago is now a *different* flower, the same plant having swapped its bloom in the time you blinked, and the dropped petals of the previous bloom are already being absorbed back into the stem that bore them, the city cannibalizing its own discarded beauty for next week's growth.

The activity is endless. The activity is not frantic — it is *serious*. This is a place that takes its own growth seriously. It is doing something. You cannot tell what. You cannot tell if it knows you're here.

## The Ecology (Of Which There Is None, And Of Which There Is Everything)

There are no animals. You have looked. There are no insects in the canopy, no worms in the soil, no birds, no bats, no spiders in the corners of the green architecture. There is *nothing with a face*. And yet the place hums with what feels like intention. The seed-mosaic by the pool. The plazas aligned to points that might be solstices. The way certain streets lead to certain buildings and not others. The way certain flowers, only at dusk, open in shapes that are almost letters.

There is no ecology. There is no food web, no predation, no pollination, no decomposition by anything that moves. What dies here is decomposed by the living. A leaf falls — it is caught before it touches the ground by a fungal thread that knows it is coming, and the thread drinks it, and the leaf is gone in a day, and the thread fruits, and the fruit is eaten by a slug-shaped root that surfaces just for it, and the root subsides, and the place is tidied. There is no rot. There is no smell of rot. The smell is of cut grass and warm rain and something resinous, something almost like cinnamon, something you cannot place.

## The Sound, Again

You have been here for some time now. You have stopped flinching at the loudness, the way you stop flinching at the sea. It is constant. It is the *baseline*. The tick of vascular pressure, the hiss of transpiration, the rustle of a million leaves, the deep groan of trunks, the drip of pooled water, the whisper of roots, the occasional sharp *crack* of a branch failing under its own weight somewhere in the canopy and being caught, instantly, by four other branches that knew it was about to fail. The cracking is part of the rhythm. The catch is part of the rhythm.

Somewhere under it all is the river.

You are sitting on a low wall of braided cane at the edge of a plaza. The wall is warm. The sun is filtered through three layers of moving leaf and falls on you in shifting coins of gold. A flower at your shoulder is opening toward you. You did not ask it to. It is opening anyway. Its petals are the color of a bruise and they smell like rain on hot stone. The plaza is empty. The streets are empty. The whole city is empty of anything that is not itself.

The city is loud. The city is warm. The city is wet. The city is dense and young and organized and impossible and full of itself and growing and growing and growing.

You sit there. The wall accepts your weight. A root, somewhere beneath you, shifts to accommodate you.
