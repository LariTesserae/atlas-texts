# The Lower Plain of the Long Engines

You arrive on a plain that has been worn almost to nothing.

It was not always flat. The land here has the long, deliberate profile of something that once had a shape and no longer remembers what it was — like a coastline argued with the sea for a million years and lost, or like a mountain that lay down and never stood up again. The horizon is a thin gray line about ten miles in every direction, and there is nothing rising against it. Nothing at all. The plain extends.

But it is not earth. There is no earth here. The ground beneath you is plated — a continuous skin of dark alloy, patched and patched again, so old that the patches are themselves ancient. It was poured, or rolled, or grown, you cannot tell. The surface has the look of cast iron that has weathered to a matte black, the way certain metals forget their shine under enough years of condensation. In places the plating has cracked open and something underneath shows through — maybe more plating, maybe a different era of plating, maybe a substance you have no name for. The cracks fill with water, which never quite leaves.

The water is everywhere, and it is not very deep. It is half an inch to a foot, mostly, depending on where you stand. It has nowhere to go. The plain is slightly dished, very slightly, and the lowest parts hold standing water the way a saucer holds tea. When the wind moves, which is not often, the surface ripples in long slow lines and you can see the sky reflected back as a kind of dim gray. Most of the time the water is still and merely dark. Cold mist lifts off it at dawn. The mist is what makes the air wet — there is no rain, or hardly ever, just this slow bleed of condensation off the cold plating into the cold air and back again.

The temperature is the temperature of a stone floor in a cellar where the furnace has been out for a long time. Not warm. Not cold enough to bite. Just the persistent cool of somewhere that has lost its source of heat and settled into the climate of the planet around it.

Vegetation is almost absent and what exists is wrong. There are thin upright stalks here and there — ten, twenty, a hundred feet apart — made of a black fibrous material that is not plant and not metal. They do not grow taller than your hand. They have no leaves. They vibrate faintly in the low frequencies you can feel in your chest but not quite hear. Whatever they are, they are sparse and they are not alive in any way you recognize. Some have collapsed sideways and lie flat against the plating, still pulsing faintly. The ground between them is bare.

And the sound.

The sound is the place. There is no moment here when the sound stops. It is a continuous pressure, almost a weather. The low note of large engines running somewhere — everywhere — far beneath the plating and inside it and along its edges. A high thin whine that you can locate nowhere. Periodic percussive clangs, far apart, like someone testing a hammer on the hull of an enormous ship. The hiss of steam or compressed gas venting from a thousand vents you cannot see. A deep thrum you feel in the bones of your feet, more felt than heard, that makes the water in the puddles jump in tiny rings, each one a different radius, so that the surface of the plain shivers constantly even when there is no wind. Together this is not noise — it is a condition, like humidity, like the wet. You stand inside it and it does not recede.

The structures are barely structures anymore. From a distance the plain looked empty. Walking it, you find they are there. Towers, or the remains of towers — most are no taller than a person, some are taller, all are worn at their tops as if a giant had rubbed them down with a thumb. They are cylinders, mostly, or columns, or stacks. Some are hollow and you can hear the wind drawn through them in a low moan that mixes with the engines and disappears. Some have apertures at the base that exhale warm breath into the cool air, so that in the lee of each one the mist swirls and you can find them in the dark by the warmth. They are spaced hundreds of feet apart, sometimes more. The plain between them is wide and wet and empty and you can walk all day and not feel enclosed. The scale is regional — you can stand in one spot and the nearest structure is a smudge and the next beyond it is invisible. From a rise that is not a rise, only a long slow wave in the plating, you can see perhaps twenty of them, scattered across the wet ground like dice thrown and forgotten.

The darkness is not the darkness of night. It is the darkness of overcast — a permanent shallow ceiling of low cloud at perhaps two thousand feet, lit from somewhere beneath it, so that the light is diffuse and sourceless and comes from no direction. Things do not cast shadows. They cast absences — places slightly darker than the already-dim ground. You can see for miles. You can see nothing sharply. The plating is black, the water is black, the towers are black, the sky is the dark gray of a wet slate. Everything is approximately the same value. Eyes do not help you much. You navigate by the feel of the vibrations in your feet and the warmth of the vents against your cheek.

There are no animals. None. You are aware of this after an hour, when the absence begins to feel like a presence. The wet stone of a real river would have insects. The dark under a real bridge would have something. Here, nothing. The puddles do not have mosquito larvae. The mist does not carry any chirp. Whatever this place was built for, it was not built for anything that breathes in the way you understand breathing. The fibrous stalks do not host anything. The warm vents do not lure anything. There is simply no fauna, and the plain is not disturbed by tracks, droppings, nests, bones, feathers, fur, eye-shine. The only motion is the mist, the water, the occasional slow collapse of a worn tower in the far distance, and the engines.

The engines.

This is the strangeness, and it is hard to put into words because nothing here is stranger than the fact that the engines are still running. This is a place that has been worn down to a smooth plate and held still for an age so long the word *ancient* is a small word for it. The plating remembers when the towers were tall. The water remembers — the water does not remember. Nothing here remembers. And yet the engines run. They run because they were not turned off. They were not turned off because there is no one to turn them off. They have been running unattended for so long that the wear on them has become part of their operation; they wear and are repaired by their own motion, or they have worn into a state in which further wear is negligible, or they are not really wearing at all but rather settling, the way the plateau settled, into a final form that will persist because nothing opposes it.

You can stand beside a vent and put your hand near it and feel warm air come out. You can press your ear to a hollow tower and hear, beneath the general roar, a particular rhythm — a slow pulse, perhaps three seconds in, three seconds out, like breath. It is not breath. But it is not not-breath. You can follow the pulse down through the plating with your ear to the ground and hear it everywhere, in every direction, slightly out of phase with itself, so that standing in the middle of the plain you hear a soft beating, an arrhythmic heart, that is the entire plain breathing in pieces.

You do not know what is being made. You do not know what is being refined, smelted, distilled, pumped, compressed, vented. You see no product. You see no input. You see only the process — the same process, on the same scale, for so long that the input and the output have become indistinguishable, the way a river at its mouth is both the sea and not the sea. Whatever is being made is being made continuously and is, perhaps, the plain itself, the worn plating, the slow settling, the towers becoming shorter by a fraction each century.

The dynamic is not dramatic. There are no storms. There are no seasons. The light does not change. The sound does not change. But within the long stability there are small events: a tower somewhere you cannot see collapses with a long grinding roar that travels across the wet plain for half a minute. A vent elsewhere changes its rhythm from three seconds to four, slowly, over an hour, and you notice because the place has so few variables that each one counts. A new patch of plating, somewhere far away, reflects light differently and you can see it as a pale smear on the dark ground. The mist thickens, thins, thickens again. The fibrous stalks vibrate more strongly when a particular engine cycles up. These are the weather. This is the climate. This is the slow life of the place, which is not alive, which is not dead, which is something you do not have a word for.

You walk. The water is half an inch deep around your boots. The plating is firm beneath it. The towers pass, one every several minutes, each one warm against the mist, each one breathing its slow breath into the cool air. The sound never stops. The light never brightens. The wet never dries. You walk all day and arrive nowhere because there is nowhere to arrive at. The plain extends. The plain has always extended. The engines have always run.

After a while you stop walking and stand still and listen, and the place listens with you, in its slow arrhythmic way, and does not ask why you are here, because it has never asked anyone that, and no one has ever answered, and the engines do not care, and the worn plating holds you up without comment, and the dark is the dark of a thing that has outlived every question about it.
