# A Pocket Just Inside the World

It begins as a sound you almost don't hear — a faint, persistent trickle that might be water or might be the place itself breathing — and the floor underfoot is slick in a way that has nothing to do with weather. There is no weather here. There is only the inside of something that is still deciding what it is.

You come down a crack. That is the closest word for it. Not a stair, not a ladder cut into stone, but a place where the surface folds inward like skin around a splinter and lets you through. The lip of the fold is rimmed with something halfway between quartz and bone, and it is still warm from the negotiation of becoming — the rock here is younger than the rock above, by an interval that doesn't quite measure in any unit you'd trust. You can feel it yielding slightly under your weight. You are one of the first things to stand on it.

Above you, the ceiling is low and bright. The brightness is the first wrong thing. It has no visible source. It pools in the hollow of the chamber like spilled daylight, the way water pools in a depression in stone, and it warms the pale mineral surfaces until they throw the light back twice. You can look up and see nothing but rock, and yet the rock is glowing. The air is full of a faint suspended mist, and the mist is what is carrying the light — each droplet a tiny lens, each lens carrying a piece of a sun that isn't above you but is, somehow, everywhere in the room, distributed. If you cupped your hands and held the mist in them, you would not catch the source. The light is *of* the dampness. It is the dampness' way of remembering the sky.

The chamber is small. Pocket-sized. The walls curve in and you can almost touch them if you stretched. There is one place where the wall has not finished choosing to be a wall — a section that is still soft, still granular, still pouring itself very slowly downward in a faint dry rattle that has been going on for as long as the place has existed, and will go on for as long as it takes. This is the emerging part. This is what it means for a place to be under construction: not that someone built it, but that it has not yet finished assembling itself out of the materials it is becoming. A seam of pale grit lies along the floor where that wall is shedding itself. In another ten thousand years there will be a dune here. In another ten thousand, perhaps, a hill. For now, the grit is just grit, and the wall is just a wall that hasn't made up its mind.

Water finds its way in. It comes from everywhere and from nowhere specific. It condenses on the ceiling in droplets that grow fat and clear and then fall, but never in any rhythm — each drop is on its own schedule, and the ceiling has more droplets than the floor has puddles, because most of the water, on its way down, becomes part of the mist again. What collects on the floor is in shallow dishes worn into the stone, irregular as thumbprints. They are warm in the morning — or in whatever counts for morning here, the slow cycling of a brightness that has no sun to track — and cool at other times. The water in them is not quite water. If you held a drop to the light it would seem to refract more than it should, to show you things at the bottom that aren't there.

The vegetation is barely vegetation. You might miss it if you were looking for anything recognizable. There are fans of pale lichen that climb two feet up the western curve of the wall, gray-green and almost translucent at the rims, breathing in a way you can see if you watch long enough. There are threads of something — mold, or a kind of plant that hasn't decided whether to be a plant — that bridge the gaps between stones in the floor like the beginnings of a web, each strand holding its single trembling drop of moisture at its lowest point. There is one small thing near the back of the chamber that might be a fern if ferns had only one leaf and that leaf were the color of an old bruise. It trembles when the air moves. It is the only piece of green that has any conviction to it.

The creatures are what you came for, though you didn't know it.

They are pale. Almost all of them. The cool, the dark of stone, the bright mist — pale things have thrived here for the entire short history of the chamber, and the ones you see now are descended from the ones that first found it. They move without sound, or with sounds so small they are indistinguishable from the room's own settling: a tick of a claw on quartz, a soft wet friction of a body sliding over damp stone, the barely-there tap of an antenna or a tendril against a wall. There is a small pale many-legged thing that walks the ceiling upside down and leaves a faint silver track of mucus behind it; the mucus catches the light and becomes a slow constellation. There are blind, slug-white worms that fold themselves into the warm dishes of water on the floor and uncurl again, longer or shorter than before, and you cannot tell if they are one worm or several that have learned to share a shape. There is a creature with too many eyes for its head — or perhaps with several heads that have agreed to share eyes — and it watches you from a ledge near the soft wall without blinking, because nothing here has eyelids. None of them. The light, you understand after a while, is why. They have no need to close their eyes against anything.

There are structures. Scattered. Not built, exactly, but *placed* — small cairns of stone balanced with a care that looks almost deliberate, except when you try to count them the number changes. Sometimes six. Sometimes seven. Once, when you look again, five, and one of the missing ones is in a different part of the chamber where you are sure it was not before. They are not tall. They are finger-height. They are made of the same grit that is shedding off the unfinished wall, lifted and arranged by something with patience and no thumbs. You do not see who arranges them. You suspect the many-eyed creature, and then you suspect something else, and then you suspect that the suspicion itself is one of the things the room is doing to you, the way the mist carries light, the way the water shows you things at the bottom of itself.

The place is quiet, but it is not still. The ceiling is always re-dewing. The soft wall is always shedding. The creatures are always in motion — small things, small arcs, the patient wandering of pale bodies across the floor and up the walls and through the mist. The mist itself moves in slow eddies, and the light moves with it, and the shadows that ought to be there from the cairns and the lichen and the creatures themselves *almost* are there, but they don't quite commit. They flicker. They slide. They are shadows the way the walls are walls.

You stay longer than you meant to. The cool air settles into your lungs and the brightness makes your eyes ache in a way that is almost pleasant, and the silence is so full of the small movements of so many pale things that you stop hearing it as silence and start hearing it as presence. The chamber is small enough to feel like a held breath. The chamber is large enough that there is always some new corner of it being born. You understand, sitting on the damp stone with your back against the unfinished wall, that this is what *emerging* means: not that something is being made, but that something is being made possible, and the making is going to take longer than you can sit here, and you are early for it, and that is why it is so bright. The light is what gets shown into a place before it has learned how to be dark.

When you finally climb back up through the fold, the lip of it is cool against your hand and slightly damp and gives a little under your grip, the way new rock does, and the brightness behind you persists for a moment in your eyes, a small afterimage of a place that was not finished, that is still finishing itself, that you have just left a few thousand years earlier than you arrived.
