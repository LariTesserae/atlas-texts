The basin has been settling for longer than rivers remember.

The first thing you register is the wet — but it is not the wet of rain. It is the wet of a medium. The air is full. The stones, where stones still exist, are sweating from every face, and when you touch them the water beads up against your skin as though it has been waiting there for you. The bark of trees is more liquid than fiber; you can dent it with a thumb and the dent fills again from within, slowly, from below. Light, when it reaches the ground, arrives having been partly drunk, so that the air between the trunks is honey-colored and unhurried.

The land is low — almost too low. The water table is essentially the surface, and the surface is mostly a kind of moss-felted give, and your foot sinks a quarter inch before it finds anything to push against. This is not a place that was carved. It is a place that has been worn down — negotiated, smoothed, licked flat by the slow work of water and root and patient chemistry — until the original shape of the world is no longer legible. What remains is the bottom of something very old. A bowl. A throat. The place where a continent has finally agreed to be still.

Vegetation here is not a covering. It is the substrate. The ground beneath your feet is a continuous body of root, an unbroken weave that flexes with the day and never quite dries out. The walls of any structure are mostly stem. The ceiling, where there is a ceiling, is mostly leaf, and the leaves overlap so densely that the sky has been reduced to a rumor seen between shifts. To call it jungle is too exotic. To call it forest is too organized. It is closer to a single organism — vast, distributed, alive in a way that is hard to point at. You can sit inside the moss; the moss will accommodate you; you will come out smelling of something between peat and green tea.

The warmth is constant. It is the warmth of a hand held over a cup. It does not vary much between morning and afternoon. It has been here long enough to be the temperature of the place itself rather than of any weather.

And somewhere inside this organism — you have to look for it; the eye has to learn the grammar — there is a settlement. It reads first as a clearing, then as architecture, then as architecture so thoroughly overgrown that it has become grove again. It is small. A parish. A clutch of structures clustered around a central space the size of a small nave, with paths between them that are more packed earth than pavement. The place is sparse in a way that is unusual for a settlement: the buildings are few and well separated, and the paths between them are long and mossy and feel unused even when you can see they are. There is air between presences. There is room.

But the infrastructure is everywhere, and it is layered. The walls are not built so much as trained — woven while growing, fibrous, breathing, and you can read the history of the building in cross-section, because each remodeling has been laid onto the last, like rings, like the records of an ongoing argument. Inside the trunk-walls run conduits that are continuous with the wood itself, not metal inserted into a plant but the plant grown around the conduit, so that to dismantle a wall you would have to decide where the building ends and the wiring begins. Channels have been cut into the floor of every path to take runoff, but the runoff never seems to arrive anywhere; it is taken up before it goes anywhere, drunk back into the body of the place. There is a faint hum, just at the edge of hearing, that does not vary much with time of day but does vary with where you stand. Near the central nave the hum is higher. In the corners it drops into something more felt than heard.

The technology of this place is not visible. It is the leaves that give it away.

If you take a single leaf and hold it up to the honeyed light, you will see, in the structure of its veins, a patterning too regular to be botanical — a lattice that repeats down to scales the eye can't quite resolve, as though the leaf is a circuit drawn in cellulose, or a page from a book whose language you don't read. The whole grove is like this, if you know how to look: a vast distributed machine that is also a forest, computing in a medium that is not quite biology and not quite anything else. The walls conduct. The roots compute. The water in the air carries information in some way that is not chemistry and not quite physics and not quite anything you have a name for. This is not a town that has been built inside a forest. This is a forest that has been taught to be a town, by a patience older than patience, by a science that does not have to make sense to you to work.

The fauna is abundant. There are birds, but they are small and live in the upper canopy, and their calls come down softened, as if filtered through felt. There are insects in such number that the air itself seems to thicken and move when you stand still; you breathe them in, swallow them with your water. There are amphibians at the path-edges — glossy, plump, unblinking, watching you pass with the patience of creatures who do not have to be anywhere. There are things that are harder to name — pale slow movers that hang in the higher air like kites, that may be animals or may be seed-pods or may be neither, and that drift without seeming to respond to the wind.

The sound, once you tune in, is loud in a layered way. There is the constant hush of water — drip, condensation, the small persistent music of things wetting and being wetted. There are the calls of birds, layered, never quite synchronous, weaving a thin counterpoint above. There is the hum of the settlement, which you feel more in the teeth than in the ears. And there is a sound like a crowd at a great distance — a many-voiced murmur that seems to come from the leaves themselves, as though the canopy is full of something between speech and weather. It rises and falls without obvious cause. It is not quite language. It is not quite wind. It is the place, talking to itself, or to you, or to whatever it is that lives in the spaces between cells.

The place is turbulent, though it does not look it.
