# The Spire-City of Kharad-Ruun

## I. Approach

You do not arrive at Kharad-Ruun so much as ascend into it.

The mountain called Khara is older than the rock that surrounds it — that is the local superstition, and like most local superstitions, it is precisely backward. The mountain is not older. The mountain is *younger*. It emerged, geologically speaking, yesterday: a basaltic thrust punched through older limestone beds perhaps twelve thousand years ago, and in the time since it has been ceaselessly *worked*. There is no erosion here. Nothing weathers. Nothing rounds. Every edge is fresh as a struck flint, and every face still holds the tool-marks of whatever carved it.

You approach along a switchback road cut into the flank at a grade so steep the drivers' ears pop. The switchbacks are paved in a dark resinous substance that is not asphalt — nothing organic here, nothing to distill from — but rather a sintered mineral aggregate pressed into place while hot. It rings under wheels like struck metal. The smell is of hot stone and ozone.

Then you round the final hairpin, and the Spire-City unfolds above you.

## II. The Vertical City

Kharad-Ruun is built *into* the mountain, not on it.

The young basalt came up as a column — a volcanic pipe, a frozen fountain of black stone rising nearly three thousand meters above the surrounding high desert. The city has colonized it the way a coral colonizes a drop, every centimeter dense with construction. There is no horizon line that is not pierced by another spire, another gantry, another smokestack. There is no sky visible from inside the city at all. You look up and you see floors above floors above floors, ascending into a kind of permanent overcast.

The lowest levels are the oldest. They are cut directly into the basalt — chambers and galleries hollowed from the living rock, their walls still bearing the chisel-striae from when they were new a thousand years ago. The chisel-marks have not softened. You can read individual tool-strokes in the dim light: who carved, when, in what direction they were facing. Each stroke is dated. Each stroke is fresh.

Above the carved levels rise the built ones: tiers of masonry, metal, and a strange crystalline material the locals call *glass-iron*, which is neither glass nor iron but a fusion of both that grows in the volcanic vents and is harvested while still hot. It has the transparency of obsidian and the tensile strength of steel. The city uses it for everything — windows, floors, load-bearing walls, staircases that seem to float. In the dim light it gleams dully, like deep water that has forgotten how to be wet.

## III. The Light

Light here is a manufactured commodity.

There is no water on Khara — none at all, not in the rock, not in the air, not condensed from any breath. There is no vegetation to convert starlight and chemical gradients into oxygen or warmth. The sun, when it penetrates the smog-and-smoke canopy, is a pale disc the color of a worn coin. Most days it does not penetrate at all.

But the city *glows*.

It glows from within. Each block is lit by its own network of bioluminescent — no, not bioluminescent. *Electroluminescent.* The mountain itself is rich in a copper-bearing ore that, when a current is run through it, throws a cold bluish light. The city has been wired into the mountain's bones since the third dynasty. Every wall, every floor, every stair is a potential lamp. The lowest levels are a deep ultramarine twilight; the middle levels a working cyan; the upper levels — closest to the vents — a hot and almost-white radiance that hurts the eyes.

You live your life in a particular shade of blue. You know your caste by the blue you live in. The deep-blues are miners, smelters, the workers who cut the new galleries. The cyan-blues are artisans, machinists, the engineers of the great engines. The white-blues are the priesthood of the vents, those who tend the industrial heart.

## IV. The Engines

The mountain is a factory.

This is not metaphor. At the core of Khara, accessed by shafts that descend through all the city's tiers and another kilometer below them, lies a still-magma chamber — a remnant of the original eruption, a lake of molten basalt that has been kept molten, deliberately, for a thousand years. The priesthood of the vents feeds it. They stoke it with carefully selected ores; they bleed off its heat through heat-exchange towers that rise above the uppermost city like the pipes of a grotesque organ. The towers exhale a constant plume of superheated vapor — not steam, there is no water to steam, but a mineral aerosol that drifts downwind and forms a permanent brown veil over the surrounding desert.

The engines take this heat and turn it into everything the city needs. Light, as above. Compressed air for the pneumatic tubes that carry messages and small freight through the city's vertical arteries. Mechanical work, for the looms and lathes and the great stamping-presses that shape glass-iron into standardized beams. And, critically, *power* — a power the locals call *the squeeze*, a hydrostatic compression of superheated mineral oil through narrow channels, so that a small flow at the top becomes a small but ferocious force at the bottom, capable of punching holes in stone, of forging metal, of driving the chisel-marks that never have time to weather.

The city is *always being extended*. There is no finished building in Kharad-Ruun. There is no ruin, because nothing is allowed to become ruin — every surface is maintained, every gallery is extended another few meters every decade, the city bites deeper into its mountain like a jaw that never closes.

## V. The Fauna

And yet there are animals.

The mountain is a closed ecology, almost. Nothing comes in from outside — there is no outside, ecologically; the desert below the city is sterile to the kilometer. Nothing leaves. And yet.

In the lower galleries, in the deep blue, there are things that move.

They are called *the chthonic* by the deep-blues, *the cousins* by the cynics. They are small — the largest is no bigger than a rat. They have no eyes. They have no mouths, not in any conventional sense; they absorb moisture from the air, and since there is no moisture in the air, they absorb it from the stone itself, leaching trace minerals through capillary feet. They eat rock. They excrete dust. They are not animals. They are something *else* — something that has evolved, or perhaps been *cultivated*, in the sealed darkness below the city, filling a niche that in any wetter world would belong to worms or insects.

There are many of them. They are *everywhere*. The deep galleries are dense with them, a seething carpet of pale soft bodies pressing against every surface, every crack, every chisel-mark. They hum — a constant faint vibration, the sound of a billion small bodies feeding, a sound just at the threshold of perception. Step on one and it bursts into a bright powder that smokes briefly and is gone.

They are not pets. They are not vermin. They are a part of the city's metabolism. They digest the dust and the spall. They keep the galleries clean. They are the closest thing the mountain has to a digestive system, and the deep-blues regard them with the same fond contempt a farmer regards his soil-fauna.

In the upper levels there are larger things — things the cyan-blues have engineered. *Mules*, they are called: six-legged, eyeless, hairless, warm-bodied from internal heat-exchangers. They pull the carts in the pneumatic-tube stations. They are docile. They are bred. They have been bred for a thousand years, and their lineage is the city's second-most-valuable secret.

## VI. The Sound

The city is mostly quiet.

This is counterintuitive — a factory-city of extreme density, populated by fauna, lit by electricity, should be a roaring place. It is not. The engines run smooth, mounted on glass-iron bearings so precise that the moving parts never touch. The pneumatic tubes whisper. The chthonic hum below is too low to hear unless you listen. The mules are silent.

What sound there is comes from the *work*.

Chimes. Clinks. The slow tap of a chisel somewhere far above. The hiss of a heat-exchange vent cycling. The thin whine of a pump. Occasionally, from very deep down, a groan — the mountain adjusting to the weight of what has been built into it, or the magma chamber burping, or something no one has a name for. The grooms are the city's clock. There is a schedule.

In the markets — yes, there are markets; nothing is grown but everything is traded, in a closed economy of ore, finished goods, and the carefully-rationed output of the engines — there is conversation. Voices. The clink of small coin (glass-iron tokens, stamped). But no shouting. The acoustic of the galleries is such that shouting is rude and unnecessary; you can speak normally and be heard three corridors away.

## VII. The Weather

Above the uppermost tier, the air is wrong.

The mineral aerosol from the vents meets the thin cold air of extreme elevation, and the result is a constant fine precipitation of metallic dust. It is not rain. It does not fall. It hangs in the air, luminous in the engine-light, and the upper priesthood walk through it wearing breathers and goggles. Their robes are permanently stained. Their lungs are permanently compromised. They do not live long.

Lower down, the aerosol disperses. By the middle levels it is invisible, though if you hold out your hand you can feel a faint grit on your skin. By the deep galleries it is nothing.

Below the city, in the desert, the grit accumulates. There is a plain of metallic dust that extends for a hundred kilometers downwind, glittering dully in the rare sun. The deep-blues call it *the shadow of the city*. No one visits it.

## VIII. The People

The people of Kharad-Ruun are dark-skinned and pale-eyed, a phenotype developed over generations in the dim blue light. They are short and broad, adapted to the thin air of extreme elevation. They are many — the city holds perhaps two hundred thousand, which is an astonishing density for so confined a space, and which is why every gallery, every shaft, every chamber is in use. There is no empty room in Kharad-Ruun. There is no *unbuilt* room either. Every cubic meter of the mountain, from the highest vent-platform to the deepest drainage-catch, is someone's home or workplace or shrine.

They are busy. They are always busy. The city runs on a tempo of constant work: extension, maintenance, smelting, forging, refining, recycling. The glass-iron tokens change hands. The engines run. The chthonic digest the dust. The mules pull the carts. The chisels tap. And somewhere, always, another gallery is being cut into fresh rock, and somewhere else, another floor is being added to an existing tier, and the city grows, and grows, and does not stop.

## IX. The Unusual

There is one thing, perhaps, that gives the lie to *normal*.

The mountain is young. The city is old. And the city remembers — in the chisel-marks, in the unbroken maintenance, in the careful naming of every stroke — that the mountain is going to *erode*. One day, ten thousand years hence or a hundred thousand, the basalt will weather. The edges will round. The galleries will collapse. The engines will fail.

The priesthood of the vents knows this. They do not speak of it. But every gallery they cut, every beam they forge, every child they raise in the dim blue light, is a small protest against the future — a refusal to accept that the city will end. They carve their names. They carve their lineages. They carve, in the deepest stone, beneath the chthonic, in the cold below the engines, a single phrase repeated a thousand times:

*Let it be known that we were here.*

The chisel-marks are fresh.

The mountain is young.

The city is busy.
