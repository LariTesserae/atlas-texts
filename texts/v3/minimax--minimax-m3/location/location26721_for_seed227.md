# The Hollow

You don't find this place so much as you slip into it.

The forest above is old spruce and mountain ash, the canopy so dense in winter that snow never reaches the ground beneath it — only the pale, cold drip of condensation gathering on needles and falling through the dark. There is a path here that no path is. A depression in the duff that gathers into a fold in the terrain, and the fold into a hollow, and the hollow into a steepening, and the steepening into a sudden absence of ground beneath one's feet. The lip is rimmed by a ring of roots gripping into stone like fingers. From above, even standing at the edge, you can barely tell. The trees lean outward from the rim, their branches interweaving into a screen, and the whole thing reads as a darker patch among other darker patches — a place where the canopy has forgotten itself.

The drop is not so far as the sound suggests.

The drop is perhaps forty feet. But the sound is forty *miles*, the sound is forty *years*, the sound is a thing that exists without scale.

It is the sound of water falling.

From somewhere beneath the overhang on the northern wall — a crack in the basalt where the rock itself has given way along some ancient fissure — a river emerges. It does not emerge gently. It is not a spring, not a seep. It is a body of water, the size of a man, that has been falling for so long that it has worn its own chamber in the cliff and then forgotten the chamber and just kept going, the falling now part of the rock, the rock part of the falling, the two of them having agreed to disagree about which is which. The water strikes the pool below and the pool has long since stopped being a pool and has become a *roar*, a continuous detonation of white noise that fills the hollow like a second atmosphere. You do not hear it so much as inhabit it. After the first hour, you stop trying to speak.

It is cold down here in a way that has nothing to do with temperature.

The walls rise vertical — almost. They lean inward at the top, so the hollow is shaped less like a pit than like the inside of an inverted bell, narrowing to a ragged circle of grey sky far above. The stone is wet, and on the stone is everything that wet stone has agreed to grow. Moss in shades of black-green and almost-black, so dark it reads as the absence of color rather than its presence. Ferns unfurling in the spray, each frond a thin question. Liverworts in pale crusts. A kind of saxifrage that has never seen sun and has become, over many generations, almost translucent — its leaves the color of an idea of a leaf. The lushness here is not the lushness of abundance. It is the lushness of patience. Things grow because they cannot stop growing, because the water will not let them stop, because the cold keeps everything at a pace where a single year is only a centimeter of moss.

The pool itself is dark. Not the dark of shallow water but the dark of depth — twenty feet perhaps, or fifty, no one has ever fathomed it because no one has ever wanted to stay long enough to lower a line into it. Something luminous moves in it, far down. Not a fish. Or if a fish, a fish with a light that does not behave the way fish-light behaves, but this might be imagination, because down here, where the roar lives, the imagination gets loud too.

The floor of the hollow is mostly stone, worn smooth in the centuries the water has been polishing it. There are places where the stone is split into irregular flagging, and in the cracks of these flags, in the joints and seams, more moss, more fern, the tiniest of trees taking root in the impossible — a single birch, perhaps three feet tall, growing from a crevice a half-inch wide, its leaves the yellow-green of something that has never had to compete for light because light has never been the point.

The structures here are few and small and old.

A ring of stones at the eastern margin, where the splash is less — weathered so that each stone has become the shape of its own erosion, rounded to a kind of pebble-personhood. Whether this was a hearth or a marker or a place where someone once sat, no one knows now. Inside the ring, a scatter of worked flint. A blade, perhaps, or the ghost of a blade. A core stone with the scars of flakes removed long ago. Beside it, a length of antler, smoothed at one end into something that might have been a tool or might have been a thing to hold when the cold and the sound became too much.

There is a shelter.

Against the southern wall, where the overhang of the rock gives the most protection from the rain that sometimes falls in from the opening above, someone — some when — has built a small structure of stacked stone and driftwood and the bones of large animals, lashed with withered hide. It is barely a room. It is a *suggestion* of a room. A corner against a wall, a low roof that has partly collapsed, a single intact piece of wall that rises to about waist height and then stops being a wall and starts being weather. Inside the shelter, on the ground, a layer of ash so old it is no longer ash but a kind of pale mineral, a thin soil. From that soil, moss. A single fern, larger than the others, has taken hold in the center and grown into something almost architectural, its fronds spreading like the ribs of a small vaulted ceiling no longer there.

The person — the people — who lived here are not here now. They have been not-here for a very long time. Whatever they did here, the water has been doing for longer, and the moss has been doing for longer than that, and the basalt has been doing for longer than any of it.

The hollow is mostly empty in the way that a bell is mostly empty. The emptiness is the point. The emptiness is the shape. The sound lives in it, and the cold lives in it, and the slow green dark lives in it, and a few stones arranged by hands that did not think of themselves as history, and a single birch growing from a crack, and the continuous falling of water that has been falling since before anyone thought to stack these stones into a ring.

A gull cries far above, from the lip, but the sound of it does not reach you. The roar eats it. Everything the world sends down is eaten by the roar, and what cannot be eaten simply does not arrive.

If you stand here long enough — long enough that your body stops vibrating in sympathy with the noise, long enough that you can hear the *texture* of the sound rather than just its volume — you begin to notice that the roar has currents in it. There are moments when it surges — a log has passed through the fissure above, or the underground river has swollen with a distant rain — and moments when it ebbs, dropping to something merely loud, merely the loudness of surf or a highway seen from a distance. In the ebbs you can hear the drop of single water. The slow drip from fern to fern. The creak, once in a long while, of the birch adjusting itself to its own weight.

In the surges, you cannot hear yourself think. You can only stand in the spray and the cold and the dark and be inside the roar the way a person is inside a church — held in the noise the way a thing is held in its own emptiness.

Nothing changes here.

The moss grows at the rate moss grows. The water falls at the rate water falls. The ferns unfurl, the lichen creeps, the basalt weathers, the flint erodes further into its own absence. A thousand years from now, the ring of stones will still be here, less recognizable but present. The shelter will have become part of the floor. The birch, if nothing finds it, will be a small tree, then a larger one, then a dead one, then a log, then part of the stone, then the stone, then nothing.

The sound will still be falling.

This is a place that has settled into what it is. It is not becoming anything else. It is not waiting for anything. It is the *having-become* of a place — the long, settled, in-its-own-state form of a thing that has finished arriving.

If you come here, you will leave.

The hollow will not notice.
