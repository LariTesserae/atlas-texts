# The White Basin of Hethran

It is a hidden place. You would not find it unless you knew where to look. A fold in the land, a deep basin rimmed with crumbling escarpments of shale and ochre tuff, entered through a single defile so narrow that a person must turn sideways to pass. The defile opens, and the world changes.

The floor of the basin is white. Miles of it — pale crust, salt flats, gypsum pans, mineral crusts so old and so dry they ring like pottery when struck. The sun is enormous overhead, low-altitude even at noon because the basin sits well below the level of the surrounding country, and the air is so clean and so dry that light does not diffuse. It arrives in slabs. It comes down and hits the white floor and comes back up, and the result is a doubled brightness, a place where shadows are sharp-edged and the spaces between them are almost painful. The horizon is a low broken line of canyon walls and the white continues forever outward toward it.

Nothing grows that you would recognize as a plant.

And yet the basin is green.

Or it is something that answers to *green*. There are forests here, of a kind. Stands of translucent stalks rise from the salt crust — some as tall as a man, some knee-high, some merely fibrous mats of crystalline threads pressed flat against the ground. They do not have leaves. They do not have roots, or they have roots that go down only into the upper crust, drinking dust. They have geometry. They are faceted. They catch the sun and throw it back in prismatic smears across the white floor, so that the whole basin, in certain hours, is patterned in moving color. The air through them is full of chiming.

---

The sound is the first thing.

You hear it before you see anything, before you round the last bend of the defile. It is a sound like a thousand struck glasses, like the inside of a chapel being slowly destroyed, like the wind through reeds except the reeds are made of something harder. The crystalline forest sings. Every stalk and lattice of it, from the smallest needle to the tallest column, vibrates in the moving air. The wind comes down the defile and into the basin with nowhere else to go, and the basin funnels it, and the forest turns the funnel into music — a layered, shrill, sustained, almost unbearable music, the kind that lives in the upper register of the ear and does not let you rest inside your own head.

The basin is a sounding box. The walls hold it in. The wind is endless; this is a place where pressure differentials across the rim are constant, so air always wants to pour in or out, and the crystalline forest is tuned to receive it.

---

There is a town.

It is not a large town — there is no room for one — but it is dense. The buildings are packed tight against the leeward wall of the basin, where the escarpment is highest and casts a partial shadow through part of the day, and they climb the wall in tiers. They are made of salt block, of cut crust slabs, of dried mud reinforced with mineral fiber from the very plants that surround them. They are low and thick-walled, with narrow windows and heavier doors. They are layered — older structures embedded in newer ones, walls from one era showing through the floors of the next, a settlement that has been here long enough to grow over itself rather than out. Roofs are flat and weighted. The streets are alleys, hardly wider than two people walking abreast. The wind, even at street level, even between the buildings, is a thing you lean against.

The people here — few in number, dressed in pale cloth wound tight against the abrasive air — do not speak in the open. They speak inside. The sound outside is too much for conversation. They have learned to mouth at each other across distances, and to use gestures, and to wait.

But inside the houses, in the thick-walled rooms, it is bearable. There is a low hum, an undertone. The walls are built to absorb some of it. The ceilings are shaped to damp it. They sit in these interior chambers, and they live there, and the outside world is a roar.

---

The ecology is closed, in its way, and old.

The crystalline flora feeds on the sun. It is photosynthetic but it does not use water — or it uses so little that none is ever visible. It takes what mineral vapor there is, what trace moisture the wind brings from distant ranges, and it does the rest with light. It is a slow life. It grows a finger-length in a season. But it has been here longer than anyone can remember. The stalks that have stood for a century are massive, as thick as a thigh, ringing like bells in the wind, and the oldest are nearly buried in the accumulated drifts of their own fallen spicules.

There are very few animals. A small lizard that lives in the cracks of the wall and comes out at dawn when the wind is briefly still, before the day begins. A pale moth that drinks at the basin air — it is not clear what it eats; perhaps the dust, perhaps the mineral spore of the plants. A burrowing thing under the salt crust, glimpsed once in a long while, that eats fallen chitin and shed crystalline fiber. There are no large creatures. There could not be. There is nothing for them to eat.

The fauna is rare, and quiet when it exists, and what makes the sound is not them.

---

The light, even in the houses, even in the alleys, is relentless. The people cover their faces. The whites of their eyes are perpetually narrowed. The buildings are dim — deliberately dim — with thick doorways and inner chambers and small high windows that admit a controlled slice of brightness. The crystalline forest, in full noon sun, is a blazing lattice, and the air through it is filled with moving color, and the color is almost the only thing the eye finds rest in, because the eye cannot rest in the white floor or the white walls or the brightness of the sky.

The strange thing is the color. It is not decoration. It is the language of the basin. The wind moves through the lattice and the lattice throws color and the color moves across the floor and the floor gives it back and the people, in their dim houses, watch it through narrow windows. They have learned the patterns. The wind from the north makes certain colors at certain hours. The wind from the south makes others. They tell time by it.

---

The rules of the place, such as they are:

The basin is a sink. Anything that drifts in tends to stay. The mineral dust accumulates. The crystalline fiber accumulates. The salt crust thickens. The town has been built on accumulated depth, and underneath the town there are older towns, fragments of older towns, the evidence of past attempts to live here.

The basin is also a sounding box. Anything that sounds in it sounds louder than it should, and the sound carries. Conversations heard inside can be heard outside, distorted. Sounds made deep in the crystalline forest carry to the walls and are held and sent back. The place echoes itself. Sometimes, in the late afternoon, when the wind shifts, the town is filled with a sound that came from nowhere — the long echo of something struck, somewhere, hours ago, returned at last.

The basin is also a sealed environment in the chemical sense. There is almost no water. The chemistry of the place is not the chemistry of a watered world. The crystalline flora is not really flora. The salt is not really salt. The dust is mineral and mineral alone, and the air is dry and the air is sharp and the air is full of suspended particulate from the constant grinding of the crust.

And yet life, of a kind, endures.

---

You stand in the basin and the sound is in you, and the light is in you, and the white floor goes out to the broken horizon in every direction. The crystalline forest sings. The town leans against the wall. The wind pours in through the one defile and cannot find its way out again.

It has been like this for a long time. The erosion is settled. The place is in its own state. It will not change quickly. The people have learned the rules. They build thick, they speak inside, they watch the color move across the floor, and they endure the sound that does not stop.
