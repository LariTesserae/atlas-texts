# The Rooting

## I. What the body hears first

A sound like a heart the size of a cathedral.

You arrive at the edge of a place and your ears do not know what to do. There is no silence here. There is never silence here. The roar is the roar of every leaf in a forest pressing its wet underside against its neighbor, the wet *slap* of it, the slow wet *squeeze*, and under that the sound of water moving through wood, water pulled up through a hundred thousand straws by a thirst that has been drinking for nine hundred years. You feel it in your sternum before you understand it is sound. It is the noise a wooden ship makes in a heavy sea, except the ship is the forest and the sea is the air and the air is also a kind of wood, in this place.

A single bird, somewhere up in the cathedral of green, opens its throat and what comes out is not a song but a *phrase* — a long, low, hollow, interrogative note, three seconds long, that the whole forest seems to lean toward, and then another bird answers it from a different impossible angle, and then another, and the three notes braid together into a chord that does not end so much as it forgets to stop, it simply becomes the weather, becomes the dripping, becomes the next breath of the canopy.

## II. The shape of the place

It is a bowl. Not a crater — a bowl, the soft kind, the kind a thumb might have pressed into clay. The walls of the bowl are the city. The city is a *vegetation*. There is no other way to say it and be honest.

The walls are a single organism, or many organisms that have agreed to become a single one, or many organisms that have been forced to become a single one — you cannot tell from outside, and you cannot ask, and you should not ask, because in this place questions made into sound become something else, become a kind of fruiting body that drops from the ceiling and sprouts in your lungs.

The walls are built of trunks. Trunks the width of a man's arms stretched out, trunks the width of rooms, trunks the width of nothing you can compare a trunk to. They are not standing as trees stand. They are standing as columns stand, as a colonnade stands, as the pillars of a hypostyle hall stand. They have grown together at their tops into a continuous vaulted canopy, and between the trunks — between the columns — there is wall. But the wall is not wall. The wall is *woven*. It is lianas, it is aerial roots, it is the felted felt of a million secondary stems pressed and felted into a fabric as dense as felted wool, as dense as felted time, and through this fabric, here and there, openings have been left, or grown, or cut. They are door-shaped. Some are tall enough to ride a horse through. Some are tall enough to drive a cart through. Some are tall enough for a heron to spread its wings.

It has been growing like this for a very long time.

The floor of the bowl is not a floor. The floor is a marsh, but a marsh that has been *considered*. There are paths through it. The paths are made of root — of root deliberately laid, of planks that are also roots, of stepping stones that are also the tops of buried trunks. The marsh is shallow. The water in it is brown and warm and smells of tannin and of something sweeter, the way jungle water smells when a fig has burst into it and the fish are eating the fig and the fig is also eating the fish in the way figs eat. The water is at the level of a child's knees in the paths. Off the paths, it is deeper, and in the deeper places things grow that you cannot see the tops of because the tops are under the water and the bottoms are above the water and which is which is the question the place keeps asking.

At the center of the bowl, where the marsh is deepest, there is a single trunk. It is the diameter of a small house. It goes up, and up, and up, and does not stop. It is the central pillar of the cathedral. It is the original. Everything else grew from it. You know this without being told. You know this the way you know the name of a thing you have never seen.

## III. What the place is, and what is in it

It is a settlement. That is what it is. People live here.

You see them on the paths. They are small under the vault. They move the way one moves in a market that has been going on for a long time — with the unhurried exactness of people who know where every root is. They carry things in baskets. They carry things on their heads. They carry things in their mouths — a long stick with a small bundle lashed to the end of it, held sideways, the way you might carry a torch except the bundle is not a torch. A woman passes you with what looks like a small animal sleeping in a fold of her garment, and it takes you a long time to realize it is her hand, and what is sleeping in the fold is the hand's own dreaming.

They speak to each other in low voices, and the forest swallows the low voices and returns them as the *slap-slap* of leaves and the slow wet squeeze of water in wood. They speak rarely. They do not need to speak often. The forest is doing most of the speaking. The forest has been speaking for nine hundred years.

There are animals. There are many animals. This is the thing the place is loudest about, besides the water and the wind in the leaves. There are creatures in the canopy that you only ever see in pieces — a tail, a wing-edge, an eye — that move with the slow, considered grace of animals who have never in their evolutionary history needed to move quickly because nothing here is hunting them, or because what hunts them is not faster than they are but is more patient. There are creatures in the marsh — you see a long brown back break the surface, and the water dimples behind it, and a heron, on a root near you, watches the wake with the satisfied concentration of a regular customer at a bar. There are creatures in the woven walls — you see a face made of leaves looking at you from a window-shaped opening, and you realize after a moment that it is a face made of leaves and that it is also a face. It blinks. It withdraws. The leaves close over it.

There are no machines. There are no tools, or there are tools so incorporated into the forest that you cannot see them as separate from it — a ladder that is a vine, a pulley that is a curled frond, a lever that is a branch. A man uses a long pole to push a small boat through the marsh, and the pole is a pole, but it is also still attached, by a living strip of bark, to the tree he cut it from. He has not cut it. He has *asked* it. The tree will grow another. The tree will grow another in a week.

## IV. The weather of the inside

It is raining, but the rain is not falling. The rain is *inside the building*. The canopy is so dense, so woven, so many leaves thick, that the rain that falls on the outside of the bowl does not come through. Instead, it *condenses*. It is the principle of a lung. The forest breathes in the wet air from outside, and breathes out the wet air inside, and the wet air, when it meets the cooler air of the shaded interior, becomes water again, and the water falls, in great slow drops, from the underside of the vault. It falls on you. It falls on the marsh. It falls on the backs of the animals. It falls on the central pillar and runs down it in sheets. The whole place is, in this way, a still. The whole place is distilling itself.

The drops are large. They take a long time to form. When they fall, they fall with a weight that is almost personal, and the sound of them on the marsh, on the leaves, on the woven walls, on the backs of the animals, on the small still heads of the people, is a sound like a slow drum being played by many hands that have agreed, without rehearsal, on the tempo.

The air is warm. The air is so warm it is almost a second water. It moves, but slowly, in currents you can feel on your skin, and the currents carry smells — wet wood, wet leaf, the sweet rot of a fig, the musk of an animal you cannot see, the iron smell of the marsh, the green smell of the cut where someone has, today, asked a branch to come down. You breathe and you are breathing the place. You are breathing its body. It is breathing you.

## V. The unreality

You notice, at some point, that the proportions are wrong.

Not wrong as in wrong. Wrong as in *negotiated*. The distance from where you stand to the central pillar is a distance you could walk in forty steps, but when you walk it, you arrive in eighty. The heron on the root is, you are certain, twelve feet from you, and then it is four feet from you, and you did not see it move. The woman with the sleeping hand passed you a moment ago, and she is passing you now, and she is passing you again, and each time her face is slightly different and her bundle is slightly different and the animal in the bundle is slightly different, and she does not acknowledge you, because for her these are all the same passing, and you are the one out of step.

The forest is not lying to you. The forest is *editing* you. The forest has been editing its inhabitants for nine hundred years, has been gently, persistently, patiently *pruning* them — taking the edges off, smoothing the transitions, making sure that nothing happens too fast, that no shape is too sharp, that no sound is too sudden, that no question is too pointed. The forest does this because the forest remembers what happened the last time a sharp thing happened in it. The forest remembers the axes. The forest has *forgiven* the axes, but the forest has not *forgotten*, and the woven walls are, in part, a record. The walls are not a fortification. The walls are a *settlement of accounts*.

This is why the heron is patient. This is why the woman does not speak. This is why the rain takes so long to fall. This is why the sound is so loud and so continuous — because the sound is the *agreement*. The sound is the forest and its inhabitants agreeing, every moment, that this is how it will be. That nothing sharp. That nothing fast. That nothing unmediated. That the rain will fall when it is ready. That the bird will sing when the chord is full. That the woman will pass when the passing is correct.

## VI. What you do, in the place

You walk on the paths. You look at the small still heads of the people. You listen to the roar.

You understand, at some point, that you have been here a long time. You understand this not because you are tired but because the central pillar has changed — it has a new ring around its base, a pale ring of fresh wood, the color of a wound that is healing cleanly. The woman has passed you seventeen times. The heron has eaten four fish. The bird has sung its phrase, and the other birds have answered it, and the chord has become the dripping, and the dripping has become the next breath of the canopy.

You sit on a root at the edge of a path. The root is warm. It is warm because something is moving through it — sap, water, a slow pulse. You put your hand on it and the pulse greets your hand. It is not a metaphor. It is a small, slow, deliberate greeting from something that has been greeting things for nine hundred years.

You do not, in the end, ask the place any questions. You understand that questions, here, are a kind of axe. You understand that the place would answer you, but the answer would be a *pruning*, and you are not yet sure which of your edges you are willing to lose.

So you sit. You listen. The roar is the roar of a heart the size of a cathedral, and the heart is patient, and the heart has been beating for nine hundred years, and it is not, you realize, going to stop.

You sit. The rain takes its time about falling.
