# The Pump-Room

The stairs begin in daylight and end in dark.

They are cut from a dark grey stone — basalt, or something close to it — and the treads have been worn into shallow scoops by use and weather until they hold water the way gutters hold water, each one a thin black mirror. Iron handrails flank the descent, and the iron has wept rust down itself for so long that the balusters are striped in long burnt-orange tears. My hand on the rail comes away cool and damp and smelling of iron and something older, a mineral smell that has no name but belongs to the inside of stones that never fully dry.

The air thickens as I go down. It is cool, and wet, and it sits on the skin the way fog sits on a field — not falling, just present. After eight or nine steps the sky is a pale rectangle above me and below it a rectangle of something darker, framed by a brick lintel where someone has set a lintel of brick over older stone. After another ten the light is a memory.

Then the chamber.

I had been told it was small. Cathedral-scale is what someone said, and that is about right — not vast, not a cave-system, but a single room with the proportions of a generous church. The ceiling is somewhere up in dimness. The walls are the dark stone again, and they are not smooth. They are cut, dressed, jointed — a built thing, this, not a natural cavity — but they are old and the joints have been colonized. Calcium has bled out of them in pale rims. Iron salts have bled in, in brown. In places the surface has softened into a kind of stucco of mineral deposit, and in those places small clustered things have taken hold, white-and-rust accretions that look, when I lean close, halfway between coral and fungus and the foam you find on a long-unmoved pond. I do not know which they are. They have no leaves and no proper caps and no obvious animal shape. They are just there, layer on layer, packed dense as barnacles on a hull.

Every surface is wet. The walls weep. The ceiling, somewhere above, sweats; the drops form, hang, fall, and you can hear each one, because this room is quiet in the way a well is quiet — not silent, but with a sound of its own that drowns out smaller sounds. I stand still and listen. The drips are layered, each one at its own rate, and they build a soft polyrhythm against the lower sound that underlies everything here: the lap and shift of water on stone.

The floor of the chamber is mostly water.

It lies in a shallow sheet across the whole space, varying from ankle-deep at the edges to perhaps knee-deep in the middle, where the original floor has dropped or been cut lower. The water is clear but dark. Through it I can see stone again — the dressed floor of whatever this room was built to be, with channels worked into it in long shallow grooves that all run toward a central grated pit. Iron grate, long ago. Some bars are gone, some are still there, dark and crusted. The channels are silted now, packed with a black ooze that is mostly silt and partly the cast-off of small lives.

And the room is full of small lives.

I had been told about the fauna and I had not quite believed it.

The frogs are on every ledge and every step and every broken brick of the ruined wall-features. They sit in rows. They are small, brown, plump, and they watch me with the flat patience of things that have nowhere else to be. As I move my foot into the water they go — not all at once but in a slow rolling wave, plop, plop, plop, each one leaving a small ring. They resettle almost immediately. The noise of them is not the noise of a forest at evening — there is no chorus here. Just the occasional *tok* of one calling, and then the answer of another somewhere across the room, and then silence again.

In the water, when I crouch and look, the fish are pale. They hang in loose shoals just above the silted channels, and they move with a dreaming slowness, drifting, settling, drifting on. They are not large — finger-length, some longer. When I bring my hand near the surface they do not dart away. Their eyes are not right. I look closer, and I look again. The sockets are there. The lids are there. But where the eye should be there is only a smooth skin of the same pale grey as the rest of the head. They are eyeless. They have been in the dark long enough that the eyes are gone, or were never properly made, and they navigate by some other means — lateral line, perhaps, or the pressure of water against their sides.

This is the first strange thing. I note it and move on, because there are more.

Along the walls, in the band just above the waterline, the snails have built a city. Their shells are small, dark, clustered in overlapping tiers, packed so close that the stone beneath is invisible. Between them, slower, longer, the worms move — not earthworms, but something darker, thinner, with a faint iridescence when the little light catches them. They emerge from cracks in the stone and return to cracks in the stone. They are not numerous, but they are everywhere.

Above the snails, on the rougher patches of wall, something clings that I take at first for more mineral growth. It is in clusters of a dozen, in plates, in fans. When I bring the lamp closer it moves. It is a colony of something — a small crustacean perhaps, a shrimp-like creature flattened against the stone, each one no longer than my thumbnail, and there are thousands of them in the lamplight, in waves of pale translucent body and dark thin legs. They pulse, all together, in a slow tidal motion. The sound of them is a sound like rain on a leaf, very faint.

Higher, on the iron pipes that run along the walls at about shoulder height, there is a rust-red smear that moves. It is insects. Many of them. They run along the pipes in a slow stream, going somewhere, coming back. I cannot see where. The pipes vanish into the wall in one place and emerge from it in another. Inside them, somewhere, is the sound.

The sound.

There is a sound here that has no obvious source.

It is low. It is steady. It is not the lap of water. It is not the drips. It is a kind of low mechanical pulse, felt more in the chest than heard with the ear, a soft *hmmmmm-hmmmmm* at about the tempo of a slow human breath. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. I walk the perimeter of the chamber and it does not get louder in any one place. I put my ear to the wall and it is in the wall. I put my ear to the water and it is in the water. I put my ear to the great iron wheel that leans against the far wall, half-submerged, and it is in the wheel.

The wheel is the size of a cartwheel and weighs, I would guess, more than I do. It is built of iron ribs and a wooden rim, though the wood is long gone in places and only the iron survives in a sagging oval. It is crusted with the same accretions as the wall. Where the rim meets the water the rust bleeds out in a slow fan of orange that turns the water there the color of weak tea.

I push the wheel, gently, with both hands. It does not move.

I push harder. It moves. It rolls one quarter-turn, and somewhere far below — somewhere below the floor, somewhere in the structure I cannot see — there is a clunk, and a shift, and the sound I have been hearing *changes*. It is not louder. It is more articulated, like a word suddenly finding its consonants. Something down there has engaged.

I let go of the wheel.

The water in the chamber begins, very slowly, to move. I had not noticed it was not moving before. Now it is. A current sets in from the far wall toward me, and the pale fish orient to it, and the silt in the channels lifts in soft plumes. Something is cycling. Something has been cycling, perhaps for a long time, perhaps since this place was built.

A pump. It had been a pump-room. The great wheel was the flywheel of a pump, and somewhere below me the piston or the impeller is still on its throw, and somewhere even lower is the source — a well, an aquifer, the water table itself — and the pump is bringing that water up and into this chamber and out again, through the grated pit, through the pipes, in a slow endless loop that has not stopped. The wheel had been there to be turned by hand, in case the engine failed. The engine has failed. The wheel is there anyway. The pump runs on what it runs on — the falling of water from somewhere higher, the pressure of the table against the level of the floor, the slow accumulated gradient of a basin that has been wet since before anyone built here.

The light in the chamber is dim. There are no lamps. There is one opening above, very far above — the shaft I descended — and it admits a column of grey daylight that falls in a square on the water and shivers there. The rest is a kind of dark that is not full dark. The walls hold a faint sheen. The accretion-fans on the higher stones give off, when I look away and then look back, a suggestion of pale greenish light. Not enough to read by. Just enough to know that the room is not empty when I turn my back to the shaft.

I sit down on a step that is not submerged. A frog settles beside me at a careful distance. A beetle of some kind crosses the step in front of me, busy with its own errands. The water laps. The wheel ticks very faintly, where I rolled it, in its new position. The sound underneath continues — *hmmmmm, hmmmmm* — and the chamber breathes with it, and the water moves, and the pale fish drift over the dark channels of a floor no one has walked dry in a hundred years.

I stay a while.
