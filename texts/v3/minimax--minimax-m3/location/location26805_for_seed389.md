# The Furnace Settlement

You arrive at altitude.

The first thing is the heat, but not the way heat usually works. It doesn't press down on you or radiate from a single source. It is simply there, in the air, in the thinness of the air itself, the way a wire carries current without appearing to move. The sun is high and white and the shadows are sharp-edged and the temperature is a thing you do not escape by going indoors. It persists. It sits in the metal. It lives in the stone.

The land is new. This is the strangest part, and you cannot quite name why.

Everything looks recently broken. The ridges of dark rock that surround the basin where the Settlement sits have not had time to round themselves. They are sharp. They are glassy in places, where the surface of the stone has the look of something that flowed and then stopped flowing and has not yet learned how to weather. The dust at the bottom of the basin is pale grey and tastes of nothing and it doesn't quite cover the ground evenly — it gathers in pockets against the wind, and the rest of the floor is exposed rock, almost black, with a faint sheen. When you step on it, the sound is small and dry and almost nothing. The whole place sounds almost nothing. Wind, occasionally. A far-off hiss. The Settlement itself, humming very low.

There is no grass. There are no trees. There is nothing growing anywhere that you can identify as a plant. What there is, in the cracks of the basalt, is a thin orange crust that may be mineral, or may be a kind of life, or may be both. You cannot tell. It does not move. It is slightly damp to the touch, and that is the first water you have encountered, the dampness of the place manifesting as condensation on every surface that has had time to cool — the inside of pipes, the underside of corrugated roofs at dawn, the lower edges of the great settling tanks in the eastern quarter. In the morning the metal sweats. By afternoon the metal is dry. By evening the metal sweats again. This is the water cycle here, such as it is.

There is one place where water collects properly. A natural basin, a low point in the rock, lined with something that has been poured into it — concrete, or a denser compound — and a pipe comes down from somewhere and feeds it. The water in it is the color of weak tea and it steams faintly in the cool of the early hours. The Settlement draws from it. The Settlement returns to it. The Settlement is the only thing that moves water here, because the clouds that pass over do not often stop. When it does rain, the rain falls in fat, separate drops on the corrugated iron and the sound is sudden and percussive and then it is over, and within minutes the streets are dry again, and the air smells of hot stone and ozone.

The Settlement is dense. This is what you notice as you approach along the access road, which is not really a road but a graded seam cut into the side of the basin, packed and rolled but unpaved. The Settlement crowds the far wall. It rises in tiers. The first tier is the workshops — long, low buildings of riveted plate, windows small and high, with stacks that go up and do not quite smoke but breathe out a slow heat-shimmer that you can see against the lighter rock behind them. The second tier is the housing blocks, square and stacked, painted in colors that have faded to a dusty uniformity. The third tier is the upper works, the mills, the separators, the long pipes that walk on their own legs across the slope toward the higher ridges. There are walkways between the tiers, some roofed, some open. There are stairways. There are rail-lines that serve the upper mills and the little ore-cars that move along them with a slow, patient motion, as if they too are operating on Settlement time, which is not the same as the world's time.

The Settlement is strange. You do not have a word for the strangeness. It is partly the geometry of the place — the way the buildings are cut into the rock, the way the rock is cut into the buildings, so that the boundary between construction and geology is unclear in places. There is a wall on the east side that is partly a retaining wall and partly a cliff face and you cannot tell, looking at it, where the engineered part ends and the natural part begins. There is a structure on the upper tier that is either a chimney or a hollow spire of stone that the engineers have built around rather than removed, and it disappears upward into the haze.

It is partly the materials. The metal is dark. The paint is faded. The dust is everywhere, in everything, in the teeth. The glass in the windows is thick and slightly green and warped in places so that looking through it distorts. Some of the older buildings are faced with a kind of tile, a pale, brittle ceramic that cracks in the heat and shows a redder body underneath. Some of the newer additions are not painted at all and are simply bare metal, which has already begun to take on the rust-orange color of the orange crust in the cracks outside, as if the Settlement is slowly being absorbed back into the geology of the place, or the geology is slowly taking the Settlement back, and no one is sure which way the process is going.

It is partly the silence. The Settlement is not silent — it is not a dead place. But the sounds it makes are not the sounds you expect from a place this dense, this industrial. There is no clatter. There is no shouting. There is the low hum, the one you have been hearing since you came over the last ridge, and which is in fact many small hums layered together — the hum of the separators, the hum of the pumps, the hum of the dynamos in the powerhouse at the western edge of the lower tier. The hum is constant and not loud. There is the hiss of steam from a dozen places. There is the creak of metal expanding. There is, very rarely, the bang of a distant hammer, and when it comes it is startling because of how alone it is in the air. The people who live here do not seem to raise their voices. They speak in the working voice, which is low. Their footsteps are soft because of the dust.

The animals are rare. You see one, on your second day, a small pale thing with too many legs that moves across a hot stretch of bare rock and is gone before you can identify it. You are told there are bats. You are told there are something larger that come down from the higher ridges at night, something with eyes that catch the yard-lights. You do not see them. The Settlement is not for them. They are visitors, the way the rain is a visitor, the way you are a visitor. The Settlement is for the people and the machines, and the people and the machines are for the rock, and the rock is for whatever the Settlement is making of it, and the cycle is tight and does not leak.

The light here is the light of a place that has not decided what time of day it is. It is bright, and the shadows are dark, and the heat is the heat of noon, but the sky is hazy, and the haze softens the sun into a disc that you can almost look at, and the disc does not move as fast as you expect. The days are long. The seasons are not seasons — there is a wetter time and a drier time, and the wetter time is when the basin sometimes fills with a thin fog in the mornings, and the fog condenses on every surface and the Settlement drips, and the drier time is when the fog does not come and the metal is dry and the air is clear and you can see the far ridges sharp and black against a pale sky. Both times are hot. The temperature does not vary as much as you think it should, given how exposed the basin is, and you realize after a while that the rock is storing the heat, the way the rock stores everything here, and giving it back slowly, and the nights are not cool, only less hot, and the mornings are not fresh, only less hot, and the cycle of cooling and warming is so gradual that it is almost not a cycle at all, just a long, slow drift.

You are told that the rock here is good for something. You are not told what. The people who work the mills do not say, and the foremen do not say, and the ore-cars that go up the rail-lines come back with loads that are covered and the covers are not opened in the yard. There is a part of the Settlement, the highest part, where visitors are not invited, and the spire rises from it, and the hum is loudest there, and the steam comes from it in a slow, white column that does not quite disperse but hangs in the air and is taken east by the prevailing wind. You are told that the Settlement has been here for a long time. You are told that the rock here is good for something. You do not, in the end, ask again.

What you take away, when you leave, is not the answer to the question. What you take away is the feel of the place. The high pale haze. The sharp black ridges. The Settlement crowded into the basin, dense and layered, humming in the heat. The dust in the air, the dust on your skin, the dust in your lungs that you will cough up for a week. The orange crust in the cracks. The pale water in the basin that steams at dawn. The thin rain that comes and goes. The way the light does not change. The way the sounds do not change. The way the rock is new and the Settlement is old and no one can tell you, looking at it, which one is being slowly consumed by the other, or whether they are becoming, together, a third thing, which is what the place is now.
