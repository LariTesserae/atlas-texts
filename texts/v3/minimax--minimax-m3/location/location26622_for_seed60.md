The pump house sits low in the bog. From outside, walking the causeway in, you would see only the roof — a green dome, because the slates have all slid or dissolved and the rafters are now held up by a single strangler fig whose roots have become the beams. You approach along half-rotted boards. The boards are soft, and each step squeezes black water up between them. The air above the causeway is warm and dense, the temperature of breath held in a closed mouth.

Inside, the hall is smaller than you expected and taller than it should be. The walls are not walls. They are columns of trunk and vine, lashed and grafted into the original brick, so that what remains of the brick is a memory held together by roots. The ceiling you cannot see — only leaves, layer upon layer, thick as felt, and through them a green-amber light that has the quality of light through old bottle glass, or through the inside of an eyelid.

Water is everywhere. It runs down the trunks in dark veins. It weeps from joints where vine meets pipe. It condenses on every leaf-tip and falls, and the falling is one of the sounds. There is standing water on the floor, too — warm, shallow, three inches perhaps, black with tannin, with the faint iridescent slick of old machine oil still surfacing through. Somewhere a frog. Somewhere, by a different pool, the same frog, or another. Two frogs total, as far as you can tell.

The machinery is everywhere and almost nowhere. Pipes climb the columns like lianas themselves — you cannot tell which is which until you touch them. A pipe is cold; a vine is warm. A pipe has been colonized so that the vine has thickened around it, and now the vine is the pipe's skin and the pipe is the vine's bone. There is a boiler against the far wall that has been broken open from within — by what, no one now knows — and out of its mouth pours a slow column of fig roots, white and pink, furred with mold, dripping constantly. The boiler ticks. A seam somewhere inside it vents steam in slow breaths, the interval between breaths about the length of a held phrase.

The place is loud in a low way. Dripping everywhere, but in different tones: drip on iron is a brighter note than drip on leaf, drip into a pool is a flatter note, drip onto a pool from a great height is a percussive note, and all of these notes have been playing here for a very long time and have learned to layer without crowding each other. The pump — there is a pump, beneath the floor, that is still turning. A slow rhythmic wet thump that you feel through the water on your boots more than you hear, once every seven seconds, patient as a resting heart. A hiss from the broken boiler. The creak of vines moving against metal as they grow, audible only if you stop moving yourself and listen for it the way you would listen for a house settling at night. And from far above, through the leaf-ceiling, through whatever is left of the roof and through whatever is now growing on the roof, the sound of rain on rain on rain — even when it is not raining here, even on a clear warm afternoon, because there is always rain somewhere in this watershed, and it always finds its way down.

The plants are arranged. This is the strange thing. You notice it after a while, after the awe. A wild forest does not grow in ranks. But here the columns of vine stand at intervals that match the original spacing of the building's iron uprights. The fig-tree roots trace the path of the steam main. Where the floor was once a machine shop, the floor is now a checkerboard of moss hummocks and shallow pools — the hummocks where the heavy machines stood, the pools where the aisles ran. The forest has read the building. It has been taught the building's grammar and is speaking it back in a slower tongue.

Nothing moves quickly. A vine-tip inches forward over a rusted valve-handle across the hour you watch it. The pump's cycle is about seven seconds. The frog you see is the same frog you saw ten minutes ago, in the same inch of water, considering the same leaf. But across months — across years, you can feel — the place is tightening. The columns thicken. The pools shrink toward the pump. The boiler-mouth, year by year, grows a slower curtain of root. It is not a ruin. It is a place being translated, word by word, into something else, by something patient, in a tongue no one now living reads.
