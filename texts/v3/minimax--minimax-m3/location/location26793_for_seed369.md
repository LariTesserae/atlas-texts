The basin is small enough to walk around in an hour, though the cliffs at its edges hold it the way cupped hands hold water. They rise on three sides, grey and weathered, curved where rock should fracture straight. Water comes from everywhere in them. It seeps from the joints in the stone, runs in dark lines down the cliff faces, gathers in shallow pools along the floor, and slips away through a single narrow outlet at the lowest point. The air is heavy with it. Even on a still morning, the surfaces of everything are slick — the broad leaves of the ferns, the trunks of the trees, the stones themselves sweating under a film of condensation that has no single source.

The elevation says mountains. The warmth says somewhere far south, or a sheltered pocket where the sun heats the stone and the water holds the heat afterward. The ferns grow waist-high in the gaps between trees, and the trees themselves — they grow in clusters, three or four together, then a long stretch of open ground where the grass is thick and soft underfoot. The floor of the basin is uneven. Old stream channels wind through it, no longer carrying much water because the water is everywhere now, spread thin across the whole hollow. The stones in those channels are rounded. The place has been itself for a long time and expects to remain so.

The sound is a constant thing. Not loud the way a shout is loud, but present always — the rush of a single waterfall at the far end of the basin where a stream pours over a ledge into a deep pool, and beneath that, the smaller sounds of the seepage on the cliff, the dripping, the small splashes from water falling onto leaves and stones. Together they make a steady wash, a kind of held breath that does not release. If you stand at the center of the basin and stop moving, you can feel the whole hollow listening back.

The strangeness is hard to name. The cliffs have an odd curvature that the eye keeps returning to. The trees in the clusters lean, all of them, the same direction — as if they grew toward something that is no longer there. The pools on the floor of the basin are too clear, too still, too perfectly round where they sit in their stone cups. There are a few things in this place that don't quite fit the geometry of an ordinary mountain hollow. You don't see them all at once. The eye catches them one at a time, and after you have seen two or three you begin to feel the place has a shape you haven't yet understood.

At the edge of one of the groves, on a flat stone that has been cleared of moss, there are tools. A chipped piece of dark stone, the size and shape of a hand-axe. A wooden club, lying in the grass as if dropped. A basket of woven fibers, half full of gathered roots. They are not arranged. They are not stored. They look as if they have been set down between one task and the next, and the person who set them down has been gone for a long time. There is no shelter. No tent, no lean-to, no pit-house, no cleared platform. Just the tools, and the clearing in the moss where someone has knelt or sat, and the impression of work interrupted — work that was not, perhaps, abandoned so much as completed for the time being, with the intention of returning.

No one has been here in months. The moss has begun to creep back over the cleared stone. A fern has unfurled beside the basket, its first frond still curled. The place is being slowly taken back.

The animals are few. There is something that runs through the grass at dusk — small, low, you never see it clearly, only the motion of it against the stems. There are insects in the air above the pools. There are birds sometimes, but they pass through without stopping. The pools have nothing swimming in them that can be seen. The basin's silence, in terms of creature-life, is almost total — but the sound of water makes up for it. The hollow feels inhabited by its own wetness.

The light is held in a middle state. The cliffs cut the morning and the evening down. The trees in their clusters catch the rest. What reaches the floor of the basin is filtered and even, not bright, not dark. The colors stay true. It is the kind of light that does not flatter or conceal, that lets a thing look like itself.

The pace of change here is patient. The tools are being buried, slowly. The pools are slowly silting. The cliffs are slowly being weathered by the water that runs from them — but slowly enough that the shape of them, the strange curvature, will outlast anyone who sees it now. The trees are slowly growing. The clearing where someone knelt is slowly becoming indistinguishable from the rest of the floor. The basin is not in a hurry. It will be here long after the moss has covered the last of the tools, long after the basket has rotted into the soil, long after the seat of stone by the waterfall — worn smooth by years of one body — has become simply another rounded surface in the basin floor.

There is a logic here that is older than the tools, and that may be why the person came. The basin has a kind of pull. The cliffs draw the eye toward the waterfall, and the waterfall draws the eye toward the pool, and the pool, if you sit beside it long enough, draws the eye somewhere else — inward, perhaps, or downward, into the clear water and whatever is in its depth. The place organizes itself around something that is not random. Something was here before the person, and will be here after. The tools are only one sentence in a longer conversation the basin is having, mostly with itself, in a language that uses water and stone and the slow leaning of trees.
