# The Reach of Whispering Columns

The first thing you understand is that nothing here is at human scale. The horizon is wrong. It has been wrong for three days of walking, and it will continue to be wrong for as long as you stay, because the trees are not trees and the plateau is not a plateau and the air itself is so saturated with sound that you have begun to sleep inside it without noticing.

You come up onto the Reach through a long, folded ramp of shale — a wash in the rock where some old water has carved its name and moved on. The shale is the color of dried blood and it crunches under your boots in sheets that ring like broken crockery. Then the ramp widens, and the first of the columns stands in front of you, and you stop walking because you have to.

The columns are not trees. They are not buildings either. They are somewhere between a stalk and a bell and a ribbed tendon, and they rise out of the cracked stone like the fingers of something buried reaching up. Each one is perhaps sixty feet around at the base and three hundred feet tall, and they are *pleated* — furred with vertical ridges so deep you could put your whole body inside one fold. The pleats are the color of bone where the sun strikes them, and the color of wine in the shadows, and the color of something unnameable in the rain. They are made of what looks like cork, except it is not cork. When you touch one — carefully, because nothing tells you it is safe — your hand comes away with a fine dust, and the surface beneath is faintly warm, and gives a little, the way a living thing gives.

The columns are spaced. This is the second thing you understand. The density is wrong. There is too much sky between them. You can see for hundreds of meters in every direction, and the columns march off across the plateau in their irregular spacing, and the *emptiness* between them is the actual ground — a flat, pale, gravelly plain where the rain never quite pools but the dew never quite stops. The plain is light tan. The plain is hot. The plain is where you walk, and where your shadow runs ahead of you in the morning, and where nothing seems to grow except at the very feet of the columns, where the pleats meet the rock.

There, at the base, in the dark wet crease where the column comes out of the ground, there is a riot.

Ferns. Bromeliads. Strap-leaves with red undersides. Tiny insect-eating pitchers the size of a fingernail. Mosses that throw their spores into the air in slow gentle plumes when you brush past. Mosses that glow faintly when it is full dark. The whole circumference of every column is a wet garden, a moat of moisture — because the column itself is sweating, or breathing, or *drinking* from somewhere you cannot see, and the water comes out at the base and feeds the garden that protects the column's foot. The ground inside ten feet of any column is damp. Step closer and your boots darken with it. Step away and you are on dry gravel again. Every column is a small wet island in a vast dry sea, and the islands are spaced so that from any one, you can see seven or eight others, and from none of them can you see the edge of the Reach.

The sky is huge and white. The white is not cloud — it is the heat itself, the way the air shimmers so that the sun becomes a flat bright smear and the shadows lose their edges. By noon there is almost no shadow at all. By late afternoon the shadows come back, long and purple, and the columns cast fingers across the plain that touch each other at the horizon. The heat is a thing in your mouth. The heat is metal. You taste it.

Do not think the place is empty because the plain is bare. You are being watched. You have been watched since you came up the shale ramp. The columns are full of watchers.

The fauna is everywhere. The fauna is the sound.

The sound starts before you see them. It is a low, taut, *thrumming* — not a roar, not a buzz, but a pressure on the eardrums as if the air itself were a string wound too tight. It is the columns. The columns are humming. Something inside them — a gas, a fluid, a current — causes the whole structure to vibrate at a frequency just below hearing, and the hum of every column on the plateau combines, and the combined hum is the floor of the world here, the carpet under every other sound. After two days you stop hearing it. After three days you miss it when you leave.

Above the hum is the rest.

The first time you hear them, you think there is a storm. The air is full of creaking, knocking, winnowing sounds — wood against wood, leather against leather, the sound of canvas in a high wind. Look up.

There are shapes in the crowns of the columns. The columns terminate, 250 feet above you, in a flaring crown — a ring of long, curved, frond-like appendages that bend outward and downward like the petals of a wilting flower. Each frond is the diameter of a small road. The fronds are grey-green and silver and they are *moving* — slowly, rocking, nodding, in the wind that does not reach the ground. Up there, in the sun and the wind, something lives.

The fauna are the fliers and the climbers and the hitchers. You cannot count them. You cannot name them. There are gliders with four wings of stretched membrane that hang in the updrafts against the white sky — drifting, angling, suddenly peeling away into a stoop that ends somewhere in the ferns at the base of a column, from which a muffled thrashing redoubles and then resumes. There are long pale worm-things, the thickness of your arm, that crawl along the fronds with rippling peristaltic waves, their mouths tasting the air. There are things that look like beetles the size of dogs, that click down the pleats of the columns head-first, their shells ringing against the cork. There are small dark things in the ferns at the bases — things that weave quickly in the leaf-litter and pause and look at you with too many eyes. There are flyers that are not animals at all but more like the seeds of an enormous plant, each one a five-foot funnel of pale tissue that tumbles end over end through the air and pegs itself into the gravel with a soft *thuk* and then is still.

The sound is all of them. The sound is the humming and the clicking and the winnowing and the long descending whistles of the gliders as they bank — and the occasional, gut-shaking, *thock* of something large and unseen, somewhere out in the columns, that hits the gravel hard and goes silent. There is a *thock* every fifteen or twenty minutes. You learn to wait for it. You hear it in your sleep.

This is the Reach. The Reach is named because the plateau extends indefinitely in every direction. No one has crossed it. The herders say it goes on for a hundred days in every direction, and then the columns thin, and then there is a different country. The herders do not travel that way. The herders stay.

The herders are the *built* of this place. They are few. A column-dwelling is not a village; it is two or three families, perhaps eighteen people, who have made their home in the gardens at the base of a single column. They have not cut the column or climbed it to the crown. They live in the wet ring at the foot, in low curving huts of woven frond-strips plastered with a kind of resin that smells, in the heat, of pine and pepper and something metallic. The huts are the color of the columns themselves. From fifty paces you cannot see them. The herders wear clothes woven from the same frond-strips, soft grey and pale green, and they are dark-skinned and they are small and they move unhurriedly.

They use the columns. They do not explain how. They have woven baskets of the frond-strips that they set into the pleats at the base, and in the morning the baskets are wet with the water that has sweated out of the column overnight. They drink this water. It is faintly sweet. They have long hooked poles of frond-wood, black and light, with which they reach up into the pleats and pull out the worm-things that crawl in the heights — and the worms, skewered, are roasted on the embers of the resin fires, and they taste, very faintly, of iodine. They eat the falling seeds of the columns — the larvae that hatch inside them, the small bright things that the ferns catch. They have knives. The knives are flakes of stone, lashed with sinew to a wooden handle, and they are sharp enough to part a hair. They have nothing else. They do not seem to want anything else.

They speak a language that has no word for *city*. They have a word for *column*, and a word for *the hum*, and a word for *the changing*, which is the name they give to the season when the columns bloom.

The blooming is why you came.

You have been here three days, and the changeling is late in the air, and tonight the herders are moving with more purpose than you have seen. They are pulling their resin-huts down. They are binding them into bundles. They are climbing — quickly, with the sure-footed ease of long practice — into the lower pleats of the column itself, worming their way up into the dark grooves with their hooked poles and their woven baskets, and they are singing. The song is low and tonal and has no words you can parse, and the herders down on the ground are singing it too, and the ones in the column are singing it back, and the hum of the column has changed. The hum is louder. The hum has a note in it that was not there yesterday.

You look up. The crown of the column, 250 feet above you, is moving differently. The fronds, which were rocking and nodding, are *lifting*. They are lifting in unison, in a slow wave that begins at one side and rolls around the crown like the unfurling of a hand. And as the fronds lift, the white sky is blocked out, and the air from above comes down warm and damp and smelling of something sweet and rotten, and the *thock* you have been hearing all afternoon is coming from beneath your feet.

You stand. The gravel is trembling. The herders up in the column are laughing — a hoarse, inarticulate, exhausted laughing — and the ones on the ground are pulling you toward the column, toward the wet dark pleats, toward safety, and you go, because the gravel is splitting, and the long pale roots of the column are rising out of the ground all around you, and the column is about to *bloom*.
