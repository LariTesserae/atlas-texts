The hollow is a small one. You could walk its rim in a quarter-hour, if the rim were a continuous thing, which it isn't — it is three uneven arcs of stone, each one curving gently inward at the top so that the sky is not so much seen as suspected, a paler patch in the dimness that never quite resolves into direct light. From the inside the hollow feels larger than it is. The walls lift up maybe forty feet. The stone is the color of old bone, of dust, of something that has been bleached and re-bleached by a sun that touches only the very tops of the lips above. The light down here is the light of a deep room. No sun strikes the floor.

The floor is hard-packed sediment, pale grey-brown, faintly cracked in a polygonal pattern that was set down long ago and has not needed to be set down again. The cracks do not grow. They do not deepen. They are a record of a wetter time, finished now. Across this floor a few things stand, and the rest of the floor is the rest of the floor — a great deal of it.

In the seams at the wall's base, where a thin line of silt must have collected once, there are small plants. They are not green the way plants in better-lit places are green. They are a dull olive, a dust-colored green, the green of something that has decided to be alive but has not committed to enthusiasm. They grow low and bunched, the size of a fist, perhaps a dozen of them in a hollow this size, spaced so that the eye travels a long way between any two. They are the only living presences besides whoever is here, and whoever is here is, at this moment, not here.

The walls are worn. The corners are gone. Everything is its own settled version of itself — a shape that has been itself for a long time and will continue, having found, somewhere far back, a form it can rest inside. There is a shallow scooped place in one wall where the stone is smoother than the stone around it, polished down to a faint silken sheen, and a few pale scrapes on the floor near it that could be from a tool or could be from water that is no longer here. The marks are old. They are not going anywhere. They have been part of the hollow longer than the plants, and the plants have been here a long time.

The air is warm and dry and tastes of mineral, a faint alkaline tinge at the back of the throat. It does not move. There is no wind down here; the curving upper walls keep it out, and the air that is inside has been inside for a while, settling, stratifying almost imperceptibly into warmer near the floor and cooler near the pale patch above. There is almost no sound. The small dry tick of a seed pod releasing somewhere up on a ledge, perhaps, once in a long while. The soft pop of a heated pebble cooling and splitting along an unseen seam at dusk. The silence is not empty. It is the silence of a place that has finished becoming what it is and is now simply being it.

The light does not change much. When the sun is high, a little more of it finds its way down between the curving upper lips of the walls, and the floor warms by a degree or two and the pale patch above brightens from grey to the color of weak milk. When the sun is low, or when it is gone, the hollow does not get dark exactly — it has been dim for so long that dim is its native condition. The eye adjusts. One learns to see by the memory of light rather than by its presence, and the world becomes a small interior thing, close and patient.

No water stands anywhere. No water moves. The cracks in the floor are dry. The plants are dry things that have made their arrangements with dryness and are not waiting for anything to change. The hollow has, in its long life, almost no water to remember, and does not bother to. It is a place for warm and dim and mineral and still.

Someone could live here. At some point someone did, briefly, with hand-tools and patience. The smoothed wall. The faint scrapes. The worn place against the stone where a person might have sat, or leaned, or slept. That is all. Whatever they made of it, they took with them, or it did not survive the dry, or it never was. The hollow is not interested in the story. The hollow is itself, and will go on being itself, and the small plants will go on being small, and the pale patch above will go on being almost-light, and the silence will rest in its own shape, undisturbed, for a long time yet.
