# The Glasswork Plateau

The air is dry enough to taste. You notice it immediately — not as a sensation of thirst, exactly, but as a faint astringency against the back of the throat, a slight crackle in each breath you take and release, as if the atmosphere itself has been lightly seasoned with something mineral. There is no wetness anywhere. Not in the soil. Not on a single exposed face of stone. The thin warm air holds no clouds because it has never, in the memory of the plateau, had anything to condense into them. Warm here means the temperature of sun-heated brick on a still afternoon — somewhere you could lean against, not comfortably, but without flinching.

You are high. The horizon is enormous and very far away in every direction. The ground beneath your feet is dark in places, pale in others, and never, in any patch you can find, more than a few decades old in its present form. Raw, still-drying geology, the kind that hasn't yet had time to be shaped into anything you'd recognize as a feature. There are no mountains in the distance. There are no mountains anywhere, because this entire shelf is itself the high thing — a lifted basalt plain, cracked and ridged, the surface still cooling from some violence that has long since stopped, leaving behind a country made of glass-flecked tufa, obsidian aprons, and the dull pale tan of unweathered ash. A few low swells. A few shallow scoops. The shallow erosion you find here is barely what you'd call erosion; it is the very first softening, the smallest possible yielding, as if the wind and the rare thermal shock have just begun to consider the rock as a project.

And across all of this — laid over it like a vast, lacy, motionless tide — is the vegetation.

You don't have a word for it at first. It is not grass. It is not shrub. It is not lichen in any lichen sense you carry. What it looks like, when you stand at the edge of a stand of it and let your eyes adjust, is a kind of frozen filigree: thin, branching, slightly translucent structures rising from the rock a hand-span to a person-tall, in colors that should not harmonize but do — the pale ivory of old bone, the smoky rose of a thing that has been slightly burned, the greenish-gold of bottle glass held up to a low sun, the deep amber of something that has held a charge and let it go. The structures are mineral at their cores. They are built, layer by imperceptible layer, of silica and sulfides and a dozen other compounds that you could name if you worked at it. They grow — or rather, they were once growing — by some chemistry that does not need water, that prefers the dry warm air, that uses the thin steady sunlight and the trace volatiles in the high atmosphere the way a plant on another world would use rain. They do not move. They have not moved, you can tell, for a very long time. They are the shape they will keep.

Stand in the middle of a field of it and you will think, involuntarily, *factory*. Not building — never a building — but the abstract quality of a place designed for production. The filigree grows in modules. Each module repeats, with a fidelity that is unsettling in anything biological-looking, across hundreds of meters. The same branching angle. The same terminal nodes. The same hairline spacing between filaments. As you walk, you pass unit after unit after unit, identical down to the second decimal, and the repetition makes the whole field read as a single installation. It is the pattern of a printed circuit laid flat against the ground and then stood up on its edge, run through three dimensions, and left to crystallize in place. You think of solar farms stretched to the horizon. You think of radio-telescope arrays. You think of the orderly, multiplied geometry of a thing that was engineered to do a job and then the engineers left and the job kept going a little longer and then stopped. There are no robots here. There is no panel, no bolt, no wire, no gear. There is nothing that anyone made. And yet the field, taken as a whole, has the unmistakable dignity and the unmistakable slight eeriness of enormous industry, frozen in mid-process.

The ground between the modules is almost bare. That is part of the strangeness — the lushness is total where the modules stand and almost absent in the gaps, so that walking through the field is like crossing a vast orchard where the spacing between trees is unfathomably generous and the air between the rows is empty and warm and silent. There is no undergrowth. There are no fallen logs. There is no leaf litter, because the filigree does not shed, and does not rot, and does not change. The bare ground shows the young basalt plainly — a pale dust, very fine, slightly warm to the touch, marked here and there by the hairline trace of a thermal crack that may or may not still be creeping wider.

There is no wind you can feel, although the air is not entirely still. Once in a long while there is a faint dry hiss — not wind exactly, more like the sigh of a warm surface giving a fraction of its heat to a slightly cooler one — and you realize that on this plateau, that hiss is the loudest sound. There is, beyond it, the smallest possible creak from the filigree itself as it adjusts, by some infinitesimal amount, to the slow daily heating. There is the faint dry tick of a fragment of obsidian somewhere cooling after a hot afternoon. These are not sounds so much as reminders that sound can exist. Most of the time the silence is what you actually inhabit — a thick, baked silence, like the silence inside a cathedral or a sealed kiln. The vast array of static crystalline modules does not hum. It does not sing. It has whatever voltage it has locked inside it forever, and it does not release it. You can stand in its middle and hear your own pulse, and that will be the most industrious sound in the region.

Nothing moves. A moth would be a disturbance here. A stray bit of seed would be a disturbance. Nothing comes. Nothing goes. The plateau is in equilibrium with itself and has been for a span of time you cannot estimate — certainly longer than the modest amount of erosion that has smoothed the basalt, certainly much longer than the warmest layer of dust on the lee side of any filigree stem. The *dynamic* sense of the place is of something that has completed whatever it was doing. The filigree is not waiting to grow further. It is not slowly dying. It has reached a chemistry that holds. The minerals that wanted to come out of the air and onto its surfaces have come out and onto its surfaces and now the air is empty of them, and the filaments are saturated, and the plateau is at a stable configuration of forces and compounds and temperatures that will not resolve itself into anything else without a change in conditions, and the conditions have not changed in a very long time and are not, in any way you can detect, on the verge of changing.

That is the deepest strangeness of the place, and it is one that you only really get after the first hour, when the novelty of the no-water lushness has settled and you can think straight. The plateau does not feel dead. Dead things decay. Dead things have a direction; they are heading somewhere downhill in their chemistry, and you can read that direction if you look. This place has no direction. It is *finished*. The volcanic rock has reached the most stable form it can reach at this elevation and temperature. The filigree has reached the most stable form it can reach. The dust between the modules has reached the most stable depth it can reach. And the whole vast, repeating, generous, empty plain holds itself in that arrested shape — an enormous still machine, made of nothing that anyone built, running no process that anyone could name, at the high dry warm edge of a world where it will continue to be exactly so long after you have left and for a long time before you came.

You begin to suspect, as you stand in one of the broad corridors between the modules, that the *lushness* you are perceiving is not a lushness of growth but a lushness of settlement. That all of this — the branching ivory, the amber and rose, the lacy green-gold against the basalt black — is what you get when a chemistry has, after a long patient negotiation with raw stone and dry air, finally agreed on a shape, and then repeated it, patiently, across an open regional surface, until the rock was dressed in it and the air above it was dressed in it and the two together were dressed in the warm ordinary light falling on them from a sun that, on this world, will go on shining steadily forever, because that is one of the few things here, perhaps the only thing, that has not yet finished being what it is.

You stand in the silence. The day does not pass. The light does not change in any way you can mark. Nothing adjusts to you, and you adjust to nothing. After a while — you cannot say how long — you simply remain.
