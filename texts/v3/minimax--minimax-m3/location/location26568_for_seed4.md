The settlement is not a village in the way that word is usually meant. It is a place *underneath*. The bog sits over it — a quilt of moss and peat and living root, perhaps fifteen feet thick in places, and beneath that root-crown there is a hollow, and in the hollow there are houses. The root-mat is the ceiling. The peat-floor is the floor. The walls are the curving sides of the sink itself, where the bog's weight has pulled the earth down into a shallow bowl. The whole thing is the size of a modest orchard — pocket-scale, the people say, when they speak of themselves in the third person that the dark encourages. You can walk its longest axis in the time it takes a slow ember to die.

The sink is young. A generation ago, perhaps two, the bog simply *gave* — a section of the root-mat rotting through, the weight of the water above collapsing it into a shallow lens-shaped cavity. The peat there is still sharp-edged. Where the floor was cut, you can see the slice-marks of the original failure: dark walls of compressed moss-fibre, layered like wet paper, each stratum a slightly different brown. The people came down after. They found the cold air, and the weeping, and the dim phosphorescent moss that had grown inside the hollow before them, and they stayed. The cold, they say, keeps the rot slow. They are right about this. The cold keeps everything slow.

The houses are grown, not built. Willow rods, bent green and pegged into the peat-floor with bone pins, woven into ribbed walls. Living wood, so the walls still leaf in the dim warmth of the seasons. The leaves are small and pale. They do not photosynthesize meaningfully, but they reach anyway, toward the faint glow of the moss, and that is considered enough. The willow grows a hand's breadth in a year, here, in the cold. Houses are pruned constantly or they close over their inhabitants like fists. A house in this settlement is a relationship, not a thing. The weavers speak to the walls. The walls listen, in their way.

Between the houses there is space. The place is sparse — fewer than forty people, by the last count anyone bothered to make. The empty wet stretches between dwellings are not empty at all. They are the territory of the fauna, and the fauna owns more of this place than the humans do, by every measure except the one the humans care about.

The fauna is overwhelming. That is not a figure of speech. It is an honest description. The root-ceiling above the settlement is not a ceiling at all but a living sheet of beetle and worm and slug, layered and moving, a slow tide of invertebrate bodies pressed against the underside of the root-mat, feeding on the rot, on each other, on the constant slow rain of plant debris that filters down through the fibres. The peat-floor is not a floor either. It is a kind of mud that breathes. When a person steps, the floor gives, and things retreat an inch, and then return. The walls of the houses are patrolled. The willow rods are grazed clean of any growth that is not willow. A moth lands on a sleeping face and is dismantled, mandible by mandible, before it has finished settling.

And the sound of it is — almost nothing. The fauna is quiet. This is the strangeness of the place, and it is hard to convey. Imagine a theatre full of people breathing. Now imagine they are all *worms*. A thousand small wet respirations per minute, a faint continuous seething, the sound of a wet cloth being wrung very slowly. That is the ambient hum. To this is added the occasional drop of water, falling from a rotted fibre in the ceiling onto the peat — a single tap, every few seconds, somewhere in the hollow, moving around as the root-mat shifts above. The people learn to read these drops. They tell the weather. They tell which direction the bog has decided to sag. The dropping is a kind of clock.

The light is negligible. The hollow has no source of its own except the moss. The moss is a kind of bioluminescent liverwort that grows only in places which have collapsed into cold wet darkness and stayed there long enough for the spores to find them. It gives a faint blue-green sheen — not enough to read by, not enough to recognize a face across a room, but enough to find the wall by, enough to see the outline of a hand. The people use peat-lamps — lumps of oily tallow-root burned in clay saucers — for the serious work, and the smell of these lamps is the smell of the place: animal-fat smoke, cold peat, and the green-rot sweetness of the root-mat above. The eyes of the fauna, in the dim moss-light, are small wet points. They are everywhere. They are the dominant feature of the visual field at ground level. A child grows up here and never learns to interpret a landscape that is not, at its base, a moving field of small dark glints.

The water is constant. The root-mat above is saturated; the bog is fed by some uphill source nobody has walked to, because nobody has walked anywhere, the way they mean *anywhere*. The water weeps down through the fibres in a hundred slow streams, condenses on the underside of the root-mat, gathers into long pendant drops, and falls. The walls weep. The willow rods weep. The peat-floor is water just barely held together by root. When the people dig a new house, they do not dig *down*. They cannot. The floor would not support them. They dig *in*, scooping a chamber into the side of the sink's curve, so the back wall of a house is the living peat of the sink itself, weeping, and the front is a willow screen, and the floor is a woven mat of cut peat laid over the wet, to give the body something firm to lie on.

The people are cold. They are cold all the time. They wear peat-cloth — layers of dried moss-fibre, oiled with tallow, that hold a surprising warmth if kept dry. They are dry never. The cold is not killing cold. It is the cold of a wet cellar in late autumn, maintained by a low ceiling that will not let the heat build. They sleep in twos, mostly, for the warmth. The houses are small. The beds are smaller. The lives are small. The bog is vast above them and they do not think about it.

The strangeness of the place is this: it is built, it is urban in the sense that there are streets — there are trodden paths between houses, worn into the peat, marked with bone stakes, and these paths have names — and yet it is hidden, pocket-scale, *contained*. A person could stand at the rim of the sink and look down and see, if they came at the right season, only a shallow depression in the bog, perhaps a faint green from the moss glowing in the day. They would see no houses. The willow rods do not grow tall enough to break the surface. The root-mat has, in places, already begun to re-grow across the open lip of the hollow, beginning to close it, beginning to make the settlement again into the dark. The people know this is happening. They have begun to argue, quietly, in the wet, about whether to cut the new growth back or let it close. Let it close, some say. Become the inside of something larger. Stay. Others say no — keep the mouth open, keep the sky above them, however rarely seen. The argument is years old. The bog is winning.

There are no seasons in the settlement, not really. There are fluctuations. The water rises and falls. The fauna surges and thins. The willow grows a little and is cut back. A child is born, and the parents must decide whether to keep the moss-glow dim in the birthing-room so the infant's eyes will not be damaged by even this faint light, and they decide this by feel, by the feel of the room, by some tradition the woman knows. The dynamic of the place is the dynamic of a closed system — slow, cyclical, the same worms, the same walls, the same drops, but each cycle slightly different, slightly more wet, slightly more overgrown, slightly closer to the closing. The settlement is active in the way a wound is active. It is alive and it is changing and it is in conversation with itself and with the larger body above it.

A visitor would last, perhaps, an hour before the fauna overwhelmed them. A visitor from the surface would scream, perhaps, at the first slug, which is the length of a forearm and as wide, and which lives in the thatch of every willow house, and which the people simply step around with the calm of long acquaintance. The visitor would not stop screaming. There is no way to stop, once started. The visitor would be carried out on a peat-mat by people who are used to this, who have done it before, who will scrape the worms off the visitor's skin with willow-switches and say, in their quiet way, *it is fine, it is fine, you are not eaten yet.* And the visitor would be brought up through the rim, into the bog, into the day, into the cold wet daylight, and would lie on the surface breathing for a long time, and the settlement would close over them again like a slow green mouth.
