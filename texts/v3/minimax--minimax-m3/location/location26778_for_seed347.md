The basin lies below the lip of the high ground like a cupped palm. You feel the descent before you see it — a slow grading of the path, the air thickening, the smell changing from dry stone and pine resin to clay, to rot, to something colder and greener: the breath of water held in shadow.

It is a young place. The slump that made it is recent in the slow language of geology — a hundred years, perhaps a thousand, perhaps less. The plateau above is old, high, dry rock, but down here the earth dropped, and the water followed, and the water stayed. Along the margins you can still see where the ground gave way: raw faces of fresh clay, sharp as knife-cuts, not yet softened by weather; boulders tumbled at strange angles; trees that fell when the sinking happened lying where they fell, half-buried, their roots pointing at attitudes no living tree would take. The features here have not had time to round. Everything still has an edge.

But this is not wilderness. The town is in the basin, packed down into the lowest part of the bowl as if the cold and the wet had squeezed it there. Timber and wattle and undressed stone, all dark with moisture. Roofs of moss-grown slate, of sod, of overlapping shakes that have gone black with damp. Walls stained the color of strong tea. The buildings crowd shoulder to shoulder, and the streets between them are narrow because no one had width to spare, and the buildings lean a little — not dangerously, but as if they were listening to each other, or as if the soft ground beneath them had settled and they had settled with it. Smoke rises from chimneys in thin pale lines that dissolve a few feet above the eaves; the smoke smells of wet wood and peat.

Water is everywhere, and it is not flooding. It seeps up between the cobbles of the streets, so that walking is a matter of choosing one's footing along the higher stones. It weeps from the walls of the houses, leaving white tracks of mineral deposit, beards of salt. It condenses on every surface — on the backs of handrails, on the rims of barrels, on the worn stone of doorsteps — so that to touch anything is to find it slick and cool. Gutters run along every roofline and down every wall, a thin ceaseless whispering of water from the higher ground to the lower, and the lower is where the town is, and the water comes down and pools in the basin at the center, a shallow dark lake where the rushes grow and the frogs sing under the surface.

The vegetation is overgrown but managed, since the people live among it. Moss on every roof, thick and spongy, so that from the rim above the town looks like a green pelt laid into the hollow. Ferns fill the gaps between houses where any light reaches, and where it does not reach, lichen paints the stone in pale grays and ochres. In the streets, watercress and mint have seeded themselves along the wet seams and are pulled for kitchen use. There are trees — alder, willow, the things that love wet feet — and they lean over the lanes, their leaves dripping onto the shoulders of anyone who passes. Up the raw clay banks at the margin, the first colonizers have already come: a scrub of birch, a wiry grass, a tough heath, all of them leaning down toward the water as if pulled.

It is cold. Not freezing — the basin does not truly freeze, only skins over at the edges of the central pool on the bitter nights — but cold in the way that sits in the fingers and makes you close your hands. The water is cold. The stone is cold. The moss underfoot in the alleyways is cold. The light has the white-gray character of a sky that has been overcast for a long time: diffuse, directionless, the shadows soft and without edge. There is little direct sun. The rim of the plateau cuts it off for most of the day, and what light there is comes in slants from the east in the morning and the west in the evening and is gone in between, and the people of the town have learned to do most of their work in those two slants, or under the soft gray middle light, or by lamp.

The fauna is everywhere, and quiet. The cold and the wet and the density of the packing mean that sound does not travel well. Small gray-brown birds in the alders give calls that are more felt than heard. There are amphibians in the central pool, and the air above the pool carries a faint haze of midges, and the midges feed the swallows that nest under the eaves of the higher houses, and the swallows feed on them in looping flights through the narrow streets. A fox crosses a lane at the edge of vision, mouth full. There are fish in the channels between the houses — pale, slow things, near-blind, for the water here has never known much light, and they are caught by children with hand-lines, sitting on the higher cobbles with their bare feet in the water, and the children do not speak either, because the place does not invite speech.

The dynamic of the place is slow and continuous. Water moves. Moss grows. Stone weathers at the rate stone weathers in this wet. The basin sinks another fraction each year, by some imperceptible amount, and the town builds up another fraction on top of itself — each generation adding a course of stone to the lower walls of its houses, raising them against the rising wet. Old water-channels are filled with rubble and become gardens; new ones are cut. There is predator and prey, but on a small scale: fox and vole, heron and frog, owl and mouse, the swallows and the midges. The larger rhythms are the rain, which falls often and does not need to be heavy to matter, and the seasons, which here are mostly two — wetter and slightly less wet, colder and slightly less cold.

There are no devices that would bear the name. No metal of any consequence. The work is done with stone flakes, bone, wood, woven fiber. Light at night, when there is night, is fire: fat lamps in the windows, a few torches in the public places, the wet catching the light and making it bloom.

The quiet is the thing you notice last and remember first. A place so full — water, plant, animal, building, person — and yet so quiet. The water does not roar here. The wind cannot get down. The birds do not sing loudly. The people speak low, if they speak. The packing absorbs sound the way the moss absorbs rain, and you can stand in the middle of it — in a lane between two leaning houses, with the water at your ankles and the moss on the lintel above you — and feel the density of things, the thickness of life all around you, and hear almost nothing but your own breath and the small lapping of water against stone.
