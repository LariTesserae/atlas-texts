# The Lowland That Was a Mill

The water comes up to your knees and then, a step later, to your ribs, and then it is only a wet film across the floor of a ruined boiler house, and then it is a wall again — waist-high against the windows. There is no rule to it. The water has been here long enough to have forgotten what flat means. It holds itself in bulges, in pillows, in slow swells that never crest. You cannot tell from the surface whether the next step will be a step at all.

This was, before, a mill. The country remembers the mill — its smokestacks, its night-shift lanterns, the smell of coal and lye that came down off the valley when the wind was right. That was a long time ago, or a short time ago. The time is not entirely trustworthy here. The smokestacks are still there. Three of them. Brick and rivet. They poke up out of the waterline like fingers out of bathwater, and from the tops of them, thin plumes still issue — not smoke, not steam, but a kind of grey-white breath that hangs in the air and does not move. The plumes meet the low ceiling of cloud and become part of it.

Everything is dim. Not dark — there is light, a wet greenish light, the color of old aquarium glass — but dim. The sun, if there is a sun today, is something that happens very far overhead and is being filtered through a hundred feet of humid air. The sky and the water and the steam are all the same color. You stand at the seam of them and cannot see it. Things lose their edges at twenty yards. A chimney across the flooded yard is fully visible; the base of the same chimney is a smear.

The water is warm. Not hot, but warm the way bathwater stays warm. It is a comfortable temperature, which is wrong. It smells of rust and of something sweeter underneath — rot, but the wrong kind of rot, too clean, as if the rot is being performed rather than occurring. The mill is being eaten, but the eating is taking too long; the eating has been paused on every surface; a film of green has crept up the brick and stopped, halfway, at a precise horizontal line, and there it waits.

There is no animal life. There has never been any. Or there was once and it left, and now the empty slots it left are visible in the air the way missing teeth are visible in a smile. There are flies, theoretically. There should be a heron standing on the smokestack. There should be a rat under the turbine housing. There should be swallows under the eaves of the dry room. There is nothing. The silence of the absence is part of the sound, which is — 

The sound. 

The sound is the reason you cannot think. There is a constant roar. It does not have a single source; it has a thousand. There is the water itself moving against the iron of the boilers, and there are the boilers, still running, still heating themselves against the oncoming cold that is not coming because the water is warm, and there are pumps somewhere in the lower works still pumping, and there are pulleys in a tower that turn against a belt that has frayed but not stopped, and there are the turbines — three turbines, out in the flooded yard, each one the size of a small house, each one turning at its own rate, none of them connected to anything. They are turning in the water and they are producing sound and the sound is not mechanical, it is more like a sigh. They have been turning for a long time. They will turn after the mill has fallen down. They will turn in open water. They are turning now in the open water that is the mill.

Above the water, between the smokestacks and the turbines and the broken rooflines, the vegetation has come in. Lush is a polite word. The vegetation is triumphant. It has taken every horizontal surface and every vertical surface and every diagonal surface and every surface that was once horizontal and is now at an angle. Ferns the size of dining tables uncurl from the tops of the boilers. Vines as thick as your arm drop from the crane arms and disappear into the water. Flowering things — there are flowering things — purple, magenta, a yellow so bright it looks fake, bloom in clumps on the conveyor housings. The flowers have no smell, or they have a smell that gets lost in the roar.

And in among the vegetation, the machinery. The mill has not stopped. The conveyors still run, hauling nothing, hauling green loads of kelp and creeper and the fibrous water-plants that grow up out of the flooded bays. The forges still burn — you can see the orange glow of them in the lowest rooms, the water shining around them, the water actually steaming on the iron tops of them. The dynamos still turn. Somewhere in the flooded basement, wires still carry current; you can tell because the lights are on, in some rooms. Faint yellow bulbs in caged fixtures, lit, humming, dangling above the waterline, illuminating nothing but themselves and a radius of three feet of wet brick and wet leaf.

There are scattered buildings. The mill proper is one. There is a small pump house out in the water, only the cupola visible, the rest of it gone under. There is a long low storehouse with the roof caved in and the water pouring in and out of it in waves that should not have a rhythm but do. There is a fragment of wall out in the field — no one knows what building it was part of. There are the turbines, of course, which are not buildings but feel like buildings, the way they stand. There is a smokestack with no stack beside it, just a circular foundation of cracked brick in the water. There is a road that ends at the water's edge and resumes, broken, on the other side. There is a fence that goes out into the water and bends under it and you can follow the line of it down with your eyes, a soft grey snake disappearing into green dimness.

The water is doing things that water does not do. You notice this only after you have been here for a while. A puddle on a tilted floor flows uphill. A sheet of water runs down a wall and then, halfway down, leaves the wall and hangs in the air for a moment, a flat shining membrane, before continuing to the floor. A channel of water that ought to be flowing into the main pool is, instead, flowing out of it, slow and reluctant, away from the turbines, away from the boilers, into a culvert that runs beneath the road that ends. If you stood at the edge of the main pool and watched for a long time, you would see the water level not rising or falling but thickening, gaining body, the surface becoming more itself, more present, more real, as if the water is being concentrated by standing.

The machinery does things that machinery does not do. A piston rises and falls in an open housing where there is no cylinder. A belt turns a pulley that turns a shaft that ends in the air, and the end of the shaft, in the air, where nothing should be, is driving something — you can hear the something being driven, somewhere up in the cloud layer, beyond sight. A gear meshes with another gear in mid-water, no housing, no frame, the teeth moving correctly, the two wheels spinning in opposite directions, supporting each other in the warm water. There is a press somewhere that is pressing, and the things it is pressing are not metal but air, and the air that comes out the other side is shaped, briefly, like coins, and these coin-shaped puffs of air drift away on the surface of the water and you can see them, faintly, glinting.

And the reality that holds this is not entirely committed. If you look at the smokestack for too long, it begins to drift in your vision, the bricks rearranging themselves into other brick patterns, an earlier version of the stack, a smokestack with a different number of flues. The flowers on the conveyor blink, slightly, between two positions — slightly to the left, slightly to the right — and you cannot catch them doing it. The turbines, when you watch all three at once, are turning at speeds that do not reconcile with one another; one of them is fast-forwarding, or one of them is running backward, or your eyes are wrong. The sound of the turbines, separately, is a hum. The sound of them together is a chord. The chord changes key, sometimes, when you are not listening for it.

You stand in the water. The water is warm. The flowers are blooming. The dynamos hum. The dynamos hum in a key that is the same key as the turbines, which is the same key as the boilers, which is the same key as the water moving against the brick, which is the same key as the wind — there is wind, here, somewhere — which is the same key as the silence where the heron should be. The mill is running. The mill has been running for a long time. The mill will run after you leave. The mill is running now in the dim green light, in the deafening chord, in the warm water, in the absence of any creature to hear it, in the slowness of the real, in the patience of the eating that has been paused halfway up every wall.

The water comes up to your knees. The water comes up to your ribs. The water is a wall. The water is a film. The water holds you. The water does not hold you. The mill is running.
