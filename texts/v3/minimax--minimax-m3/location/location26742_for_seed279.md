# The Resounding Low

The land is wet and the land is young, and the land does not yet know what it wants to be.

You arrive, if you can call it arrival, onto a broad floor of cracked basalt that stretches further than the dim light permits you to see. The cracks hold water — black, shallow, very still — and the surface of that water carries a skin so thin it might be oil, might be breath, might be the air itself condensing because the air is so close to saturation that it cannot hold another gram of moisture without complaint. Everything is damp. The rock beneath your feet is damp. The air that moves past you is damp. The dim sky — if it is a sky — is damp, the color of a pewter bowl turned upside-down and forgotten in a cupboard for a season. There is no sun behind it. There is a low, sourceless brightening, as if the ceiling of the world were a single stretched membrane of pale fungus, lit from within by a hunger for light that never quite catches.

The sound comes before you understand the land. That is the first thing, and the right thing, because the land is loud before it is visible. A low, constant, *thrum* — not mechanical, not biological, not geological in any way you have a name for. It is as if the basalt were a single enormous drum, and something beneath it were tapping, irregularly, with the patience of a thing that has all the time there is. Sometimes the thrum resolves into a chord. Sometimes it drops out and you are left with the wet silence of your own breathing, and then it returns, and the return is worse than the sound itself, because the return tells you the sound was not background. The sound was the *foreground*. The sound is the land speaking, and you are the small and temporary thing that happens to be standing on its tongue.

Out across the floor, the basalt rises into something. Not mountains. Not yet. The land is too young for mountains, too impatient, too recently emerged from whatever delivered it here. There are *towers* of rock, dozens of them, perhaps hundreds, rising sudden and sharp from the wet plain like the teeth of something that has not yet decided whether to close its mouth. They are black. They are wet. They are dense. They stand near enough to one another that the spaces between them are corridors rather than avenues, and from some of those corridors the thrum is louder, and from some it is softer, and you learn quickly that the towers are not silent stone but are themselves resonating, each at its own pitch, and the chord you hear when the land speaks is the sum of ten thousand small stones remembering to hum. When a drop of condensation lets go from the underside of a tower's lip and falls two meters to the standing water below, the *plip* is not a plip. It is a note. It is a soft, bright, struck-bell note that rings and rings and rings in the stone above and the stone around, and the ringing does not fade so much as *settle*, like dust finding the floor of a room no one will enter again.

The water between the towers is shallow — ankle-deep at the most, often less — and it is warm where the sun is not, which is everywhere, but the warmth is not the warmth of a sun. The warmth comes from below. Something underfoot is hot, or was hot, or is hot in patches, and the water finds the patches and sits on them, and you can see the heat as a faint shimmer, a thin distortion of the air, and where you step into a patch the warmth runs up through the sole of your foot and the underside of your thought and you feel briefly, irrationally, that you are being read. There is no smell of sulfur. There is no smell at all. The air is wet and the air is dim and the air is *full* — full of moisture, full of the thrum, full of the slow settling of every note that has been struck since the last time you were not here to hear it.

There is almost nothing alive.

That is not quite right. There is *nothing* you would call alive in any way your mouth has a word for. There is a faint, pale dust on some of the lower rocks, the kind of dust that is not dust at all but a slow accumulation of *something* — a mineral precipitate, perhaps, or the shed skin of a process too patient to be called growth. In the stillest pools, where the water is deepest, you can see, if you crouch and let your eyes adjust, a faint threading on the underside of the surface film, as if the water were knitting itself a lace collar to wear. It does not move. You watch it for a long time. It does not move. You stand up. When you look back, it has moved. Not much. Not in any direction you could point to. But the lace is *different* lace, and the water has rearranged its small interior, and the fact that you cannot say *how* is the first gift the land gives you, which is also the first warning.

The towers are dense, and the land between them is dense with towers, and the density is the second thing the land tells you, which is that you are not in an open place. You are in a *place*, capital P, a thick place, a place with its own opinions. The dimness does not come from a sky. The dimness comes from the towers themselves, from the way they stand shoulder to shoulder and block out whatever pale and sourceless light the membrane above is offering. The light that reaches the floor is a *borrowed* light, a light that has been passed from stone to stone to stone and has lost, with each passing, a little of its conviction. By the time it arrives at your feet, it is the color of an old letter, the color of a moth's wing, the color of something remembered rather than seen.

You walk. The basalt underfoot is sharp in places where the young stone has not yet had time to be patient with itself, and smooth in places where the standing water has been quietly polishing it for a season, and in places it is neither, and gives under your weight with a soft, gritty, almost-sigh, as if it were not stone at all but a very dense, very wet cake of something that has forgotten its own name. Your footprints fill with water almost as soon as you make them. The water does not erase them so much as *adopt* them. After a few steps you cannot tell which prints are yours and which are older, and after a few more you cannot tell the floor from your passage through it, and you understand, with a small, clean dread, that the land is not hostile to you. The land is *indifferent* to you in a way that is more frightening than hostility, because indifference of this magnitude is the indifference of a thing that has not yet decided whether *beings* are a category it intends to host.

The sound, again. It changes as you move. It changes in a way that suggests the towers are not merely resonating but *listening*, and what you hear is the land's own attention, returned to itself, a vast and low self-regard. When you stop moving, the sound does not stop. It rearranges. It comes to include your stopping, the way a chord comes to include a note you did not know you were playing. The land has added you. You are a small new frequency in the chord, and the chord has not objected.

In one of the wider corridors between towers, you find a structure. Not built. *Congealed*. It is the size of a small room, and it is the same black basalt as everything else, but it is shaped — doorways, low and arched, that you must duck to enter; a floor that is slightly more level than the floor around it; a bench, or something that wants to be a bench, rising from the wall with a smoothed lip; a hollow in the ceiling that has, over time, become a small bowl of perfectly still water, and the water in the bowl does not drip down. The water in the bowl is held there by *something* — surface tension, perhaps, or the agreement of the stone — and it is the clearest water you have seen, and if you cup your hands and drink from it, which you do, because the land has not yet told you not to, it is the coldest water you have tasted, and it tastes of nothing, and the nothing is so clean it feels like a memory of rain you never stood in.

There are marks on the walls inside. Not writing. Not symbols. *Impressions.* The shapes of hands, pressed flat into the stone, dozens of them, at various heights, and you understand with a slow, hot wave at the back of your skull that they are not the marks of beings who lived here, because nothing has lived here, but the marks of beings who *arrived* here, and stood for a while, and pressed their hands into the stone, and left. The hands are of different sizes. Some are very small. Some have too many fingers, or too few, or fingers that bend in directions you would need a different skeleton to accomplish. They are all gone now, whoever they were. The land has kept only the proof that they came.

You step back outside. The thrum rises to meet you, a small and almost affectionate uptick, as if the land is pleased to see you return to it. The dim light has not changed. It will not change. You understand, with the patience the land is teaching you, that the light is not dim because something is wrong. The light is dim because the land is *young*, and young things are still gathering themselves, and a great deal of the gathering is happening below your feet, in the hot dark, where the basalt is still deciding whether it prefers to be basalt or something else, and the decision is slow, and the decision is the slow dynamic, the only dynamic, the long patient wheel of becoming that the land is turning in the absence of anything that would call it time.

You are a temporary thing in a young, wet, dense, loud, dim, and not-yet-alive place, and the place has noticed you, and the place is patient, and the place is, in its vast and water-heavy and stone-heavy and almost-silent-if-not-for-the-thrum way, *kind*. The water will adopt your footprints. The towers will add you to their chord. The pale dust will, in a thousand years, accumulate a single new grain because you breathed here. The dim light will not brighten. The thrum will not quiet. The lace in the still pools will continue to rearrange itself into lace you cannot name.

You walk on. The basalt gives under your weight. The sound follows.
