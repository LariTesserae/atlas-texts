The slot.

A cut in the upper flank of a young range, where the rock is still sharp-edged and the fractures still breathe cold air from the deep. The mountains here were pushed up recently enough that nothing has rounded them. The granite is splintered. The dikes that cut through it are still crisp, dark against lighter stone. The whole face of the range looks like something broken open yesterday, and the air has the thin, mineral taste of altitude.

Into this fresh-cut geology, water has found its way. From the névés higher up — small snowfields that linger through the summer because the altitude is severe — a stream pours off the upper cliffs and falls into a slot canyon, perhaps thirty feet across at its widest, perhaps three hundred feet deep. The scale of it is closer to a cathedral than a valley. You could fit the nave of a church in the bottom, and the walls would still rise above the roof.

The water doesn't flow so much as it assaults. It comes over the lip at the top in a sheet, then hits the first obstruction — a jagged outcrop of basalt — and detonates upward into spray. The sound is the sound of an industrial process, and this is not a metaphor. There is a hammering, a rhythmic percussive quality, as the sheet of water meets successive shelves of rock and falls, meets and falls, meets and falls, all the way down. From inside the slot, the sound is so constant, so unwavering, that it stops being sound after a while and becomes a kind of pressure, a vibration in the teeth and the sternum. It is the sound of a place that is always working.

The spray is what makes the slot what it is. It rises off the falling water in a continuous exhalation, and because the cut is narrow and deep, the spray has nowhere to go. It hangs. It condenses on every surface. It saturates the walls, the mosses, the ferns, the lichens. The water is intermittent in the larger sense — there are dry weeks in late summer when the snowfields dwindle and the flow is reduced to a thread, and there are weeks in the wet season when the whole slot becomes a flue of white water — but in here, in the air itself, the dampness is permanent. You are standing in a cloud that has forgotten how to leave.

The vegetation has taken ruthless advantage. Every horizontal surface, every crack, every shelf that holds even a thumb's depth of soil, is colonized. The walls are furred. Mosses hang in beards from the lip. Ferns spread in flat rosettes wherever a stone is stable enough to root in. There are even — improbably — small trees, krummholz that have seeded into the cracks and grown gnarled and sideways, their branches reaching across the slot like dark green hands. The light that reaches this depth is gray and dim. At midday, on the rare day the sky above the range is clear, a diffuse greenish glow makes it to the floor. Otherwise, the place is dark in the manner of a forest at midnight, even at noon. The plants have adapted. The leaves are darker here. The chlorophyll is concentrated. Everything that grows in the slot is a deep, almost blackish green, the green of plants that have learned to live on what little light filters through.

Nothing moves in here that has legs or wings. The place is too loud, too wet, too unstable. Pebbles are always clattering down the wet stone. The spray itself moves like weather. Birds do not nest in a slot where the sound pressure would drown out their songs; insects do not colonize a place where every surface is sheeted with moving water. So the vegetation rules alone. Plants and water and rock. The three-way conversation of a place that has not yet learned to host the fourth.

The industrial quality of the place is not in any machine. There is no machine. There is no structure, no path, no rail or road that leads here — the approach is over shattered rock and across snow grass, and it admits only the determined or the lost. What is industrial is the relentless, mechanical, productive nature of what happens here. The water is producing erosion at a rate that would be alarming if anyone were watching. The slot is deepening, year by year, by fractions of an inch and by larger chunks when a frost-shattered slab falls from the wall and crashes down through the spray. The spray is producing calcium deposits in the cracks where it evaporates, slowly gluing the rubble together even as the water tries to take it apart. The plants are producing biomass, rotting, falling, becoming soil for more plants. The whole slot is a kind of factory, but a factory whose product is the slot itself — the steady, indifferent, ongoing manufacture of a deeper, sharper, wetter, louder place. It will not be finished in any human timescale. The sound will not stop. The dark will not lift. The walls will not soften.

You stand in the spray at the bottom and you feel, more than hear, the percussion of the falling water in your chest. A fern brushes your shoulder, heavy with moisture. A pebble the size of a fist breaks loose somewhere above you and comes down in three long bounces, each one producing a small sharp note that is immediately swallowed by the greater roar. The light gives nothing a hard edge. The water falls. The water falls. The water falls.
