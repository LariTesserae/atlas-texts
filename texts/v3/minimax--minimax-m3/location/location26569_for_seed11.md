# The Wind-Plain of the Last High Place

You come up through the pass expecting stone and you find mostly air.

The first thing the place does is take your hearing. The wind has been at this elevation for so long that it has forgotten how to stop — it roars in the throat of every gap and over every ridge with the patient, unvarying scream of something that will never tire. You do not walk onto the plateau so much as you lean into it, bent forward, hair horizontal, eyes watering, every sentence you might speak ripped out of your mouth before it forms. The sound is not weather. The sound is geography. The plateau itself seems to be made of it.

What the eye finds, once it adjusts, is a near-flatness that has nothing gentle in it. You are standing on what was once a mountain — the tallest thing for a thousand miles in every direction, once, when the world was young and stone still had angles — and the patient work of billions of years has chewed it down to a rumpled shoulder. The horizon is closer than it should be at this altitude. The sky is the wrong color of blue, deeper, thinner, and the few clouds that survive at this height move with the look of things being chased. The edge of the plateau drops away in steps to the north, each step a cliff worn soft as a bedsheet, and beyond those cliffs is a brown country of low cloud and, far below, a patchwork of green and ochre that might as well be on another planet for all the use it is to you here.

The stone beneath your feet is not stone so much as the memory of stone. It has been worked so long by wind and water that it has taken on a quality of age beyond age — it looks almost organic, almost like bone. There are places where the rock rises in ribs, low and parallel, like the back of something sleeping; places where it has been hollowed into shallow bowls that hold a thin film of water after rain; places where it has been drilled through entirely, leaving freestanding arches no thicker than your forearm that the wind sings through with a note so pure and so wrong it feels like a language you almost remember. The strangeness of the place is in these forms. Nothing here looks like geology. Everything here looks almost like architecture — but architecture that was never built, only arrived at, the slow consensus of erosion on what shape a stone would prefer to be if given enough time.

Vegetation is everywhere and nowhere in particular. The wind is too constant for trees — anything that puts up a vertical trunk is sheared off at knee height — but what grows instead is a relentless low mat of mosses, lichens, alpine cushions, tough wiry grasses, a dozen species of small fern that have learned to flatten themselves against the warmth of the rock. The colors are wrong for altitude. There is so much green here, such a saturated and unyielding green, that you keep expecting to find water, and you keep not finding it, except in the films and the threads. The dampness is in the air rather than the ground. Every surface is wet to the touch, not from rain so much as from the constant condensation of the clouds that brush the plateau like wet wool — every few minutes the visibility drops to a few yards, and you are briefly inside a grey, moving room, and then it passes and the whole impossible openness is back, and the wind hits you again as if it has been saving itself.

The lushness, when you stop to look closely, is a kind of violence. Things grow here only because they are willing to be broken and rebuilt constantly. The wind and the storms do most of the killing; the dampness and the warmth do most of the rebuilding. A patch of moss that you look at in the morning will be a different patch by afternoon — not the same individual plants, the same carpet, but a continuous churn of growth and destruction, an anthill of tiny vegetable urgency. It is the most alive and the most battered place you have ever stood.

The emptiness is harder to describe because it is not the emptiness of absence. It is the emptiness of having been swept clean. There are no animals in any direction. You might walk for a day and see nothing but the wind-driven spiders that have learned to anchor themselves with silk and run in short frantic bursts between stones. Sometimes, rarely, you will see a bird — a hawk, once, riding the air as if it had been born from it — and once or twice in a season a herd of something low and pale will move across the plateau far away, looking like a slow cloud, gone before you can decide what you saw. The plateau is too high, too cold at night, too battered by weather to support much beyond what clings and what flies over. The carrying capacity of the place, in animal terms, is close to zero. What lives here does so on the margin of survivable.

But there were people here. You can tell because the stone, in places, has been moved. There is a scatter of low walls running across a saddle to the northeast — not walls so much as alignments, the foundations of walls, single stones laid end to end in lines that the wind has not yet managed to bury or to scatter. There is the footprint of what might have been a shelter against one of the larger wind-ribs, a hollow lined with flat stones that the moss is slowly digesting. There is a cairn, perhaps, or what was once a cairn, now just a heap that might be deliberate or might be the natural conclusion of frost-heave. The scale of these marks is small. Whatever was built here was small. Whoever built it had hands and stones and nothing else — no metal, no mortar, no plan beyond staying through another season, and they did not stay. The plateau is too loud, too empty, too much. They were here and they left, and the wind has been smoothing over their traces ever since, with the unhurried patience of a thing that knows it will eventually win.

What remains is the turbulence. Nothing about this place is steady. The wind shifts direction four times in an hour; the clouds come and go like breath; the temperature, which seems moderate when the sun is on your face, drops twenty degrees the moment the sun goes behind a cloud and another ten the moment the cloud becomes a storm. The storms are frequent and short and total. They come across the plateau from the west with a noise like tearing cloth, white rain that stings horizontally, lightning that seems to strike the same rock over and over, and then they are past, and the sun is back, and the wet stone is already steaming in its aftermath, and the mosses have been beaten flatter and are already reaching upward again. There is no calm hour. There is only the kind of hour you happen to be in.

You sit with your back against one of the wind-ribs and you listen, and the listening becomes a kind of presence, and you realize after a while that the roar is not one sound but many — the deep base note of the wind through the larger gaps, the high thin whistle through the drilled stones, the slap and rustle of the vegetation, the occasional distant percussion of a rockfall somewhere across the plateau, the trickling of the threads of water that run briefly downslope after each storm and then vanish into the cracks. Underneath all of it, so low you cannot quite hear it so much as feel it in your teeth, there is the sound of the place moving — the soft, continuous cracking of stone that has been heated by the sun and is now cooling, the slow give of mosses underfoot, the patient settling of everything that is being slowly lowered, by fractions of a millimeter, toward the level it will eventually reach, when the wind has had its full say and the plateau is finally, completely, indistinguishable from the plains below.

It will take longer than there has been so far.

For now, the wind is still winning.
