# The Marshside Works

The town has no trees. That is the first thing anyone notices when they arrive, and the last thing they ever quite stop noticing.

It sits low — barely above the level of the harbor, barely above the level of the river that feeds the harbor, barely above the level of the salt marsh that lies east of both. Built on a slight rise that was once an island, or perhaps never an island at all, just a place where the water forgot to go. The streets are paved with cobblestone set in the late century of some previous arrangement, and the cobbles have settled into the mud beneath them so thoroughly that the difference between street and ditch is a matter of inches and habit. In heavy weather the difference disappears entirely.

The air is wet in a way that doesn't feel like weather. It feels like a substance. You breathe it and it is in you; you step out of a doorway and it is on you before you have gone three paces. The river exhales its fog each morning and the fog does not leave, it merely thins, and by afternoon you are walking through what feels like the same fog, only warmer. Sweat does not evaporate here. It forms, accumulates, and slides off in sheets when the body finally shifts. The stones of the buildings weep constantly — condensation soaks out through every joint, every window, every seam where mortar meets brick. You can stand against a wall in summer and feel the wall be wetter than you are.

And it is hot. Hot the way only wet places can be hot. Not the dry blaze of open country, but a held heat, a heat that won't move on, that sits in the streets and courtyards like poured water. The brickwork stays warm long after dark. The iron bridges over the canals are too hot to touch after noon. Work begins before sunrise and ends before the worst of it, and during the worst, the town empties as best it can — shutters closed, awnings rigged, the whole population withdrawing into whatever shade a stone building can offer a stone building.

Which is not much. Because there are no trees.

---

The town is built of brick, mostly, and of iron, and of timber that was brought downriver from somewhere that does have trees and assembled here into things that did not last. The timber is largely gone now — eaten, rotted, replaced, eaten again — and what remains is the brick and iron, which have settled into their own permanence. The chimneys of the brickworks lean, not from any imminent collapse but from the slow persuasion of a century of water finding its way into every load-bearing joint. The cooling towers of the foundries are made of a local stone that has gone dark and slick with constant moisture. The rail yard, where it meets the marsh, has simply sunk a foot into the ground over the years, and the rails curve into the reeds like something trying to leave.

There are no gardens. There are no lawns. The yards between buildings are packed earth or cobble, and where they are not, they are puddle, and where they are puddle they breed mosquitoes in such quantity that the mosquito has become, in some taxonomic sense, the dominant herbivore of the region — the only animal that eats anything green, and there is very little green to eat. The herons know this. The herons come anyway, and stand in the shallows of the marsh in their patient congregation, waiting for the mosquito's brief adulthood.

---

The fauna is the thing you cannot get away from.

It is the people, first of all. The town is not large by a city's measure — perhaps fifteen thousand souls in the denser wards, another ten thousand in the outer courts and the bridge-ends and the worker housing that climbs the slight hill toward the freshwater springs. But fifteen thousand wet and hot humans, working, eating, sleeping in shifts, pressing through streets that were sized for half their number, gives the impression of a place being overrun. The shift-changes at the foundries are the loudest event of the day, which is to say they are the moment when several thousand bodies are all moving at once through the same narrow streets, sweating, smelling of iron and sulfur and the day's work, and they do not speak much because there is no breath to spare. They move like a single thick liquid.

But it is not only the people.

The river gives the town its commerce, and the commerce gives the town its animals. Draft horses, in long lines, hauled off the barges at the loading docks and put to the heavy carts that bear the rolled iron and the pig metal up to the rail yard. The horses are the largest things in the town and they are not gentle — they have been driven hard, fed hard, and they smell of the heat they themselves produce. Their coats are never dry. Their handlers are rough men who are themselves not gentle, and the horses and the men have come to an arrangement that is not affection but is workable.

There are pigs in the southern ward, kept by the rendering works, fed on the offal of the slaughterhouses, which are fed by the cattle. The cattle come up from the southern grasslands on the hoof, hundreds at a time, and are held in pens along the river until their number is called. They low constantly, a lowing that carries across the water at night and is the closest thing the town has to a song. There are rats, of course — rivers of them in the cellared districts, fat and unhurried, owning the underground the way the people own the above. There are cats, kept by the milliners and the warehouse keepers, doing what they are kept to do.

And there are the dogs. The town is dog-haunted. Every dock has its dog, every yard its dog, every tavern its dog. They are not pets in the way the word implies. They are scavengers with attachments. They keep the streets in some kind of order — the bones and offal do not accumulate as they would otherwise — and they are fed for it, irregularly, in scraps. They are wary of people and people are wary of them and the wary is mutual and balanced. At night, in the small hours, they sing — not bark, sing, a long communal howling that rises from every ward and is answered ward to ward across the rooftops until it feels as if the town itself is making the noise. It is the only time the town is loud, and most people sleep through it because most people have learned to.

The herons on the marsh. The gulls over the harbor, screaming in their idiot way. The flies that come and go with the weather. A few pigeons in the eaves of the brickworks. No songbirds. The town has no songbirds because the town has no trees and songbirds require trees or at least the promise of trees. There is a mockingbird, reportedly, that has lived for some years in a tangle of ivy on the wall of the old Customs House — ivy being one of the very few plants that has managed to take hold here, on the north-facing walls where the brick stays cool — and people point it out to visitors the way other towns might point out a landmark. The mockingbird has become a small civic emblem, in spite of itself.

---

The light is wrong.

Not dark, exactly. There is light. The days are long in summer and the sun is high. But there is something between the sun and the streets — a permanent haze, a humid suspension — that takes the edge off every shadow. The sun comes through it as a bright disc, slightly diffused, the way a coin looks at the bottom of a pale pool. Shadows are soft. Colors are muted. The brick, which would in another climate be a fierce red, reads here as a kind of bruised maroon, water-darkened, salted. The iron is black with rust, not the bright orange of dry rust but a deep wet black that does not flake. The air itself seems to carry a tint — a yellowish-grey, the color of old paper, the color of things that have been left out.

In winter the haze thickens into fog, and the town becomes a thing half-imagined. The foghorns on the harbor moan at their intervals. The bells on the buoys ring. You cannot see across a street. You navigate by feel and by the calls of the dockworkers and the smell of the rendering works. Winter is when the town feels most itself, some say, though others prefer summer and the thin haze and the heat that lies on you like another garment.

---

The sounds, apart from the dogs at night, are few and far between.

The constant background is a low industrial hum — the foundries, the rolling mill, the small engine works down by the water — that never quite stops because the works run through the night on skeleton shifts, and the hum penetrates everything. It becomes, after a time, inaudible, the way the blood in your ears becomes inaudible. But when it stops — when a furnace goes down, or the mill shuts for repair — the absence is louder than the presence ever was.

The foundries give off their own sounds in irregular bursts: the clang of the drop-hammers, the long hiss of steam, the rattle of the conveyor that brings the ingots up out of the casting bay. The rail yard gives off its own: the slow clunk of cars coupling, the diesel rumble of the switching engines, the squeal of brakes. The river gives off its own: water against stone, water against hull, water against water, all of it indistinguishable, all of it ceaseless, all of it too even to count as sound after a while.

And the voices, when there are voices, are muted. People do not speak loudly here. The heat discourages it. The closeness discourages it. There is an understanding that you keep your voice down because everyone is overheated and overclose and no one wants more of anything, least of all noise. Conversations happen at the volume of a complaint. Commands on the docks are half-shouted but the half is enough. The town hums and works and does not raise its voice, and the dogs at night are the only release, the only time the accumulated sound of the place is permitted to escape.

---

The marsh to the east is its own country.

The town ends and the marsh begins, and the line between them is a long negotiation. The marsh is reed and tidal flat and salt pan and shallow standing water, miles of it, and it is mostly empty of people but not empty of life. The herons are there, and the rails, and the small crabs that go click-click through the mud at low tide. There are wild pigs that have escaped the rendering works over the years and reverted, in the marsh, to something bristled and tusked and quick. They are hunted, in season, and out of season they are tolerated because they keep the rat population from the southern cannery district in some kind of check.

The marsh is also where the town's dead are put. There is no proper cemetery — the water table is too high, the soil too thin. The dead are weighted and taken out to the deeper channels at high tide, and what the tide does with them after is the tide's concern. This has given the marsh, in certain lights and at certain hours, a quality that the older residents do not discuss. The herons do not seem to mind.

---

The town has been here longer than anyone can remember and longer than the brick suggests. The brick is a hundred years old, perhaps a hundred and fifty. Before the brick there were timber and wattle, and before that there were the marsh people, who built their pile-dwellings out in the reeds and fished and hunted pig and had no word for the place that would eventually be built on top of them. The marsh people are gone, or their descendants are the dockworkers and the draymen and the wives of the foundry hands, indistinguishable now from the rest. The pile-dwellings rotted down into the mud they were built on and became, in time, the foundations for the first of the brick buildings.

Things built here settle quickly. The water is high. The ground is soft. Every structure sinks a little in its first years, then stops, then settles into its settled form, which is a slightly tilted and slightly sunken and slightly warped version of what was intended. Doors that were meant to close have to be pushed hard against their frames. Floors that were meant to be level have a gentle list to them, a tilt that is different in every room but that everyone in the room has learned not to notice. The rail yard sinks faster than the track can be re-laid, and the workers have stopped trying to correct it and have instead learned the new curves, the new grades, the new rhythm of a track that is always very slightly going downhill toward the marsh.

The erosion is not a thing that is happening. It is a thing that has happened, and that continues to happen in the manner of a thing that has happened — slowly, imperceptibly, the brick softening grain by grain, the iron oxidizing in its wet black way, the mortar turning to a kind of paste in the joints. Nothing is being lost. Nothing is being gained. The town is in its form, and the form is the form of a place that has been wet and hot and worked for a very long time, and the work and the weather have agreed on what the place looks like.

---

You arrive on a barge, probably, because most people do. The barge comes up the river at dawn, when the fog is still thick and the town is only a smell and a sound and the suggestion of chimneys in the grey. You step off at the public landing and the planks are slick under your feet. A drayman is waiting with his cart, or a porter from one of the boarding houses, and they do not greet you, they simply take your trunk and walk, and you follow because there is nothing else to do and no one is looking at you and the town does not care that you have arrived. The town is working. The town is always working. You may work with it, or you may leave on the next barge downriver, and in either case the town will not have noticed.

You walk the streets. They are narrow and crowded and wet. The smell is iron and sulfur and rendered fat and horse and humanity and river-water and the particular sweet rot of a place where things do not dry out. The people you pass are damp and intent. The dogs eye you from the gutters. Somewhere a foundry horn sounds — a long single note — and the shift changes, and the street fills briefly with hundreds of bodies moving in the same direction, and then thins again, and you are walking in a street that is almost empty except for the rats and a single dray cart coming up out of the haze.

You find your lodging. The room is small and the bed is narrow and the sheets smell of bleach and river water. The window looks onto an alley. The alley is wet. From the alley comes the sound of someone's child, crying, and then the sound of someone else's hand on the child, and then quiet.

You sleep. The town hums beneath you, below the threshold. Outside the window the haze thickens. The foghorns on the harbor begin their first low moan of the evening.

And the dogs, somewhere, begin to sing.
