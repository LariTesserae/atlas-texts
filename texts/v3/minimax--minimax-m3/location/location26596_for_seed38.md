# The Long Plain

You arrive on foot, though how you got here is already slipping. The trail behind you was mud and root and you kept choosing left, choosing left, and the path closed behind each choice with the soft patience of a mouth. By the time you understand that you have arrived, you have been standing for some time in the rain, and the rain is not falling so much as being here.

This is the Long Plain, though that is not a name anyone uses, because there are no ones. It is what remains when the mountain has finished being a mountain. A continent's worth of weathered stone, planed by wind and water and time over such a span that the word *old* has come unstuck from anything useful. The plain sits high — high enough that your chest has a thought about it, a small reminder that air is borrowed — but the warmth is unaccountable. Down here, in the green hush, it is always twenty degrees. Always summer, the way only a place that has forgotten seasons can be summer.

## What the ground is doing

The ground is not ground. The ground is a negotiation, conducted over centuries, between mineral and vegetal, and the plant side is winning the way slow things win — not by event, but by accumulation until the event stops being notable. You cannot dig here without a tool you do not have. To press your hand into the surface is to meet a yielding that turns, a few centimeters down, into a fibrous resistance, a thing that was once a root, that was once a tree, that was once a seed that arrived on the back of a bird that is no longer here.

Underneath, somewhere far underneath, there is the old stone. It is granite, or was. It has the look of bone that has been in the earth a long time — yellowed, water-stained, mineral-veined, soft with decomposition at the edges. You can find it in the few places where the living layer has peeled back: along the cliff faces where the rivers have been cutting for ten thousand years, along the hollows where something large fell and took the cover with it. In those places the rock *weeps*. Water moves through it the way blood moves through a bruise, finding the cracks, finding you, eventually. You do not get to choose whether to be damp. You are damp.

The rivers here are slow and dark. They are not so much flowing as making up their minds. They braid through the green in ribbons of black water and you cannot tell whether they are moving or you are standing still, which is to say: here, you are both. They are warm. They smell of tannin and leaf-rot and something faintly sweet, the way a forest smells when the forest is digesting itself.

## The plants

Everything is plants.

Not in the sense of *a lot of plants*. In the sense of *plant is the category and the substrate and the building material and the climate and the citizen*. There is no dirt. There is no stone wall. There is wood, layered into wood, layered into wood, all of it alive at its outer surface and dead at its inner, and the layers are stacked into shapes that look, after you have been walking for a few hours, like architecture — arches, colonnades, walls that bow inward, ceilings that hold back a sky you can barely see.

The canopy is not a canopy. It is a ceiling, at varying heights, made of a plant that has no name in any language you speak and which does not have a single form. Sometimes it grows as a tree. Sometimes as a vine. Sometimes as a sheet of green-black leaf that stretches between two larger growths and holds still rain on its upper surface for weeks. Sometimes as a pillar of pale wood that has forgotten it was a stem and is now, functionally, a column. When the wind — and there is wind, sometimes, slow and warm — moves through it, the whole ceiling moves together, and you understand you are indoors, in a building without a builder, that has been being built for longer than building has been a concept.

The light that comes through is the color of old copper, dim and green. There is no direct sun here. Not because the sun has failed but because the ceiling is too deep, too layered, too busy with its own photosynthesis to let the photons pass without comment. By midday, if you could see a sun through the canopy, it might be noon. Here inside, it is always three in the afternoon, the hour when the light goes long and dull and the shadows stop being useful.

The plants drink constantly. They drink from the air, which is wetter than air should be — the relative humidity hovers somewhere between *saturated* and *inside a cloud*, and you cannot tell the difference because the distinction has been removed. Water condenses on every surface. The leaves hold it in droplets the size of grapes; the bark streams with it; the back of your hand, if you hold it out, gathers a fine mist within seconds. You breathe in water. You breathe out water. Your clothes will never be dry again, and this is fine, because the plants have not been dry since before they were plants.

## The animals

There are animals. They are not in charge.

A pale thing the size of a deer, jointed in places that deer are not jointed, moves through the underlayer without breaking stride. It is the color of the wood. It eats — you think it eats — by leaning its soft mouth into the bark and letting the bark run into it, the way a person drinks a long drink. It does not notice you unless you stand very still, in which case it notices you and goes on with its afternoon. There are insects, enormous ones, with wings like wet paper, that hum in the high canopy and whose larvae tunnel through the deadwood in paths so old they have become structural. There are amphibians everywhere — small ones, transparent ones, things with red eyes that cling to the underside of leaves and sing one note at a time, then stop. The whole ecosystem is, like the geology, a thing that has been worked out. There are no longer niches open. Every niche is occupied by a creature so specifically itself that you cannot imagine it filling any other role, the way you cannot imagine a particular key opening any other lock.

The animals are quiet. They are not silent — there is the hum, there is the drip, there is the occasional low bellow from something far off that you never see — but they do not call across distances. They do not need to. The place is small to them and large to you. They have their own ranges and their own paths and they move through them with the assurance of long tenancy. Some of them are very old. Some of them have been alive for as long as the largest growths in the canopy, which is to say: a very long time, in a place where long is the unit of measure.

## The strange parts

The plain is not flat. It is *settled*. The high points have been rounded down over millennia until they are suggestions. The low points have been filled in over millennia until they are also suggestions. The result is a landscape of long, slow swells, like the breathing of something asleep. When you stand on one swell you can see, between the trees, the next swell, and the next, and the next, going off to a horizon that is not a line but a softening, a place where the green meets the gray meets the gray meets the green.

And in the swells there are the marks.

Not ruins. Nothing was ruined because nothing was built, not in the way that word implies. But there are arrangements of stone — a circle of pale granite, waist-high, with the lichens thick on it; a long line of stones that goes straight for a hundred meters and then stops, the way a sentence stops; a square pit, lined with flat stones, that holds still water at a temperature warmer than the air, and in the water, small pale fish that have no eyes. You find these things the way you find shells on a beach: as evidence. Evidence of something that was here, or that the place itself once did, or that you did, on a previous walk you cannot now recall.

The fish with no eyes are the strangest thing. They live in the warm water. They are not fish, exactly — they have no scales, no clear bones, just a soft white body that pulses when you disturb it. They are, you begin to suspect, plants. Or were. Or are some third category that the Long Plain has not bothered to distinguish, because in a place this saturated with life, the distinction between a thing that grows toward the light and a thing that moves away from your hand has stopped being interesting.

There are other small wrongnesses. A pool of water that is slightly warmer than the pool next to it, and the next, and the next, as if you were standing over a buried engine that had been running for a hundred thousand years. A tree that has grown into the shape of a chair, not approximately, but precisely, with a back at the right angle and a seat at the right height for someone who no longer exists. A clearing in which the air smells of smoke, though there is no fire, has never been a fire, and the smoke is coming from the wood itself, from the slow combustion of the heartwood, which has been burning at sub-ignition temperatures for centuries and will continue to burn for centuries more. The Long Plain is its own engine. It runs on time and water and patience.

## The weather of itself

Because the place is vast — vast in the way that oceans are vast, in that you can stand in the middle of it and never see the edge and never know, with certainty, that there is one — it makes its own weather. You cannot call it weather. It is too slow for weather. But on the second day, you understand that the rain falls harder in one valley than in the next. That the mist, in some places, never lifts. That the warmth is not uniform — that there are pockets, slow-moving pockets, of cool air, where something cold is leaking up from a place you cannot find. The plain has circulation. The plain has currents. The plain has, you realize on the third day, something like a mood, in that when you walk north for an hour the birdsong thins and the undergrowth gets thicker and the light gets greener and the whole place seems to lean on you a little, the way a forest leans on you when the hour is late.

You stop walking north after that. You start walking east, which is the same as walking west. Nothing here is oriented. The Long Plain is the kind of place that has eaten its own compass.

## What you hear

Mostly, what you hear is the sound of water not falling. The leaves catch it. The moss catches it. The bark catches it and lets it run down in long slow threads that hit the floor and are immediately drunk. There is a constant soft percussion, everywhere, of water arriving somewhere after a journey you did not see. Underneath that, the hum of the insects, which is less a sound than a texture in the air. Underneath that, your own breath. Underneath that, occasionally, a deep wooden creak, the sound of a growth so large it is settling under its own weight, the way a house settles, the way a glacier settles. The creaks come at no predictable interval. They come, you begin to suspect, when you are not listening.

The Long Plain is mostly quiet the way a library is mostly quiet. Not because nothing is happening, but because what is happening is large and slow and has stopped needing to announce itself.

## The rule of the place

If there is a rule, it is this: *nothing is in a hurry, and the things that were in a hurry are now stone*.

The plain does not resist you. It does not welcome you either. It has been here longer than welcome and will be here longer than you. It will outlive your visit by the same amount it outlived the visit of the thing that arranged the stones in a line, by the same amount it will outlive you, by the same amount it has outlived the mountain that used to be here before the mountain was a thing that was here. It does not need you to do anything. It does not need you to find anything. If you stand still for long enough, you will see a leaf fall, and then another, and then you will understand that the leaves are not falling, the canopy is rising, slowly, imperceptibly, by the amount the leaves have grown, and you are the one who is sinking.

Stay, if you like. The Long Plain is patient. It will keep its weather. It will keep its slow dark rivers and its small pale eyeless fish and its trees that have become chairs and its smoke that comes from no fire. It will keep its dim green afternoon, which is, you realize, not dimming but deepening, the way wine deepens, the way a long life deepens.

The rain will resume the moment you stop thinking about it. It was never not resuming.
