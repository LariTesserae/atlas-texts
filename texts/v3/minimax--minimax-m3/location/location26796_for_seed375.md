# The Dry Cathedral

It is the size of a cathedral. That is the first thing. Not the size of a town, not the size of a country — the size of a single room, only stretched up until its ceiling is somewhere a thought might get lost. The boundaries are close enough that a voice, if there were one, would reach the walls in a breath. The boundaries are far enough that the walls do not appear to be walls at all, but a kind of weather — a permanent mineral weather, standing up.

There is no water here. That is not a deficit. The stone does not remember water. The stone is not stone. The stone is older than that word, and drier, and sharper, and it has the texture of something that was never wet to begin with — a thing that precipitated directly out of cold air into form, like the inverse of rain. Where a river might have carved a valley, nothing here was carried away. The surfaces have edges. The edges have edges. Every surface is a small cliff, and the cliffs are fresh, and the cliffs are sharp in the way that only unhandled things are sharp.

The room is built. That is the strangeness. There are walls that do not touch the ceiling. There are arches that have nothing to arch over — they stand in the open like the ribs of something unfinished. There are towers, narrow as standing men, that rise sixty feet and stop in midair, the way a column stops, because a column only ever means to stop. They are arranged. There are streets between them — narrow gaps, right-angled turnings, small plazas where three or four of these towers meet and lean against each other in configurations that are not random. A city. The architecture of a city, but no city has ever been here. No hands. No voices. No planning intelligence. Something else arranged it, something that does not plan, something that grows the way cities grow, and is just as unintentional.

The arrangement is biological. The walls are calcium, or chitin, or a mineral that is neither, layered in a slow patient accretion — ring by ring, the way a tree makes its year, except the year here is something else, something longer, something that does not need a sun to keep its count. The towers are tubes, hollow inside, and through their hollows something still moves — a faint breath of cooler air, rising. The air is cool. It does not warm. It does not cool further. It is the temperature of the inside of an earth, of an unwarmed room, of something held at a constant that has no opinion about it. The breath that rises in the towers carries no scent. Or carries a scent so faint and so dry that it is indistinguishable from the smell of having a nose.

Light. There is light, but it does not come from anywhere. It comes from the surfaces themselves. A pale phosphorescence, embedded in the calcium, in the chitin, in whatever these things are. Not glow-worm light, not firefly light. Something more even than that, more architectural — a luminescence that has been built into the material the way pigment is built into a wall, a thing of construction rather than of life. The result is dim. The result is the dimness of a room at the end of an afternoon, or the dimness under a thick tree, or the dimness inside a cloud. Things are visible. The far wall is visible. The ceiling is a guess. The towers cast shadows that are barely darker than the ground they stand on, and the shadows are the same temperature as the light.

Vegetation. There is vegetation, but it is not what that word usually means. From cracks in the towers, from the seams where two walls meet at an unintended angle, there are growths — pale, fibrous, dry as cork. They do not leaf. They do not flower. They push outward in thin stalks, sometimes a foot tall, sometimes three, branching into nothing, into a fine brittle mesh that snaps if you breathe on it wrong. They are the color of old bone, of dried salt, of paper left in a window. They are alive in the way that lichen is alive — which is to say, slowly, indifferently, on a clock that does not keep time with anything you would recognize. They are the only green thing here, and they are not green. They are the only living architecture besides the towers, and they are not architectural. They are an accident that keeps happening.

The creatures are rare. This is important. You could stand in one place for what would feel like hours and see nothing move. Then something would move. A small thing — the size of a hand, the size of two hands — pale, jointed, multi-legged, with a head that is mostly two long palps and a darkness where eyes would be. It crosses a plaza in a fast deliberate diagonal and disappears into a crack. Then silence. Then another, smaller, follows the first at a careful distance. Then the first one is not where it was; it is somewhere else now, on a wall, climbing. Then nothing, again, for so long that you begin to wonder if you imagined it. They have a predator and they have a prey. The predator is larger and slower. The prey is smaller and faster. Neither is often visible. Their activity is a rumor, conducted in the gaps.

The sound is silence. Not a true silence — there is the faint hiss of air moving up through the towers, and the faint tick of the calcium cooling by fractions of a degree, and the very faint rustle of a fiber stalk settling under its own weight. But these sounds are so small that they are below the threshold of registering, and what registers instead is the absence. The room is so quiet that your own pulse becomes a presence. The room is so quiet that swallowing is loud. The silence here is not peaceful. The silence here is the silence of a place that has not been spoken in, ever, by anything that knew what speaking was.

And yet — active. This is the part that the silence contradicts, and the contradiction is the place. Inside the walls, in the hollows, in the tubes of the towers, things are happening that are too slow to see and too constant to stop. The calcium is still accreting. The towers are still growing taller by a fraction in a year, by a fraction in a decade, and the rooms between them are still narrowing by the same fraction. The growths on the walls are still pushing out their thin stalks, which die at the tip and grow again from the base. The creatures are still hunting, still fleeing, still crossing their plazas in their careful diagonals. The phosphorescence is still emitting. The air is still rising through the towers and sinking back along the floor, in a convection so slow it would look, if you could film it, like the room itself was breathing. The room is breathing. The room has been breathing. The room will continue to breathe long after anyone who ever entered it has stopped.

It is cool in here. It is dry in here. It is dim in here, and silent, and strange, and small, and full of architecture that no architect built, and full of life that no life is witnessing, and the place has no opinion about being visited, and the place will continue, and the place does not end at the walls — the walls are only where the dimness and the towers end, and beyond them is just more of whatever made this possible in the first place, which is older, and drier, and sharper, and not for you.
