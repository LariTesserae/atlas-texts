# The Long Lapse

The first thing is the wind. It never stops. It comes down off the plateau rim and falls for half a vertical mile before it hits anything, and by then it is no longer wind but a continuous howl, a waterfall of air that pours into the basin and pools there, swirling in slow, room-sized gyres that grind against the metal rails of the overhead tramway until the whole lattice hums like a struck tuning fork. You hear the place before you see it. You hear it from the staging platform at the top of the lift, where the cable car rocks against its yoke and the operators shout into each other's collarbones. You hear it all the way down, the sound getting bigger instead of smaller, as though you are descending into the mouth of something that is breathing you in.

The basin is a circle. That is the first strange thing. Most of the erosion in the uplands is soft, rounded, the patient curves of a continent that has been weathering since before anything with a name lived on it. But the rim of the basin is a near-perfect ring, a wall of grey-green basalt scalloped into cusps and shelves, and the engineers who built the tramway forty years ago chose their route along the only place where the rim dips low enough to admit a cable. They called it the Notch. The lift descends through the Notch into the basin and the basin is where the work is.

The water is the second thing. The basin holds it the way a cupped hand holds rain. A lake fills the floor, fed by a thousand small falls that come down the inner wall in pale threads, and the surface of the lake is never still because the wind never stops, and the wind on the lake is not the soft chop of a windy afternoon but a standing pattern of standing waves, ridges of water a meter high that march across the basin and collide at the center and send up a continuous low mist that hangs over everything below the rim. The mist is what makes the light. It is a normal light, a midday light, but filtered through a million liters of suspended water, so it arrives diffused and softened, a flat silver that flattens the shadows out of the machinery and the buildings and the bodies of the workers until the whole bowl looks like a photograph taken through gauze.

The lake is not fresh. It tastes of iron and lime and the faint sweet of some mineral the geologists have a name for. The workers drink it anyway because the alternative is hauling water up the cable and the lift is already running at capacity just hauling ore.

The ore is the third thing. The whole basin is ore. That is what the basin is for. Three billion years ago, give or take the age the university people are still arguing about, something hot came up out of the planetary interior and did not quite make it to the surface and then never finished the journey and then, for the rest of geological time, sat there while everything above it weathered down to almost nothing. The basalt rim is what is left of the cover. The ore is what is left of the intrusion. It is a green-black rock dense with a metal the smelters want, and it outcrops along the inner wall of the basin in continuous benches that have been worked, by various methods and various hands, for as long as anyone has been coming down the Notch. The benches are the color of dried blood where they are freshly cut and the color of wet slate where they have been standing a season. There are dozens of them. They wind around the basin in spirals that descend toward the lake.

There is vegetation. The benches that have been abandoned long enough have been taken back. Hard things, wiry things, things with small thick leaves and pale flowers that close in the wind. They grow out of the cracks in the cut stone and they hang over the edges of the ledges in tangled curtains that move continuously, and where a bench has been abandoned for a decade or more the vegetation has filled the cut so completely that you cannot tell the bench from the original slope. The basin is in the middle of being eaten. The wind eats it. The water eats it. The plants eat it. The ore has been exposed by erosion and is now being unexposed by the slower work of everything else, and in another ten thousand years it will be a green-banked lake in a green-banked bowl with no mark of the mining at all.

The fauna is on the water and in the air and on the ledges. There are pale fish in the lake that come up in the still moments between gusts and dimple the standing waves. There are long-limbed birds that nest on the basalt shelves and ride the thermals along the inner wall. There is a fox-sized thing with a thick coat that lives among the ore benches and scavenges the workers' middens. It is not afraid of the tramway. Nothing here is afraid of the tramway. The cable car has been running since before the oldest fox was born.

The built environment is the fourth thing. The basin has been worked for a hundred and forty years. The first seventy years of that were a small operation, hand tools, a single adit, a rail line down to the lake where the ore was loaded onto barges. The last seventy years have been something else. There are buildings along the benches in clusters — crushing works, smelting sheds, winding houses, lamp rooms, mess halls, barracks, the chapel that is no longer used but no one has taken down. They are dark stone and dark metal and they leak smoke from a dozen short stacks and the smoke joins the mist and the mist drifts around the buildings until the buildings appear to be standing in cloud. The tramway runs above them all. The cable car passes every fifteen minutes and the operators wave to the workers on the ledges and the workers do not wave back because they are watching the cable, watching for fraying, watching for the day a cable fails and a car drops into the basin. It has not happened yet. It is the kind of thing that has not happened yet.

The dynamic of the place is the fifth thing and the loudest thing. The wind and the water and the ore and the smoke and the cable cars and the crushers and the shift whistles and the bells in the winding houses all run together into a single continuous mechanical weather that the basin has been generating for a century and that does not pause for anything. The ore is brought up the benches in skips that clatter along the narrow rails. The skips dump into the crushers and the crushers hammer day and night and the sound of the hammering travels down through the rock and comes up through the lake and is felt in the soles of the boots of the workers on the other side of the basin. The smelters pour. The slag is wheeled to the rim and tipped over and falls half a mile into the outer slope and the fall takes long enough that it cools in the air and arrives as a black rain of pebbles that adds a centimeter a year to the apron of waste around the outside of the mountain. The cable cars go up and down. The lake pours over the lowest notch of the rim in a spillway that is only active in the spring melt, and the spillway has cut a channel in the outer basalt that is, in its own small way, as old-looking as the basin it drains.

The oldest stable form in the basin is the lake itself. The basin has been a basin for so long that the lake has had time to settle into its final shape, a shallow lens of mineral water over a floor of fine grey sediment. The sediment is the dust of three billion years of weathering, plus, now, the dust of a hundred and forty years of mining, and the two kinds of dust are indistinguishable. The fish in the lake do not care. The foxes on the ledges do not care. The cable operators do not care. They have work. The ore is still there. The wind still blows. The cable still holds. The lake still pours over the spillway in the spring, and the mist still rises from the standing waves, and the light still comes down through it in a flat silver wash, and the green-black benches still wind around and down toward the water, and somewhere on the lowest bench, in the oldest workings, a small steady stream of water comes out of the rock face and runs across the ore and disappears into the lake, and it has been running since before the first cable was strung.

That is the place. It is a mine. It is a lake. It is a bowl in an old mountain. The wind blows. The cable hums. The ore is taken. The basin is patient. The basin is older than the work and will be here after the work is done, and the mist will still rise from the standing waves, and the fish will still dimple the water, and the foxes will still scavenge the middens, and the spillway will still pour in the spring, and eventually the last cable will be cut and the last building will fall and the green-banked lake will lie in the green-banked bowl with no mark of the mining at all, and that will be the place's final form, the form it was always tending toward, the form that the wind and the water and the plants have been working toward in their slow and patient way for three billion years.
