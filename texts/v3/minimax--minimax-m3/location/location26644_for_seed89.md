The place is a crack in the world, or feels like one. You reach it from above — over a lip of black basalt where the wind has stripped the lichen away — and then the ground simply falls out from under you, and you are inside something much older than the mountain it lives in.

The crack runs nearly straight down, a fissure that opened in some epoch when the rock was still pliable, and over the eons its walls have been polished by water and heat until they are almost glass-smooth. From the lip you can drop a stone and not hear it land for many seconds. The walls go down and down, narrowing slightly, then widening into a chamber — the first of several chambers, if you had the courage and the rope and the time to find out — and this first chamber is the size of a cathedral, perhaps larger, but the scale is impossible to judge because there are no straight lines to measure against. Everything is curved.

It is dark here in a way that feels inhabited. The light that comes down from the opening above is a kind of diluted gray, and it falls maybe forty meters into the chamber before it gives up, and below that is a thick warm gloom in which shapes can be sensed more than seen. Your eyes adjust and find: walls, glistening; ceiling, somewhere lost; and all around, a presence of things moving. The walls themselves seem to move, but that is only the insects. There are so many insects that the rock appears to have a texture of its own volition, a pelt of legs and folded wings and pale antennae, layers of them, and where they have clustered thickest the rock is no longer visible at all.

The air is warm and wet. You can feel it in your lungs before you can feel it on your skin — a humid thickness, almost milky, as if the air itself were a kind of warm water you are wading through. It smells of iron and of something sweet and rotting, the smell of a great many small lives lived in close quarters. There is a permanent mist in the lower half of the chamber, exhaled by the creatures, condensed by the cold air above, and it moves in slow rolls and coils like something breathing in its sleep. It drips constantly. Everywhere water is making its way downward: from the rim of the opening, where the snowmelt from the peaks above has been seeping through the mountain for weeks; from the walls themselves, sweating in the warmth; from the ceiling of distant places you cannot see. It collects in pockets at the lip of every shelf and in every bowl carved in the stone, and in the deeper parts of the chamber there is a kind of standing water, black and warm, that no one has ever disturbed.

The warmth has no obvious source. There are no vents you can see, no glowing cracks, no rivers of molten stone. But the rock itself is warm to the touch, warmer as you go deeper, and the air temperature rises in such a gradient that you could, with patience, find the place where a human body would begin to suffer. Below that — but this is for the creatures, not for you. They live there in their layered millions, doing whatever it is they do in the warm dark. Some of them are quite large. From time to time something displaces the air nearby and you feel it pass, a soft pressure against your face, the suggestion of a wing or a flank. You hear them more than you see them.

The sound is constant and layered. There is the water: a thousand drips at a thousand intervals, overlapping into a kind of rain that never stops, and sometimes a heavier plunk as a pool overflows and spills to a lower pool. There is the wind, which finds the opening above and pours down in slow howls that set the whole fissure humming, a low resonance you feel in your molars before you hear in your ears. There is the sound of the creatures — the high thin whine of insects, the clicks and shuffles of many small bodies, and now and then a deeper utterance, something between a croak and a sigh, from down in the warm dark where the larger things are. Together these sounds don't diminish one another; they fold into one another, and after a while you stop hearing them as separate things and hear them as the place itself speaking.

Nothing here was made. There is no worked stone, no tool left behind, no carved mark. And yet — somewhere, in some part of the chamber you haven't reached, you can sense that things have been arranged. Sticks gathered. Stones piled into low ridges. A smear of something white on the basalt. Someone — some creature, or some early small climber, some ancestor of the things now swarming in the dark — has been here before you, and has left the faintest trace of having understood this place enough to use it. The walls have wear at hand-height in places. A few of the floor stones are suspiciously smooth, as if gripped. You cannot tell if the inhabitants are still here, watching from a tier of rock above you, or if they died out and were replaced by the swarm, or if they are the swarm now, in some way you cannot parse.

Time moves differently here. The fissure is patient. The water is making its slow calcite additions to the walls, dropping a bead at a time, building back toward the opening it fell through. The insects eat the calcium and the stone-dust and the waste of one another and are in turn eaten. A stalactite takes a hundred years to grow a finger's length; a bat, if any bat lives here, takes a year to raise a pup; the bigger things below, whatever they are, take longer still, and their young are born already knowing the dark. The whole place has been finding its balance for a long time, and will continue to find it for a long time after you have climbed back up to the lip and crawled out into the wind. If you leave something here — a scrap of cloth, a knife, a hair — it will be consumed within a season, broken down into the standing water or woven into a nest. Nothing lasts that does not belong.

From the lip, in the daylight, it is just a crack in the mountain, very deep, very narrow, the kind you might walk past without a second glance. But from inside, looking up, the opening is a coin of gray sky, and the sound of the wind on the ridge reaches you only as a distant singing, and the things in the warm dark go on with their lives, and you understand that this is a place that has been here longer than language, and will be here after.
