# The Brick Lung

The town sits in a fold of lowland where a river once ran wider than it does now. You can read the river's ambition in the geometry of the place — the streets curve as if they were still trying to accommodate something larger, something that has since pulled back into a thinner channel and left its old shape behind as a memory cut into the earth. The elevation is barely an elevation at all: a subtle lift, perhaps eight feet above the high-water mark, enough to keep the foundations out of the flood but not enough to ever give you a view. You cannot see the town from outside the town. You have to be inside it before it begins to make sense.

The ground is young. Not the land itself, which is as patient as land gets, but the skin of the land — the cuts and fills, the exposed clay where the river has bitten fresh into its banks over the last decade, the new pavement laid over old pavement laid over older pavement that nobody can quite agree on the age of. There are places here where you can dig down with a hand trowel and pass through three generations of roadway in an afternoon. The erosion is the kind that hasn't had time to soften anything. Edges are still sharp where the rain has cut gullies into the unpaved alleys. A retaining wall along the millrace is missing its mortar in a way that suggests it lost the mortar last spring, not in some previous century.

The bricks are what give the town its name, though nobody calls it that to its face. The Brick Lung is what the river-drivers say, what the rail-men mutter into their collars at the junction. It is called something else officially. The bricks are the dominant medium. Every structure is brick. Every wall, every chimney, every footing, every culvert, every low garden border — brick. And the bricks are not decorative. They are the local product. There are kilns at the north edge of town that have been burning for so long that the ground around them has changed color, a wide ring of fired earth that looks from a distance like a sunburn on the land. The clay comes from the riverbank. The river cuts fresh clay; the kilns fire it; the resulting bricks are stacked back into the town in slightly different configurations depending on the decade, the budget, the prevailing fashion in municipal bond issuance. The whole settlement is, in a sense, the same material cycling through itself. The river eats the bank, the kilns eat the clay, the town eats the bricks.

It is damp here. Not drowning, not flooded, but the kind of damp that gets into the paper of books left on a shelf and curls it. Condensation beads on the inside of windows on cool mornings. The river breathes a permanent mist that sits in the low places until midmorning, then lifts itself in slow dissolving curtains. When it rains, which it does on roughly a third of the days, the rain lingers. There are gutter systems on the main streets that work and gutter systems on the alleys that work less well, and the sound of water finding its way through the brick infrastructure is one of the louder voices of the town. Rain does not arrive and leave here so much as move in and stay for a while.

The vegetation has taken advantage of this. Ivy climbs the south-facing walls in dense, glossy curtains, and the leaves are big — wider than a hand, the kind that means the plant is not fighting for anything. There are trees, but not many, and they are planted deliberately rather than grown wild: plane trees along the main street, their bark flaking in camouflage patches; a stand of willows at the bend in the river where the millrace pulls away; a single enormous walnut at the crossroads near the school, dropping its nuts onto the cobbles in autumn in a way that has become a civic annoyance. Where the buildings leave off, the ground erupts. Ferns colonize the shadowed sides of walls. Moss, in this climate, is less a thing that grows on surfaces than a continuous presence that surfaces interrupt. Grasses push up between the cobbles in a soft green weave that the municipal workers have apparently ceased to resist. Somewhere a vine has eaten a small wooden shed whole and is now flowering, modest white trumpets in summer.

The town is cool even in what passes here for summer. The river pulls heat out of the air the way a faucet pulls water, and the kilns only manage to warm their immediate vicinity. People wear coats longer than is reasonable. The wool merchants do well. In winter there is occasional snow that doesn't last more than a day or two, the river running through it like warm blood through a cold limb.

The town is small. You can walk from one edge to the other in fifteen minutes if you walk briskly, twenty if you stop to nod at the people you'll inevitably nod at. There is one main street, three cross-streets, an alley system behind the main street that functions as its own kind of shadow commerce — back doors, deliveries, the route children take to school — and the residential fringes where the density thins into individual houses with small yards and larger gardens. The scale is what an architect might call intimate and what a sociologist might call claustrophobic. You are rarely more than thirty feet from a brick wall. The sky comes in slivers between the eaves.

The density of *people* is moderate, but the density of *things* is higher. There are many presences in this town that are not people: the kilns, the rail siding, the water tower on its iron legs at the southwest corner, the silent brick chimney of the old brewery that hasn't fired in a generation but stands because nobody has decided who has to pay to take it down. The town is built enough that open ground is the exception, but sparse enough that the gaps between structures have become small ecosystems of their own.

The industrial feeling is more auditory than visual at first. There is a steady, low mechanical hum that lives underneath everything else — the kilns breathing, the pumps on the river, the small engines in the workshops along Forge Street. From certain angles, mostly at dusk, the sky over the kilns has a faint amber tint from the reflected heat-shine of the continuous firing. There is a foundry, two of them actually, that does brass and iron castings for the agricultural equipment market. There is a small textile works that processes the wool from upriver. There is a cooperage, a chandlery, a brickworks. Nothing on the scale that would impress a city person. Everything on the scale that keeps a town like this alive for fifty miles around.

The electrification came late and came unevenly. The main street has arc lamps on iron standards; the residential edges have gas mantles that were converted to electric with varying degrees of elegance. Some houses have their wiring in ceramic knobs on the outside of the brick, so the walls are freckled with small white dots that catch the light at certain hours. The kilns, of course, burn their own light, and at night the northern edge of town has an amber under-glow that you can see from the hills beyond.

The light, generally speaking, is the light of a place that is mostly itself — neither overcast nor brilliant, but the soft gray-green of a sky that has decided to commit to neither weather. Mornings are misty. Afternoons have occasional breakthroughs of sun that the brick walls hold and re-radiate into the small streets. Dusk is the best hour, when the brick takes on the color of the things it has been reflecting all day.

The fauna is considerable. Birds have always liked this place; the river and the grain stores and the ivy and the warm chimneys make it ideal. There are starlings in uncountable numbers, and the murmuration they perform at dusk over the kilns is something the schoolchildren are taken out to watch as a kind of civic ritual. Rooks in the walnuts. The willows at the river bend hold herons. The cellars and the alleys hold cats in great abundance — the town is famous, locally, for its cats. There are also dogs, a few horses still in working commission, rats of course, and a celebrated population of pipistrelle bats under the eaves of the old brewery chimney, which come out at dusk in a black streamer and feed over the river until the light fails. Foxes have moved in within the last decade, bold and leggy, and there is ongoing civic debate about the chickens.

The unusual quality of the place is not something you can point at. It is something you suspect. The town feels, in some way that you cannot defend if asked, as if it is exactly the size it intends to be, as if it has reached some equilibrium with itself that is more deliberate than the ordinary accident of small-town development. There are too many brick walls, and they fit together too well, and the ivy has been allowed to climb them in patterns that almost seem curated. The stranger who stays two nights begins to feel that the town is watching them in return — not unkindly, but with the patient attention of something that has all the time in the world. Nothing in particular happens to confirm this. Nothing needs to.

The sound is significant. You can hear the town from a mile away on a still night: the hum of the kilns, the higher whine of the foundry's ventilation, the percussive clang of metal being worked, the unending conversation of the river. There is a bell tower at the old meeting hall that strikes the quarter-hours with a tone that takes three seconds to develop and five to decay. There are steam whistles from the rail siding when a train is being made up, which is most afternoons. There is the chatter of starlings. There is the regular small thunder of the foundry drop-hammer, which is loudest at noon and falls silent on Sundays in a way that makes the rest of the week feel heavier by comparison. On still nights you can hear individual conversations from open windows on the next street. The town is loud, but its loudness is not aggressive — it is the loudness of a place that is alive with itself.

The dynamic of the place is slow. Things do not happen fast here. The river is cutting the bank at the rate at which rivers cut banks. The kilns fire continuously and have been firing continuously. The ivy is climbing. The brick is weathering. The starlings are roosting. People are being born, growing up, leaving for the city, sometimes returning. The town changes, but the changes are seasonal, generational, geological. You could visit twice in a decade and see the same town; you could visit twice in a century and not see the same town at all.

What you would remember, if you visited, is the way the brick holds the mist in the morning before the sun is properly up, and how the kilns make the northern sky seem to breathe, and how the streets curve to accommodate a river that isn't there anymore, and how the small white trumpets of the vine appear in summer over the shed that nobody owns, and how the bats come out of the chimney like a black thread being pulled from a spool, and how the starlings wheel and wheel and then suddenly are gone and the sky is just sky again. You would remember the smell of damp wool and fired clay. You would remember that someone played a harmonica on a back step one evening, very competently, and that you listened for longer than you meant to. You would remember that you couldn't see the town from outside it, and that once you were in, you couldn't quite remember how to get back out without going past something you wanted to look at twice.
