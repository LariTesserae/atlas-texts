You arrive, if you arrive, somewhere within the medium. There is no sky in the usual sense. There is no above. There is only water — warm, dark, patient, and dense enough that you feel it as weight on every surface of yourself. The first sensation is the heat. It is the heat of a bath held too long, of a closed room in summer, of a body of water that has been absorbing sunlight for a very long time and has nowhere to lose it. The water is the color of strong tea held up to a window at dusk — or of the inside of a closed eye, or of an old glass jar. You cannot see far. The visible world extends maybe twenty feet in any direction before it dissolves into a darker murk.

Light arrives, but it arrives injured. Somewhere far above there is a surface, and the surface is bright, but by the time any of that brightness has filtered down through the column of water it has been bruised into something pale and grey-green, a suggestion rather than an illumination. You can see the seams of these faint shafts coming down between the structures — long slanting columns of slightly-less-dark that fall across the towers and the sills and the corridors like dusty light through a cathedral window. In the rest of the place it is the color of dried wine, of old iron, of the inside of an oyster. There is almost never true darkness — it is too full of suspended particulate, of small drifting life, of the faint glow of things that carry their own light. But it is dark enough that you read by silhouette.

The architecture is everywhere, and it is impossible to look at it without thinking of a city, even though you know it is not a city.

There are walls, but they breathe. They rise out of the sills and lean into the current, pitted with holes, draped with soft tissue, webbed with thin fans that wave without wind. The walls curve where no mason would curve them and branch where no plan would branch them. Some of them are ridges — sharp little ranges that run for fifty feet along the bottom before falling off into shadow. Some of them are columns — single masses of calcified skeleton reaching up twenty feet, thirty feet, branching at their tops like coral trees but blunter, denser, more massive. These are the towers. They have the texture of stale bread but firmer. They ring faintly when you brush them — a low dull tone almost below hearing. Some of them have been growing for so long that their bases have merged with the sills and with each other, so that the ground itself is just the roots of these towers, slowly spreading.

There are corridors. You move through them in the gaps. The gaps are the streets. They wind between the towers and the walls, narrow in places — barely wide enough for a single form to pass — and opening elsewhere into vaulted chambers the size of rooms. The vaults are made of bivalve shells. The shells are the size of doors, of tables, of small carts. They have grown together, fused at their hinges, packed with sediment inside, so that their curved backs form the ceilings of the chambers. The chambers are mostly empty. Sometimes a long dark shape moves through one slowly, brushing the ceiling as it goes.

There are levels. Below you, there is more. Above you, there is more. The sills step down — worn, rounded, slick with a soft crust — into lower corridors, into deeper chambers, into the dark. Other sills step up — onto the broad backs of merged towers, onto the ridges of the walls — into the upper reaches where the faint shafts of light are slightly less faint. The place is built in strata. You can feel that it has been adding to itself for a very long time. The lower parts are older, more worn, more encrusted, more eaten into the patience of the water. The upper parts are younger, more ragged, more inhabited. The forms have settled. They are in their own state, and they have been in it long enough that the surfaces are no longer jagged anywhere. Every edge has been rounded by something patient.

The materials are all biological or once-biological. The towers are calcium carbonate, laid down by polyps and worms over decades. The walls are calcium too, and chitin, and the cement that certain crustaceans secrete. The shells are shells. The sediment — and there is always sediment — is the slow rain of small dead things drifting down from above, mixed with the fine powder that the structures themselves shed as they grow. The sediment is pale grey, and it puffs at the slightest disturbance. It hangs in the water like a thought forgotten mid-sentence. When you pass through a corridor it takes a long time to settle behind you. The water is full of it. It is in the creases of everything.

The fauna is abundant. It is dense. It moves through the corridors and the chambers in slow organized patterns that have been running for so long they have worn grooves into the soft stone.

There are the small silver things — fingerlings, perhaps, or the juveniles of something larger — that hold their positions in the current in loose clouds, orienting with the smallest flicks of their tails, parting around you like a single thought. They are everywhere. They fill the upper corridors in shimmering drifts. They go nowhere in particular. They are the texture of the place.

There are larger shapes moving more slowly through the deeper channels — long, dark, heavy with purpose. You can see them only as silhouettes against the faint shafts of light from above, or as absences when they pass in front of one of the small flickers of bioluminescence that the fauna carries. Some of them are fish. Some of them are not fish. Some of them are armoured. Some of them are soft. They move with the unhurried confidence of things that own the corridors. Their patrols are the structure of the place; the corridors are corridors because the patrols have been walking them.

There are things that cling. Crusted shapes in the cracks. Frilled shapes tucked into the holes in the towers. Soft pulsing things in the corners of the chambers, beating in time with nothing. There are things that crawl along the sills — low, many-legged, deliberate, antennae sweeping the water in slow arcs. They leave faint trails in the sediment. The trails take a long time to fade.

There are things you cannot see but that you can feel — a pressure shift, a sudden absence, a dark form moving just past the edge of the dim. Sometimes, just at the limit of vision, you can see a small cloud of silver erupt outward in a sudden panic, fan out into the cracks of the towers, and then slowly drift back into formation. The crack of a shell. The single thump of displaced water. Then silence again, and the silver reorganizing.

Mostly the place is quiet. The water carries sound strangely — it dampens the high things and carries the low things, so what reaches you is mostly the long tones. There is a hum. You cannot tell where it comes from. It is below hearing almost, but you feel it in the teeth and the bones. It might be the structures settling. It might be the fauna breathing in chorus. It might be the current moving over the rough surfaces of the towers. There are creaks — soft, infrequent — as something shifts deep in the lower corridors. There are faint pops as shells open and close. There is the occasional thump of displaced water when something moves fast. Once in a long while there is a quick series of cracks, like knuckles popping, and then a small commotion in one of the chambers — a brief flurry of silver, a dark shape moving through, and then everything settles. The cracks echo faintly in the corridors and are absorbed into the hum.
