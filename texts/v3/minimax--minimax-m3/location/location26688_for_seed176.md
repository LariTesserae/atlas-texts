# The Drip Cathedral

You arrive through a cut in the cliff wall, where the road gives up and becomes stairs.

The mountain is young. You can tell because it still cuts you. Black basalt, still shaving its edges, every boulder a fresh fracture, every fracture a thing that happened in some geological yesterday. The rock is glassy in places where the magma came up wet, and in those glassy places the light sits like spilled mercury, pooling and refusing to run. The cliffs above the basin are steep enough that snow doesn't gather on them — only on the ledges where it can find a shelf, and even then it slides off at noon when the warm air climbs.

The basin is small. Cathedral scale, yes — you can stand at the altar stone and have the walls on three sides — but it is not a cathedral that was built. It is a cathedral that has only just been *allowed*. The walls go up two hundred meters, three hundred, and then the sky. The sky here is a strange thing. It is close. It feels low because the walls are high, and because there is always a little fog in the basin — water that has come down off the high stone and has nowhere to go. It condenses on everything. It runs down the backs of your hands. It beads on the iron.

The iron.

There is a forest of iron in the basin. Not trees, exactly — pipes. Pipes coming out of the cliff face at every height, pipes threading down the rock, pipes that branch and rebranch like a root system or a vascular system or the veins of a leaf, all of them sweating. Condensation pearls on every joint. The pipes are old in the way industrial things get old when they are not quite abandoned — blackened, oil-stained, the paint gone, the flanges crusted with a kind of mineral bloom that is half rust and half the green-white deposit of mountain water. They were here before the forest was, or the forest grew around them, or both. It is not clear which is the guest.

The forest is lush in the way a tongue is lush, or a wound. Ferns as wide as your open hand. Moss that climbs the pipes and spills off them in sodden curtains. A fig, or something like a fig, with roots that have pried a flange open. Small pale flowers, the color of underthings, blooming in the crevices where the pipes meet the stone. Everything is dripping. Everything is breathing out water. When the sun comes, the steam comes with it — thin, sweet-smelling, smelling of hot iron and warm moss, rising from every surface in slow threads that braid together above the basin into a single low cloud. When the sun goes, the cloud sinks back down and you walk in it.

It is loud here. Not loudly. Loudly *patiently*. The sound of water, mostly, but water at scale: a dozen seep lines whispering down the cliff, the steady drum of a condensation fall at the far end where the pipes come together, the hiss and tick of a place that is always a little too warm for the air. And under the water-sound, the engines. There are engines somewhere — deep, you can feel them more than hear them, a vibration in the floor of the basin, in the altar stone, in your teeth. They have been running a long time. They will run a long time still. Some of the pipes are cool. Some are not. You learn which is which by listening.

The buildings — there are buildings. Scattered ones. A pump house crouched against the north wall, its door rusted open, the floor inside a foot deep in ferns. A line of switchboxes along the eastern ledge, their glass faces fogged over so you cannot read the dials, the handles corroded into place. A shed, lower down, with a corrugated roof that has bowed under the weight of a single enormous rhododendron. The shed has a chair inside it and a cup on the floor of the cup and a smear of something at the lip of the cup and you decide not to think about it. There was someone here. There might be someone here. The engines would not run themselves.

The fauna is shy. You see — over the course of an hour, of a day — a bright thing moving in the canopy of moss on the western wall, too quick to name. The droppings of something larger in the dry(ish) crook of a pipe junction, near the altar stone. A frog, absurdly, sitting on a flange that is gently warm. That is most of it. The basin is too wet for reptiles and too warm for snow-creatures and too noisy for birdsong to carry, though birds must pass through; you sometimes see a feather caught in the geometry of a T-joint.

The mountain is changing. Slowly. The pipes are calcifying; the flanges are sealing themselves with their own mineral weeping. The forest is taking the machinery back at the rate forests take things back, which is to say, without hurry. The engines will eventually choke. The water will win — not all at once, but by degrees, by patient degrees, the way water wins everything in warm wet places at the foot of young mountains. You can stand at the altar stone and feel it happening. The basin is becoming a swamp. The swamp will become a meadow. The meadow will be high and cold and empty for a very long time.

For now, there is the hiss of steam in the ferns. There is the slow bright thread of a drip falling from a pipe onto a leaf onto a stone. There is the sound of the engines. There is the fog. There is the green-white bloom on the iron. There is the warm wet air.

There is the cathedral, still saying its only prayer.
