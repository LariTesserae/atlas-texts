# The Pale Plateau of Long Air

The plateau has had so long to settle into itself that even its irregularities have become a kind of regularity. Erosion here is not a process but a memory — a thing that happened, was recorded into the stone, and then went on happening so slowly that it has become indistinguishable from stillness. The mountains that once stood above this place have been worn down to their roots. Those roots remain as low rounded rises that break the horizon at distant intervals, so smooth and so long-smoothed that their shadows are barely a shading in the dim light. Between them stretches the plateau: pale ochre rock, shot through with vein-minerals that have oxidized over geologic time into slow rivers of green-blue and rust, the color of an old bruise. The rock is flat enough that you could lay a city across it without breaking the line of the horizon, and old enough that no one remembers what it used to look like before it was this. The colonists call this the Long Air. They have not given the plateau a name. Names, the old-timers say, are for things that might leave.

There is no water.

This is the first thing the new arrivals ask about, and the last thing they stop thinking about. The plateau is high — high enough that the air is thin and cold, high enough that the sky has a permanent violet-gray cast from the haze that hangs above it. The colonists have searched. They have drilled, they have dug, they have flown drones over the deep basins below the plateau where ancient lakebeds glitter with salts that are not quite salts. They have not found water. They have found dry rivers, dry seas, dry ice in the shadowed craters of the far peaks. The water they need they bring in sealed tanks from off-world, or they recycle it with an efficiency that would have impressed their ancestors, or they drink what little condenses from the air on the coldest nights and runs down the sides of things into waiting cups. There is no water in the soil. There is no water in the rock. There is water in the colonists themselves, and they are very careful with it.

The pillars do not miss it.

The pillars are the substrate. They are the vegetation, the forest, the ground-cover, the architecture, the weather, the calendar. There are perhaps four thousand of them visible from any one vantage, scattered across the plateau like a forest seen from a great distance, but each one is its own presence, its own neighborhood. Some are as thick across as a city block at the base, rising straight up for two hundred meters before branching. Some are slender as an arm but tall as a tower, their tips lost in the haze. Some are broken and continue as broken, half-collapsed, leaning against their neighbors in slow parabolas. Their color is the chalky-bone white of dry coral, with a faint yellow undertone where the dim sun catches them. Their surfaces are deeply pitted, porous, almost lacework in places, and the pits breathe. You can stand beneath a pillar on a still morning and feel the air move — a soft intake, a soft exhale — as the pillar drinks the atmosphere.

What they drink is not water. The atmosphere of this world is mostly nitrogen, with enough methane and carbon dioxide to give it weight, and a thin fraction of something that is not oxygen but performs the same trick for creatures who never expected oxygen. The pillars pull this in through their porous surfaces. Their internal chemistry is not the green trick of Earth plants — no chlorophyll, no splitting of water, no glucose from carbon dioxide and light. They work with another solvent. The chemists in the colony still argue about what it is. Some say it is a hydrocarbon that condenses in the pillar's hollow internals at certain temperatures. Some say it is closer to liquid ammonia. Some say it is something the colonists have not yet named and may not be able to, because their instruments were built for water-chemistry worlds and the pillars do not care. What is known is that the pillars grow. They grow over centuries. They grow visibly, slowly, in the way of glaciers and continental drift. Some on this plateau are ten thousand years old. Some, perhaps, are older. They do not die in any way that can be easily told; they persist until something happens — a collapse, a sickness, a strike of the strange lightning that sometimes walks the high peaks — and then a portion of them withers and the rest continues. Each pillar is more like a clonal colony than an individual, and each is genetically singular. There are no two alike.

Between the pillars, the sky. Between the pillars, the wind.

The wind is the loud thing.

It never stops. The rotation of this world is slow and the atmosphere is thick, and the air is always in motion across the high places. It comes off the higher peaks to the north and is funneled across the plateau in long ribbons, eddying around the pillars the way water in a river eddies around stones. When it meets a pillar, the pillar sings. The porous surface resonates. Each pillar has its own fundamental note, depending on its size, its shape, its age, the distribution of its pits. A young slender pillar sounds close to a flute. An old thick one sounds close to a stopped organ pipe. A broken pillar that has been hollowed by its own collapse sounds close to a conch. The wind passing through a forest of pillars makes a chord — not a harmony but a congregation, each pillar offering its tone at its own volume, the ear catching fragments, melodies, drones, the slow glissando of an old pillar whose surface has been eroded into new shapes by the very wind that is sounding it. The chord changes hour to hour as the wind shifts. At dawn it is lower. At midday, when the thermal drafts rise off the rock, it climbs. At night it falls again into a long bass note that the colonists sleep inside.

The colony lives inside this chord.

The settlement does not look like a settlement. There are no walls. There are no streets in any conventional sense. There is a network, distributed across the plateau across perhaps sixty kilometers, of habitation units that are not built but grown. The technology for this is something the colonists brought with them from before — or developed here, in the long centuries, when they stopped being colonists and became something else. The pillars can be shaped. Slowly, gently, with the careful application of certain fields that are not fields as physics has named them, they can be coaxed to grow in particular directions, to thicken or thin on command, to hollow themselves into chambers, to seal those chambers against the porous outer surface and admit light through windows grown thin as paper. The colonists live in the pillars. They have coaxed great rooms inside the chalky-bone trunks, with windows that look out through the porous skin into the dim light. The light comes in mottled and soft, each room receiving the sun as a pale gray-yellow through the bone, and the colors inside are the colors of old paper — cream, ivory, faint gold where a window has been grown extra thin.

Outside, the chambers are connected. Suspended walkways run between pillars, narrow and swaying gently in the wind. Small vehicles — the colonists call them shuttles, though they do not run on wheels — hang from cables strung pillar to pillar, or hover at the height of a person's chest, or take advantage of the planet's slightly weaker gravity by some mechanism no one fully explains anymore. Between pillars, a person on a walkway is exposed to the open sky. The wind passes them. They hear the singing of the pillars on either side and the wind through themselves. They wear layers; it is cool here, always cool, even at midday, even in the brief summer when the dim sun climbs a hand's breadth higher and the haze thins for a few weeks. They wear masks sometimes, when the methane fraction rises, which happens in the long evenings. The masks filter what their lungs cannot yet process.

The colonists themselves are adapted, in their way. Not all of them. Some of the older families — the ones who have been here for nine generations — are no longer entirely water-based. Their blood has thickened. Their children are born with a slightly different set of assumptions. They still need water, but less of it, and they have learned to pull moisture from the air on the cold nights with a skill that the newcomers find uncanny. They are not a different species, not yet. They are becoming one.

The fauna are louder than the colonists.

The fauna here are not animals in the way the new arrivals expect. They are creatures of the pillar-chemistry, or of something in between — some of them, the larger ones, have water in their bodies, scavenged, hoarded, recycled with an efficiency that makes the colonists look wasteful. Most do not. They shelter in the pillars. The pillars are their world. The colonists have named a hundred species, knowing there are more.

There are chitter-things that crawl through the porous outer surfaces of the pillars and eat the pillar-substance, leaving behind trails of pale dust that the wind takes. There are gliders, which are not birds, which have no feathers, which are the size of a small dog and leap from pillar to pillar on bladders filled with methane lighter than the air they displace, their thin membranes stretched between long fingers of silica-bone. They coast across the gaps in silence except for the soft hiss of their bladders venting. There are burrowers that live in the rock between pillars and surface only at night, leaving small craters in the pale stone. There are call-birds.

The call-birds are the loudness.

They are not birds. They are fist-sized, with hard shells the color of the pillars, and they have no wings but they have long thin legs and they move through the porous surfaces with a scuttling patience. They sing. They sing all day. Their voices are small but they are many, and the dense air carries the sound far, and there is no water to absorb it. From a single pillar at midday, when the call-birds are most active, you can hear a thousand competing songs overlapping into a constant static of melody — short phrases, repeated phrases, long descending trills, two-note calls answered by three-note calls from a neighboring pillar. The static does not resolve. It just continues, layered, like a choir that has never agreed on what to sing. The wind adds its chord beneath. The shuttles add their hum. The walkways creak in the breeze.

It is loud. It is a loud place, and the eye does not expect it, because the eye sees a sparse dim plateau with widely spaced white pillars and pale rock and a violet-gray sky, and the ear hears a city.

The colonists have adapted to this. They speak loudly, or they use the pillar-substance as a conductor. The bone conducts vibration beautifully. A tap on the wall of a chamber travels through the pillar and out through the network of roots that runs beneath the rock between pillars, and emerges in another pillar as a soft knock. The colonists send messages this way. They have names for the different rhythms — the morning call, the meal-knock, the long slow pulse that means a birth in a far pillar and the soft answering pulse that means acknowledgment. Visitors from off-world find it unsettling. The locals find it quieter than speech.

At night the place does not quiet. It changes key.

The pillars release something at night — a gas, a mist, a slow exhalation that is part of their cycle. The colonists call it the breath. It rises from each pillar in a pale column, visible in the dim light, and where two pillars' breaths meet they braid and rise together. The breath catches whatever light is in the air — the faint glow of the dim star, the soft luminescence of certain rocks that hold a trace of radium — and holds it. At night, walking the suspended walkways between the pillars, you see the columns of breath rising and meeting, and you hear the call-birds quieting but not silent, and the wind still moving, and the hum, and the soft knock of someone in a far pillar sending a message through the bone.

This is the place. It is cool. It is dim. It is old — older than the colony, older than the pillars perhaps, though no one knows. It is loud. It is strange. It does not look like a forest, though it is one. It does not look like a city, though it is one. It does not look like a place where anything could live, though it is full of living things, all of whom have made their peace with the absence of water in their own way.

The pillars stand. The wind moves through them. The call-birds sing. The colonists knock softly through the bone.

The plateau goes on.
