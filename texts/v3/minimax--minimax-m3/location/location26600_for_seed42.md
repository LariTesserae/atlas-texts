# The Reed-Vale of Anrath

The water comes first. It is the medium, the climate, the weather. Everything below is held inside it as a cathedral holds its congregation inside stone. Cool water, dense enough to press — not painfully, but with the steady insistence of something that has been there longer than anything living. Visibility is reasonable: a green-grey, filtered light, the color of lichen on old glass, that reaches down perhaps forty meters before the place dissolves into murk and suggestion.

The place sits high — that is, shallow — atop a ridge of volcanic origin, a long drowned mountain whose flanks drop away in either direction into the abyssal black. The ridge crest forms a kind of terrace, broad and flat, at a depth of roughly thirty meters. This is the Anrath shelf, though the Anrath people would not call it a shelf. They would call it the floor of the world, because they have no other floor, and because the floor itself is alive.

And it is alive.

The plants are not decoration here. They are substrate, ceiling, wall, road, fence, and food. The dominant species is a kind of giant kelp-analog — *Thalassia magnifica*, in the tongue of the naturalist-divers who sometimes come from the drowned cities of the lowlands — whose stipes can rise the full thirty meters from ridge to surface, anchoring at the bottom and fanning into a tangled canopy just below the tideline. Where the current is right, these form forests. Where the Anrath people have lived for a long time, they have done something more interesting.

They have made a town.

The town is not large. The ridge, at the depth where it can bear rooted growth, is perhaps three hundred meters long and eighty wide. Within that space, a dozen or so structures cluster near the center, with a few outliers — a watch-tower-kelp, a drying-rack-island, the great communal rookery — spaced more sparsely toward the edges. From above, looking down through the green ceiling, it would appear as a small constellation of denser shapes, woven together by rope and growth, surrounded by the looser forest.

Each structure is built of living kelp, trained and pruned over generations. The technique is primitive in the literal sense — no metal, no concrete, no processed materials beyond woven reed-fibers and shaped shell. But the labor involved is immense, and the architecture that results is something between a barnacle colony and a cathedral.

A house here is a hollow grown inside a single colossal plant, or across several. The kelp is induced, by careful cutting and binding, to form a chamber: the outer stipe thickened and curved into a wall, the fronds woven into a roof, the holdfast spreading into a floor that breathes. The interior is dim, lit by the same filtered greenish-grey as everything else, and smells of iodine and the sweet rot of organic matter. A doorway is a slit in living tissue that closes slowly when the family inside does not want visitors, or opens when they want trade. Smoke does not exist here — there is no fire, no oxygen to feed it, no need. The houses are warm because the water is cool and the bodies inside are warm, and the warmth is held by the kelp walls in a way that stone could not replicate.

The community is small. Perhaps sixty, eighty people. They are scattered, by the standards of a village, but they are bound tight by the structures they have made together, and by the structures' density — for these are not simple huts. They are layered. The kelp has been growing for centuries. Houses stand on houses' ruins; a new chamber is cut into the back of an old one; the great central hall — the *veth-kar*, the gathering-stomach — has been extended four times in living memory, each addition a careful graft of new kelp onto old. The result, from inside, is a place of unexpected monumentality: cathedral-scale chambers in a hamlet-sized town, dense with structure and history, dim with water-light.

Around the town, the forest continues.

The forest is loud. This surprises visitors, every time. The stillness of underwater places is a myth, or at least a myth of the open ocean. In the Anrath forest, sound is a constant weather. The dominant noise is the percussion of snapping-shrimp colonies — *Alpheidae*, the small pistol-shrimp that live in the kelp holdfasts by the tens of thousands — a sustained crackling, like a field of static, like rain on a tin roof, like a hearth fire. Beneath that, the lower sounds: the rhythmic grunting of territorial fish; the long moan of current through narrow channels in the canopy; the high chatter of the cleaning-wrens (small iridescent fish that dart at parasites); and, sometimes, the deep boom of something large passing along the ridge, a whale or a giant squid, its passage felt before it is heard.

The fauna are abundant, in the sense that the place is full of them. Every surface that is not kelp is something else living. Anemones colonize the lower fronds. Brittle-stars forage in the holdfasts. Eels sleep in the hollow chambers, tolerated by the Anrath because they eat the creatures that would otherwise eat the people. Schooling fish — sardine-analogs, anchovy-analogs, things with long vernacular names — move through the canopy in silver-blue clouds that darken the green light when they pass overhead. The children of Anrath learn to swim before they learn to walk, and to read the mood of a school of fish before they learn to read the woven marks that stand in for writing.

Slow.

Everything here is slow. The kelp grows at its own pace — perhaps a meter a month in good current, much less in winter-storms when the surface waters churn and the nutrients scatter. The houses grow with it. A chamber carved this year will be a slightly different chamber next year, as the living walls thicken, as new fronds push through the woven roof, as the doorway slowly migrates. The town is not built. It is *tended*, the way one tends a garden or a forest, except that one lives inside it. The Anrath have a saying, which the naturalist-divers record phonetically as *"veth karu, karu veth"* — the town is the garden, the garden is the town. They mean it literally.

Because of this slowness, the place has a quality of having been there forever. The kelp on the eastern flank of the ridge is older than the oldest living Anrath, and it has been part of the town-structure for as long as anyone remembers. When a storm tears a roof open, it is not repaired — it is regrown. When a child is born into a family, the family's house is extended in welcome. When someone dies, the chamber is sealed and a new one is cut. The town accretes, slowly, in the manner of coral or a termite mound, but on a scale of centuries rather than seasons.

There is a quality to the light here that one learns to recognize after a few days. It is not the blue of deep ocean — that would require depth this place does not have. It is not the clear yellow of shallows, either. It is the green-grey of a forest interior, of a grove under heavy canopy, of the inside of a tree where moss and lichen have filtered the sun for so long that the light itself has become a kind of moss. It falls into the cathedral chambers in shafts, when the canopy opens at high tide, and the shafts move across the woven floor with the slow precession of the moon.

The town is unusual, though the Anrath themselves would not say so. To them, of course it is like this. To the naturalist-divers who come down from the drowned cities of the lowlands — from Vorr, from Saltreach, from the long shelf-cities of the continental edge — it is something they record. They note the kelp-architecture with the careful fascination of people who build in stone and steel and who have never considered that the alternative might be to grow your walls. They note the sounds — the shrimp-crackle, the eel-murmur — and remark on how loud a quiet place can be. They note the fauna, abundant and unafraid, because the Anrath have not hunted them in living memory, only gathered them. They note the scale: small. Cathedral-scale rooms, but a hamlet of them, and the whole of it sitting on a drowned mountain in the middle of a very large ocean.

What they do not note, because they cannot, is the way the place feels after a week. The way the green light becomes familiar. The way the snapping of shrimp becomes the sound of having a roof over one's head. The way the slow growth of the walls becomes the rhythm of the seasons, of the year, of a life. The way a place that is unusual to a visitor is simply *home* to the people who were born inside its living walls and who expect, when they die, to become part of the substrate that new houses will grow from.

The water holds it all. Cool, dense, green-grey, loud with the percussion of shrimp and the low moan of current and the constant soft rustling of a forest that has been here, in one form or another, since long before anyone thought to call it a town.

It will be here, slowly, for a long time after.
