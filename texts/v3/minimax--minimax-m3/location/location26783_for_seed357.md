Begin in the deep middle of it, because that is where the loudness lives.

—

The air is a thick, almost-warm thing, nearly drunk with moisture, and everything that can hold water is holding it. The light comes in flat and white from a sky that does not quite commit to being cloud or haze — there is no sun to see, no shadow to flee, only a luminous ceiling pressed low over a continent of green. The light is honest in the way overcast light always is: it shows the shape of everything without flattering any of it.

What it shows, mostly, is leaves.

Leaves below. Leaves above. Leaves draped over leaves. The whole valley is a cathedral whose flying buttresses are stems, whose vaulting is a continuous, breathing canopy of foliage so dense it would be solid, except it isn't — it's moving, everywhere, all the time. A breeze that the eye cannot find is pushing the upper layers in slow oceanic swells, and below, in the understory, the air is restless with the smaller weather: leaf-tips tapping, fronds lifting, a thousand pale undersides flipping in hesitant unison as the wind direction flickers and dies and flickers again. The sound of it is the first thing you would learn. A papery, continuous, **thwap-thwap-thwap**, not rhythmic exactly, but textured, like a crowd clapping out of sync.

This is a place with no animals. Or: this is a place where the difference between animal and not-animal has gotten thin. The plants have, over the long recent, taken on the burdens animals usually carry. There are no insects here, no worms, no nematodes in the soil, no birds — but the work gets done. The work is getting done right now, with violent industry, and the forest is loud with the doing.

Watch a stem. Any stem. Pick one of the verticals holding up the roof, a pale-green pillar as thick as your thigh and twice as tall as you. Watch it for twenty seconds. It is *breathing*. Not metaphorically. The surface shivers, and small purse-mouthed openings along its length open and close in slow waves, and at the rim of each opening a thin mucous curl extrudes, glistens, retracts. The stem is drinking the air. It is taking in vapor, in this saturated heat, and the vapor is being fed down, drawn along internal channels toward a tangle of roots that are not under the soil, because — look, there isn't soil, not really. There isn't any dirt here. There is **structure**, but it is built, and it is built from the plants themselves, and the plants are built from the plants themselves, and you have to stop somewhere because the recursion is the point.

The ground is a lattice. Hewn, or grown, or both. From a distance the valley floor looks like a single dark-green carpet, but standing on it, you see it is a mesh — a tight weave of stems laid flat, each one the width of a forearm, knotted and braided into a vast living mat. The mat has been walked on, has been sat on, has been rained on, has been *bled on*, and it has responded by growing thicker. In the gaps of the weave, black water sits in shallow pools, very still, very warm. If you kneel and look down into one, you can see the underside of the lattice: root-tips descending into the dark like fingers dipped in ink, each one tipped with a white, hairy, sucking pad. The pools are not really pools. They are the place where the lattice stops drinking long enough for water to gather. These are the forest's moments of overflow.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It weeps out of every cut stem as a pale latex that turns clear on contact with air. It condenses on the underside of leaves in fat trembling drops that fall without warning and detonate against the lattice with a sound like fingertips snapping. It rises from the ground as visible mist — low, knee-high, moving in shoals, sometimes parting to reveal a stem-trunk that emerges dark and dripping and is re-swallowed an instant later. When the heat intensifies, the canopy exhales a cloud that fills the valley to the chest, and you stand inside a white room whose only furniture is the suggestion of pillars.

This is young country. It has not been here long. There is a sharpness to the geography, a rawness, that betrays a recent arrival. The valley is high — an alpine hanging-bowl, rimmed by ridges that still show the clean geometry of recent fracture: angular flanks of exposed stone, scree slopes that have not yet rounded into softness, cliff-faces with tooled edges. The plants are colonizing this fresh terrain in a hurry, in the way you might pack a room when you knew the guests were coming soon. They have colonized it almost entirely. The bones of the place, the gray and tan and rust of the rock, show only at the very top — the crests, the outcrops, the places where wind shear keeps the green from holding. Below the ridgeline, you cannot find a single square meter of bare stone. The lattice has been laid over the slopes in great tongues, advancing downhill, each tongue edged with a brighter green that marks the fresh growth, the tip of the expansion, still wet, still translucent, curling at the margins like the lip of a wound that is not a wound.

And it is turbulent. The whole system is.

Turbulence is the verb here. The forest is not a thing that is; it is a thing that is **happening**. A patch of lattice at your feet thickens as you watch, a stem fattening visibly over the span of a minute, a new shoot punching up between two older ones and unfurling its first leaf with the slow impatient violence of a hand opening. A few meters away, something is dying — an older stem has gone brown, has lost its grip on the air, has been unmoored from its place in the weave, and now it is sinking, sagging, the leaves around it leaning inward like neighbors at a funeral, and the brown stem is being *consumed*. Other stems are already wrapped around it, white threads of mycelial-looking tissue feeding into it, sucking its moisture back into the lattice, recycling it. The death does not look like death. It looks like a parcel of land being rezoned, quietly, over the span of an hour.

This is what loud means. Not a sound loud. A *loudness* loud. A density of event per square meter. Leaves sprouting and being reabsorbed. Stems thickening and hollowing. Lattice weaving and unweaving. Mist boiling up, rain falling in fits, the underside of the canopy always dripping, always tapping, with occasional great claps of heavier drops released in cascades from the highest leaves when the cloud weight exceeds what they can hold. There is also a deeper bass note, almost felt rather than heard — a low creak of stems under their own growth, a subsonic complaint from the roots as the lattice heaves. The whole valley is busy. The whole valley is *at work*. There is no stillness to find. Whatever you are looking at is in the middle of becoming something else.

The strangeness is not in any one feature. The strangeness is the alignment.

It is a forest with no animals, but it is loud. It is a forest with no soil, but it is rich. It is wet, but the water is not falling from the sky so much as circulating through the system, drunk in, breathed out, sweated, wept, pooled, drunk again. It is hot, but not uncomfortably: the canopy holds the heat like a tent holds breath, and the moisture manages it, and you are constantly at the edge of a sweat. It is built, but no one has built it. It is constructed, but the constructors are the constructions. It is total — there is nothing in this valley that is not the plants, and there is no part of the plants that is not the valley. The lattice is the floor and the foundation and the plumbing. The canopy is the ceiling and the weather. The leaves are the walls, and the rain, and the breath.

It is high country. The thinness of the air should be felt — a slight drunkenness at the edges of exertion. The light is strong but filtered, UV-rich when it strikes the canopy but reduced to a green half-light below, where the eye has to adjust to find shapes. The plants up here are not the great broad-leafed giants of lowland jungle; they are wiry, sun-loving, hot-tempered things, with small stiff leaves and pale flowers that are not for pollination but for *signaling*, displaying colors no eye will see, because there are no eyes. The colors are for the other plants. The whole forest is talking to itself in ultraviolet and crimson and pollen-yellow, a chemical semaphore, a coordination without anybody being in charge.

Stand in the middle of it. Don't try to make sense of the boundaries — they aren't there. There is no edge where the forest ends and something else begins; the forest tapers, thins, and eventually meets bare stone up at the ridges, and the bare stone has its own thin crust of lichen that is not quite the same plant but is in negotiation with the same water. The whole thing is one big slow argument about where one organism stops and another begins.

The heat is at your collar. The wet is on every surface. The sound is continuous, layered, almost a single chord — the tap of drops, the creak of growth, the hiss of transpiration, the distant shudder of a stem somewhere giving up and being embraced. Above, the leaves are restless with a wind you cannot feel. Below, the black water in the lattice gaps is being drunk.

You are inside a single, very old, very young, very busy thing, and it does not know you are here, because it has never known anything that was not itself.

That is the valley. That is also the whole country. The ridges to the east are the same. The ridges to the west are the same. Walk for a day in any direction and you will find it the same. The plants have come here and they have *finished the job*, in the sense that there is no space left they have not staked, no surface they have not claimed, no drop of water they have not already taken a draft of. They are now in the long, loud, turbulent business of staying that way.
