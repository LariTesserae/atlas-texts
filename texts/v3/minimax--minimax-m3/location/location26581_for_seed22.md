The basin is young. You can tell because the ridges are still sharp — every fault scarp, every lava fold, every collapsed tube still holds its edge instead of having rounded into smoothness. The drainage cuts are still V-shaped where the rains have been at them, and where the water has pooled in the low places, the shorelines are crisp, not yet bitten into. The rock is dark, glossy in places where glassy surfaces haven't yet weathered to matte, and it rings underfoot when struck, almost metallic in tone. Recent. Within a working memory of the planet, recent.

But the climate hasn't waited for the rock to settle. Warmth and damp have come in together — almost no season divides them — and the basin has greened over with the violence of a place that has had everything it needed and no time to be polite about it. The vegetation is wet. Everything is wet. The air itself feels like something you could wring out. Ferns uncurl in the still-shaded cracks, and where their fronds touch the basalt, the moisture beads and the beads slip down. Mosses, liverworts, climbing things with adhesive pads — the rock is losing its shine, losing its edges by the molecule, but the geological features still read as sharp at any human scale. There are cycad-like growths in the deeper folds, flowering vines that hang in long curtains between standing stones, and low trees with prop roots that arch out over the standing water and drop new stems into it. The leaves are large, dark, often waxy, sometimes iridescent. Everything blooms, and everything is in bloom simultaneously; nothing is taking turns.

There is a sound.

It is not one sound. It is the compounding of so many sounds that the ear gives up trying to separate them and registers them as a single pressure against the side of the head. From any single direction, broken down, you can pull threads: a chirr-chirr-chirr rising and falling in a long pulse; a low, subsonic throb that you feel in the soft tissue of your inner ear more than you hear; a metallic, almost xylophonic ring that has no source you can locate, that seems to come equally from above and from the stones themselves; a periodic crash like something heavy falling into water; a continuous, rustling hush that could be leaves, could be chitin, could be the wind in some material you don't recognize. Together, layered, never quite repeating, never quite ceasing, they form a kind of weather of sound. You have to speak loudly to be heard three feet away. You do not get used to it; you merely survive it.

The light is the dim companion of the sound. The sky is overcast — there has been no break in the clouds for as long as anyone present can remember, though the locals, if there are locals, may have a different sense of what counts as a memory. The cloud is thick and low, sometimes brushing the upper branches of the taller trees, sometimes pouring down the sides of the basin in slow tongues of fog that lose themselves in the canopy. The light that reaches the basin floor is filtered through this: diffuse, sourceless, the greenish-grey of a photographic darkroom safelight. Shadows are weak. You cannot tell where the sun is. You cannot tell whether the sun is.

It is in this light, in this sound, in this wet, in this warmth, that the constructions appear.

They are not many. They are extremely not many. Between any one of them and the next, you might walk for an hour, or two, and find only vegetation and damp stone and the occasional animal passing through. The basin is empty in the way a cathedral is empty: a single presence in a vast vaulted space. But it is empty with the emptiness of a place that has been deliberately, carefully, left open.

The constructions themselves do not look like buildings, at least not at first glance. They have edges, yes — clean, mathematical edges that the rock around them does not have, edges that should have weathered by now and have not. They are tall, some of them, but their height is not the kind that asserts itself against the sky; rather they seem to be in conversation with the canopy, the way certain towers are said to have been in conversation with the stars. Their material is not glass, not metal, not stone, not any composite you have a name for. It is dark. It is unreflective. Where the dim light falls on it, it does not so much reflect as absorb and re-radiate very faintly, as if it were a thing that had only agreed to be visible on sufferance. When you approach one, the sound changes — or your perception of the sound changes — and a single very high, very thin tone emerges, not unpleasant, that seems to be measuring you.

What they do, you cannot say. You can say that the air around them is subtly different: the humidity, the temperature, the way sound carries, the way light filters. There is a sense that they are not sitting in the basin but holding it — that they are the reason the basin is the way it is, that the wetness, the warmth, the dimness, the deafness are all, in some sense, a single artifact maintained at a particular setting. Or perhaps they are merely the markers of something larger, the way a fence post marks the edge of a field. You cannot tell. You cannot tell with anything you have, and yet you know, standing near one, that it is not merely a building.

The fauna is the loudest thing of all, in many ways, even though it does not seem to be what is making the sound. It moves through the vegetation in a constant glancing traffic — six-legged, eight-legged, no-legged at all; some of them half the size of a human, some of them the size of a thumb, some of them, glimpsed only in the corner of the eye, much larger. They have color in this dim light that should not be visible and yet is — saturated reds and violets and a particular orange that the eye reads almost as a sound. They move through the undergrowth on paths that look almost maintained. Some of them pause, near you, and watch you with arrangements of eyes that suggest a mathematics you cannot follow. None of them, as far as you have observed, have attacked you. None of them have spoken to you. They are abundant the way a school of fish is abundant: a single decision made by many bodies, distributed across the space.

There is a path. Or there are paths. They are not worn by feet but suggested by the way the vegetation thins and the stones lie flatter and the constructions begin, in the far distance, to appear more frequently. Following one, you begin to suspect that the basin has a center. The sound grows louder, but only fractionally, because it has been at capacity the entire time. The light does not change. The vegetation does not thin so much as shift in register — the same lushness, but different species, different habits, different greens. After a while, you understand that you have not been walking through wilderness at all. You have been walking through a city — a city with the population density of a forest, the buildings spaced as trees are spaced, the roads as game trails. You have been inside it the entire time.

And in the center of the city, where the constructions are no longer rare but begin to crowd toward one another, the central one stands. It is larger. It is taller. It hums with the hum that you have been hearing all along without quite identifying — the bass note under the whole chord — and around its base, in a ring, the fauna congregates in numbers that suggest ritual, or at least habit. They do not look up at it. They simply are near it.

You stand at the edge of the ring and you cannot hear yourself think. The light from the sky, the warmth from below, the moisture on your skin, the sound in your ears, the slow turning of unfamiliar constellations of behavior in the undergrowth — all of it, taken together, is the place. It is not a place you have been before. It is not a place that has been, before you, very much. But it has been, and it is being, and it intends, you suspect, to continue.
