# The Gullet

A tunnel, but not yet. A cathedral, but still being decided.

You stand at the bottom of an excavation that has been underway for thirty-one years, or thereabouts, by the reckoning of the men who work here — though "work" is a generous word for what happens, since most of the slow time is spent waiting for the machines. The heat comes up through the floor like breath from an open mouth. The air is wet. The wet is on everything, all the time: a skin of condensation on the steel ribs of the temporary supports, a glaze on the rock, a slow drip from every bolt-head and cable tray, the way a glass sweats in summer. It is hot in the way that saunas are hot — not the killing heat of a desert but a thorough heat, the heat of a fever, the heat of being inside something alive.

The light is dim. This is not a place of bright work-lamps and crisp shadows. The lamps are old industrial sodium jobs, caged in wire, hung at long intervals along the ceiling, and half of them have developed the habit of flickering, or of holding a sullen orange that does not actual illuminate so much as *propose* illumination. You see shapes more than you see things. The far end of the chamber, where the tunnel bore continues into raw basalt, is a darkness that has weight to it. The basalt itself, where the cutters have left it, is a glistening grey-black, and where the cutters haven't yet reached it, it is just a guess.

The place is small. Not in the way a closet is small, but in the way a room is small — you can feel the walls. There are walls of poured concrete on three sides, grey and sweating, with the wooden grain of the formwork still pressed into them like fossils. There is one wall of raw stone. The ceiling is perhaps eight meters up, ribbed with steel I-beams and cross-bracing, hung with cable and pipe and the looping intestines of ventilation. The floor is the most confused surface: a patchwork of older concrete, newer concrete, temporary steel plating, puddles that never quite drain because the drains are always half-clogged with the grey-green silt that migrates up from somewhere below.

It is hot. I want to keep saying this. The heat is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing they stop noticing. It sits in the chest. It makes the lungs work slower. It is not unpleasant, exactly — the men who work here have got used to it the way one gets used to a warm coat — but it is present. Geothermal, the engineers say, though the engineers have been saying it for years in tones that suggest they wish it were otherwise. There is something warm beneath this place. The water that seeps in is warm. The rock is warm. The concrete cures and then never quite stops curing.

The water is everywhere. It runs in shallow channels cut along the floor, narrow troughs of it, dark with iron. It condenses on the ceiling and falls in fat, slow drops that take their time about it — you can hear each one gather, hesitate, and let go, and then a single percussive *tok* somewhere on the steel decking or the rock below. It weeps down the walls in long vertical stains, marking the concrete with the brown lacework of dissolved minerals. It pools in the tie-rod holes left from the formwork, little perfect circles of dark water that never dry. In the older corners of the chamber, where the construction has paused and the place has had years to settle into itself, the water has begun to grow things.

Vegetation, such as it is, is sparse and unambitious. A few pale ferns have taken hold in the cracks between old concrete pours — not green exactly, more the color of weak tea, leaning toward the dim sodium light as though hoping for better. Moss, the color of an old bruise, climbs a few feet up one wall where a leak has been steady for years. There is a stand of something with white stalks and no leaves that nobody has formally identified; the men have started calling it "the celery." It grows in a place where warm water drips continuously from a pipe joint, and it is the only living thing in this chamber that appears to be thriving in any obvious way. Everything else is merely surviving.

The construction is the construction of a thing that is not yet itself. This is one of three planned platforms on a new transit line — a line that has been on the city's drawing boards since before most of the men here were born, and that will, when it is finished, connect this district to a district that does not yet exist, in a city that is still deciding where its edges are. For now this chamber is a stub: a box carved into basalt, walled in concrete, waiting to be joined to the next box, and the next, eventually making a tunnel that will go somewhere. The men speak of the line in the patient, slightly amused tone of men who have heard the same projected completion date revised downward many times.

The machinery, when it runs, is loud in the way that a sustained physical effort is loud — not the sharp percussion of demolition but the long grinding moan of the borer as it chews into the far wall, the cyclical pneumatic thud of the concrete pumps, the high whine of the ventilation fans that never turn off and that constitute, together with the dripping, the permanent background hum of the place. The fans push warm wet air from somewhere deeper in, down tunnels that have not yet been named, and pull it through this chamber and out through a shaft somewhere above. The air moves. You can feel it on the skin. It smells of wet concrete, machine oil, the warm mineral breath of the rock, and faintly of something organic that no one wants to investigate too closely.

The fauna is the thing that surprises the new men. It surprises them, and then it stops surprising them, and then it becomes the central fact of the place, the way the heat becomes the central fact, and they stop being surprised by that too.

There are cockroaches, but the word *cockroach* is not adequate. These are cockroaches that have had generations to themselves in the warm dark, fed on the leavings of the work crews and the things that grow in the wet cracks, and they have become something — not larger exactly, but more present, more architectural. They move in palimpsests across the floor, layers of them, each one going about its business with the unhurried patience of an animal that has nowhere better to be. There are pale grubs in the standing water. There are rats, but also larger things that the men have given up trying to name — long sinuous shapes that move along the cable trays overhead with a slow liquid grace and disappear into holes in the raw stone wall. There are bats, improbably, hanging in clusters from the steel beams in the highest reaches of the ceiling, just at the edge of the light; they were here before the construction began, the old foreman says, though no one believes him. There is something that lives in the warm water channels and surfaces occasionally to consider the lights with flat, disinterested eyes — a kind of pale lizard, perhaps, or a kind of fish, the men disagree.

The fauna is not hostile. This is important. The fauna does not bite, mostly, and when it does it is because a man has put his hand somewhere he should not have. The fauna is simply *there*, in numbers, with the indifference of things that have claimed a place and do not feel they need to justify their presence. It is the overwhelming fact of the chamber: more animal life than plant life, more animal life than human life, more bodies in motion in this small dim wet cathedral than there are engineers and laborers and inspectors combined, by an order of magnitude. You learn to step around it. You learn not to set your hard hat down on a bench without looking first. You learn that the brown smear on the cable you were about to grab is alive, and you grab the next one over.

The construction goes slowly. This is not a project of urgency. The funding arrives in drips, the permits in larger drips, the workforce in occasional pulses. The men who work here are mostly long-term, some of them second-generation, sons of men who worked the first platform on a different line, twenty years ago, and who grew up hearing about underground concrete and warm rock and the particular patience of carving a city into the belly of the earth. They move at the pace the rock allows them to move. They pour a section. They wait for it to cure. They advance the borer ten meters. They wait for the surveyors. They pour another section. In the meantime the water finds new paths through the new rock. The fauna colonizes the new surfaces. The moss, given years, will reach a little farther up the wall. The celery will grow another inch. The cockroaches will work out a new traffic pattern around the new obstacle.

The place is becoming itself. That is the dynamic — slow, embryonic, the slow accumulation of decisions and accidents that will, someday, make this into a platform, and the platform into a station, and the station into part of a line that carries people from where they are to where they want to be, and that for now is just a warm dim wet chamber in the rock, with men in it occasionally, and fauna in it constantly, and the slow patient sound of water and fans and the occasional distant groan of a machine that is, at this very moment, eating the next ten meters of stone that will eventually become the next room.
