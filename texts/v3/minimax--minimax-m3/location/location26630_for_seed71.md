# The Long Plateau of Inhôk

There is a place you could call a continent, though no map survives that places it correctly. Maps drawn there do not survive the journey home. They re-ink themselves in the saddlebag, redrawing coastlines and rivers that were never surveyed, and by the time the traveler unfolds them in some known city the parchment shows a different country entirely, older, with a sun in the wrong corner and a compass rose that spins when no one is looking. So cartographers have stopped going. The few who return do not speak of it, and the few who speak of it do not return.

This is the Long Plateau of Inhôk.

It sits high. The air is thin and the cold is not the cold of winter — there is no winter here in the usual sense — but the permanent cold of altitude, the cold of having risen as far as a piece of ground can rise and then having held there, for a very long time, while the weather worked at it. The plateau is old. The mountains that once stood on it are not mountains anymore. They are low humps, long whale-backs of stone, shoulders of harder granite emerging from softer country like the knuckles of a buried hand. The rivers have ground the valleys open and then ground them wider, and then ground them almost flat, and the plateau has the soft, sad, resigned profile of land that has been weathered so long it has forgotten what it once was. From a high enough vantage you can see almost to the curvature of the earth, and the land is just land, going on, all the way to a haze that may be distance or may be the place where the world gives up trying to be coherent.

The cold is not bitter. It is the kind of cold that comes in through the soles of your feet and never quite leaves, that keeps the lichens the color they are — that particular yellow-green, that almost-grey-green, that dusted silver of things that grow in a thin persistent cold. The ground is wet. Not rain-wet, mostly — it rains here, but rarely, in long slow drenches that fall for days and then vanish for months — but condensation-wet. The air carries water in it always, a held moisture, and when the temperature drops at night the moisture falls out onto every surface and in the morning the whole plateau is jewelled. By midday a thin fog breathes along the river-valleys. By evening it is gone. The rocks never dry out. The mosses never stop drinking.

And there is vegetation. Lush, here, in a way that should not work — lush at this altitude, in this cold, with this little rain — but lush because the place has had time to learn. The plateau has been itself for longer than memory, and the plants on it have been themselves for nearly as long, and they have settled into an arrangement that does not need much water because every drop is held, every drop is reused. The trees are short and old. They grow in slow, twisted shapes, sometimes leaning hard against the prevailing wind, sometimes growing in tight clusters where one trunk has split into five and the five lean apart like the fingers of a hand pressed against a window. Their bark is dark and soft and grows a deep fur of moss and lichen that no one ever peels off. The undergrowth is thick where it can be — a carpet of low shrubs, hard ferns, the kind of plants you can push a hand into and feel all the way down to the wet soil and never be sure where one plant ends and another begins. Between the trees, the open country is a plain of moss. Walking on it is soft. Walking on it is quiet.

The plateau is vast. Vast enough to have weather of its own. Clouds form over the interior and break over the interior. There are storms here that begin as a pressure in the ears and end as a horizon gone white, and in between the lightning moves in long slow sheets across the underside of the clouds because the air is too thin and too cold for sharp thunderbolts. When the storms have passed, the air smells of ozone and of the green wet smell of every growing thing suddenly more alive. The clouds in the dry season are thin and high and they cast moving shadows across the land that the eye can follow for an hour, and the shadows are themselves a kind of weather — the shadow weather, the slow weather, the weather of a place that takes its time.

There are people here. Sparse. Not many. A town every sixty or seventy miles along the river valleys, and between them, smaller — hamlets, single steadings, places where a road crosses a road for no reason that a stranger can see. They build with stone. The stone is local, the grey-green stone of the plateau itself, quarried or gathered from the outcrops, and the buildings sit on the land the way the outcrops sit on the land, as if they grew there. Houses are low. Roofs are pitched against snow, though it rarely snows — a precaution, a habit. Walls are thick. Windows are small. Doors are heavy and hung on iron pintles that have rusted to a deep red-brown, the only red-brown color in a landscape that otherwise keeps to green and grey and the pale blue of distance. Smoke rises from the chimneys in the cold of morning and dissolves into the fog. Chimneys are tall because the cold comes down through them and they want the smoke drawn high.

The towns are not large. A main street, cobbled with river stones, running between two rows of stone houses. A market square with a well in the middle, the well lined with worked stone and covered with a wooden lid that lifts off. A temple, or what passes for one — a single hall, longer than it is wide, with a roof that looks like a ship upside-down, and inside a place for a fire that is never put out. There is no metalwork to speak of. The tools are stone and bone and wood and a little iron, hoarded, never bought or sold at any price you can pay. Cloth is woven from the inner bark of a tree that grows only on the lower slopes. Leather is rare and mostly the hide of nothing anyone will describe to you, because there is nothing to describe. The towns are quiet and orderly and the people who live in them move with the unhurried patience of people who have lived in the same place for a long time and do not expect to leave it.

There are no animals.

That is the first thing the visitor notices, after the cold and the green. There is no bird. There is no insect. There is nothing that moves through the undergrowth with a small rustle and a small heartbeat. The streams have fish in them, but the fish are pale and they do not seem to eat anything; they hold their position in the current with a stillness that is not quite the stillness of fish. Sometimes a frog. Sometimes, very rarely, a moth at a window. Otherwise nothing. The people do not keep dogs. The people do not keep cats. The people do not seem to notice this absence, and if you ask them about it they look at you with the polite confusion of someone asked to account for the color of the sky.

The quiet is enormous. It is the quiet of a place that has stopped making the sounds most places make. There is wind, and there is water, and there is the creak of a door, and there is the distant strike of a stone hammer on a stone chisel from somewhere down the road, and that is almost all. There is no birdcall. There is no barking. There is no hum of insects in the grass. The moss, when you walk on it, makes no sound. After a few days in such a place the ear begins to invent sounds that are not there, and the visitor should not trust what he thinks he hears in the second week, because by then the place is already inside him, adjusting him, smoothing the joints of his attention.

And the place is strange.

The plateau is old and the cold is permanent and the rain is rare and the vegetation is lush and there are no animals and there is weather of its own and the people are sparse and they live in stone towns of a primitive craft — none of this would make the place what it is. What makes the place what it is something else. Something that does not have a name in any language spoken here, though there are words that circle it the way the lightning moves under the clouds, slowly, far below.

It is easiest to begin with geometry.

If you walk due north from the town of Inhôk for what feels like three hours, you arrive at a stone outcrop you have passed before. You know you have passed it before because it has a mark on it, a chiseled chevron that one of the townspeople made years ago to indicate a landmark, and the chevron is weathered but still visible. The outcrop is in the same place. The chevron faces the same way. But the land between the town and the outcrop is different. The valley is wider than it was. The river is in a different place, an arm of it that you do not remember, the water moving in a direction you cannot account for. The walk took three hours by your body, by the sun, by the growing ache in your knees. You arrive and the chevron is the same chevron and the stone is the same stone and the town behind you is gone. There is no town behind you. There is only more land, more moss, more low twisted trees, and a fog in the distance that may be a town or may be more land.

If you ask a person from Inhôk about this, they will not be surprised. They will say something like: *the land moves*. Or they will say nothing at all. They will offer you a bowl of something warm and look at the fire.

It is not only distance. The sun rises in the east in the morning. It rises in the east in the afternoon. It sets in the west in the evening. It also, sometimes, rises in the west and sets in the east, and the day is longer than it should be, and the shadows fall in directions that have nothing to do with the angle of the light. The shadows are wrong. They are not wrong by a little. They are wrong by a great deal, sometimes twice as long as the thing casting them, sometimes pointing toward the light source, sometimes falling *up*, lying along the surface of the snow or the moss like a stain. When the shadows are wrong, the air has a thickness to it. The cold deepens. The fog, if there is fog, stops moving. The people go indoors.

Buildings have, on certain mornings, more rooms than they had the evening before. A house that had two rooms on the ground floor may have four. A corridor that ended at a door may end at a window, and the window may look out onto a courtyard that is not on the outside of the building. The rooms are furnished. There is a bed. There is a lamp. The lamp has oil in it. The bed has been slept in. The occupant has not been seen in the town for years, possibly ever. The townspeople do not enter these rooms. They walk past them, politely, with the same polite confusion they showed when you asked about the animals.

A road that ended at a cliff last week ends this week at another town, and the town is one you have been to before, and the people in it recognize you, and they offer you a bowl of something warm, and they ask you how your mother is, and your mother has been dead for eleven years. You sit with them. You drink. You do not answer.

There is no malice in this. That is the unsettling thing. The plateau is not hostile. It does not attack. It does not consume. It is old, and the cold is permanent, and the rain is rare, and the moss drinks, and the people live quietly in their stone towns, and the land rearranges itself around them the way the lichens rearrange themselves around the branches of the trees, slowly, without urgency, without hatred, without any motive that a human mind can hold. The land is not trying to frighten you. The land is not trying to teach you. The land is behaving the way land behaves when it has been left alone for a very long time and has begun to forget the instructions.

You can stay a long time. You can stay forever. The plateau does not push. You will find, if you stay long enough, that the plateau has begun to look like you. The cold you feel is colder to you than it was. The dampness sits in your joints the way it sits in the joints of the older townspeople. You begin to lose the distinction between what you remember of the world before and what you remember of the plateau. You begin to lose, eventually, the distinction between yourself and a stone outcrop on a hillside, between your breathing and the wind, between the bowl in your hands and the bowl in someone else's hands in a town you have not yet visited that is the same town you are sitting in now.

The plateau is vast, and the cold is permanent, and the rain is rare, and the moss drinks, and the people live quietly in their stone towns, and the land rearranges itself, and the shadows fall wrong, and the rooms appear, and the rivers move, and the days are longer than they should be, and there are no animals, and the quiet is enormous, and the weather is its own, and the place is patient, and the place is old, and the place is still here.
