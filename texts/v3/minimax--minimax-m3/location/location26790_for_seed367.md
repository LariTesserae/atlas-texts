# The Hollow of Yhvel

There is a place inside the mountain that the mountain no longer remembers.

The approach is a long descent through strata so old they have begun to forget their own names—chalk that has become something other than chalk, granite that has become something other than granite, the whole spine of the range ground down across epochs into a soft, near-flat basin, a saucer cupped in the bones of what was once a peak. The wind has had its way with the rim. The rim is a suggestion. You could walk over it without knowing you had crossed into somewhere else.

But you know, because of the sound.

It starts as a low thing, felt in the sternum before it is heard. A presence in the stone, the way distant thunder is a presence before it is weather. By the time you have gone down through the last switchback of the crumbling lip, the sound has risen into something like a fabric of noises woven too tight to take apart—a thousand small percussions, the hiss and rasp of countless bodies moving against each other, the wet click of mandibles, the high thin piping of things that are not birds but have made birdsong their own. It is the sound of saturation. It is the sound of every cubic foot of the hollow holding more life than any cubic foot should hold, and the hollow being too small for the life inside it.

The light is the light of late dusk that has decided to stay. It comes from somewhere overhead, filtered through a skin of mineral dust and the bodies of slow-winged insects so numerous their drift has the texture of weather, and it arrives at the floor as a kind of amber milk, dim enough that eyes have widened here across generations, dim enough that the things which hunt do so as much by vibration as by sight. Nothing casts a hard shadow. Everything is its own faint glow, or holds the glow of its neighbors.

The vegetation is not what you have seen before.

It does not drink. There is nothing here to drink—nothing aqueous, nothing that would be called water by anyone standing in a place where water has meaning. The basin has been sealed by its own erosion for longer than the species climbing its inner walls can count, and the cycle that once passed through has been broken and broken again until only the residue of residue remains, a humidity so low it is less a fact than a rumor. And so the green things have learned other chemistries. They are thick-lobed and pale, the color of old ivory stained with copper, of fungi the size of fist or face, clustered so densely along every surface that the rock itself has become an afterthought. They take what falls. They take the bodies of the dead and the shed skins and the fine powders that drift from the ceiling. They hold the nitrogen that the insects bring. They have become, between them, a closed ledger, a self-perpetuating account of matter cycling in miniature. They are lush, and they are not green, and they are not wet, and they breathe something other than oxygen in the way you understand it.

The settlement is built into them and out of them. The structures are layered—a first settlement of fitted bone and the dried husks of large creatures, pressed down and compacted by their own weight into something almost like stone, and on top of that a second settlement of the same materials but smaller, more careful, the joins tighter, the windows fewer, and on top of that a third, and a fourth, and a fifth, the uppermost layer standing on the compressed strata of all the ones below, the whole stack rising like a stalagmite of accumulated habitation in a space barely large enough to hold one. The walls are alive. The walls are the vegetation, encouraged to grow in certain patterns, pruned in others, woven through with the cast-off skeletons of creatures that have given their calcium to the architecture of the ones who came after. Ladders descend from one layer to the next. Ladders ascend from one layer to the next. The dim light makes it hard to tell, sometimes, which is which.

The fauna is the problem and the answer and the only reason anything here persists.

It moves in sheets and columns. It moves in the cracks between the stacked dwellings and along the ropes that bind them and in the hollow chambers behind the walls. It is dense in the way a crowd is dense, except that no one here has ever been outside the crowd. The creatures range from things the size of a fingernail to things the size of a chest, and they have divided the hollow among themselves in a partitioning so fine that a fingertip placed anywhere on any surface is likely to be touching one of them, or resting on the remains of one, or both. They are not all friendly. The sound of the place includes, woven into its fabric, the smaller sounds of predation—a sudden silence where a moment ago there was movement, a brief clatter, a returning rhythm that has adjusted around the absence. Predator-prey, predator-predator, prey-prey, the relationships cascading in fractal recursion. The ecosystem is in motion. The ecosystem has always been in motion. Nothing here has ever known a still hour.

Tech is the kind of tech that the body remembers. There are tools of cut bone, shaped by long use against a thigh or a palm until they fit the hand that has worn them. There are blades of stacked chitin that hold an edge only because they are replaced often. There are cords of braided fiber, hair, gut. There are vessels of polished carapace. The making is done by hand and the makers are always within arm's reach of someone else, and the sounds of making—a rhythmic tapping, a drawn-out scraping—are part of the place's continuous percussion, indistinguishable from the sound of the creatures themselves. There is no metal here. There is no metal anywhere up to the rim and beyond the rim, where the strata have been stripped of everything soft and left only what cannot be taken.

The ancientness is everywhere. It is in the worn floor, which is not a floor but the remains of a thousand floors, each one reduced over time to a thin stratum of compacted residue, a kind of pressed-down memory, and each stratum distinguishable by its color and its grain if you know how to read it. It is in the way the dwellers speak of themselves as the fifth or sixth or seventh, and mean it literally, each generation the successor of a settlement that itself succeeded a settlement. It is in the lichen-fungi that has had more time than any individual creature to learn how to survive without what the place will not provide. It is in the air, which has been breathed and rebreathed so many times that the composition is something a chemist from outside would have to invent a vocabulary to describe. The place has settled into its final form. The place has been in its final form for so long that the final form has had time to erode, and what is being eroded now is what erosion has already made, and that which is being made is what was already here.

The dim light does not brighten. The sound does not quiet. The fauna does not pause. The vegetation does not wait.

If you stand in the middle of the hollow and look up, what you see is a confusion of layers, stacked dwellings climbing toward the amber ceiling, the drift of slow insects, the suggestion of the rim where it has worn nearly away, and beyond that—beyond that, the mountain, and beyond the mountain, the world, which goes on without this place and will go on without it, because the hollow has sealed itself and the cycle within the hollow has become its own weather, its own time, its own law.

The dwellers do not know they are sealed. The dwellers have never been unsealed. They are the second, the third, the fifth, and they build their bone-and-husk dwellings on the compressed strata of their forebears, and the fungi climb their walls, and the insects fill the cracks, and the predators take what is owed to them, and the sound goes on.

It has gone on for a very long time.

It will go on.
