# The Lifted Shelf

---

The rock here is young. That is the first thing, before anything else — that the stone itself is young. A continental plate shoved itself against another some sixty million years ago, and then sat, and sat, and sat beneath its own weight until something shifted in the mantle beneath it and the whole shelf lifted, dragged upward by a magma intrusion that hasn't finished cooling. The plateau is still rising. You can feel it if you live here long enough: a faint upward pressure in the bones, a near-subsonic hum in the teeth that may be the rock expanding and may be something else.

It has been high for a geological eyeblink. The rivers have not finished cutting. They are at it constantly — this is the sound, this is what fills the air — the water falling. The water falling everywhere, all the time, over edges that have not yet smoothed, down faces that still hold their original fracture. The rivers pour off the plateau's perimeter in a thousand places, none of them graceful, all of them loud, and the sound joins itself together overhead into a constant low roar that you stop noticing only because it never stops. The plateau is a caught breath, exhaling water.

The stone is pale. A granite-granodiorite, mostly, with the mica-flecked cream-grey of a thing that has not yet weathered deeply. But it is being weathered, oh it is being weathered, because the wetness is absurd up here. The clouds sit on the shelf like a held hand. They come in from the west — there is an ocean somewhere, distant, that feeds weather systems which climb the new fault scarp and condense on the cold pale rock. It rains often. It is always raining somewhere on the shelf, and where it is not raining, the mist is moving, and where the mist is not moving, the surfaces are weeping. Condensation gathers on every exposed face. The rock sweats. There is no dryness here, only a kind of saturated patience.

And so the mosses have come.

Not forest. There is no time for forest. No soil yet — just a thin powdering of frost-shattered stone, organic acids beginning their long patient work, a centimeter here, a centimeter there across millennia. But the mosses don't wait for soil. They spread across the bare rock itself in dark green-black mats, in liverwort sheets, in slow orange lichen crusts that paint the granite the color of a healing wound. They have come from somewhere — wind-blown spores, bird droppings, a few determined wind-carried seeds — and they are colonizing everything. They cover the platforms, they climb the fractures, they fill the shallow basins where water pools and stands. They are the first skin of this place.

The vegetation is total. That is the way to say it: plant as substrate. If you kneel and put your hand down, you will touch plant, or the dead scaffolding of plant, almost everywhere. The rock is becoming a memory. In another hundred thousand years nothing of the granite will show at all, but for now it still glints through in places — pale wounds where the moss has not yet reached, where a rockslide has scraped the surface clean, where the rivers have torn down to fresh stone in their haste to escape the shelf.

But the moss is sparse. Do not confuse coverage with density. The vegetation covers the world, but it is thin. A centimeter here, two there, never more than the thickness of your thumb. It is a living film. Beneath it you can feel the rock. The mosses do not yet have the mass to be architecture; they are still painting the world green and amber and the orange of crustose lichen. There is space between them. Bare rock glints. The ground between the moss-patches is pale and wet and slowly warming toward the day when something larger will root.

There are no trees. There are no shrubs. There are a few determined flowering things — cushions of pink saxifrage pressed tight against the stone, white-flowered crowberry, a yellow composite I cannot name — and they are strange and small against the vast pale openness. A crow will land here, sometimes. A ptarmigan, white in winter, brown in summer, picking at the moss. The fauna is rare. A beetle under a stone. A fly that the wind has carried up. Something must be pollinating the saxifrage, but I have not seen it. The shelf is too young for the larger migrations; the birds are the forerunners, and they are few.

The light is extraordinary. The shelf is high enough that the air has begun to thin, though not so high that breathing is difficult — perhaps three thousand meters, perhaps a little more. The sun strikes with a clean intensity, casting shadows that are sharp-edged and blue-black. When the mist parts, which it does for an hour or two most days, the light is almost white, and the wet rock glitters so that you have to squint. There is a quality to the light that feels provisional, as if the sun is still getting used to shining on something so newly uncovered.

And the machines.

Scattered across the shelf — far apart, each one alone in its valley or on its ledge — are the stations. They are not large. A turbine hall, perhaps. A penstock snaking down a cliff face. A small powerhouse of poured concrete and weathered steel. They are the early hydroelectric installations of the new watershed, built before the rivers had finished cutting, built in the first decade after the uplift, when engineers came up here and saw all that falling water and understood what it meant. They hum. They hum and roar and make the constant sound of generators turning, of water being forced through steel, of electricity being wrung from the descent. Their sound joins the sound of the falling water until the whole shelf rings with a continuous mechanical roar that you cannot find the edge of.

There are perhaps thirty of them now. Spread across the watershed. Connected by buried lines, by switchyards no larger than a house. Each one is alone in its part of the shelf — you can walk for hours across moss and pale stone and see nothing, and then come around a shoulder of rock and find a turbine hall tucked into a gorge, water roaring past it on every side, the building small and grey and steady against the noise. They are not beautiful, exactly, but they have begun to belong. Moss climbs their concrete. Lichens crust their steel. The stations are twenty years old, some of them, and the moss is beginning to overtake them — not because they are being abandoned, but because the moss will overtake anything that sits still here long enough.

There is no town. There are maintenance crews that come up on rotation — a road winds across the shelf, gravel-pale, and you can trace it from station to station by the darker wet line where it has compacted the moss. There is a single landing strip where the small aircraft land with parts and personnel. There is a depot at the center of the watershed, half-buried against a north-facing cliff, with a mess hall and bunks and a radio mast. Beyond this, the stations are alone with their falling water.

The dynamism here is slow. Nothing is changing quickly. The rivers cut a little deeper each year, but only a little. The moss spreads another centimeter across the rock, but only a centimeter. The turbines hum and the water falls and the granite sweats and the lichen paints itself orange in the wet air, and these things continue. The shelf rises a little more — or perhaps it has stopped; the geodesists disagree. The climate may be warming; the glaciers that never quite formed here may be considering it. The pioneer species give way to something more permanent, slowly, over decades. The stations age. The moss creeps up their walls.

You stand on the shelf. You are small and the openness is total. In every direction the pale rock and the dark green moss stretch out, and the mist moves across them, and the sound of falling water and turning machinery holds the whole world in a low continuous roar. The light is bright and sharp and clean. There is almost nothing alive here that you can see — no animals, no trees, no movement except the slow drift of the mist and, far down a gorge, the faint silver flash of a turbine's overflow. The place is unfinished. The place is becoming. You are standing on a thought the world is still having about itself.

The wet rock gleams under your boots. A rivulet finds your footprint and follows it for a meter before veering off toward the next station. Somewhere a generator adjusts its pitch by a half-tone, and the sound of the shelf shifts with it, and then settles again.
