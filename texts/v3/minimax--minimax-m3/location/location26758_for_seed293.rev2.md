The place announces itself before you enter it. Sound first — a low, pervasive hum you feel in the sternum, layered beneath the croaks and drips and the long slow calls of something large and wet moving through the undergrowth. Then the smell, which is rot and bloom in equal measure, a smell that means a great deal of life is dying and being born at the same time. Then the mist, rising off the standing water in lazy ropes and tangling in the canopy.

You stand at the edge of what is locally called *the Garden*, though the word is a courtesy. The Garden is small. You can walk its perimeter in a morning. It is bounded — by a low ridge of older root on one side, by a sluggish creek on another, by a stand of pale trees on a third — and within its bounds the world has been working on one problem for a very long time.

The ground is not ground. The ground is a dense mat of root and rhizome, a felt of living wood so thick you bounce when you step, woven over standing water that ranges from ankle-deep to chest-deep depending on where you place your feet. The water is warm. The water is brown and faintly sweet. The water moves in slow currents that pull at the rhizomes and carry silt and the small white flowers that drop constantly from the canopy above. The ground is also, in places, a roof. The ground is the floor of a building that grew.

The plants here are not plants. Or they are plants and also other things. They have been engineered — or have engineered themselves, the distinction has long since blurred — into forms that approach architecture. There are trunks wider than houses that have hollowed themselves into rooms, with the rings of their growth still visible on the inner walls. There are buttresses that arc from trunk to ground like the flying buttresses of a cathedral, only green and still growing, still thickening at the edges where new wood spirals out in pale curls. There are walls that are not walls — fields of dense planting that have been trained, or trained themselves, into barriers that hold back water and shape channels. There are towers.

The towers are the strangest thing in the Garden. They rise perhaps ten stories. They are trees, but they are also towers in the sense that something has climbed them and built upon them and lived in them for a very long time. Each tower is crowned with a different structure: one holds a platform of woven vines from which something hangs and slowly turns, casting shadows in a slow rhythm that suggests it is measuring something — perhaps the light, perhaps the humidity, perhaps a quantity that has no name in the language you think in. Another tower is bare for its last three stories and ends in a burst of silver-gray fronds that move without wind. The fronds are not moved by air but by something else, a force you can feel prickling along your forearms when you stand beneath them. They fold and unfold in patterns that do not repeat. They have been doing this for a long time. A third tower has no crown at all but instead a single opening near its top from which, on certain evenings, a long pale shape emerges and hangs in the air and considers the Garden below. The other fauna give this shape a wide berth.

You learn, walking through, that the place has been here for an extraordinarily long time. The erosion is not geological — there is no rock to erode. The erosion is biological. The oldest structures have been reabsorbed. They were built, and then they died, and then they became soil, and then the soil became plant, and then the plant became built again. The Garden is layered. You can stand in a clearing and look down and see, half-submerged, the curving flank of something that was once a dome. You can step off the rhizome mat and feel, beneath your foot, the hard corner of a thing that is not plant, something made, something that has not decayed because the chemistry of the place has not yet figured out how to eat it.

These older things, the unrotting things, are the technology of the Garden. They are not metal. They are not plastic. They are not any material the engineers of the city it once was had a name for, and the engineers of that city did not, in the end, fully understand them either. They are dark, very smooth, faintly cool to the touch, and slightly heavier than they look. The Garden's present inhabitants know them as *the bones*, or *the teeth*, or, in the older speech, *the first things*, and they use them as anchors, as cornerstones, as the points around which new growth is coaxed.

The bones operate on principles that current physics does not fully describe. They are warm to the touch on certain days and cool on others, with no pattern you can detect. Sometimes a bone will hum, and the hum will cause the plants around it to grow in specific directions for weeks afterward, as if the hum were a song the plants were trying to harmonize with. Sometimes a bone will go dark and cold and stay that way for a year, and during that year nothing grows within ten meters of it; the rhizome mat pulls back like a tide, the water rises in that patch, and the inhabitants go around it in silence. They do not fear the bones. They simply give them room. Some of the bones, in the deepest parts of the Garden, are sunk so far into the mulch that only a curve of dark surface remains visible, and around those the water lies still and perfectly clear, and small bright fauna gather as if at a shrine.

The fauna is abundant. There are frogs the size of your head that perch on the buttresses and watch you with flat gold eyes. There are long pale invertebrates that hang in curtains from the lower branches and pulse softly in time with no rhythm you can identify. There are things in the water — you see them as disturbances, as shadows, as the slow turning of a great back just at the surface — that are larger than you would like to think about. Small bright shapes with too many wings move in the canopy and chirp in frequencies that overlap the edge of hearing and make your teeth ache. A long coiled thing lives in each tower; you only see it when the light is at a certain angle. The fauna do not fight each other. The Garden is too small and too full for fighting. They eat each other gently, in passing, and the eaten things grow back.

The inhabitants are sparse. They live in the rooms inside the trunks, one or two to a room. They are small, dark-skinned, and move carefully. They tend the plants. They tune the bones, by which is meant: they sing to them, or near them, in a low liquid tongue that sounds like water moving through a throat. They prune the walls. They plant new towers in the slow way the place requires — a seed set in the lee of a bone, a season of patient waiting, a season of coaxing. They do not seem to mind the wet, which is constant; the place is always wet, always condensing, always dripping, always weeping from somewhere. The standing water is not a feature of the Garden so much as its medium. The Garden is a marsh that has grown up around and into the bones of something older than itself, and the marsh and the bones and the plants and the inhabitants are all one thing, in the way a coral reef is one thing.

At night the light is normal — the same light the place has during the day, only less of it. There is no electricity, in the modern sense. The bones glow a little, in patches, a faint amber that does not travel far. The fronds on the silver towers unfold in the dark and begin to move faster, and the pattern of their movement throws pale shapes across the water that look almost like writing. You can stand in the clearing with the half-submerged dome and watch the shapes and feel that you are being read.

The place is loud. The sound does not stop. Water drips from a million points. The frogs call. The invertebrates pulse. The bones hum, off and on, at frequencies that overlap with the calls of the fauna and sometimes with the speech of the inhabitants. Under it all is the hum, the one you felt in your sternum at the edge — the sound of the place itself, the sound of growth and decay and growth, of the rhizome mat expanding and contracting by millimeters, of the towers settling, of the water moving through channels that were once streets.

The place is turbulent. Things are always being built and always being taken apart. A new shoot will erupt from a buttress overnight and by morning be a wall. A wall will sag and soften over weeks and by season's end be a mulch. A bone will go cold and a clearing will open. A bone will warm and the clearing will close in a year. The inhabitants move through this with a kind of patient attention, like gardeners in a garden that is also a city that is also a single vast organism. They plant. They prune. They wait for the bones to decide.

It is warm. The warmth is held in the water and in the trunks and in the bodies of the fauna and in you, if you stand still long enough. It is the warmth of a place that has been composting itself gently for a very long time, the warmth of the inside of a thing that is finished becoming what it is going to be.

You leave, if you leave, by the same way you came, walking the rhizome mat back to the edge where the mist thins and the sound thins and the smell fades. You carry the hum in your sternum for an hour after. You carry the smell in your clothes for a week. You do not entirely leave the Garden. The Garden has a way of staying with you, of growing in the small dark spaces behind your thoughts, of setting a bone down somewhere you cannot find and waiting to see what grows around it.
