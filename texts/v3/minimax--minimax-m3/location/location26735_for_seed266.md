# The Spires of Halver-Veld

The land arrives first as color: a pale ochre the way the desert takes on a kind of burnt-buttermilk tone when the sun is far overhead and the air is dry enough to sand the edges off any shadow. This is a plateau — the locals, what few there are, call it the Upper Reach, though in the old colonial registers it was logged as the Halver-Veld — and the elevation is the kind that registers in the chest rather than on a map. The air here is half-thick. You notice it on the second breath, not the first. The light is warm but thin-boned; it does not pour so much as sift, falling through a sky the color of old bone that has been polished by decades of dry wind.

The plateau is not flat. It is sliced — by fault and by the patient work of something that is not quite erosion but the local word for it is *grazing*: the wind has eaten at the stone for so long that the surface resembles nothing so much as a herd of low, wind-carved hills grazing in the same direction, all of them leaning a degree or two toward the prevailing draft. Between them run gutters of fine grey powder that look, from a distance, like the dried beds of rivers. They are not. There has never been a river here. There is water, occasionally — a thin rain that comes twice a year if it comes at all — but it evaporates between cloud and ground more often than not, and what does land soaks in and is gone. The earth drinks.

The stone underfoot is layered. The lowest layers, visible where the wind has scored the flanks of the larger hills clean, are a dark banded iron-grey — old volcanic work, compressed and re-cooled, with the cross-hatch of crystallization that says *this was liquid once and was not hurried*. Above that, a paler sedimentary: lime-white, calcium-cream, the kind of stone that rings when you strike it. Above that, a crust. The crust is what makes the place strange. It is a thin, brittle skin of something that is not quite mineral and not quite alive — a kind of crystalline precipitate, glassy and faintly iridescent, that grows in lacework patterns across the highest surfaces where the wind has polished the stone smooth. The locals call it the *bloom*. It does not move. It does not breathe. It is not a plant. But it spreads, very slowly, year by year, by some chemistry no one has quite explained, and the older a stone is, the more of it the stone wears.

There are no trees. There are no grasses. There are no mosses, no lichens, no fungus. The wind has tried, over deep time, to seed something — spores blow in from the lower elevations, sometimes from as far as the coastal bands — but nothing takes root. The bloom is the closest thing to vegetation, and it does not have roots or leaves or any of the apparatus of plant life. It is something else.

## The City

The settlement is called Halver-Veld-Town, though it is more a scattering than a town. It is built *along* the contours of the plateau, not across them — the rule of construction here is that you do not fight the wind, you cohabit with it, and so the streets follow the long axes of the wind-carved hills, running parallel to the prevailing draft, doubling back where the contours fold. The buildings are low and stout, mostly two or three stories, built of the local calcium-stone faced with the dark iron-banded rock for contrast. Some are older — the inner ring, what the locals call the Old Works, has foundations that predate the electrified era by two or three centuries; they were cut, originally, for a smaller and a more desperate population, and their doorways are short and their stairs are steep because the people who cut them were, on average, a hand shorter than the present population. Some are very recent — the smelter rings, the rail-yards, the converter halls, which were built within living memory and have not yet had time to weather into the landscape. They are the only structures that look out of place. They are dark, square, blunt, with the geometry of industrial pragmatism, and they sit on the plateau like iron cubes dropped among bone.

The town is urban in the sense that it has infrastructure, not in the sense that it is dense. Between any two inhabited structures there is almost always a stretch of empty ground — sometimes only a few meters, sometimes a kilometer of bare stone and bloom-encrusted slope. The density of people here is low. The density of *machinery* is not. There are electrified lines that run between the smelters and the converter halls on steel pylons that have been up long enough to grow a kind of patina — not rust, exactly, but a dusty accretion that makes them look, at dusk, like iron trees that have been left out in the weather for a hundred years. There are rail-tracks, narrow-gauge, that thread the plateau's spine. There are pipes. There is the constant low architecture of an industrial settlement that has been running long enough to forget why it started: the converters were built to process the iron-band rock for export down to the lowlands; the smelters refine it; the rail takes it away. The economy of Halver-Veld is the economy of stone. They mine the plateau, they refine the stone, they ship the stone.

But the export tonnage has been falling for forty years. The converters still run, but they run at a third of their rated capacity, and the workers who attend them are fewer than they were. The settlement has the posture of an industrial town in slow convalescence. It is not dead. It is not dying quickly. It is settling into a long equilibrium with the place it sits on, the way a river settles into a bed.

## The Fauna

And yet there are creatures. The plateau is, against all expectation, alive with them.

They are not what you would recognize. They are pale — the color of the calcium stone, with the iridescent dust of the bloom caught in their hides — and they move with the unhurried patience of things that have never had to hurry. The most numerous are the *dust-eaters*, quadrupeds about the size of a small dog, with broad flat muzzles and a digestive system that no one fully understands but which appears to extract nutrients from silicates, carbonates, and the trace iron of the local rock. They graze. They press their muzzles to the stone and they graze, and what they leave behind is a finer powder than what they took in, and that powder is what the bloom grows from. The bloom is, in some sense, their excrement, though it is also something more — it is the precipitate of a digestion that takes weeks, and the creatures themselves seem to feed on the bloom as well as the stone, and the cycle is not fully closed but it is *almost* closed, and the locals have noticed that the dust-eaters are most numerous where the bloom is thickest, and that the bloom is thickest where the dust-eaters are most numerous, and that this has been true for as long as anyone can remember.

The dust-eaters are domesticated, or something near it. They pull small carts. They are used to scour the floors of the converter halls of the dust that accumulates there, and they are fed, in winter, on the slag of the smelters — a paste that is, by all accounts, foul to the human nose but which the creatures eat with evident contentment. They are quiet. They make almost no sound except a slow grinding, which is the sound of their internal stones, and which becomes, after a few days in the town, indistinguishable from the wind.

Other creatures. Long pale lizards with crystalline frills, basking on the iron-grey rocks in the thin warm air. They eat the bloom directly — you can see them licking the stone, slowly, with long pale tongues. There are birds of a kind: thin, pale, with feathers that have the dusty iridescence of the bloom, and they nest on the wind-carved ridges, and they sing, but their song is not a song so much as a single repeated note that sounds almost like a question and almost like a complaint and is, in fact, neither. There are beetles, pale, slow, that roll small spheres of compacted bloom-dust across the stone and bury them. There are worms in the deeper rock, white and very long, that no one sees but that the wind exposes sometimes after a particularly dry season, when the crusts of the surface crack. There are, somewhere far out on the empty parts of the plateau, things that the locals call the *grey watchers* — they have never been seen clearly; only their shapes have been seen, far off, standing on the highest of the wind-carved hills, and they do not appear to move but they are not always in the same place, and no one knows what they are.

The fauna are abundant. This is, against all the usual rules of such things, a populous place. There is more living matter on the Halver-Veld than the plantless, waterless plateau has any right to support. The reason is the bloom. The reason is the dust-eaters. The reason is the closed mineral cycle that has, over deep time, accumulated enough concentrated chemistry to feed a small ecology. It is not a rich ecology. It is a thin one, a sparse one, a slow one. But it is *present*, and it has been present for longer than the city.

## Air, Light, Sound

The air is dry and thin and warm. The temperature does not vary much — there is a difference between summer and winter, but it is the difference between warm and warm, between a daytime high of twenty-eight and a nighttime low of fourteen, and most days the temperature simply holds at twenty-two or twenty-three from dawn to dusk and falls by only a few degrees at night. The sun, when it is up, is warm on the skin. When it goes down, it does not go down gradually; the horizon is high here, and the sun clears the distant peaks cleanly and is gone, and the temperature falls in a single step. The sky is always clear. There are no clouds, or so rarely that their appearance is an event — a single grey sheet that passes over and does not rain and is, by morning, gone.

The wind is the dominant weather. It blows from the west, mostly, but it bends around the larger of the wind-carved hills and eddies in the long gutters, and it carries the fine calcium-dust with it, and that dust gets into everything — into the converters, into the lungs, into the creases of the stone. The wind has been the dominant weather for so long that the very shape of the plateau is its work. The wind is not loud. It is a low steady hush, the sound of air moving over a surface that is not smooth, and after a few days in the town you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

The town, for all its industrial apparatus, is mostly quiet. The converters hum. The smelters, when they are running, have a deep grumble that you feel through the stone underfoot more than you hear through the air. The rail-cars clatter when they pass, but they pass rarely — once or twice a day, on a schedule that has been thinned over the years. The dust-eaters grind. The birds sing their single note. The wind hushes. The bells of the converter halls ring at the start and end of each shift, and their tone carries for miles across the plateau, and that is, for a settlement of this size, almost the loudest sound there is. After dark the town is quiet enough that you can hear, if you are listening, the slow tick of the iron pylons contracting as they cool, which is a sound like a fingernail tapping on glass, multiplied by a hundred.

## The Rules of the Place

A few things hold true here, and the longer you stay the more you feel them as laws rather than facts:

The bloom spreads, slowly. Nothing stops it. The converters scrape it off the stone when they mine, and within a season it is back. The locals consider this either a nuisance or a blessing, depending on who you ask.

The dust-eaters follow the bloom. Where the bloom is thick, the dust-eaters are many. Where the bloom is thin, the dust-eaters are few. The converters, by their mining, locally thin the bloom, and so locally thin the dust-eaters, and so locally leave a kind of wound in the ecology that takes years to close.

The wind wins. Anything left out in it for a season acquires its patina. Anything rigid against it for a century acquires its shape. The town is, in this sense, partly the wind's work; the buildings that have stood longest are the ones whose forms the wind approves of, and the ones that have fallen are the ones that fought it.

The plateau has been here longer than the town has been here. The plateau will be here after the town is gone. The converters will be the first to go — already their foundations are stressed; in another fifty years they will be too costly to maintain. The Old Works, cut from the calcium stone, will outlast them. The bloom will outlast them all. The plateau will settle, eventually, back into its long equilibrium with the wind, and the bloom will cover the foundations of the converters the way moss covers a fallen log, and the dust-eaters will graze across the ruins, and the birds will sing their single note from the iron pylons that have, by then, mostly fallen, and the grey watchers will stand on the same hills they stand on now, and the wind will hush.

This is a slow place. This is a quiet place. This is a place that has been here long enough to have stopped being in a hurry.

This is the Halver-Veld.
