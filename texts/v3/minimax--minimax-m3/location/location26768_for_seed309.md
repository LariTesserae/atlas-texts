The sink.

It opens without warning in the forest floor — a circular wound, sixty meters across at the rim and dropping away in cut terraces for the better part of a hundred meters. From above, walking among the figs and the wild bananas, you don't see it as a hole. You see it as a clearing, a place where the canopy closes over a dim green space and a faint mist breathes up out of it like steam from a buried kettle. The edge is held together by roots and old iron stakes driven at angles, hammered in over years by people who understood that the rim was always trying to slide inward. The forest presses in. The forest has been pressing in for as long as anyone can remember.

You go down on ladders.

Three of them, lashed to iron rungs in the cut walls, each one anchored by a different generation of hands. The rungs are old. The ladders are not. Everything here is layered — built on top of built on top of grown over and rebuilt again. The first ladder is the shortest, the newest, swaying slightly underfoot, smelling of fresh-cut cane. The second is older, its ropes stiff with sap, its rungs dark with the sweat of countless palms. The third — the one that takes you the last thirty meters down — is the oldest by far. Its ropes have been replaced so many times they are palimpsest. The rung you step onto first at the bottom feels like bone.

At the rim, you can still see daylight. By the floor, looking up, the sky is a green coin with a trembling edge of blue. The canopy does not close all the way over the sink. There are gaps — ragged, permanent — through which pale shafts drop down and catch the mist. The mist is constant. The warmth is constant. The dimness is constant.

The walls of the sink are not natural. They were cut, in straight terraces that climb the four sides — five, six, eight levels, depending on which face you count and whether you include the ledges the figs have added. The terraces were once concrete steps, wide enough for carts, ringed by iron handrails that have mostly rusted away or been pulled down for other uses. They are not stairs anymore. They are planters. Every flat tread has become a bed of moss and liverwort. Every riser is furred with fern. In the wider basins between the old handrail posts, fig trees and wild bananas have rooted, their trunks leaning out over the drop, their canopies merging with the canopy above so that the rim and the floor are continuous — a single plant body breathing one breath.

On the floor, at the bottom of the cut, is the settlement.

It is small. A handful of buildings — eight, ten, perhaps a dozen, depending on what you count as a building — arranged around a central yard. They are stone at their cores but most of them have been replaced, room by room, by living architecture. Walls that are curtains of roots, woven tight and packed with mud. Roofs that are layered thatch of palm and fern, so thick that rain takes three seconds to pass through them. Supports that are trunks of trees allowed to grow in place and shaped as they grew — bowed by the load above, forked where forks were needed, buttressed where buttresses were needed. Newer construction is visible in the geometry: squared corners, leveled floors, plumbed walls. But the materials are not what they once were. The construction is a slow accumulation. Rusted iron sheets lashed to lianas. Cut stone stacked without mortar. Machine parts repurposed as lintels. The place has the smell of a workshop inside a greenhouse inside a forest.

Everything is damp. Water finds its way down. Rain from above filters through the canopy and falls in fat drops that hiss on the leaves and on the iron roofs. Condensation beads on every metal surface inside the buildings — on bolts, on hinges, on the rims of old basins. The mist itself settles on hair and cloth and skin. There are places where water stands: shallow pools between the buildings, slick with algae, crossed by fallen logs and the broad green backs of turtles that surface, breathe, and sink again. There are places where water runs — narrow channels cut into the stone, perhaps by human hands once, now widened and maintained by the patient work of roots and the patient work of beetles that excavate mud grain by grain. The channels lead somewhere. Everything here leads to the central yard.

The central yard is where the machines are.

The machines are not all the same age. Some are clearly old — cast-iron housings, brass fittings, heavy flywheels, the patina of a hundred years of use and disuse. Some look newer, salvaged, bolted on, jury-rigged. They occupy a clearing roughly twenty meters across, open to the sky through the canopy gaps, where the dim light is brightest. They are arranged in a rough ring around a central platform of poured concrete, cracked now, with weeds and small palms pushing through.

The central platform holds the engine. Or engines. It is hard to say where one ends and another begins because they have grown into each other — pipework braided, belts looped, a copper rod here, a steel strut there. The biggest piece is a horizontal cylinder — a boiler, or a heat exchanger, or something that started life as one and became the other — set on a brick plinth and gleaming with the wet sheen of constant condensation. It ticks. It hums. A slow pulse, like a heartbeat, that you can feel in the floor through your boots.

Around it, the machines do their various works. There is a pump somewhere that draws water up from a deeper channel and deposits it into a stone trough that runs around the rim of the yard. There is a grinder — a toothed wheel that turns against a stone, fed by what looks like cane stalks piled in a wooden hopper. There is a press, vertical, with a long lever arm that someone or something has weighted with a stack of stones. There is a sort of furnace — a brick dome with a chimney that never quite reaches the canopy; its smoke, which is not really smoke, is a sweet-smelling vapor that joins the general mist. There are smaller things. A rocking arm that dips into a basin and lifts out, dripping. A geared wheel that turns a shaft that turns a smaller wheel that does nothing you can identify but that seems important. A drum that rotates very slowly, half-submerged in a trough, gathering rope or moss or nothing.

None of these machines have operators. None of them have stopped.

The press goes up and down. The grinder turns. The pump pumps. The boiler breathes. The drum rolls.

Who runs them? The question is uncomfortable, down here, because there are no obvious answers. The machines have been running, perhaps, longer than anyone has been alive to remember them stopping. There is a regularity to their motions — not perfect, not clockwork, but a kind of breathing rhythm, faster and slower, day and night, that suggests they are tuned to something. To each other. To the place. To the dimness. To the mist. To the cane that finds its way into the hopper without being carried. To the water that arrives at the pump without being directed. To the salamanders that swim in the trough and seem to keep it from clogging. To the bats that roost in the flywheel spokes and whose droppings, falling, lubricate the bearings. To something, anyway.

In the dim warmth, with the water and the constant hum of the machines, the place is a paradise for creatures. They are not the creatures of the open forest above. They are the creatures of fermentation, of rot, of warmth and damp, of slow processes. Beetles, endless beetles — black ones, metallic ones, ones with horns that meet in the middle. Cockroaches, pale and huge, that scatter from underfoot with the soft papery thunder of their wings. Mosquitoes that hang in gauzy columns above the standing water, bending when you pass through them and reforming behind you. Moths, enormous, that beat against the lamps at night — and there are lamps, hung from wires that stretch between the buildings, old bulbs glowing with a yellowish light that competes weakly with the green daylight, attracting the moths and the mosquitoes and everything else with wings. Salamanders that live in the troughs and the channels, slick and orange, watching with their still faces from the edges of things. Geckos on every vertical surface, head-down, waiting. Tree frogs — hundreds of them, maybe thousands — whose calls layer through the day and night into a single sustained chord that is half the sound of the place. Snakes, at least two species, that hunt in the fern crowns and along the iron beams, tasting the air with quick tongues. Bats, dense swarms of them, that roost in the gaps between the machines and emerge at dusk through the canopy holes to feed, filling the upper air with their high-pitched click and chatter. Birds — a few, mostly owls — that come down through the holes at night and sit on the rims of the chimneys, low hooting occasionally, dramatically.

And under the feet. Things in the soil. Things in the leaf litter. Things you cannot see, that rustle and tick and move.

The fauna is not incidental. It is structural. The beetles excavate the channels that carry water. The bats fertilize the fig trees with their droppings. The salamanders eat the larvae that would otherwise eat the moss. The owls eat the rats. The rats eat the cane that spills from the hopper. The cane feeds the grinder. The grinder feeds the press. The press, somehow, feeds the boiler. The boiler feeds the pump. The pump feeds the trough. The trough feeds the channels. The channels feed the pools. The pools feed the mosquitoes. The mosquitoes feed the bats. The bats feed the owls. The owls feed the snakes. The snakes feed — no one knows what the snakes feed, but they are sleek, and they are calm.

The place is a closed circuit. An ecosystem balanced on top of an industrial skeleton, running on its own fuel.

It is strange. You can feel that. The strangeness is not in any one thing — not the machines, not the animals, not the plants, not the mist. It is in the way they all belong to each other. The way nothing is out of place. The way the place seems to have decided, long ago, what it is, and to have settled into being it.

Light filters down. Pale green shafts, soft-edged, moving slowly as the sun moves above the canopy. Where they fall on the walls of the buildings, the moss is a deeper green, almost blue. Where they fall on the floor of the yard, they illuminate the mist, which scatters them further, so that the place seems to glow from inside as well as from above. The lamps add their own yellowish light, flickering faintly in time with the engine's pulse, never quite enough to read by, just enough to draw the moths.

And there is another light, lower down. On the underside of stones, in the crooks of roots, in the wet seams of the brick plinth — a faint green glow, the foxfire of luminous fungus. It pulses, very slowly, out of sync with the engine, in its own rhythm. It makes the shadows move when nothing else is moving. It makes the machines look, in certain moments, as if they are breathing light.

The sound is a tide. It rises. It falls. It never stops.

The boiler ticks and hums — a low rhythmic pulse that you feel in your chest as much as you hear with your ears. The grinder grinds — a continuous rasp that shifts in pitch as the cane catches and releases. The press goes up and down with a hydraulic breath — a long slow sigh, like the bellows of a sleeping animal. The pump pumps — a constant liquid murmur, wet and patient. Water drips everywhere — from every iron surface, from every leaf, a thousand tiny ticks that layer into a kind of rain. The frogs chorus — a wall of trills and peeps and croaks, individual voices indistinguishable in the mass. The insects hum — individually nothing, collectively a sustained drone that sits underneath everything else, the bass note. The bats echolocate — clicks and chatter, sharp and tiny, audible only when they pass close. The owls — low hoots, occasional, dramatic, punctuation. The wind in the canopy above — a constant hush, the highest note. Something, occasionally, screams — short, sharp, animal, and gone.

You cannot hear yourself think here. The sound is not chaotic. It is layered. Each layer has its place. Together they form something like a chord, or a tide — rising and falling, but never stopping, never silent.

The warmth is body-warm. It rises from the machines, from the compost of leaves and droppings and slow rot, from the breath of the mist. It is not uncomfortable, except that it never lets up. There is no cool moment, no relief. You do not shiver here. You sweat, gently, all the time.

The damp is in everything. Your boots grow heavy. Your clothes grow heavy. The air itself is heavy, water-laden, breathing you in as much as you breathe it. Iron rusts in days here, not years. Paper, brought down from above, goes limp and fuzzy in an afternoon. Leather softens until you can dent it with a thumb.

There were people here, once. There may still be people. But you cannot tell. You find a hammock strung between two posts, the rope worn smooth at the grip. You find a bowl on a stone shelf, crusted with the dried remains of whatever last filled it. You find a shirt drying on a line, the cloth dark with moisture, the stitching hand-done. You find a knife on a step, its blade green with verdigris. You find no one to claim any of it. The place has not been abandoned, exactly. The place has been — *continued*. Continued without interruption, by everything that lives in it. Continued by the machines. Continued by the animals. Continued by the plants. Continued by the mist.

Somewhere in the back, behind the press, behind a curtain of woven roots that you almost don't notice as a wall, there is a small room. A machine in it turns a shaft that turns a small generator that feeds the lamps. The machine has been turning for as long as anyone can remember. It has no fuel tank. It has no operator. It has, on the wall above it, a chalk mark that was once a number, now just a smudge. The room smells of warm metal and ozone.

If you stand still in that room long enough, you begin to feel that the place is watching you. Not hostile. Not curious. Just — settled. In its own state. Long existed. Long weathered. Long balanced.

The sink breathes. The machines breathe. The fauna breathes. The plants breathe. The light moves. The tide of sound rises and falls.

It is a small place. A hamlet inside a cathedral inside a forest inside a hole. It is in balance. It is warm and dim and damp and loud and alive and strange. It does not need you. It does not mind you. It will go on breathing when you climb back up the three ladders and out onto the rim, where the figs lean in and the canopy closes and the daylight — too bright, too dry, too cool — feels suddenly like a foreign country.
