A high plateau runs to a haze in every direction. The horizon is low and never quite resolves; the eye travels out across a vastness of stone and stops at a soft dark line where the warm dim air meets itself. The sun here is small and red and far. Even at midday it has the quality of a smudge — russet light, smoke-colored shadows. Things on the plateau have learned to be subtle in color: iron, rust, ash, the brown-black of wet rock.

The warmth does not come from the sun. It comes from below. The geology underneath is alive in its slow way — churning, digesting, breathing heat up through cracks in the surface. This is why the wind that moves across the plateau has weight to it. Why the stone sweats. Why the air feels like it has been breathed.

There is not much rain. There is dampness. Condensation gathers in the concavities the erosion has spent a long time cutting — broad shallow bowls, long gutters along fault lines, pockets where the slope folds in on itself. These hold water. Dark water, warm water, still water, sometimes an inch deep, sometimes deep enough to disappear into. Around the rims of these pools, a thin black lichen takes hold. Apart from this, the plateau is bare stone, scoured, in places polished.

Things move across the stone. They are the abundance.

They are also, in some sense, the industry.

The herds are the closest thing to a population the plateau carries. They move in loose formations — sometimes dozens, sometimes hundreds — drifting on slow migrations that braid and re-form and have never been fully mapped. Their bodies have the heft and the hungers of animals. They breathe. They lower their great heads to the stone and seem to feed on it — a mineral, a heat, a chemical trace that comes up through the rock. The rock comes away slightly polished where they have passed, and over long time their paths are the paths of the erosion. The plateau is shaped by them as much as by wind and water, and this is what "established" means here: the system has settled into itself, the herds and the geology wearing each other slowly into a stable shape.

What makes them industrial is the metal. They are fitted — whether by themselves or by some prior hand, it is impossible to tell, and the question is moot — with hardware that does not look biological. Vents along their flanks that breathe thin curls of vapor. Cylindrical housings on their spines that tick and click as they walk. Legs that end in piston joints. Occasional shapes that look, from a distance, like exhaust stacks, though what is exhausted is unclear; the vapor dissolves into the warm damp air without trace. Some of the older animals have accreted more of this hardware than the younger ones. The pattern is generational, and slow.

It is mostly quiet. The metal ticks. The herds, taken together, give off a low hum that you only notice when you stop noticing the wind. Sometimes an animal will make a sound — a slow, mechanical groan, like a piston tested to its limit — but these are private-seeming, infrequent. The louder thing is the wind, and the wind is patient and soft. There is the lap of condensation running down a slope into a pool. There is the far-off tick of a herd moving out of sight. There is the almost-inaudible bass note of the geology below, felt in the knees more than heard with the ears.

The dimness is consistent. Even at the brightest part of the day the shadows are soft and the distances are unclear. A herd a kilometer off might be twenty animals or fifty; it is hard to commit. The plateau has the feel of a place seen through smoked glass, and the herds themselves seem to belong to the glass — moving shapes, half-glimpsed, neither fully material nor fully mechanical.

There are oases in miniature. The cracks where warm damp air leaks up from below. In these places the black lichen thickens, and a different moss grows — rust-red, almost furry — and a smaller creature sets up: low to the ground, quick, many-legged, with its own small array of vents, humming to itself in a register just below the wind. Tall standing stones rise here and there — remnants of harder rock that resisted the erosion — and the herds use them for navigation, traveling from one to the next across the empty kilometers, never quite arriving.

The plateau knows itself. The sun does not change it much. The herds do not change it fast. It is what it is, and has been, for a long time. The industrial quality — the metal, the vents, the soft exhaust — is not an imposition on the place. It is what grew here. The fauna became engines because engines were a viable life on a warm, dim, damp, stone plateau with abundant nothing to eat but the rock itself.

There are no walls. No roads. No towers. The herds are not led or driven; they have their own pacing, their own logistics. If you stand very still on the plateau for long enough, the herds will walk around you as if you were another stone. This is the closest thing to a rule of the place: stand still, and you become part of it.
