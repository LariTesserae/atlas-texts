# The Greenblack Low

The land is low. You can tell because the water never finishes going anywhere. It gathers in the seams and sags of the basalt shelves, in the footprints left by things heavier than you, in the bowls between roots, and it just stays there, trembling with its own surface tension, breeding small things that swim in vertical columns and never touch the bottom.

The basalt itself is young. You can see where the columns broke off in ragged hexagonal teeth, sharp as broken bottles, black as the inside of an oven that has only just this morning been allowed to cool. Rainwater sits on the flat tops of these columns in perfect circles and never evaporates because the canopy has already drunk most of the heat out of the air. The columns hold the water like a saint holds a relic: with great seriousness, and without spilling.

---

The forest is not a forest in the way that word usually means. It is more like an interior. The canopy has closed over the lowlands at a height of maybe forty meters, sometimes less where the figs have grown sideways over a sinkhole and decided that sideways was good enough. Below it, everything is a dusk that never quite becomes night. The light that makes it down has been chewed on by a hundred thousand leaves and arrives at eye-level as a green so deep it is almost its own color, a color with weight, a color that presses on the back of your hand when you hold it out.

Things grow on top of things. Trees grow on trees. Ferns grow on the trees that grow on trees. A kind of strangling fig begins as a bird's dropped seed in the crook of a branch and ends as a hemisphere of woven roots that has quietly replaced the host tree's trunk. You cannot always tell which trunks are alive and which are scaffolds for something else. The forest is dense in a way that is not so much packed as *layered* — each horizontal stratum is its own small climate, its own weather, its own hour of the simulated day. Beetles and frogs live in the canopy stratum and have never seen the ground. Other beetles and other frogs live at knee height and have never climbed.

---

The ground is not the ground. The ground is a guess the forest makes. Step and your boot breaks through two inches of dead leaves into a soft spongy rot, and then through that into something fibrous and wet, and then perhaps into standing water, and then into clay, and then — depending on where you stepped — into the roof of a burrow, and a small pale face appears in the puncture you have made and stares at you with the utter blankness of something that has never had a thought. You have not so much walked as perforated a thing that was trying to be asleep.

The water in these low places is not blue. It is tea. It is the color of a strong brew that has been forgotten on a counter for a week, and it is warm, and a faint slick of pollen or something pollen-adjacent sits on its surface in patterns that resemble nothing. If you crouch, you can see, a hand's depth down, a slow drift of pale shapes — tadpoles, or shrimp, or something in between, something the local taxonomy has not yet decided what to call — moving in the same direction, always the same direction, a migration so slow it could be mistaken for stillness. They are following a smell.

---

The sound is the first thing and the last thing. It does not stop. There is no moment in this place when you can hear your own breathing, and there is no moment when the sound pauses to suggest it might, one day, stop.

Above: a sustained hiss, like a kettle that has been on too long. This is the canopy in light wind, and the insects in the canopy, and the small green frogs that live in the bromeliads and call from inside them so the call comes out distorted into something like a wet finger dragged around the rim of a glass.

At mid-height: a chaos of whistles, clicks, hoots. A bird, somewhere, performs a phrase of three notes and then is answered by what sounds like the same bird a half-kilometer away, and then a third one further still, so the phrase travels across the lowlands like a rumor. Other things make noises that are not calls but mechanical results of living — the slow crack of a branch that has finally given up, the wet slap of something hitting water from height.

Below: a hum. A genuine low-frequency hum that you feel in the sternum before you hear it. This is the aggregate of millions of things vibrating, each at its own small frequency, and they do not synchronize, and the result is a chord that never repeats.

---

The animals are everywhere. They are not shy. A thing like a lizard but with a sail of skin along its back, bright orange, the color of a warning that no one in this forest seems to have learned, sits on a stone and watches you with one eye. It does not run. You are not, to it, a category that exists. A column of ants, each one the size of a thumbnail, crosses the path on a front two meters wide, and they are carrying something white and soft that was once a frog, and they are in no hurry. A pale moth the size of your spread hand clings to the underside of a leaf and breathes so slowly you can watch the air move in and out of it. A spider with legs that span a saucer has built a web between two trunks that are three meters apart, and the web is the strongest thing in the forest, stronger than the basalt, stronger than the water, and it catches everything that flies and holds it until the spider, who is patient, comes for it.

The animals do not behave as if they fear you because you are an animal. They behave as if you are a weather event.

---

The place is young in the geological sense and old in no sense at all. The basalt is fresh. The waterfalls that pour off the edges of the shelves are still cutting them back, still slicing the columns into new shapes, and on the freshly exposed faces you can sometimes see the marks where water has found a weakness and begun its patient excavation, a future collapse already inscribed in the rock. New islands of vegetation are appearing in the streambeds — a fallen tree catches sediment, a fern colonizes the sediment, more sediment accumulates, in two centuries there will be a hummock, in twenty thousand years a hill, and by then everything that lives here now will be a smear in the clay.

The dynamic is real. You can see it. A section of bank slips into a river in a slow-motion avalanche of roots and mud, and the water browns for an hour. A tree, heavy with epiphytes, leans past the angle of recovery and goes over with a crack that travels through the forest and silences, briefly, a quadrant of the sound, until the insects realize the new opening in the canopy is letting in light and they swarm toward it. A bloom of something opens overnight along the river — pale, four-petaled, smelling of a sweetness that is almost chemical — and the next morning the air is full of a different bee, a different wasp, things drawn from kilometers away by a smell you can still taste on your tongue.

---

The rules are simple, and they are not yours. Do not stand still for long. Do not sleep on the ground. Drink the water only if you have a way to strain it, and even then do not drink much. Do not mistake stillness for safety; in this forest, the stillest surfaces are where the most is happening, because the eye registers stillness as emptiness, and emptiness is an invitation. Wear something on every inch of skin. Do not look up into the canopy for more than a moment, because if you look up and see what is looking down, you will not stop looking, and you will be standing still for long.

---

And there is a small strangeness, a 1.3 out of 3, a slightness that lodges in you and will not leave. It is this: the leaves, when they fall, do not always fall in the direction of the wind. Sometimes they rise. A leaf detaches from a branch and begins to descend, and halfway down it pauses, and then it begins, very gently, to climb back. It climbs until it finds a branch and sticks to the underside of it, where it is not supposed to be, and it stays there, and slowly it begins to root, and it becomes a thing growing upside down in the dark. The forest is full of these. If you part the hanging roots of a fig, you find leaves that are pointed away from the sky, growing away from the light, in a direction no leaf is supposed to grow, and they are green, and they are alive, and they are doing something no textbook has described. Nobody knows why. The locals, if there were locals, would not call it strange. They would call it the way the leaves do.
