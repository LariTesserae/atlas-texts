The valley is a low thing, lower than the country around it, and the country around it is the same kind of valley, only flatter, and beyond that, the same kind of country, and so on, until you are not sure where the valley ends and the plain begins, or whether the whole region is one large shallow depression that has been filled, in a patient and unmoving way, with matter.

Everything here is made. There is no natural ground to be seen. The floor of the valley is a continuous pavement of dark, flat stones, fitted close, some of them worn smooth, some of them split, some of them replaced by a brick of pale, raw clay that is still drying. The walls are walls, and the towers are towers, and the channels are channels, and the standing pool in the central plaza is a pool, and the water in it is water, and the rain, when rain comes, falls on stone it did not fall on before, and is caught, and runs. Nothing is not made. The place is a thing that has been put together by hands, and the hands have been at it for a long time, and they are still at it, and the place has the quality of an object that is being assembled while you look at it and disassembled while you look at it, and the two motions are about equal, and neither is in a hurry.

The light is poor. The sky is low and the same color as the stone, and what light comes through it is the color of a tarnished spoon — enough to make out shapes, not enough to make out faces. In the brightest part of the day, if there is a brightest part, a thin band of paler gray slides along the tops of the tallest towers, and the upper courses of slate glow like wet coal for a few minutes, and the rest of the valley is darker by contrast. Then it goes. The valley lives mostly in the dimness. The slate is dark, the brick is dark, the mortar is dark, the standing water is black, the channels in shadow are black, the channels in half-light are a slow glint. The shapes of the place are made out by the dimness, not by light. A person walking through at noon can lose the path between two walls and not know which way the center is.

The damp is everywhere, and it does not fall. It hangs on every surface in a thin skin. It beads on the slate, swells, and drops in a slow irregular percussion into the channels. It darkens the lower courses of every wall. It softens the freshest clay so that a thumb pressed into it will leave a print that holds its shape. The air is cool, the kind of cool that sits at the back of the throat and in the lungs, and the damp rides the cool, and the place smells of stone and of wet clay and of something faintly sweet, like old wood or like the inside of a cellar that has been closed for a long time and then opened.

The valley is loud if you stand still. The sounds are mostly low, but there are many of them, and they layer. The drip from every surface, falling into every channel, at slightly different intervals, makes a continuous soft random rain, even on a day when no rain is falling. Beneath the drip is the grinding: a low continuous shifting of stone against stone, the slow breathing of the place as it settles and resettles in the damp. The channels carry a small constant trickle that, where it meets a lip or a step, makes a brighter note. The air moves through the narrow gaps between the towers with a sound like wind in a chimney, but there is no wind; it is the air of the valley itself, finding its way down, finding its way up. From the standing pool in the central plaza comes a slow, very low tone, almost a hum, as if the water were singing to itself, or as if the ground beneath it were. And beneath all of these, deeper than any of them, a rhythmic thud, far apart, like the footfall of something very large walking somewhere out of sight, and the thud rolls through the stone and can be felt in the soles of the feet and in the teeth.

The structures are dense and layered. The towers are of dark slate, laid in thin courses, separated by thick bands of mortar that vary from wall to wall: this band has straw in it, that band has crushed shell, the next band has nothing but clay and water. The pattern in the walls is a herringbone of dark brick, the bricks narrow, the bricks long, fitted close. The pattern in the floor is a grid of flat stone. The pattern in the channels is a smooth pale clay, washed often, sloping gently toward the central pool. Each pattern is the work of hands. Each brick was set by a hand. Each slab was held in place by a hand until the mortar began to set. The hands were not all the same kind of hand. The patterns change as you walk, and the changes are abrupt, and a person who knows mortar can read the change as a date, or as a maker, or as a turn of mind.

The valley is under construction. The newest walls are the brightest: a pale, almost yellow clay laid in fresh courses against the dark older brick, still soft, still taking the print of a thumb. The newest bricks are not yet dry. The newest channels are not yet scoured dark. The oldest walls are losing their courses: a corner has fallen here, a cap has slid off there, a herringbone has slumped into a slope. The valley is being built and unbuilt at the same time, and the two motions are roughly equal, and the place is not a ruin. It is a place that has not yet decided what it wants to be when it is finished, and may never finish, because the hands that built it are mostly gone, and the hands that remain are few, and the place is patient, and the damp is patient, and the sound is patient, and time is the least of the materials.

The rare creatures of the valley are small, dark, and quick. Something rustles in the channels, gone before the eye can find it. Something sits on the cap of a leaning tower and watches with a single wet eye and then is not there. A pale thing moves once across the central plaza, low to the ground, and is gone into a gap in a wall. The creatures have no names the valley knows. They do not need the valley to be more than it is: a place of damp, of dimness, of many small gaps, of many small sounds, of slow patient making. The valley does not need them, either. It continues with or without them.

It is a strange valley. The dimness, the damp, the cool, the layered sound, the slow building, the slow unbuilding — all of it is happening at once, and none of it is in a hurry, and none of it is waiting for a person to come and look. A person could stand in the central plaza and hear the water, and the stone, and the air, and the far thing walking, and feel the damp gather on the back of the hand, and not know whether the place was new or old, or whether it had been finished, or whether finishing was the point. The valley is denser than the country around it, and cooler, and damper, and darker, and louder in a low way, and more made, and more under construction, and more full of slow small strange life. It is the kind of place that, once you have stood in it, you cannot quite put out of your mind, even after you have walked out of it and into the flatter country beyond, and even though the country beyond is made of the same kind of matter, only less of it, only less loud.
