The cirque opens like a cupped hand. Eleven thousand feet, give or take — high enough that the sky doesn't quite sit on the horizon but bends down to meet the walls of stone, so the whole valley is a basin of light. The sun is unfiltered. There is no haze, no canopy, no cloud cover at this hour, and the air is so thin it seems almost to vibrate in the lungs before it cools. Cool it is, but the cool is dry-cool at first, before the water touches skin.

Every inch of the floor is occupied.

There is no soil here. There is no moss, no lichen creeping along the rock, no grass that might soften a footprint. There is only the array — thousands upon thousands of basalt columns standing upright, packed shoulder to shoulder in the patient hexagonal jostle that lava learns as it cools. They begin at the lower walls of the cirque and march inward toward the middle, and they are not weathered to pebbles or dunes. They are not boulders tumbled into ruin. They stand as they stood, perfect in their geometry, the joints between them hair-thin cracks that admit neither root nor blade. Each column is roughly the height of a man, sometimes two men, sometimes much more — varying widths, varying heights, a forest of dark stone trunks with no crowns. Between them, at the level of the foot, runs water.

Everywhere, water.

It wells from somewhere above — from the snowfields on the upper cliffs that never quite vanish in any season, or from the cloud itself when the cloud settles into the bowl at evening, or from springs that have been running since the rock was formed — and it threads downward through the packed columns in a million parallel sheets and threads and films. It does not pool into lakes. The density of the columns forbids any standing body to form; the water finds its way only by sliding, by beading, by the thin capillary insistence of a film across polished surfaces. So the whole valley is moving-water over standing-stone, and the effect on the ear is —

— is the sound.

This is what you hear when you enter. A roar. Not a roar of single source, not a falls, not a torrent, not any one identifiable throat of water shouting into a canyon. A roar that is composed, instead, of millions of tiny voices, each one the trickle over one more column, the spill from one more joint, the sheet-glass rush of a film down a single face of basalt. Layered and layered and layered, the trickles accumulate into a continuous wall of sound that does not crest or die down. It is the sound of equilibrium. It does not get louder when it rains, because the rain adds almost nothing to what is already there; it does not get quieter in dry spells, because the springs are steady. It is, in this valley, simply the air made water-shaken and delivered to the ear.

The light catches this.

Each wet face of basalt reflects the sun, and the columns are so densely set that the reflections form a shimmer across the whole interior of the cirque, as if the stone were sweating light. The reflections are mobile only in a very small way: the water films shift, the light flickers by fractions, but the overall brightness is constant. Walk into this valley on a clear morning and the glare is almost painful — you squint against a field of small bright blades, ten thousand little suns leaning every which way. The walls above the columns, where the cliffs rise toward the snowfields, are bare grey rock, also wet, also gleaming, also layered with thin water, but darker because the angle is wrong for full reflection. Up where the snow begins the light turns white and diffuse.

There is no wind here that you can feel. There may be wind above — certainly there must be — but inside the packed columns the air moves in slow currents drawn down by the water and the temperature difference between the bright upper cliffs and the wet floor. The sound fills any gaps between these slow currents. Nothing flaps, nothing sways, because nothing has anything flexible to give. The only motion in the valley is the descent of the water and the small movements of light it carries with it.

A few things live here, but they are few.

A pale lichen, almost invisible, in the very cracks of the upper walls — not really plant-like, more a stain that has organized itself slightly. A kind of black fly that lives on the water film, lays eggs in it, skates on it; you see them in the stiller channels where two columns lean to make a small pocket of slack water. They make almost no sound over the roar. A larger thing — a small mammal, perhaps, or something between mammal and amphibian — that haunts the spring mouths where water emerges cleanest, and that darts back into the joints when shadow passes. Birds occasionally cross the cirque, very high, casting no shadow down among the columns; their calls are stolen whole by the roar and absorbed.

None of these have altered the place. They are guests.

The valley has been in this state for a long time. The erosion has found its level: the columns stand at the height they were left by the original cooling, the joints are open to exactly the width that admits water and no more, the floor beneath them is itself columnar and lies a few meters down like a second forest tipped on its side — the fallen trunks of a generation of stone. The water finds no new paths and forgets no old ones. The spring mouths stay where they stay. The columns fall only very rarely — once in decades — and when one falls it makes a great single crash that the roar does not quite cover, and then the column lies there, beginning the very slow business of being worn back into roundness by the water film, which will take centuries.

The weirder thing is this, and it takes a while to notice.

The columns are not all the same height. In a normal basalt formation they would be — the cooling front moves through at a fairly steady rate and leaves a fairly uniform result. Here, the heights vary. And the variation is not random. The columns step down toward the center of the cirque in long curved bands, so that from above the valley floor reads as a series of concentric terraces, each terrace one or two column-heights below the next, a kind of frozen amphitheater built of dark hexagons and trickling water. The geometry is too regular to be accident and too large to be design. It is the kind of thing a glacier might leave, except there is no sign of a glacier now, and the columns themselves are not scratched. It is the kind of thing a lava flow cooled into, if a lava flow cooled with a plan. Most people who come here, after the first hour of squinting and listening, begin to look at the terraces and stop talking.

There is nothing else to say. The place is what it is. Bright, deafening, cool, wet, packed full of dark hexagonal stone in its hundreds of thousands, very occasionally broken by the small black flies or by the soft splash of a falling column far away on the upper terrace. The snowfields above feed the springs. The springs feed the films. The films feed the roar. The roar feeds nothing back. The system is closed.

You can stand in this valley for an hour and not be able to hear your own voice. You can stand in it for a day and find that you have begun to sleep well, because the body finally stops trying to listen. You can stand in it for a week and find that the rare events — the falling column, the bird that does come down, the occasional sun that goes behind a rare passing cloud and dims the whole shimmer by a factor — feel monumental. The place does not change. You begin to understand, standing in it, that the place has been waiting, and will go on waiting, for the columns to wear down at last into sand and the water to spread into an actual lake on an actual flat. That is some time off. There is no urgency in the cirque about this. The cirque does not hold its breath. The cirque does not hold anything. The water is on its way. The light is on its way. The roar is already there.
