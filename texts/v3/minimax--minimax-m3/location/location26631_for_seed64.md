# The Compressor Garden

The chamber is roughly the size of a chapel, and it is full.

Not full the way a room is full when too many people stand in it — full the way a lung is full, every cubic centimeter accounted for, no vacancy anywhere for air that hasn't already been breathed and rebreathed by the walls themselves. You enter through a hatch, if you can find it, set into a flank of pale granite that goes up and up beyond the reach of any weather. The granite is the color of old bone. Up close, in the banded light that spills from somewhere inside, you can see the rock has been cut — not recently, not recently at all — and the cut marks are filled with the same growth that fills everything else.

The first thing is the smell. Not green — nothing green grows here, nothing green has grown here in any meaningful sense — but something older, drier, more mineral. The smell of hot solder that has gone cold, of iron filings left out in fog, of the dust you find inside an unplugged refrigerator. It has a sweetness underneath, a kind of perfumed dust, like the inside of a long-sealed cabinet where someone once kept dried flowers. The cold flowers have outlived their keeper. The cabinet has been running on its own fumes ever since.

The second thing is the sound.

The chamber is loud. The chamber has been loud for a very long time, and the loudness has nothing left to give. It is the loudness of a single broken machine that has worn through its failure mode and come out the other side into something that resembles function. A compressor somewhere — beneath the floor, behind the wall, threaded through every pipe — turns over and over at a fixed rhythm, two and a half beats a second, never faltering, never quickening. Higher harmonics layer over it: a thin hiss from a thousand places at once, like steam escaping from a field of pinpricks, never quite resolving into a single source. Then lower, almost below hearing, a thrumming that you feel in your ankles first, a vast subsonic pressure that suggests something the size of a cathedral is breathing somewhere just out of sight.

None of it changes. That is the strange thing, the thing that makes a person stand very still in the hatch and not move for a while. None of it has changed, possibly, for a hundred years.

---

The growth is what fills the chamber.

It is packed as tight as coral, dense as a stuffed mattress, every inch of floor, wall, and ceiling claimed by it. There are no paths. There are no passages. The thing about scale here is that the chamber is small — you could throw a stone from one wall to the other, if you could find the wall — but the density is so absolute that what you actually see, when you let your eyes adjust, is a forest compressed to the thickness of moss. A forest that has been pressed, like a flower in a book, into the volume of a cupboard.

The growth is not one thing. It is many things, or it is one thing pretending to be many. Up close, individual elements can be picked out, though picking them out is hard because they merge into each other at every seam.

There are fans. Fans of a stiff material the color of tarnished brass, ribbed along their length, that rise in broad flat sprays from every horizontal surface. They are not leaves — they have no veins, no chlorophyll, no give — but they grow like leaves, in rosettes, in spirals, always oriented to the same unseen source of dim illumination. When the air moves from the compressor's turning they shiver, and the shiver runs through the whole chamber in waves, a soft silver migration of reflected light across a million brass fans at once.

There are stalks. Tall — taller than they have any right to be, taller than the ceiling ought to allow, taller than physics in this volume should permit — but they are not standing tall. They have folded over, knotted, braided themselves into ropes and mats and hanging swags. Some have found the ceiling and rooted into it, growing upward by growing down, an entire grove suspended from the roof like a sea of stalactites. They are the color of dried tobacco, of weak tea, of the inside of a beehive. They are warm to the touch, faintly warm, the warmth of compost or of an idling engine.

There are pods. Hard-shelled, walnut-sized, clustered in grape-like bunches along the stalks, hundreds of thousands of them, every one closed. They never open. They have never opened. They are full of something dense and quiet — you can tell because they are heavier than they look, and because sometimes, when you put your ear to one, you can hear a faint ticking inside, a tiny replica of the compressor's rhythm, as if each pod contains its own miniature version of the whole machine. They do not fall. They have not fallen. They are held by the stalks with the grip of something that has learned exactly how much force it needs and no more.

There are threads. Silvery, copper-colored, dark as dried blood. The threads run everywhere, through everything, in and out of the fans and the stalks and the pods, binding them all into a single mat. Some of the threads are still recognizable as wires — they have insulation on them, or they once did, cracked and peeled now, the colors faded to a uniform gray — and some of them are clearly not wires at all but the living threads of the growth itself, indistinguishable from the dead ones except that they pulse, faintly, with the same two-and-a-half-beats-a-second.

This is how the growth eats.

The threads are in the machines. The machines are in the threads. There is no longer any difference.

---

The machines are partial.

That is the strangest part — or the part that is strangest until you have been in the chamber long enough for the strangeness to shift. The machines are partial because the machines are being eaten, slowly, by the threads, and what remains of them is only the parts the threads have not yet needed. A console rises out of the mat on the far wall — half a console, the upper half — its lower half buried in a tide of brass fans that have grown over it like barnacles over a wreck. The screen is dark. The keys are gone — eaten, replaced with rounded buttons of plant-stuff that fit the holes exactly, the same color, the same weight as the missing plastic. A label above the console is still legible: **MAINTAIN PRESSURE — 1.4 ATM**. The label is in two languages. The second language has been eaten.

A pipe runs along the ceiling, heavy, sweating a faint perpetual dew that is not water — it smells of ether, of something between alcohol and kerosene — and where the drops fall, the fans grow thickest, and where the fans grow thickest, the pipe is thinnest, eaten from below. The pipe has been repaired. No — the pipe has been *grown*. The thinned section has been thickened from inside by a sleeve of woody fibers that match the stalks exactly, and the sleeve is in turn being eaten by the threads on its outer surface. The whole system is balanced. The pipe feeds the growth. The growth feeds on the pipe. The growth replaces the pipe. The pipe is eaten by the growth. The growth is fed by the pipe.

There has been no net change in a long time.

There are other fragments. A motor, vertical, half its casing missing, its shaft spinning forever at the compressor's rhythm, the shaft wrapped now in a sleeve of fibers that turns with it. A bank of gauges, their faces clouded, their needles frozen at angles that mean specific things to someone who once read them — 1.4, 0.9, COOL, HOLD, HOLD, HOLD. A chair, or what was a chair, the metal frame intact, the seat and back eaten and replaced with a thick cushion of the tobacco-colored stalks, woven tight as a basket, sprung to the weight of whoever last sat in it. Whoever last sat in it was eaten too, eventually. The calcium remains, somewhere in the threads.

There is no skeleton. There is never a skeleton in these places. The threads take the phosphorus first.

---

The light is dim and never changes.

It comes from a single panel set into the far wall, a long rectangle of some older kind of illumination — not LEDs, not fluorescent, but the thick greenish glow of something chemical, something that should have run out years ago and has not. The panel is cracked in three places and the cracks glow brighter than the rest, as if the breakage is what keeps the chemistry going. The light falls on the brass fans and makes them flicker when the air moves. The light falls on the pods and does not enter them. The light falls on the floor of the chamber and the floor of the chamber is not visible — only the lower surfaces of the fans that have grown there, facing upward, holding what dim photons they can against the general dark.

It is cool. Cooler than it ought to be at this depth in the mountain, or wherever the chamber is — the chamber is in a mountain, that much is clear from the granite outside the hatch and the silence that the compressor's hum has long since replaced. Cool as a wine cellar. Cool as the inside of a stone in spring. The growth does not seem to mind. The growth does not seem to notice. The growth is at its working temperature and has been at its working temperature for so long that the working temperature has become the temperature of the rock.

There is no animal life. There is no animal life possible. There is nothing to eat that is not the chamber itself, and the chamber has reached the point where eating itself would mean the loss of the system that allows eating to continue. The threads know this. The fans know this. The pods, ticking softly in their bunches, know this.

---

Weirdness is a word that does not apply here, because weirdness is a word for things that have not yet been understood, and the chamber has been understood for a very long time — by itself, if by no one else. The chamber is an equilibrium. The chamber is a closed system. The chamber is a lung that breathes its own exhalation, a heart that pumps its own blood, a stomach that has learned to digest only itself, slowly, in such small amounts that the loss is replenished by the eating of the next thing in line.

The compressor turns. The threads thicken. The fans shiver in their millions. The pipe sweats its not-water dew. The pods tick their small ticks. The gauges hold their HOLD. The light glows its cracked green glow. The man in the chair, what is left of him, holds his pose at the console with the kind of stillness that comes from having nowhere left to go.

Nothing changes.

Nothing has changed.

Nothing will change, until the pipe finally gives, or the panel finally fails, or the threads finally finish the motor, and then — in a single slow afternoon — it will all become rock again, and the chamber will be quiet for the first time in centuries, and a hiker on the mountainside above will pause, and not know why, and walk on.

In the meantime, the compressor turns.
