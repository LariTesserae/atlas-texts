A shelf of mountain holds this place. High, narrow, the way land holds itself together where it shouldn't — a saddle between two higher crags, a flattened ridge that the winds pass over without tearing at. The shelf is barely wide enough for the stones and the things between them, but it holds.

The settlement crosses the shelf in a band, like a belt tightened around the mountain. Stone houses, low, some of them barely taller than a person standing on another's shoulders. They are built shoulder to shoulder, the way stones settle into each other when there isn't room to spread. Their walls are the same stone the shelf is made of — gray, lichen-mottled, fine-grained — and the eye has to work to find where the mountain ends and the building begins. There is a forge at one end of the row, marked only by a soot-stain above a small stone chimney. A wooden door, hand-forged hinges, a shutter of scraped leather that lets in a dull yellow light when it is closed. These are the signs of people, but small ones, easy to miss.

Dampness everywhere, but not the dampness of recent rain. The dampness of long presence. The walls darken at their bases, streaked where water has moved through them slowly over years. Moss — green, but a deep muted green, the color of patience — colonizes every seam between stones, every crack in the shelf, every joint where wood meets rock. Where a roof channel has cracked or come loose, a thin line of seepage has worn itself a path down a wall face and into the ground, and that path has been there long enough that the stone beneath it has changed color permanently. Moisture collects on flat surfaces in the early morning and beads there, unwilling to evaporate, until the sun's angle changes and it slides off or is drunk back into the stone.

There is one source of water. A spring, somewhere behind the uppermost house, where the rock face is steepest. It emerges from a fissure no wider than two fingers and spills into a basin — carved, or worn, or both — set into a hollow at the base of the crag. The basin overflows in a slow continuous sheet down the back of the settlement, into a channel that runs between two rows of houses and disappears through a grating let into the shelf. The water is very clear, very cold, and its sound is the sound of the place: a constant faint tinkling, never loud, never absent, the only sound that is constant.

Vegetation is sparse. The mosses, the lichens — orange and pale green and gray. A few low shrubs in the wider cracks, something with small tough leaves the color of dust, that doesn't grow above knee-height. Tufts of grass where the channel water has made the soil deep enough. One tree, near the spring, gnarled and wind-shaped, leaning out from the rock face, small enough to be missed by someone walking through. Nothing blooms here in any obvious way. Nothing is in season. Nothing is out of season.

Light is the light of high places. Clear even when there are clouds, because the clouds are usually below. Shadows are sharp but short at midday; they lengthen and the buildings cast them across each other in long stripes toward evening. The stone takes on a faint warmth in direct sun, but the warmth is borrowed and leaves quickly when a cloud passes or the sun drops. Colors are muted. The grays of stone, the muted greens of moss, the dark browns of old wood, the pale yellow of leather — these are the colors. Nothing brightens. Nothing is meant to.

Few animals. Ravens, sometimes, on the upper crags, their calls the only loud sound the place ever makes. A small herd of goats that may or may not be tended — they move along the ledges above the settlement and come down to the channel for water and then climb back up. Occasionally a bird of prey, very high, almost out of sight. Otherwise the place is quiet of creatures. Tracks in the channel silt, droppings on the shelf stones. Life, but lightly distributed.

The sounds: the spring water. Wind, which here is not strong but constant — it moves between the buildings and makes small sounds, a soft whistling at gaps, a low moan through the channel grating, a hum along the rooflines. The creak of old wood. The distant, very distant, occasional sound of a tool against stone, or a voice, or a door. These are rare. For long stretches the only sounds are water and wind, and they layer into something like a single sound, the way a river and its banks are a single thing.

The air is cool. It requires a layer. It smells of wet stone, of old wood, of metal, of moss — the smell of a place that has been itself for a long time. It tastes thin. Breathing deeper does not help.

The technology is old. Hand-cut stone for everything visible. Wooden beams, dark with age. Doors of plank, hinges hammered from local iron. Ropes of twisted fiber. No glass. No metal that hasn't been shaped by hand and fire. No machine. The settlement is built as it could have been built a thousand years ago, and as it will be built a thousand years hence, because the way of building has found its form and there is no reason to change it.

Nothing is being built. Nothing is being torn down. The moss grows but no faster than the stone wears. The water flows but not enough to erode. The buildings stand because they were built to stand, and no force present is strong enough to bring them down except very slowly, over time longer than the buildings have already been here. The settlement is not abandoned — abandonment would be a change. There may be no people, or a few people, or many people who have learned to move so quietly that they do not disturb the equilibrium. From outside, looking in, it is impossible to tell. The place holds itself in the same configuration it has held for a very long time, and it will hold itself in that configuration after the looker has gone.

Step into it and the world outside falls away. The shelf is small. You can see from one end of the settlement to the other in a single glance. Beyond is height and emptiness and the suggestion of a much larger mountain landscape, but the place itself is contained, the size of a room or a grove or a long-held breath. It is its own size, no larger.
