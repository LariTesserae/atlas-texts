The Plateau of Half-Sleep

There is a high place here that has not yet decided what it is.

It rises in long, ragged ridges toward a summit that isn't a single summit but a wandering — a succession of shoulders and broken tables of stone stepping upward through the warm dim air like the vertebrae of some vast unfinished creature. The stone is dark. Most of it is dark. It looks dark even where the light falls because the light itself is dark, a low amber wash that drifts in from a sky that is not so much a sky as a ceiling of suspended ash, miles thick, that lets through only the bruised orange suggestion of a sun somewhere beyond. The light does not throw shadows so much as thicken against objects, settling on them like dust that prefers them.

It is warm. This is the first strange thing. At this altitude — and the altitude is enormous, the air thin enough that nothing with breath would feel at ease, the lungs of any creature would labor against it — the ground gives up a steady, patient warmth. You feel it through the soles of your feet before you understand it. The stone is hot not from any sun but from beneath. Something is cooking down there, a great slow engine of magma turning over far below, and its breath comes up through cracks and held-through seams and through the porous body of the rock itself until the entire high place feels like a stone that has been left too long by a fire. Warm rock, warm dust, warm air that tastes of iron and sulfur and something older and drier than either. If you pressed your ear to the ground at certain spots, you would hear a sound like the sea heard from inside a shell, but slower, and not water.

There is no water. There has perhaps never been water here, or only the ghost of it — pale dry salt crusts that appear in the lowest bowls like the memory of a lake that forgot itself. The salt is white and powdery and lifts in small plumes if you disturb it. It is the only color on the whole plateau that is not in the family of umber and rust and the deep red-brown of cooling lava. The salt bowls are the quietest places. Nothing moves through them. They sit between the ridges like the empty hands of the land.

The land is still being made.

You can see it happening if you stand long enough. Not in any one place, but everywhere. Ridges that were not here last season — or last century, or last geological breath — push up from the plateau in long slow folds, their spines still sharp, their flanks still raw, the stone there a slightly different color from the stone around it, a darker black or a wetter red where the heat has not yet leached out. Other ridges slump. Their shoulders sag inward as if tired. You can find a place where a shoulder fell only recently — the rubble at the foot of it is loose and unweathered, the broken faces of the boulders still bright inside, the dust around them not yet settled, and in the still air above it you can almost see the air itself trembling with the memory of the fall.

The change is not dramatic most of the time. There is no spectacle. The plateau does not boil or shake. It is turbulent in the way that a sleeping animal is turbulent — the muscles working, the heart turning, the breath moving in and out, but the body at rest. Hours pass and nothing. The warmth from below is constant. The dim orange light shifts a little with the slow turning of the unseen sun but you cannot mark it except by waiting. Then, somewhere out across the plain of broken stone, a sound. Not loud. A deep, upholstered sound, like a door closed at the bottom of a well, or a drum struck in the next valley over. The ground considers it. A thin crack appears in the dust a few hundred yards from where you stand, and from the crack a thread of pale vapor rises — not steam, because there is no water to steam, but something else, a gas that smells of sulfur and hot iron and which makes the inside of your nose sting.

Then it is quiet again.

These are the eruptions of a world that has not finished learning how to be a world. The ridges will grow for a while and then the flank will fall and the rubble will be reheated and become part of the floor again and then rise as a different ridge elsewhere. The salt bowls will shift. New bowls will open where the crust has thinned. The ceiling of ash above will thicken or thin depending on what the deeper engine decides. The plateau is a draft. The plateau is a thought being thought very slowly by something that is mostly asleep.

In the dimness, at wide intervals across the high place, there are things.

Not many. You can walk a long time and see nothing made, only geology. Then something. A single low ring of fitted stones, the size of a room, with no roof and no door and the floor inside swept clean to the salt underneath, as if the wind has been polishing it for years. A cairn, smaller than a person, balanced on a ridge that should not be able to hold it, the upper stones resting on the lower in a way that looks intentional and looks impossible and yet does not fall. A line of three standing stones set against the slope, far apart, leaning slightly, facing down the long fall of the plateau toward something you cannot see. A pit, square-cut into the stone, lined with thin slabs of a darker stone that does not occur elsewhere on the plateau, full to the brim with the same white salt powder as the bowls outside, as if something was being stored there and has long since been used or forgotten.

There are no tools near them. No machines. No wires or vessels or anything that would suggest a maker who knew how to make things. Whatever set these stones was using only stone and patience and the long warmth, and whatever set them is not here now. The cairns do not lean further. The rings do not spread. They are quiet presences, almost geological themselves, as if they are part of the emerging land and only happen to be in shapes that suggest a hand.

You cannot tell if they are old. The place is so dry that nothing weathers in the usual way. A stone placed a thousand years ago would look the same as a stone placed yesterday. They might be very old. They might be very new. They might be being placed now, very slowly, by something you will not see.

The wind moves but does not speak. It is a thin, hot wind, the kind that carries no moisture because there is no moisture to carry, and it slides along the ridges without lifting the dust much — the dust is too heavy here, and the air too sluggish, and the dust is also warm and likes to stay where it is. From time to time the wind brings a faint smell, a long way off, of something burning. That is the lower country, where the magma is closer to the surface and the eruptions are larger and more frequent and the light there is brighter, a duller red, almost visible at night. The high plateau is a quieter province of the same world. The plateaus and the burning country below are part of the same unfinished system. From here, on a clear evening, if the ash thins, you can sometimes see the far glow.

You do not see anything alive.

You might think you do. A patch on a boulder that moves when you move, but it is only the heat-shimmer from the stone, and the stone is not alive. A faint rustling at the edge of hearing when the wind drops, but it is the rock itself, ticking as it cools, the way a fire ticks in a hearth long after the flames have gone. Once, in a salt bowl, you might kneel and see a faint pattern in the powder, a curling like the ghost of a footprint, but it is only where the salt has settled unevenly and you have disturbed the surface with your breath. There is nothing here that breathes. There is nothing here that eats or sleeps or makes a sound with intention.

And yet the place does not feel empty. It feels occupied by its own becoming. The ridges and the salt and the warm stone and the long slow sounds from below — these are presences. They are the only presences. The scattered rings and cairns and standing stones are an addition so small they barely change the silence, but they do change it, the way a single word changes a long page. Someone or something stood here long enough to leave a mark. The mark is going to outlast whoever made it. The plateau is going to outlast the mark. And the plateau itself, this warm dark unfinished draft of a continent, will not outlast what is underneath it, which is only heat, which is only time, which is only the slow stirring of something that has not yet woken up.

It is mostly quiet here. Hours will pass with no sound louder than the wind. Then, somewhere far across the broken plain, that low upholstered door-closing sound again, and the ground considers it, and a new crack appears in the dust, and another thread of pale vapor rises, and the smell of iron and sulfur comes faintly across the long warm dimness, and the plateau, in its slow sleep, turns itself a little more into what it is becoming.

If you stand very still at the rim of one of the rings, in the center of it, in the swept place, and wait, you can feel the warmth coming up evenly through the floor. The stones around you are warm. The floor is warm. The air above you, faintly, is warm. You are standing inside a small quiet circle of made, surrounded on all sides by the much larger quiet circle of the still-being-made, and above you the ceiling of ash is dim and orange and very high, and below you the stone is moving, very slowly, the way a chest moves when it is breathing.

Nothing here knows your name.

Nothing here is asking for it.
