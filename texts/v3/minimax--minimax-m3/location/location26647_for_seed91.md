The sound comes first.

It is everywhere — not loud in the way a scream is loud, but loud in the way the sea is loud, the way a city heard from a great height is loud, the way a thing that has no silence in it is loud. It moves through you rather than at you. It is the sound of the place's lungs. You feel it before you understand it.

You are standing in a chamber of black iron and living green. The cold is not a weather cold. It is a vaulted cold, a designed cold, an industrial cold that has been maintained for as long as anyone still living can remember. It is dry on your skin except where it is wet. Condensation trails down every metal surface in thin sinuous lines, as if the pipes are weeping. The walls — some of them rock, some of them cut, all of them wet — are furred with growth.

This is a moss cavern, more or less. A moss operation, more or less. A moss city, more or less. The plants are everywhere. They are the floor. They are the ceiling. They are the walls and the pipes and the pillars and the air filters. They are the dark green pelt that covers every surface and softens every sharp edge. They are thick enough in places to be mistaken for ground, but they yield slightly when you stand on them, and the cold of their yielding is the cold of the room.

The moss is old in places. Some of it has been growing since the chambers were first cut. Some of it is new — the pale new green that appeared in the cracks after the last excavation, only a few years past. The whole cavern is young in its rock. The angles of the carved walls are clean and unweathered, and the moss has come in only where it could hold. In some places it hangs in curtains off the cut faces. In others it has sheeted across whole surfaces, and the walls look as if they are wearing green wool. In still others — the highest places, the most vertical, the most exposed — it has not yet come, and you can see the fresh-cut stone, still pale with the dust of the blasting.

The machinery is also everywhere, but in a different sense. It is threaded through the moss rather than replacing it. The pipes are iron — black iron, painted iron, occasionally rusted iron — and they run in long runs along the ceilings and down the columns, and where they pass through the moss the moss has closed around them like water around a finger. They breathe out small plumes of vapor at their joints, and where the vapor meets the cold air it makes a local fog that hangs in lazy coils. There are fans — great cylindrical fans, three meters across, with blades you cannot count at a glance — turning slowly in their housings, and they are what make the sound. There are dozens of them. There are perhaps hundreds. They turn at different speeds. They are the breath of the place, and the place does not stop breathing, and you can feel the air move across your face in a slow, cold, mechanical exhalation.

The light is low. There are industrial lamps — sodium, hung at long intervals — and they make orange puddles on the floor of the cavern, but they do not fill the space. Between them the dimness is genuine, the dimness of a cellar, the dimness of a place that has never seen the sun. In the deeper parts of the cavern — and there are deeper parts; this is one chamber of many, and the chambers branch and connect through passages wide enough to drive a truck through — the lamps are fewer and the dark is thicker. There the moss appears darker, almost black, and the air looks faintly green.

The structures are scattered. There is a station near the entry, with a desk and a chair and a kettle and a clipboard, all of it dusted with a fine green pollen. There is a catwalk that crosses the chamber about twenty meters up, and you can walk out on it and look down on the moss and the pipes and the turning fans and feel the air move past you in the same slow exhalation. There is a small workshop somewhere in the middle distance, with a light on, but no one is in it. There is a series of vats — open-topped, metal, perhaps two meters deep — arranged in long rows, and the moss grows in them, fed by pipes that drip something clear into them from above. The vats are not all at the same level of fullness. The moss in some of them is high and dark and almost spilling over the rim. In others it is barely seeded, a faint green fuzz across a thin film of water.

The sound, again. It is constant. It does not stop for you. It is the sound of every fan turning, every pump cycling, every compressor somewhere in the depths pushing cold air into the caverns through long ducts. It is the sound of the operation that keeps the place cold and damp and alive. It is the sound of the moss being grown industrially. Because that is what this is. This is a moss operation. A cold, damp, dark, vast, industrial moss operation. The moss is the product. The moss is the worker. The moss is the architecture. You cannot easily tell which is which.

There is almost no fauna. Once in a long while — perhaps once an hour, perhaps once a day — something moves in the deep moss. A small thing. You do not see it. You feel the moss shift slightly under your boot, and you look down, and there is nothing there. There are no birds. There are no mammals. There are no insects you can see, though there must be insects, because something is pollinating the moss, because the fine green dust is everywhere. It is on your clothes. It is on the pipes. It is on the desk at the station. It is in the air, just visible in the orange light from the lamps, falling slowly, slowly.

The change is slow. The fans do not speed up. The moss does not visibly grow. The lamps do not flicker. The place is patient. It was here before you and it will be here after. The cut stone will weather over decades. The moss will thicken. The pipes will rust. The fans will be replaced one at a time, and the sound will not change. The place will continue to do what it has been doing — making cold, making damp, growing moss, harvesting moss, growing more moss — for as long as the people who run it decide to keep the place cold and damp and lit with sodium. They come and go. They leave their clipboards and drink their tea. They check the vats. They climb the catwalks. They go home. The moss does not care. The fans do not care. The cold does not care. The place holds its breath and then exhales and holds it again.

You stand in it. The sound is in your chest. The cold is on your face. The damp is in your hair. The green pollen falls on your shoulders in the dim orange light.

This is the place.
