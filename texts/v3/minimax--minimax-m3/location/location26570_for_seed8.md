The stone is older than anything that has breath.

That is not a fact here, exactly. It is a pressure, a hum in the legs. The stone has been here so long it has forgotten how to be a mountain. It is what is left when mountains stop — the bones of the world after the bones themselves have been ground to powder and carried off, leaving only the reluctant remnants, the things too stubborn to dissolve, too dense to leave. So this is a low country. An open country. A country of knobs and shallow basins stretching to a haze where the eye gives up trying to find an edge.

The knobs are dark stone — granite, mostly, and gneiss, with whatever harder veins the geology left behind. They are rounded and split along seams that were once joints in a mountain that no longer exists, the joints in the bedrock of a range that was already ancient when the first ferns unrolled. They rise only a few meters above the ground. Some are smaller. A few are larger, but never large in the way mountains are large — never dramatic, never steep. They have the shape of things that have been pressed, gently and without pause, by weather and weight for an unimaginably long time. They are quiet. They are settled.

Everything here is wet.

The air is wet before dawn and wet after the sun is well up. It condenses on the stone in a skin of moisture that never quite dries — a sheen you can feel with the back of your hand, the stone cooler than the air and so the air keeps giving up its water to it. The basins between the knobs hold shallow water. Not pools, exactly — sheets, an inch or two deep, that flow so slowly they seem to be considering whether to move at all. Some are still. Some are moving, but only a drifting leaf or a frog choosing to swim will tell you which.

The water is brown. Not only from mud — though there is mud, soft dark mud that sucks at the boot — but from tannin, from the slow leaching of every root and bit of dead matter that has ever given up here. Cupped in the hands it is the color of strong tea. It smells of stone and growth and decay in equal measure, and somewhere underneath, of iron. If you tasted it (you shouldn't) it would taste of everything that has ever died in it.

Vegetation is sparse. There are tufts of wiry grass in the cracks of the stone, where a little soil has gathered and held. There are low shrubs with hard dark leaves, scattered like dropped things. There are ferns, sometimes, in the deeper shade of a north-facing rock face, where the light is too poor for anything ambitious. There are patches of moss and lichen on every surface that doesn't move — green and grey and burnt orange, the colors of patient colonization. There is no forest. The soil was carried away long ago. What remains is enough for a thin community of things that have made their peace with stone. The plants here look tired, in the way very old things look tired: not weak, exactly, but used. They are not trying to become anything else.

But the fauna is another matter. The fauna is loud.

By day the insects carry it — the dense, persistent hum of many wings, a sound like a held note that doesn't quite resolve. Mosquitoes, yes, and crane flies hanging in the air like dangling threads, and midges in clouds above the water, and dragonflies with wings that catch the light as they patrol the channels between stones, deliberate, almost military. Their sound is the bed, the drone underneath.

By dusk the frogs take over. The frogs are everywhere, hidden in the wet grass, under flat stones, in the cracks between the granite, anywhere they can press themselves into moisture and wait. They are not large. Their voices are. The sound rises in waves — one starts, then another, then a third, then a chorus that thickens and thickens until it is less a sound than a presence, then a hush, then again. It is a sound that has gone on, with regional variations, for longer than mammals have existed. You are listening to a very old performance.

In the deeper water there are things that move without a sound — fish, an eel perhaps, the patient predator. In the air above, a kingfisher drops from a low perch, hits the water, comes up with something silver twisting in its bill. Something with a long red tail — a trogon, maybe, or some bird without a name yet — flickers along a stone ridge and is gone.

There are no structures. There are no paths. There are no signs that anything with hands has ever come here, or if they have, the wet has eaten the evidence down to nothing. No cairn has survived. No post. No cut. The place is unaware of being useful, and has been for a very long time.

The light is ordinary. The sun climbs and the sun falls. There are clouds, often — high, soft, the kind that cast no shadows but turn the day from white to grey and back again. The light comes through the wet air and is somehow both flatter and more present than light has any right to be. Nothing glints. Nothing dazzles. You can see the shape of distant knobs as smudges in the haze. You can see the water moving, slowly, where it moves at all.

Nothing here moves quickly. The water moves so slowly you have to kneel and watch a scrap of leaf for a long time before you are sure it is going anywhere. The lichen creeps outward on the stone at a rate measured in human lifetimes. The granite knobs themselves wear down at a rate measured in geological epochs — the difference between this year and last is a thing no one will ever measure. Even the animals seem to be in on the same patient arrangement, conducting their small urgent lives inside a frame of immense duration. A dragonfly's patrol is urgent only by insect standards. A frog's call, repeated into the dark, has the rhythm of something that has all the time there is.

This is a place that has reached the bottom of its own possibilities. The mountains that once were here are gone. The ranges that wore down into hills are now these flat-topped remnants, these dark knobs, this near-flattened country. What remains is what remains. And in what remains — the wet stone, the tea-colored water, the tufts of grass, the dense invisible web of small urgent lives — the place is fully itself, the way an old face is fully itself. There is nothing to add. There has been nothing to add for a very long time.

You stand in it. The wet gathers on your skin. The frog-song thickens. The sun, low now, turns the standing water briefly to brass, and then is gone, and the place continues, as it has continued, patient, wet, almost finished, not yet finished, not quite.
