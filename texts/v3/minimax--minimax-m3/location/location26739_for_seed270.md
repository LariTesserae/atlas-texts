The place is a hollow. A shallow depression worn into a flat expanse of pale stone so old that the stone has nearly forgotten it was ever anything else — a salt-pan, a dry lake, a mineral table that has been settling into its final form for longer than anything still remembers. The hollow is small, only a few dozen meters across, sunk just deep enough that you could walk past the rim and miss it entirely, and from above the rim it is only the faintest shadow against the rest of the pale. The light here is unremarkable. The air is cool, the way a stone cellar is cool: the warmth of the day goes into the rock and is held quietly and given back slowly. There is no water anywhere. There has not been water here in a very long time. There are no plants; there has never been any reason for plants.

Inside the hollow, a small industrial operation sits in its last stage of usefulness. A row of grinders along one wall, their housings gone to a thin oxide crust but their shafts still turning, slowly, in the patient way that machines turn when no one is left to ask them why. A separator, a long trough with a torn rubber belt still inching. Pipes that run nowhere and end in capped flanges. A shed, mostly — a frame of metal studs with a corrugated roof that has become more hole than roof. A tank on its side, half-buried in dust. The machines were here for grinding and sorting stone; that is still what they do. They pull the pale rock in and let the pale rock out, finer, and what falls between is a powder so fine it hangs in the cool air for a long time and never quite settles. The machines have been doing this so long they have worn shallow bowls into the floor of the hollow beneath themselves.

The fauna is what you notice first, if you step down into the place.

It is everywhere. The floor of the hollow is hidden under a dense, moving layer of segmented things the size of a finger, ridged along the back, the color of dried blood or old rust, each one with many small legs that move in coordinated waves that run through the carpet like wind across a field. They crawl up the walls, over the pipes, across the shed's skeleton, through the gaps in the corrugated roof. They are packed so close in places that you cannot see stone beneath them, only a slow pulse of overlapping bodies. They flow around the machines the way water would, except they do not flow — they crawl, and the whole layer crawls in the same direction at the same time, a slow current, and the direction is always the same: through the grinders, around the separator, under the shed, out toward the rim of the hollow, and back. There is a circulation in it. There is an economy.

The machines feed them. The grinders reduce stone to a powder that is fine enough to be eaten, and the powder is what they eat, and what they do not eat is what they excrete, finer still, and what they excrete is what the machines pull in again on their slow belts. They have been here long enough that the cycle has tightened. They do not drink, because there is nothing to drink. They do not need to. Their bodies are dry and clicking and they move through the dust like small parts of a much larger system.

The sound of the place is steady and loud and layered. There is the grind of stone inside the housings, a low continuous rumble that vibrates in the floor. There is the hiss of fine dust being shaken loose. There is the click of chitin on metal and chitin on stone — a million tiny impacts, very fast, running together into a kind of white noise with a sharp edge. The shed amplifies it; the hollow holds it. From outside, on the rim, you can hear the place before you can see it: a hum with a pulse.

It is active. The machines turn. The dust is shifted and shifted again. The carpet of segmented bodies flows in its slow rotation, a current of red and rust over the pale stone, and somewhere in the carpet things are happening at a pace you cannot easily see: a smaller one being eaten, a shed exoskeleton left behind, a cluster forming at a pipe-end where the dust is finer. The grinders wear themselves down a little each day. The carpet wears the machines down faster than the machines wear themselves. The hollow is settling into its final form, and the hollow is being settled into, and the two processes are the same process now and have been for a long time. The stone is older than anything else here. The machines are older than the carpet. The carpet is the youngest thing in the hollow, and the carpet is winning, slowly, the way water wins, except it is not water.

It is cool. It is loud. It is hidden. It is dense with small red moving life and tired turning engines and a powder of itself, and it has been doing this for a long time and will do it for a long time, because there is nothing here that has any reason to stop.
