# The Sunken Room

You come upon it as you would come upon a place the land has chosen to forget — a rim of cracked clay standing a little higher than the surrounding scrub, and beyond that rim, a drop. From the surface it reads as a simple thing: a circular wound in the hardpan, the kind of depression the desert opens when it has eaten enough of itself. The lip is weathered smooth in long, pale arcs. A few lizards flatten themselves there, watching your shadow arrive with the unhurried patience of creatures who have never been hunted. Below the lip, the color changes. The earth is suddenly wet, and the wetness is suddenly green.

You climb down. There is no path, only a fold in the wall where a fig root has been persuaded, over decades, to serve as a kind of staircase — knots and knuckles of wood smoothed by the hands of whatever has come before you, which is not much, which is almost nothing. The wall is terracotta where the sun has not reached it, and near-black where the water has stained it. The air that meets you at the bottom is heavier than the air above. It is the weight of a different climate.

The room is small. A cathedral could not fit here, but a grove can, and a grove has. The fig tree is the chief inhabitant, though it does not announce itself so much as occupy — its trunk leans at the angle the ceiling-fall left for it, and its crown spreads sideways into the dimness, taking the room it was given. Its roots have worked themselves into everything: into the cracks between flagstones, into the mortar of a wall that is no longer a wall, into the dark lip of a basin that holds the water. The roots are old and patient, and they have made the floor a kind of slow, breathing thing. Beneath them, the soil moves.

The basin is the room's center. It is a rectangle of cut stone, and it is older than the fig. You kneel and see the masonry: dressed blocks, fitted, with a lip worn into a curve by something other than weather. There is a mark on one of the stones — a chevron, or an eye, or the letter of a script that has no descendants. Water sits in it, black as ink, the depth of which you cannot tell. The fig has sent two pale tendrils down into it, and they lie on the surface like the fingers of a drowned hand. Condensation gathers on the rim in the cool minutes and falls in single drops, and each drop makes the only large sound the room permits.

The light here is a guest. It comes down a single ragged opening in the dome of the ceiling — a piece of old sky that the collapse spared — and it falls in a pale column that moves across the room as the day moves. By midday it touches the basin and the water answers with a faint, dark sheen. By evening it has crept up the opposite wall, where it lingers on the wet clay like a slow fire. The corners of the room, which is to say most of the room, are in a dimness that is not quite dark, because the surfaces here glow — moss, lichen, the white threads of fungus that have colonized the damp stone and put out a pale, sourceless shine of their own. You can see, but only as a creature adapted to it.

The heat is the kind of heat that is not a temperature so much as a presence. It comes from the walls, which are warmed by the long hours above; it rises from the basin; it is exhaled by the composted floor; it hangs in the canopy of the fig as the air inside a greenhouse hangs. You sweat, and the sweat does not dry. Everything that touches your skin leaves a faint residue. The fig, when you push past it, releases a heavy, vegetal breath, and the breath is sweet and a little rotten, and it is the smell of the room.

The sounds of the place are kept very small. You hear them only when you stop walking. The drop, the drop, the drop of the condensation. A slow, papery rustle that is the fig deciding something, leaf against leaf. A chirr, sometimes, from somewhere you cannot locate — not a cricket, or not exactly, but a thing with a cricket's idea of music, repeated in a low register. And beneath all of this, almost below the threshold, a continuous, wet, clicking sound: many small sounds, too many to count, the sound of an entire room full of small mouths doing small work. You do not see what makes most of them. You see what they make.

The fauna is the dense thing here. The walls, where they meet the floor, are a slow-moving pattern. Lizards — slender, gray-green, with a thread of yellow at the throat — hunt along the cracks and pause on warm stones with one foot raised, as if listening. They eat what they can catch. What they catch is most of what is in the room. There are salamanders in the basin, pale and almost translucent, lying in the dark water with only their nostrils showing; they surface, breathe, and disappear with a motion that is more suggestion than sight. There are things under the bark of the fig that look, when you peel it, like small white hands. There are insects — many, in many states — and the air near the basin has a haze to it that is not all vapor. The floor is not entirely floor. Things have been eaten, dissolved, reincorporated. A pebble that was here yesterday may be the bright casing of something tomorrow.

The dynamic is older than anything you could call a thought. A moth comes down the light-shaft once, batting at the column, and it is taken — by what, you do not see; only the soft displacement of air, and then the moth is gone, and a lizard on the wall is chewing thoughtfully. Water is brought in, in small amounts, on the days when rain is sent into the upper world and runs through the cracks; in dry weeks, the basin lowers by a finger's width, and the fig drinks what is left, and the room grows drier, and the things that need wetness retreat into the walls. Then a rain comes, and the basin is filled from below as well as above — the water rising through channels in the stone that you cannot see — and the room swells with moisture, and the things come back, and the cycle begins again. The whole place breathes on this schedule, and you are a guest inside its breath.

What was built here, and by whom, you cannot say. The flagstones are a pavement of a kind. The basin is a font or a cistern. The chevron is a sign. The carved stone that you find under the fig — half-consumed, its upper edge flush with the soil and its lower edge still in the bedrock — is the corner of a wall, and the wall continued out into the room that is no longer a room, and above the wall was a ceiling that is now the sky the fig leans against. Whoever made this place made it for water, and for the ritual of water, and for the small, dim, hot company of things that water keeps alive. They are gone. The room has not noticed them go. It has simply continued to be the kind of place that holds water, and lets the fig have it, and lets the salamanders have the fig, and lets the lizards have the salamanders, and lets the whole slow, dense, quiet engine of it turn in its small bowl in the earth, in the silence that is not really silence, in the dimness that is not really dark, in the heat that does not stop.
