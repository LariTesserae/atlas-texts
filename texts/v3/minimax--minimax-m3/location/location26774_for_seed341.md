# The Greenworks

The town does not have a name anymore, or if it does, no one left to speak it.

It stands in the lowest fold of a low country, where the rivers have grown tired and spread out into a kind of standing, and the ground never quite decides whether it is land or water. Concrete pilings driven into silt, brick foundations laid directly on clay, iron stanchions rusting at the waterline — the settlement was built to last, and it has lasted, and the lasting has taken on its own character. The place is old enough now that the edges of things have softened. Rounded. Half-eaten by humidity.

Steam hangs in the air at all hours. The light comes down through it the way light comes down through a fish tank, filtered and greenish and slow. It is dim even at midday, even when the sky above the steam is technically clear, because the heat and the moisture together make a ceiling of their own. Everything sweats. The walls run. If you leave a piece of paper on a flat surface, in an hour it will curl and darken, the damp drawn through it as if the air were drinking.

And yet — plants. The town is overcome with them. Not the riot of jungle, because the air is too hot and too wet and the soil underneath is largely slag and cinder; but a determined, exotic greenery has taken hold on every horizontal surface and many vertical ones. Ferns have rooted in the gutters. Moss climbs the valve housings. A kind of thick-bladed grass, oily and dark, pushes up between the cobbles of the central square. There is a vine — locally famous, perhaps locally unique — that has wrapped itself around the great copper drum of the primary boiler and continues to grow, year on year, in defiance of the heat that comes off the metal in waves. The vine is as thick as a man's arm in places. It flowers, somehow, with small pale blossoms that never fully open. The blossoms are the only white in the place.

The central structure is a furnace hall. The town grew up around it. It is not large by the standards of industry — the scale here is closer to a cathedral, or a great barn — but it is tall, and it dominates. Its chimney is a brick column, badly buttressed, listing a few degrees from true. The chimney breathes. Day and night it exhales a slow plume of white vapor that mixes with the natural steam of the lowland and becomes indistinguishable from it. From a distance, the whole town looks like a single organism, a thing with a slow respiration, fog drifting from its top.

Inside the furnace hall the heat is its own weather. Condensation runs down every strut and beam. The metal catwalks, when you step on them, are slick with a kind of permanent sweat. The light inside is what you would expect of a forge that has been running for a hundred years on partial fuel: orange, intermittent, a deep pulse that comes and goes. The great iron doors of the furnaces open and close on their own schedule. Nobody winds the clock. Nobody has wound it for a long time, and yet it keeps its rhythm — a heavy mechanical breathing, a sigh of pressured air, the doors parting, a bloom of yellow heat, the doors closing, the long exhale of cooling. Something in the system has learned to maintain itself. Or the system was always like this. It is hard to know which.

Pipes run everywhere. They run along the streets, mounted on low iron saddles; they run between the buildings at every height; they dive under the water and come up again, sweating at their joints, weeping slow lines of rust into the standing pools. Most of the pipes are cold now. Some are not. The ones that are not have a faint tremor in them you can feel by laying your hand on the metal, and they make a sound — a low, sustained note, almost below hearing, that you feel in the teeth. The whole town hums with this note, the way a kettle hums before it boils, except the kettle has been on the stove for decades and shows no sign of either boiling or being lifted.

The sound of the place is its most assertive quality. From the furnace hall, the deep pump of the bellows. From the pipes, the hiss and complaint of pressure, and the percussive knock of steam valves cracking open and shut. From the upper tanks, a periodic gulping, as if the town were drinking. From the wet brick and the standing water, a constant soft rain of condensation, drip on drip, never stopping. Somewhere in the secondary works there is a wheel — a great spoked iron thing, twenty feet across, that turns without visible cause. It turns slowly, with a groan and a sigh on every revolution, a sound like an old man getting out of a chair. There is no animal sound at all. No birds. No insects. The frogs that you would expect in a wet warm lowland are absent. The water does not even move with the soft plop of small things dropping into it. The town is silent of every voice but its own mechanical ones, and the mechanical ones are many, and they are loud, and they have been loud for so long that the place seems to be made of them.

There are few people. That is the strangest part, or one of the strangest parts. The town was built to house hundreds — there are rows of small brick houses with iron stairs, laid out on a grid, each one identical, each one with a door that opens onto a narrow wet street. The houses are not empty. Some of them are. Most of them have a kind of life in them: a curtain in a window, a chair pulled to a table, a kettle on a stove that has not been lit in a long time. The kettles are full of clean water. The windows are clean. Someone, or some few someones, are keeping up appearances. But the people themselves are not seen. You can stand in the central square at any hour and see no one, and yet feel, persistently, that you are being attended to. A door is closed that was open. A light is on that was off. The clean water in the kettles is a fact that has to be explained by someone, and you do not see who explains it.

The vegetation has noticed the absence. It presses in. The ferns in the gutters have begun to climb the doorframes. Moss has colonized the stairs. A whole house on the eastern row has been taken by a single banyan-like growth, the trunk rising through the kitchen floor, the roots walking down the street. The door of that house is closed and latched. The window is clean.

The water is everywhere. It lies in the streets in thin reflective sheets that move when you move and settle when you settle. It beads on every metal surface in perfect spheres, because the air is so saturated that nothing will absorb — the moisture has nowhere to go except onto things, and it goes. The bricks of the houses are dark with it. The cobbles are slick. The water is warm. It has a faint taste of iron and of something older, a mineral sweetness, as if it has been standing a long time over old stone. The water is not foul. Nothing here is foul, exactly. The place smells of hot metal, of wet brick, of the green exhalation of ten thousand leaves, of the ozone that comes off the great wheel when it turns. It is not an unpleasant smell. It is the smell of a thing that has been running for a long time without being cleaned, and has settled into its own nature.

The light, when you can see it, is the light of late afternoon in a place where the sun is low and the air is thick. Even at noon the quality is of four o'clock. The shadows that fall are not sharp. They are smudged, the way shadows are smudged in a steam room. The faces of things are softened. The angles of the ironwork, the sharp edges of the brick, the corners of the houses — all of it is slightly rounded by the perpetual damp, the way a river stone is rounded. The whole town has the aspect of a thing that has been tumbled for a long time in its own weather.

And the weather is the weather of a low place in a hot climate with much water. It rains often, but the rain is warm and slow, and it does not change anything. The streets are already wet. The buildings are already dark. The air is already full. The rain adds to a sum that is already complete. After the rain, the steam from the chimney is more visible. The flowers on the furnace-hall vine open a quarter-inch more than they usually do, and the pollen from them, which is the color of old ivory, drifts down into the square in a slow pale fall, and lies on the surface of the water there, and is not disturbed, because there is nothing to disturb it.

The town runs. That is the other strange part. There is coal moving — small carts on rails, pushed by no one, arriving at the furnace hall at the right intervals, returning empty, arriving again. There is the wheel turning. There are the furnace doors opening and closing. There is the chimney breathing. There is the periodic gulp of the upper tanks. There is the hum. None of this is fed. None of it is watched. It has been going on for so long that the continuation of it is no longer remarkable to whatever remains here. The carts, perhaps, are loaded by the green. The vine has been seen, on certain still evenings, to lean toward a particular cart, and the next morning the cart is full. This may be a fancy. It is not certainly a fancy.

The town is small. You can walk across it in twenty minutes if you do not stop. It has a grid of perhaps thirty streets and one main way that runs from the furnace hall to the gate at the southern edge, where the road — what is left of it — goes out into the wet country and is not followed. The gate is iron. It is rusted open. Beyond it the world is green and flat and uninflected, and the steam from the chimney trails out across it and thins, and is lost.

You stand in the square. The wheel groans. The furnace sighs. The vine flowers its half-open flowers. The water on the cobbles is still. You are alone, or you are not alone, and the difference, in this place, has begun to seem less important than it used to.

The town continues.
