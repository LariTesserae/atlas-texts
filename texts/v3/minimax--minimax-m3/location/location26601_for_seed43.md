# The Halls Below the Hush

You reach the place by a fissure in the flank of a low hill where the soil thins to gravel and the grass grows in tufts that lean away, as though listening. The crack is wider than your shoulders. The earth exhales when you step in — a steady warm breath, rich with minerals and old water, with something underneath both of those that is not quite smell but gets into the back of your teeth. After a few paces the light from outside becomes a memory and you are walking by what the place gives you itself.

It is a hall. It was made.

That is the first thing you understand and the thing you keep understanding, over and over, as your eyes adjust: every surface here is *made.* There is no raw rock. The walls are dressed stone fitted so tightly that a fingernail cannot find the seam — not perfect, not machined, but the work of hands that understood the stone and what the stone could be persuaded to do. The blocks are small, the size of a man's two hands cupped, and each one is slightly different. Some are grey, some are the dull ochre of river clay, some are almost black, some have a faint blush of iron-red that looks, in the low light, like a stain of breath. The mortar between them is the same color as the stones themselves, as if the wall were a single living tissue with bones.

The hall runs roughly north-south, or what you take to be north-south — there is no sun to argue with, only the slow direction of a draft. It is narrow. Your arms could almost touch the walls if you stretched them. It is also not low; you can stand straight and still have a hand's breadth above your hair, and the ceiling is vaulted in a way that gathers the breath and sends it forward, so that when you exhale, your own breath comes back to you a moment later from somewhere ahead, carrying news of what is there.

The floor is fitted flagstone, slightly dished in the middle of the passage so that any water runs to the edges and into shallow channels carved along the base of the walls. The channels are wet. They are always wet. Somewhere further in you can hear them talking to each other across junctions, a small persistent whisper that never stops, the way a creek sounds under ice except it isn't cold here, it is warm, warm as a held hand, warm as a throat.

The temperature does not change. That is the second thing you understand. You walk and walk and the air is always the same temperature, the same weight on your skin, the same faint humidity that gathers on the inside of your wrists and behind your knees. Warm. Damp. Like the inside of a mouth. Like a greenhouse at night. The place is breathing, and you are inside the breath.

---

The first branching.

The hall splits into three, then five, then seven. Each branch is fitted with a low lintel of darker stone, and above each lintel, in the dimness, you begin to make out a single carved mark. They are not letters. They are shapes. A circle with a notch. A vertical line crossed by three horizontals. A shape that might be a leaf, or a hand, or a flame. They are worn almost smooth. The chisel-strokes are gone. Only the intention remains, the way a path worn into grass is still a path even after the grass has forgotten it.

You choose the middle one. You do not know why. Your body knows before you do — you are already halfway down it before the decision catches up with you like a dog you forgot to call.

The middle passage is wider. Here the ceiling lifts and you realize the hall is not a passage at all but a chamber, and the chamber opens again into a larger one, and the larger one into a still larger one, and the proportions keep shifting, getting taller and more narrow, then wider and lower, in a sequence that is not random but which you cannot predict. The stone is older here. Some of it is the same warm grey but some of it is blue, a slate-blue, and some of it has the long pale stripes of sedimentary rock laid down when the world was deciding what kind of place to be. The masons chose it for the stripes. They set the blocks so the stripes run in the same direction, and the direction changes from chamber to chamber, so that as you walk you feel as if you are moving through a series of statements, each one correcting the last.

---

The first light.

It comes from the stone itself.

You notice it gradually, the way you notice a sound that has been going on for a long time before you attended to it. The walls glow. Not brightly. Not in any way that would make them useful for reading. But the dark ochre stone has a faint internal warmth to it, an amber undertone that you cannot say is luminous so much as *less dark than it should be*, and where two blocks meet there is often a thin vein of something paler — quartz, perhaps, or some stranger cousin — that catches whatever meager light there is and refuses to let it go. In some places the veins are thick and the stone there glows like a banked hearth: you can sit beside it and feel, in addition to the warmth you already feel, a faint extra warmth on one side of your face. In other places the veins are absent and the dark is complete, and you walk through those stretches with your hand out, finding the wall by the absence of wall in front of you.

There are no torches. There are no lamps. There are no sconces. There is no smoke-blackening on the ceilings, no drip of tallow, no iron bracket bolted into stone. Whatever light there is comes from the stone itself, or from the water, or from something else you cannot name.

The water.

In every chamber there is water. It is not in basins. It is not channeled. It comes up through the floor in slow places where the flagstones have been laid with gaps between them, and it spreads in shallow sheets that follow the slight dish of the floor and find the channels at the edges, and so the place is, at every height, laced with moving water. Ankle-deep in some rooms. A film in others, just enough to darken the stone and make it shine. It is clear. It tastes of iron and of something fainter, a sweet mineral note, like the memory of a fruit you ate once as a child. It is warm. Your feet get used to it in a few steps and then you forget it is there, except for the sound, the constant small sound of it moving, the way a body sounds to itself from the inside.

The water is loud. That surprises you, the first time you really listen. Not the sound of the water itself, which is soft, but the *place* the water makes by being in it: every channel, every sheet, every drip from the ceiling joins with the others, and the joined sound is a low continuous roar, not loud the way a waterfall is loud, but loud the way a city is loud when you stand in the middle of it and try to hear a single voice. It is the sound of a great many small things being themselves at once. The hall is loud with its own wet working.

---

The first living thing.

A shape moves in the shallow water at your feet. You look down and there is a thing the length of your hand, pale, segmented, with many small legs that move too fast to count. It is the color of the ochre stone. It is the color of the iron-red stone. It is banded. It does not flee from your foot. It angles around it and continues on its way, a small purposeful traveler with a destination in mind, or at least with a sense of where the current is going. There are more of them. The water is full of them. Where a channel joins another, they pour over each other in a small patient mass, none crushed, none panicked, a thousand bodies agreeing on a direction.

The ceiling has its own population. Things hang from it — clusters of them, in the dimmer stretches, where the wet stone glistens. They are like pods, like wax, like the paper nests of wasps but without the geometry. They pulse, faintly, in time with the breathing of the place. When you pass beneath a cluster, you hear them, a soft collective ticking, like the inside of a clock laid open. Some of them are open at the bottom and inside you can see a pale wet thing coiled and waiting. You do not stay long under those.

On the walls there is moss. Not green moss, or not only green — there is a moss that is the green of a bruise, and another that is black, and another that is the color of old copper, and another that is so pale it is almost the color of the water. It grows in the seams between stones and on the stones themselves, in slow lobes and fans, and it is what is making the walls breathe — the breathing you feel on your face, the faint mineral exhalation, is the moss's. The moss is the lung of the place. The water is its blood. The stone is its bone. You have walked into the body of something and it is letting you stay.

The pale moss, where it grows thick, is faintly luminous. That is the source of some of the light you could not place. The light is alive. The light is a kind of breathing.

---

The first sound besides water.

It is a call. It comes from ahead, from around a corner, from somewhere where two passages meet at an angle you have not yet walked. It is low. It is long. It has the shape of a question asked in a language you do not speak but whose vowels you recognize. It goes up at the end. It is answered from a different direction, higher, with a different set of vowels. Then a third answer, from below, from beneath the floor, from the water itself, a long bubbling call that breaks the surface in a ring of small bubbles.

There are dwellers here.

You see the first one when you round the next corner. It is the size of a child sitting. Its skin is the color of the dark stone, and it is sitting in a shallow pool in the exact center of the chamber with its legs crossed and its hands on its knees, and it is not moving except for the slow blink of its lidless eyes. It is watching you. It has been watching you for some time, you understand, the way you understand that a thing was there before you noticed it. It blinks. It tilts its head a fraction. It opens its mouth and the call you have been hearing comes out of it, low and long, up at the end, and from somewhere else in the dark the answer comes back.

It does not approach. You do not approach. There is an understanding between you that is older than approach. It is the understanding of two things that have noticed each other across a distance and have decided, for now, that the distance is correct.

There are more. In the next chamber, three of them, standing at the edge of a deeper pool, each holding a long pole of pale wood that they dip into the water and lift out. On the end of each pole is a small net, and in the net is a pale wriggling thing like the banded things in the channels, only larger, the size of a forearm. They are fishing. They have been fishing for a long time. The poles are worn smooth where their hands hold them. The nets are patched in many places with fibers of different colors.

Their tools. The first tools you have seen in the place. They are simple. A pole, a net, a short blade of sharpened stone tied to a wooden handle with a strip of something cured and pale. The blade is the color of the slate-blue stone. They do not look at you. They are aware of you. The way you are aware, in a room, of where the door is.

---

You walk further.

The chambers repeat and vary. The proportions shift. Sometimes a chamber is a long nave with a single channel running down the center, and you walk along the raised stone beside it like a path beside a stream. Sometimes a chamber is small, a cell, with a low bench carved from a single block of the iron-red stone, and a niche in the wall with a thing in it you do not look at closely. Sometimes a chamber is a well, a round opening in the floor with steps going down, and from below you hear the louder roar of the water, and the smell rises up at you, mineral and warm, and the air that comes up is warmer still, and you can feel, on your face, the slow pulse of something vast.

You go down some of the wells. You come up out of others. In one, the steps take you to a chamber where the water is knee-deep and the ceiling is low enough that the dwellers in it must crouch. There are many of them here, and small pale things moving in the water around their legs, and they are doing something with the things in the water, something patient and repetitive, sorting them by size or by color or by some quality only they can see. They sing while they work. The song is the sound you have been hearing, the long low calls, the questions and answers, and up close it is a music that is not music in the way you usually mean music. It does not have melody. It has *weight*. Each note sits on the previous one and the previous one sits on the one before, and the pile of them is a structure you could almost walk on, almost live in, the way the hall itself is a structure you can walk in and live in.

A child — a small dweller, half the size of the others, with the same lidless eyes and banded skin — comes and stands near you in the water. It looks up at you. It holds out, in its small dark hand, one of the pale wriggling things from the water. It is offering it to you. It is offering it the way you would offer a guest the best thing in the house, the way you would offer a guest a thing you were proud of. You take it. It is warm. It pulses gently in your hand. The child watches you with an expression that is not quite expectation, not quite patience, something older than both. You do not know what to do with it. You hold it. The child nods, once, slowly, as if something has been confirmed, and goes back to its work.

---

The place is made. You keep understanding this. Every wall, every channel, every lintel and step and bench is the work of hands, and the hands are here, and the hands have been here for a long time, and the work is still going on, and the work will go on for a long time after you. The masons cut the stone and fitted the stone and carved the marks above the lintels and they did not sign their names because they did not need to. Their names were the place. The place is their name.

But the place is also not made. The moss is not made. The water is not made. The things in the water, the things in the walls, the things hanging from the ceiling, the dwellers themselves, banded and blinking — none of these are made. They have grown into the made thing, and the made thing has grown around them, and the two have become, over a long time, a single thing that is neither. The place is a body. The place is a building. The place is a body-building. The dwellers live in it the way cells live in a lung, the way blood lives in a vessel, the way breath lives in a throat.

You have been here for a long time. You are not sure how long. The light has not changed. The temperature has not changed. The water on your feet has not dried. But you have a sense, in your stomach, that a long time has passed, and that when you go back up through the fissure, the grass outside will be a different length, or the wind will be from a different direction, or the season will have turned while you were down here, the way seasons turn in a single night in the stories.

The dwellers have accepted you. Not welcomed — accepted, the way a body accepts a warmth, the way a wall accepts a sound. You are in the place. The place knows you are in it. The child gave you the pale wriggling thing and you are still holding it and it is still warm and it has not struggled. It is resting in your hand. It is being held. This is what it was made for, or this is what it grew for, or these are the same thing here.

You sit on one of the low benches in one of the small cells, in a chamber where the light from the wall is a little brighter and the sound of the water is a little softer, and you hold the thing in your hand and you listen to the dwellers sing in the chamber below, and you breathe the warm mineral breath, and you are inside the body of something older than the hill above you, and the body is letting you stay, and you are letting it stay around you, and the two of you are agreeing, in the dark, on a distance that is correct.
