The train is late. You already know this before you arrive, because the platform is full of people pretending not to know it, arranged in that particular posture of working-class patience — leaning but not sitting, reading but not absorbing, watching the dark mouth of the tunnel as if it might confess something. The arrival board has been cycling through a number you stopped believing in twenty minutes ago.

You came up from the low country, and the climb has been the whole story.

Out the window for the past hour has been this: a valley-shoulder pressed against the sky, terraced and sloped, the city's older neighborhoods climbing the hillsides in stacked stone and rust-stained concrete. Above them, the bare ridges. Below them, a river — swollen, slow, the color of a bruise healing — threading through the floodplain in a series of slow deliberate bends. Between the water and the city there are marshes, and between the marshes and the city there are the train yards, and between the train yards and the city there is the city.

That's the right way to put it. The city arrived last.

This is a warm place. Warm and wet. The air that comes off the river in the late afternoon is the kind you wear rather than breathe — it settles on your forearms, it blurs the edges of things, it puts a film on the screen of your phone you keep wiping away. Somewhere up on the slopes the rain forest begins, what's left of it, and the clouds come down to meet it. By five o'clock the upper neighborhoods will be inside the cloud. You can see them now, half-swallowed, the tallest of the older buildings emerging from the gray like the tops of standing stones.

The sound is the first thing you noticed when you stepped off the connecting line, and it has not stopped being the first thing. It is not a pleasant sound. It is not the sound of the place *being* — it is the sound of the place *working*, which is different, which is louder, which never modulates. A continuous roar that lives somewhere behind the teeth rather than in front of them. Foundries, you are told. The whole eastern valley is given over to the foundry district, and the foundries have been there for a hundred years, and the city has been built around them, and the people who live nearest them have the highest rates of something they call *the respiratory*, which is the word everyone uses, as if naming it by its full name would make it more present than it already is.

You can see the foundries from here. Not well — they sit below the river bend, behind a screen of eucalyptus planted, you read somewhere on a placard you no longer remember the location of, in the 1940s to mitigate the dust. The trees are sixty meters tall now and the dust still gets through. Beyond the trees the sky has a permanent orange undertone at that altitude, as if the sun is setting into something that is not the horizon. At night — you are told, you have not yet seen it — the clouds above the foundry district glow.

But here, on the platform, you are above that. The platform sits on a viaduct. From it you can look down on most of the city, which is a useful position, and also a particular kind of trap, because it shows you how the city is arranged.

It is arranged in folds.

The colonial center is at the river's edge — the old port, the granite customs house, the cobbled plaza where the tram still runs in a tight oval past the cathedral. That part is dense and low and square, built before anyone was thinking about what the river would do in a hundred-year flood. (The river has done it three times. There are plaques. There are also buildings with waterlines at the second-story windowsill, just kept.) From the center, the city climbs. It climbs in waves of construction, each wave a different material, each material a different decade. The 1880s gave it yellow brick, soot-blackened at the cornices. The early 1900s gave it the pressed-concrete wedding-cake style, all balusters and swags, which has aged strangely into something that looks almost biological, like calcified coral. The mid-century gave it the gray slab blocks, brutal in their honest way, full of birds. And then there is the new construction, the glass-and-cladding of the past fifteen years, going up on the few remaining empty lots, on the demolished older buildings whose ghosts still haunt the zoning maps, on the rooftops of the 1880s buildings whose owners needed the rent.

It's a city of seven or eight hundred thousand. The basin holds maybe that many. Beyond the basin, the slopes. On the slopes, the *barrios altos* — the high neighborhoods, which have their own climate, their own dialect, their own altitude-adjusted cuisine of heavier grains and preserved meats. You can take a funicular up to them. You can also walk. The walk takes an hour and a half and gains you six hundred meters of elevation, and halfway up you pass through the cloud layer and everything goes white and you hear nothing and then you emerge into a different kind of green, the high green, the moss-and-fern-and-wet-bark green, the green of a forest that has decided to take its time about being a forest.

There is wet here in many ways. There is the wet of the river, which is fed by rains that fall on the mountains you cannot see today and will never see from the city. There is the wet of the marshes, which the city has been trying to drain for two centuries and has not, because the city does not have the upper hand. There is the wet of the air, which is constant, which is the medium in which this city exists, which the architecture admits to — every older building has a deep eave, a covered gallery, an inward-facing courtyard where the rain is invited in and then ignored. There is the wet of the pipes, which are old, which are made of several different materials depending on what decade a given section was last opened, and which lose something like thirty percent of what they carry before it arrives. There is the wet that comes off you, in this weather, the perspiration that does not evaporate because there is nothing for it to evaporate into.

And there is the wet inside the city. The water table is high. You can see this in the basements of the older buildings, which are always half-flooded and have been turned into other things — storage, mushroom farms, in one case a small chapel with the Virgin standing ankle-deep in permanent water, her blue robe stained brown at the hem. In the lowest neighborhoods, near the river, certain streets flood when it rains heavily, and the residents have built the ground floors of their houses to be sacrificial, with entryways at the second-story level accessed by external stairs. These are not slums, exactly. They are *adaptations*.

The fauna is sparse. There are dogs, of course. There are the small brown birds that live in the eucalyptus and come into the city at dusk in undulating clouds, the size of them registering on radar as weather. There are the rats, who have a stable arrangement with the city. There are, in the foundry district, the green parakeets, which arrived as escaped pets sometime in the 1970s and have become a pest and a delight; they are deafening at certain hours; they nest in the cooling towers and the unfinished buildings; their calls blend with the foundry noise in a way that has, over decades, become a single sound. You will get used to it. You will, in three days, not be able to separate the parakeets from the foundries in your memory.

The trains here are electric, drawn up the steepest grades by a rack-and-pinion system installed in the 1920s by engineers from the country you have come from. The cars are old. They have wooden slat seats, brass fittings that have been polished by decades of hands, windows that you have to push open with your shoulder, advertisements for products you have never heard of and will never see for sale. The third rail is exposed, which means you cannot stand too close to the platform edge, which means there are yellow lines, which means there are announcements about the yellow lines, which means there is the faint electric ozone smell that is the actual smell of the city's circulatory system. The train will come. The train is coming. The train is, eventually, here.

What you have not yet learned, and will learn only by living here, is that this city was built for a river that no longer exists in the form it was built for. The original river course was different. The marshes are what was once the riverbed. The floodplain is what was once the river. The city grew into the shape of a river that kept moving. The relationship between the city and the water is older than the relationship between the city and the nation, older than the language most of its citizens speak, and is conducted in a register that the city understands and the planners do not. The city knows where the water will go. The city has always known. The planners, with their drainage projects and their concrete channelization, are tolerated the way a person is tolerated who has misunderstood something fundamental about you but is trying to help.

The light is the light of a place that has chosen not to be brightly lit. The latitude is low. The sun is high overhead at noon and never really sets cleanly — it sinks into the cloud, into the haze above the foundry district, into the wet air, and the day does not end so much as dilute itself into an evening that lasts an hour and a half before it becomes a morning of the same general character. There is no darkness here in the deep sense. The sodium lights of the colonial center, the LED of the new construction, the foundry glow — all of it combines into a permanent low-level amber that is reflected off the low cloud and returned to the streets as a kind of warm residue. You will sleep with a cloth over your eyes, eventually, or you will not sleep well. There is no third option.

And there is the strange. The strange is the slow strange, the strange that has settled into the place over the centuries and become its orthodoxy. The city has a cathedral, but the cathedral has a tree growing out of its bell tower. The city has a tram, but the tram passes through a section of tunnel that is also a sewer, and the windows fog up in a particular way, and no one speaks of it. The city has a museum of pre-Columbian gold, and the museum sits two blocks from the foundry, and the security guards tell each other that the gold has begun, in some way they cannot specify, to sweat. The parakeets. The waterlines on the second-story windowsills, kept. The river, moving slowly, the color of a bruise healing, carrying silt that is, if you took a microscope to it, partly the eroded residue of the buildings that have fallen into it over the centuries, the city feeding the river, the river feeding the marshes, the marshes feeding the clouds, the clouds feeding the city. The cycle is not a metaphor. It is a plumbing diagram.

The train is here.

It is the old wooden train, with its brass fittings and its advertisements, and it pulls in with the sound of an electric motor that has been singing the same note for fifty years, and the doors open with the particular pneumatic sigh of a thing that is weary of opening, and you step aboard, and the doors sigh again, and the train moves, and the platform recedes, and you are taken up into the folds of the city, into the noise, into the wet, into the warm.

You will arrive where you are going in fourteen minutes. You will, eventually, leave. But the city will not. The city is the slow thing, the long-eroded thing, the thing that has settled into its own balance and will continue, patiently, in its own time, with its river moving, its foundries glowing, its parakeets screaming, its cathedral tree growing.

You step aboard. The doors close. The train begins to climb.
