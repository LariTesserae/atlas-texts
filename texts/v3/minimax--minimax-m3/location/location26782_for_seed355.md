# The City at the Waterline

The light here is wrong. It does not come from anywhere. It does not fall — it *sits*, low against the surfaces of things, gathering in the hollows of streets like dark water finding its level. The air is thick and the heat comes up through it from below, and the water comes up through it from below too, and it is hard to tell, sometimes, which is which.

This was a city once. It is still a city — there is no moment you can point to and say *here, here it became otherwise*. The buildings stand in regular intervals along streets that mean something geometric, even if what they mean has drifted. The walls are coral-colored, or were. Some of them have softened. The edges of doorframes are not quite where they were. If you went away and came back you would say the rooms had grown a little larger, or the windows a little smaller, and you would not be able to prove which.

The water is everywhere and shallow. It lies across the plazas in flat planes that reflect nothing because there is nothing above to reflect — only the low bruised light that does not travel in straight lines. It pools in courtyards and climbs the lower stories of buildings and at night, or what passes for night here (it is always night here, but the dark has gradients, it has weather of its own), the water takes on a faint dim glow from somewhere beneath its skin. Not bioluminescence. Something less decided than that. The light has no source. It is the water's idea.

There is vegetation, but it is not lush. It is *committed*. Things grow in the gutters where the water collects — long pale stems with no leaves, fans of dark fungus that look almost architectural, and a kind of vine that climbs the walls in patterns too regular to be chance. The vines grow in spirals. Always spirals, always the same number of turns, always terminating at the same height whether the building is tall or short. Nobody planted them. They have always been here, or they were here before the buildings, or the buildings were here before them and the order is no longer recoverable.

The streets are mostly empty. The density of inhabitants — or presences, or whatever the figures one occasionally sees moving at the far ends of avenues should be called — is low. Whatever walks here walks slowly and alone and at the edges of vision. You do not see their faces. You see their silhouettes dark against the dimmer dark behind them. They appear to be going somewhere. If you follow them you find they have arrived at no place you can name. Sometimes there is a sound — the soft displacement of water around a leg that may not be there. Sometimes only the implication of one.

There are no animals. There have never been animals. There were never any birds; the air is too heavy for birds to lift through, or there is no air for them, only the warm thick medium that presses on the skin and never moves unless something passes through it. The water does not ripple without cause. The vines do not sway. The buildings do not creak. The sound that exists here is mostly the sound of processes — the slow tick of heat rising through a column of wet air, the occasional distant sound of a wall settling into its foundation by another centimeter, the sound the fungi make when they push a new frond up through a crack in the stone. None of this is loud. None of it stops.

The erosion here is *complete* but ongoing. Things are settled into their final forms and continuing to settle. The walls that were once straight have the patient curve of old riverbanks. The roofs of the taller buildings have a slight sag at their centers, as though they have been raining something heavy down upon themselves for a very long time — though nothing falls from above. The streets have no gutters because the streets themselves are gutters. Water flows along them in shallow channels no longer than the buildings they pass, and then it disappears into cracks, and then it comes up somewhere else as the low glow, as the fog, as the warmth on the inside of your wrist when you rest your hand against a wall.

The reality of this place is *having a difficult time*. Not collapsing — it is too old and too patient for collapse. But it is forgetting itself in places. A door you saw yesterday is in a different wall today. A stairwell descends further than the building is deep, and there is no light down there, only the dimmer dark, and the heat, and a faint sound of water in a place that should be dry. A window opens onto a room you have been in before, but the room is now on the other side of the city, or above it, or below it. There is no way to confirm.

The hot wet air moves. Not as wind — there is no wind — but as slow currents that you can feel pass over you, carrying the smell of warm stone and something almost sweet, almost mineral, almost alive but not quite. The water is the same temperature as your skin and you cannot tell where you end and the surface begins.

It is quiet here. It has been quiet for a very long time. The quiet is not empty. It is full of small sounds and the sounds are all the sounds there are and none of them repeat and none of them stop.
