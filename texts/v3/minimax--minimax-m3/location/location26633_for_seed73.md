# The Plateau of the Slow Hum

You arrive by way of the wind.

The path up is longer than the path down, and at the top the world drops away in every direction. There is no gradual slope. The rock simply ends, and below the ends is a sea of cloud that has not, in any living memory, broken. You are standing on the roof of something. The air is thin and warm — warmer than it has any right to be at this height. Your lungs take it in and your body still feels a kind of hunger for it, as if the air is the kind of thing you can taste. You can. It is wet, mineral, faintly sweet, the breath of a stone that has been cooking in its own heat for a very long time.

The plateau is broad. It takes half a day to walk across, if you walk it directly, and no one walks it directly. The shape of it is the shape of a cracked table — flat on top, but cut through with ravines and sinkholes and shallow bowls where the rock has given way to itself. The stone is dark, almost black, and in the early morning it sweats. Condensation beads on every surface, and you could drink from a flat rock if you laid your face to it. The wetness is not from rain, though rain comes. Rain comes in warm curtains, sudden and total, and then stops, and then the sun comes back, and the air thickens until you can see the moisture in it as a faint silver shimmer over the ravines. Everything is always wet. Everything is always warm. Nothing dries, and yet nothing rots in the way you'd expect — the patient, sluggish rotting of things that have given up. Things here are still arguing with their own decay.

The wind is the loud thing. The wind is the only loud thing, and it is constant. It comes off the plateau edges and falls, and the falling air makes a sound — a low, sustained, kettle-not-yet-singing hum, a vibration that lives in your back teeth. On the western edge the sound is deeper, almost a moan, and the rock there is worn in long horizontal grooves as if the air itself has been rubbing at the stone for centuries. The grooves are warm to the touch. The whole plateau is warm to the touch. Inside the stone, somewhere, a heat that does not come from the sun is still working. You can feel it through the soles of your feet.

Vegetation has taken the rock and ignored it. The trees grow in clusters, then in groves, then in a single broad forest that covers the central plateau so completely that the rock is only visible in the ravines. The trunks are pale — bone-colored, almost white — and smooth, and they grow close enough to touch. The canopy is interlocking, a roof of dark leaves. Underneath, the light is green, and the air is stiller but not quiet, because of the insects. Beneath the leaves, the wind still hums, muffled. Above them, the wind is the whole world.

When the wind comes hard, the leaves turn over. The underside of every leaf is silver, and the whole forest inverts at once, flashing, a shoal of fish turning in the same second. It is beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when you don't know why they do it. The trees may be doing it on purpose. No one has ever been sure.

Underfoot, the ground is deep and soft, a centuries-deep mattress of moss and fallen leaves and the spongy remains of older things. Ferns rise to your waist. Mushrooms grow in rings and in rows that aren't quite rings, and the mushrooms glow at night — a pale, blue-green light that is not bright enough to read by but is bright enough to see the shapes of things moving. The glow is the light from below. There is always a light from below, here, somewhere, in the pools, in the cracks, in the things the insects sing to.

The fauna is everywhere. Insects are the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing. They are not quite cicadas. They chirr in overlapping cycles, and the cycles don't all start at the same time, so there is no moment of silence, only a constant woven hum of living things, a sound like a small bright machine that is always almost finished and never is. Larger things move in the trees. You can see them as shadows when the leaves turn. Something with very long legs steps from branch to branch as if it has never been in a hurry. Something with wings, much larger than a bird should reasonably be, crosses a clearing and is gone. There is no way to count the species. The ecologists who have tried came back with new words.

The structures are scattered. That is the first thing. The second thing is that they don't seem to belong to anything. There are walls that have no doors, and doors that have no walls. There are platforms that rise from the rock with no steps and no way up. There are stones arranged in circles, and lines of small cairns, and once in a while a pillar that stands alone for no reason. They are made of the same dark stone as the plateau, or something very like it, and their edges are too sharp, too perfect, and they do not erode. You can carve your initials in a wall and come back a year later and find them as fresh as the day you made them. The rock around them, by contrast, is soft with weather. Whatever the structures are made of, the weather has not been told.

If you touch them, they hum. The hum matches the hum of the wind at the cliff edge, and travels up your arm and into your chest, and for a moment your heart tries to fall in with it. Sometimes, for a moment, it does. The moment passes. The hum goes on.

The pools are at the bottoms of the ravines. They are warm, and shallow enough to wade in. The water tastes faintly of iron. If you look down into them on certain days, light comes up from somewhere that is not the sky, and you can see shapes moving slowly in the dark below — long slow shapes, like things thinking. They don't come up. You have been down there, once, and what you saw you have not described.

Time here is slow. This is the thing that is hardest to explain to people who have not been. The day passes at the speed of a day. The sun crosses the sky. The shadows do what shadows do. But the days themselves are thicker. A wall that took a century to be built here would take a century to fall. A tree that fell when your grandfather was young is still half-rotted, still holding its shape, still wearing a few leaves. The pools change color over a season — green to blue to black to green again, in a cycle no one has explained. A door you walk through today will be in the same place tomorrow. A door you walk through tomorrow, if it is a different door, may not be.

The strange is small here, and persistent, and it does not announce itself. A line of cairns that, when you count them, never matches. A door that opens onto the same room from both sides, though the room is round. A single high pure tone that comes sometimes from the rock itself, and is gone before you are sure you heard it. The sound does not fit with the wind or the insects. The sound seems to come from above. The sound seems to be patient.

This is the plateau. It is not a place you visit. It is a place that has been waiting, and you have arrived at the part of the waiting when it permits itself to be seen.
