The light arrives without warmth. It is the cool white of a polar morning, persistent and sourceless even when the sun is visible — a flat plate of illumination that gives every surface the same luminosity and refuses to let any shadow pool. The lowland spreads beneath this light to every horizon, and it is not empty. It is so filled it could almost be solid.

There is no water. The eye expects it — a river, a canal, a glistening runoff from the unseen towers — and finds instead a dry mineral skin, pale tan, cracked into polygons like the bed of an evaporated sea that never had a sea. The cracks are narrow and precise. Nothing flows. Yet the world is green, or what passes for green here: a deep lacquered olive, a pewter-sage, a near-black that drinks the light. The color of olive trees under snow.

The plants are the substrate. They are also the buildings, the roads, the engines, the smokestacks. There is no other material. Where one might expect a wall there is a bole — a trunk so wide that a man walking its circumference would be gone for an afternoon. The bark is worked: planed, jointed, pegged with wooden pegs into a surface so regular it could be plank. But it is not plank. It breathes. It swells and contracts with the day. New growth laps over old peg-holes like a tide.

Walking through the lowland is walking through a thing that is also a workshop. There are shapes here that the eye registers as built before it registers as grown: a long rectangular hall of woven trunks, each trunk curved into a beam and lashed with braided vine; a chimney-stack rising eighty feet, its top crowned with a flat cap of splayed leaves that should be belching smoke but instead exhales a faint dry pollen-smell; a gear the size of a house, its cogs interlocking at three depths, turning slowly without oil — the teeth, polished by use, are polished wood, and the gear has been turning for what looks like centuries.

It turns anyway. There is no one watching.

The coolness holds. It is the coolness of deep shade on a sunny day, but here the shade is total — the canopy above is not leaf but membrane: sheets of pressed fiber, woven at some level the eye cannot follow, into a translucent skin that admits the bright light but never the heat. Underneath, the air sits at a permanent cool. There is no winter here. There may not be a summer.

Above the canopy, on the rare open days when a gap is cut or a sheet fails, the sky is the same color as the sky above any plain: an even milky blue, polarized at the horizon, slightly too bright. Below it, the carpet of leaves is so thick that rain — when it comes, which is never, or only in a form the unaccustomed eye would not call rain — never reaches the floor.

This is not a forest. The word forest assumes a separation: tree here, ground there. Here the tree is the ground. Roots become floorboards. Roots become roads. The main arteries are old roots, thickened, paved over with newer growth — each generation laid on top of the last like cobblestones, until the road is a laminated loaf of woody strata three meters thick and still growing at the bottom. To dig a road is impossible. To pave it requires only patience and a chisel. The road heals itself overnight; by morning the chisel-mark is buried.

The buildings are not separate from the roads. A trunk broadens into a wall, a wall arches into a roof, a roof descends into a floor that is also the next road. The whole is a single body. An inhabitant moves through it the way a cell moves through a body — along channels that were laid down before any cell existed and will be here when the last is gone.

The engines are inside this body. They are not hidden. They are simply grown around. There is a loom here — a hundred meters long — whose frame is a single living branch bent into shape and buttressed with new shoots. It weaves with no thread: the warp and weft are extruded from the branch itself, a continuous fiber that hardens on contact with the cool dry air. What it weaves is rope. Where the rope goes is a question no one answers, because no one is here to be asked, or the ones here do not think to answer, or the rope goes to other ropes and the question is the wrong shape.

There are pumps. There must be pumps — the system moves. The fluid is not water. The pipes are the hollow stems of a cane-like growth that stands in stands like bamboo. The fluid is a thick amber resin that the cane expresses and that the pumps push. It runs slow. It runs cold. It carries heat away from somewhere and into somewhere else. The pipes do not leak. The pipes are alive; they grow into their own joints; they close around any breach with a slow, purposeful thrust.

The light that reaches the floor is green. Everything below is green and gold and shadow. The eye adjusts and finds the gold in the lichen-furred undersides of the membrane-leaves, in the polished tracks of the gear, in the resin itself when it pools in a junction-box. The eye finds the green in everything else. After a day here the eye is no longer trustworthy. Things farther away than a hundred meters cannot be focused. They smear. They repeat. A pillar in the middle distance and a pillar farther and another — each appears as the same pillar, or as a pillar that has been here before. The brain gives up the question of distance.

This is the first sign that the world is not obeying its own rules.

The second sign is the workers. There are workers. They are not numerous — one to several acres, a figure glimpsed, a shape moving between pillars — but they are present. They wear wooden clothes: cuirasses of bark stitched with vine, helmets of hollowed gourd, gloves of laminated leaf. Their faces cannot be seen. Their faces are the same color as the wood. They do not speak. They do not make a sound. They move between the great trunks carrying tools that are also growths: a hammer with a wooden head grown around a stone; a saw that is the tooth of a larger creature, donated; a wrench that is a thickened tendril, already curling to fit the nut it has never seen. The workers set these against the machines. The machines answer. A bolt is turned. A gear resumes. A pipe pulses.

The workers do not eat. They are not seen to sleep. When one stops, in the middle of a hall, looking at nothing, another walks around it. After a while the stopped one begins again. They are patient the way the wood is patient. They have been here as long as the wood.

The world is almost silent. A sound exists below the threshold of attention: the creak of wood under its own weight as it grows, a continuous tiny protest that resolves, when one listens, into a single low note that does not change. Above it, nothing. No birdsong — the fauna are rare and elsewhere. No wind at floor level — the membrane canopy diffuses any breeze above into stillness. The only other sounds are occasional: a thud, far off, of a trunk settling into its new footing; a creak louder than the others, signaling a joint somewhere adjusting itself; and once, on a still afternoon, the slow grind of the great gear picking up speed for no apparent reason and then slowing, like a sleeping animal breathing. The sound fades. The low note resumes.

The light, meanwhile, does not fade. It is cool and bright at morning, cool and bright at noon, cool and bright at dusk. The eye cannot tell when the day ends. The workers do not stop. The world does not sleep. It is suspected, by anyone who lives here long enough to suspect anything, that there is no night. The membrane canopy at dusk becomes the membrane canopy at dawn becomes the membrane canopy at noon. The sun, if it goes, goes behind a sky that remains the same.

But there are moments when the light flickers. Not dimly — fully. One moment the floor is bright green, and the next it is bright green again, but in between, for a fraction of a second, the light comes from below. The floor glows. The underside of every leaf, every pillar, every rope glows a faint pale gold. Then it is gone. The workers do not react. The machines do not pause. It happens twice a day. It happens more often than twice a day, if one stays. It happens whenever one looks away.

This is the second sign that the world is not obeying its own rules.

The light that comes from below is not the same light. It is older. It moves differently — it does not cast shadows in the same direction; it casts shadows outward from the source, as if the floor were the sun. Things in that light appear briefly as their own negatives, and then resume. The buildings remain. The roads remain. The workers continue. But in the negative flash, in the fraction of the second the floor is bright, the eye registers: the world is hollow. Below the floor of laminated roots there is another floor. Below that another. The lowland extends downward as much as it extends outward. It is a stack. It is a forest turned on its side, roots below, canopy above, repeating.

What is down there? The workers do not go down. There are no stairs. There are no lifts. There are hatches in the floor — wooden doors set flush with the laminated cobble — that open, sometimes, of their own accord, and close again. Through a hatch one can see a space below that is the same as the space above: pillars, membrane-leaves, the cool green light, the slow amber pulse of the resin-pipes, perhaps a worker, perhaps a gear. The hatch is not a passage. It is a window onto the same world, repeated.

It is this that the worker, stopped, looking at nothing, is looking at. Through the floor. Through all the floors. At the bottom of the stack, where there is said to be — by the workers, who do not speak, in the only way they communicate, which is to occasionally pause and look downward — a heart.

The heart is the engine that does not need to be wound. The heart is the loom that weaves with no thread. The heart is the pump that moves the resin. The heart is the gear that turns when no one watches. The heart is grown from the bottom up; the world is its body; the workers are its cells. The cool light is its temperature. The bright light is its waking. The silence is its listening.

One does not go down to the heart. The hatch closes.

The world continues. The low note continues. The great gear turns. The resin pulses. The workers, rare and patient, set their grown tools to grown machines. The plants grow where the machines end. The machines grow where the plants begin. Nothing distinguishes them. Nothing needs to.

The eye, by now, has stopped asking. The body, cool and lit and quiet, walks the laminated road between pillars of olive-black bark, under a canopy that admits only light and never weather, past a loom it will never understand, toward a horizon that is also a wall, that is also a tree, that is also alive.

There is no water here. There is no need.
