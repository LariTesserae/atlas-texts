# The Lung

It is a place the size of a closet, or a chapel — no one has ever been able to settle on which. The chamber exists below the surface of something, though "below" may be the wrong word. The walls curve inward as though the place were breathing out, holding that exhalation forever, a frozen lungful of air that has forgotten how to be inhaled again. Everything is still here. Everything has been still for so long that stillness has become a kind of substance, thick enough to wade through, almost visible in the way it bends the light.

The light is the first thing that betrays the place. It pours in from everywhere and nowhere, a sourceless radiance that arrives already diffused, already warm, already having traveled. It catches on the walls in a way that makes the stone look like it's perspiring. The rock itself is young — barely rock at all, more an ambition of rock, a draft of itself. The surfaces are ridged and folded like the inside of a shell that hasn't finished calcifying. You can press your thumb into the softest folds and they will take the print, and tomorrow the print will still be there, because tomorrow is a concept that hasn't quite been introduced. The stone is a proposal. In a thousand years it might be granite, or marble, or something no one has named. For now it is just beginning to be itself, and the chamber is just beginning to be a chamber, and the two facts are the same fact.

But the chamber is packed. Oh, the chamber is impossibly, unreasonably packed, for a place that is barely a place, that is still deciding what shape to settle into. Every inch of every surface is occupied. This is where the strangeness lives, and the strangeness is total.

The walls are furred with a kind of growth that isn't quite a plant and isn't quite an animal and isn't quite a mineral. It has the color of old coral and the texture of wet felt and the behavior of nothing that has ever been catalogued. It grows in tight rosettes, each rosette no larger than a coin, and the rosettes press against each other without gaps, without the courtesy of a millimeter between them, so that the entire wall becomes a single breathing carpet of these small folded organisms. They pulse. Not visibly, or not quite — but if you sit very still (and you will sit very still, because the place demands it) you can feel the pulse through the stone, a slow collective throb that is the only clock the chamber has. The pulse is the same beat everywhere, every rosette in sync, a single heart the size of a room. When you finally notice it, you cannot stop noticing it, and you realize it has been the loudest thing in the chamber all along, louder than the sound.

The sound. The sound is not the pulse. The sound is everything else. The sound is a steady bright roar, the way a conch shell roars when you hold it to your ear, except the conch shell is the size of a cathedral and the ocean inside it is the ocean of a place still being born. Water moves somewhere in the chamber, though you cannot always see it. It moves in sheets along the ceiling and gathers in beads on the underside of every overhang and falls, in slow fat droplets, onto the growth below. The growth drinks it. The droplets are loud when they land — impossibly loud, each one a small bell-strike, a clear bright *tok* that rings and rings against the soft pulsing undercurrent. There must be a thousand droplets falling every second, and each one rings, and none of them overlap, and the sound is a pure sustained chord, a chord made of water and stone and a kind of attention.

In the center of the chamber, the growth climbs upward in a column. It is the only vertical thing. It rises from the floor to the ceiling and beyond — the ceiling opens for it, the way a forest canopy opens for a strangler fig, and the column keeps going up into a darkness that is the only darkness in the place, a darkness that drinks the light and gives back nothing. The column is denser than the wall-growth, more tightly packed, and the rosettes on it are smaller, and they are not all the same color. Some are the color of old coral. Some are the color of bone. Some are almost black, and the black ones are the oldest, you understand, the founders, the ones that were here before the chamber knew it was a chamber. The black ones do not pulse. They are finished pulsing. They are the still point that the rest of the column organizes itself around, the way iron filings organize themselves around a magnet.

Creatures live here. They are not large. They are not small. They are the exact size of the spaces between the rosettes, because that is what evolution has made them, in a place where there are no other spaces. They are flat and pale and they move the way that water moves between the growth, in thin obedient sheets, and they eat the condensation where it gathers, and they are eaten in turn by something smaller that lives inside them that lives inside something smaller still, a recursion of mouths that has no obvious bottom. When one of them dies, its body becomes a rosette. This is not a metaphor. You can watch it happen if you are willing to sit very still, and the place will make you willing. The pale flat creature slows, and its edges curl, and over the course of what might be an hour or might be a day — time is unreliable here, the pulse of the growth is the only clock and it does not subdivide — the body rounds itself into a disc, and the disc thickens, and the disc unfolds into a rosette of the wall-growth, and joins the pulse, and is indistinguishable from its neighbors within a single beat. The chamber is not losing anything. The chamber is only changing the names of its contents.

There are no tools here. There are no marks of tools. There is no suggestion that anything with hands has ever stood in this place and considered what to do with it. The chamber does not want that. The chamber wants only to be what it is, which is a place where a small flat creature becomes a rosette and a rosette becomes a wall and a wall becomes a chamber and a chamber becomes, in some distant and perhaps-imagined future, a mountain. The mountain is implicit in the stone. The mountain is the stone's ambition. The chamber is the stone's adolescence, that awkward time when a thing is too large for its previous container and not yet large enough for its next.

The brightness does not dim. The water does not stop falling. The rosettes do not stop pulsing. The pale creatures continue their slow commerce with the condensation and with each other. The sound does not end, because there is no moment at which a droplet has not just landed and is not about to land and the place has no use for silence. Silence is a later invention. Silence requires ears that are not here. The ears that are here — whatever ears the flat creatures possess, whatever sense the rosettes have of each other's pulsing — are full. They are exactly full. There is no room in them for quiet.

You sit in the chamber, if a visitor could sit in the chamber, and the chamber sits in you, and the two of you pulse together for a while, and the water falls on you, and the water is warm, and you understand that this is not a place you have found. This is a place that has allowed itself, briefly and without enthusiasm, to be found. The light is bright. The sound is loud. The growth is packed against you on every side, soft and patient and utterly indifferent to your presence, busy with the long work of becoming stone.

You leave, or you do not. The chamber does not notice either way. It will still be here when you return, which is the same as saying it will not be here, because the chamber is not a place that distinguishes between being and not-being. The chamber is a place that is in the middle of arriving. It will be finished arriving long after you are finished being. The droplets will still be falling. The rosettes will still be pulsing. The flat pale creatures will still be sliding between the folds, becoming wall, becoming chamber, becoming mountain, becoming the slow bright roar of a place that has learned to sing in a language made entirely of water on stone.
