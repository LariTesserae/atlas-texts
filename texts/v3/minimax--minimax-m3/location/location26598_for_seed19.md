The high place has been high for so long that height has lost its meaning. What was once a range of mountains — perhaps the first mountains, perhaps the last — has been ground by weather and time into a single, vast, gently tilted plane. The plateau stretches until it meets the pale sky at a horizon that is always the same distance away, regardless of how far you walk. The air is cool and thin and absolutely still, and has been still for so long that dust doesn't fall. It hangs.

There is no water. There hasn't been water here for so long that even the memory of rain is gone from the stone. What remains in the shallow basins and low places is a crust of mineral — pale salts, lime, the powder of feldspar — that has become almost geological itself, indistinguishable from the bedrock beneath. In a few protected places — the lee of a fallen slab, the inside of a deep crack — there is a faint coloration that might be growth. A pale lichen, or something that calls itself lichen. It doesn't spread. It has reached its limit and stopped. A scattering of muted green-grey on an otherwise colorless stone.

The stone is what dominates. Bone-colored, dried-milk-colored, ash-colored. Smooth in some places, so smooth that wind-polish has made it almost reflective; broken in others, where ancient joints have given way. There are a few outcrops that rise above the general surface — low humps and shoulders of harder rock — but nothing that would be called a hill. The plateau is in its final form. There is no higher form to reach. Erosion has done its work, and the work is complete.

And yet, despite the finality, despite the absence of water and the near-absence of growth, this place is not empty. It is dense. It is saturated.

The fauna is everywhere.

It hangs in the air in pale layers. It clings to the stone in pale layers. It moves — or seems to move — through cracks, through seams, through the spaces between mineral grains. There is no way to see a single creature clearly because they are too numerous and too close together, layered like the pages of a book left out in weather. If you focus on one, it dissolves back into the mass. The closest analogy is snow, but snow that doesn't fall and doesn't melt, that is alive, or was alive, or is something else for which the word "alive" is a convenience.

The creatures are pale. They are roughly the color of the stone, and in many cases they are the stone, or the stone is them. The line between animal and mineral is one of the rules that has broken down here.

Up close — though getting close is difficult, because the creatures shift slightly as you approach, not out of fear but because you have become part of their equilibrium — up close, you can see that they have shapes. Some are flat and cling to surfaces, like small pale hands pressed to glass. Some are round and hang suspended, beating with a motion so slow you might watch one for a long time before noticing. Some are long and thin and lie along cracks like tendons in a body. They are all roughly the same size, or vary in size only within a narrow band, as if the place has settled on a dimension it prefers.

They make almost no sound. If you stand very still for a long time — and time passes strangely here, so *long* is uncertain — you can hear a faint hiss, a faint creak, a faint rhythmic whisper that could be many tiny things moving at once, or could be the stone settling, or could be the hum of a place that has become something between alive and not. The sound is at the very edge of perception. You have to want to hear it.

It doesn't change.

This is the rule of the place, the rule above all rules. The creatures hang where they hang. The lichen grows where it grew. The shadows of the low outcrops lie in the same direction at the same length from one day to the next — or what passes for day, because the light is even, the sky is pale, and there is no sun visible, only a uniform luminance that comes from everywhere above. The wind does not blow. The temperature does not shift. A stone placed on the surface of another stone will remain perched there for as long as the eye can follow, and if you return to the same place — if returning means anything here — it will be in the same position.

The plateau has reached equilibrium. The creatures have reached equilibrium. Even the dust, which hangs in pale layers above the stone, has reached equilibrium. Nothing is in the process of becoming. Everything is in the state of having become.

The structures of the inhabitants — if there were inhabitants, if the word fits — are scattered across the plateau as if placed there by a slow, deliberate hand. There are cairns, low piles of flat stone balanced one on another, sometimes with a larger capstone, sometimes with nothing. There are low walls that don't quite enclose anything, single-course walls that begin and end as if the builder lost interest, or as if the wall was never meant to enclose but only to mark. There is, in one place, the remains of a shelter: four low stone walls and a partial roof of flat slabs, the kind of structure that could be made with hand tools, with patience, with stone hammer and bone wedge and perhaps a small bronze blade for the fine work. The shelter is partly collapsed. Tools are scattered around it — a stone axe head, a bone needle, a bronze knife with a wooden handle that has long since gone to powder. There are no bodies. There are no signs that anyone left. The shelter simply stopped being maintained, or was never finished, or is finished in a way that doesn't require maintenance because nothing here decays.

The tools are primitive, hand-made, simple-fabricated. They are the right size for hands. The hands that made them are gone, or have become part of the place in some other way.

And the place — the place is broken.

Not violently. Not dramatically. Broken in the way that a pane of glass is broken when the crack runs through it but the pieces haven't fallen apart: still holding shape, but no longer trustworthy. Geometry here is approximate. Two observers standing some distance apart, looking at the same outcrop, will not agree on its shape, or its size, or the number of stones in it. Distances are uncertain. A walk that should take an hour takes two hours, or half an hour, or doesn't finish. The horizon, when you look at it, seems to be at the correct distance for the curve of a much smaller world — closer than it should be, as if the plateau is sitting on the inside of a bowl rather than the outside of a sphere. Shadows fall slightly to the left of where they should, or don't fall at all in some places, or fall upward. The creatures, when watched, seem to be on the near side of the stone and the far side at the same time. Light arrives without having traveled.

This is the place. The high place, the old place, the final place. Cool and dry and pale and dense with life that is not quite life, broken in its geometry, quiet in its sound, frozen in its becoming. A place where the mountain range ended and something else began, and that something else has now ended too, and what remains is the after-shape of ending — still faintly alive, still faintly moving, suspended in a state that is not death but is not anything else either.

If you walk across it, you leave no footprints. The dust closes behind you. If you sit, you find that the creatures settle around you without touching, at a distance that feels respectful, and you become part of the equilibrium, and the place accepts you as it accepts itself, and nothing changes.
