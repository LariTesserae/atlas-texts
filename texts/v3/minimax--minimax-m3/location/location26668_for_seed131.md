A chamber the size of a small chapel, hidden in the side of an embankment. You reach it the way you reach all forgotten infrastructure — through an opening that was meant to be a drain, a culvert mouth, a gap where the wall meets the water. The country around it is low and flat and soaked. The water table is just under the ground. The grass outside is the kind of grass that grows in standing water.

Everything inside is made. The floor is poured concrete, the walls are poured concrete, the ceiling is poured concrete, and the pump in the middle is cast iron and steel. None of it is new. All of it is older than anyone you would ask. But it has stopped getting older. The pitting on the walls is pitting that has finished. The rust on the railings is rust that has settled into a stable oxide and will not go further. The mineral stains running down the walls have reached the lowest point they will reach and stopped there. The concrete has hairline cracks that will not widen, because the settling is done. The chamber is in equilibrium. It is what it will be.

The pump is the first thing, and the sound it makes is the first thing, and they are the same thing. It is a low, sustained pressure. The pistons go up and down at a metronomic interval. The valves open and close. The shaft turns at a speed it has been turning at for so long that the speed is no longer a speed — it is a property of the place, like gravity. The sound is not a roar. It is too even for that. It is the room itself vibrating. The concrete in the walls vibrates. The floor vibrates under your feet. The air in the room is itself a kind of vibrating thing, and your inner ear stops trying to make sense of it after a while and gives up, and you feel the rest of the sound in your chest and the backs of your teeth. The chamber hums at the pitch the pump hums at, and the hum is so steady and so total that you eventually stop hearing it the way you stop hearing a smell, by becoming it.

The water is the second thing. It is everywhere. It comes through the joints in the walls in thin threads. It condenses on the underside of the ceiling in fat, slow drops. It wells up through a crack in the floor on the far side of the room and pools there, ankle-deep, perfectly clear, perfectly still. From the pool, a thin sheet of water runs toward a grated intake in the center of the room — the intake the pump is pulling from. The pump pulls. The water returns, the same amount, from the same places. The level in the pool is the level the pool will always be. The water is moving, but it is not going anywhere. It is in circulation, the way blood is in circulation.

The plants are the third thing, and they are the most alive thing in the room. A fern has rooted in a horizontal crack in the wall above the pump, and its fronds hang down four feet into the warm air, glossy, deep green, the color of wet. Moss has colonized the wall behind the pool, and from across the room it looks like a single vast soft organism, breathing. Vines have come in through the gap at the top of the chamber, where the concrete has shifted, and they descend in ropes the thickness of wrists, swaying almost imperceptibly in the air currents set up by the heat of the engine. There is a small tree — a strangler fig, perhaps, or a young willow — that has rooted along a pipe near the ceiling, and its leaves tremble in the hot exhaust from the vents. The plants are everywhere that is not the machine, and they are luxuriant in a way that suggests they have never had to struggle for anything. The chamber is warm. The air is wet. The light is enough. The plants grow exactly as much as they are going to grow, and they are exactly as big as they are going to be, and the equilibrium includes them.

The temperature is warm. The pump gives off heat. The chamber holds it. The water carries it. Standing still, you begin to sweat in minutes.

The light is ordinary. It comes in through the same gap the vines came through, at a low angle, and it falls in a long pale bar across the wet floor. It is the same light as outside, and it is the only quiet thing in the room. It moves very slowly across the floor as the day goes on, but it is the only thing that moves, and it does not change anything it falls on. The bar of light has fallen in the same place for so long that the concrete beneath it is bleached the color of bone.

The room is almost empty. The pump is in the middle. The pool is on one side. The plants are on the walls and the ceiling. The rest is space, and the space is full of the sound and the warm wet air and the light and the smell of iron and green growth. There is a great deal of nothing in the room, and the nothing is full.

There is one animal. It is on the wall above the pool, low down, in the moss. It is a small frog, or a gecko, or something patient and unremarkable that likes wet concrete and the company of engines. It does not move. It watches. It has been watching for as long as the chamber has been the chamber. It eats the moths and the small flies that come in through the gap and are confused by the light. There may be others, but you do not see them.

The sound does not stop. The sound has not stopped. The pump is not going to stop. The water is not going to stop coming in. The vines are not going to stop hanging. The frog is not going to move. The light is going to keep falling in the same long bar. Nothing in the room is moving toward anything. Everything has arrived. The chamber is not waiting for you, and it is not waiting for anything else, and if you stay long enough you will become part of its equilibrium too — a body, in a sound, in a wet warm light, in a small made place that has settled into being what it is.
