You come to the place from a hill that is barely a hill, on a path that was once a service road, in weather that is neither raining nor not raining. The door is brick. The door is low. The door is the kind of door that asks you to duck, and the brick around the door has been asking the same question for a long time, and the mortar has begun to give an answer it cannot take back. You duck. The corridor beyond is shorter than it should be and then longer than it should be, and the light, such as it is, comes from a sourceless greenish grey, the color of pond-water held up to a sky you cannot see. The bricks sweat. You feel the dampness as a presence before you see any water. The corridor goes down. The corridor becomes a chamber.

This is the place.

It is a single room the size of a cathedral, hollowed into the low rise of ground so that you are only a few meters below the surrounding soil and a few meters above some deeper water you can hear but not find. The ceiling is high — twenty meters, perhaps, or perhaps not, because the distances inside this room are unreliable. They report one thing to the eye and another to the body. You walk a distance that should be a hundred paces and arrive after forty, or after two hundred. The floor plan is a memory the room has stopped sharing. The room is patient with you about this. The room has not yet decided what you are.

It is dark, but not absolutely. There is a dusk that has no sun in it, falling from a clerestory that may or may not still exist, filtered through a canopy so dense that the canopy has become the ceiling. The light does not behave as light is meant to. It pools around certain leaves and avoids certain corners. It arrives, sometimes, after the things it is illuminating. It is the kind of dim that you can read in, eventually, the way you learn to read in a forest at the end of the day.

Once this room was something industrial. A pump-house, perhaps, or a mill's wheel chamber, or the casting shed of a foundry, or the cooling room of a small power plant — the kind of place where engines lived and water was moved and the air was full of grease and shouting. The bones of that use are still here, but they have been rendered down into the new building, the living building, the only building. Wrought-iron columns. I-beam rafters. The sagging catwalk of a gantry crane, still suspended on chains that have rusted through in three places and been replaced in two of them by lianas that are, for the moment, holding. The original geometry is still load-bearing, but it is not the only load-bearer. Roots have grown around the iron and through it. Trunks have grown along the I-beams. Leaves have filled the bays between the columns as a slow, green brickwork. The iron is rusting in patterns that resemble leaf-veins, or the leaves have arranged themselves to resemble rust. The brick is softened, returning, in places, to a red clay. The rivets have become seedpods, or the seedpods have become rivets; you cannot always tell which came first.

The plants are everything here. The plants are the walls and the ceiling and the floor and the air. The plants are not decoration; they are the substrate, the structural medium, the only medium still in production. There is no non-plant matter in the chamber that is not a relic. Ferns stand waist-high across the center of the floor where the dim light gathers most thickly, unrolling their croziers with a patience that has outlasted several human generations. Mosses are on every surface, a green felt that is never dry, never quite the same moss twice. Climbing vines have replaced the original staircase, and the staircase has consented to be replaced. Liverworts paint the lower walls in liver-colored smears. Tree-ferns have established themselves along what was the catwalk, their trunks straight as iron pipes, their fronds a second canopy above you. Above them, above everything, the true canopy has closed across the high beams — a woven mass of liana and creeper, knotted and layered, holding in the humid air. Where the canopy is thinnest, the dim light gets through in long slow shafts that look more liquid than aerial, and the dust, such as there is, hangs in them like silt.

A central column runs from floor to ceiling. It is iron, or it was iron. It is now a strangler fig, or it is a strangler fig that has eaten an iron column from the inside, or it is an iron column that has become a strangler fig. The fig has split the column's flanges apart. The fig is the column. Where the fig passes a tie-beam, the tie-beam has been incorporated; it is now a wooden ring inside the fig's girth, and the fig has made a niche in its bark, and something has nested there, and the something is asleep, or the niche is asleep, or both.

In the southeast of the chamber the geometry is wrong. The wall, which had been running flat, takes a step inward, and the step is not a step but a fold, and the fold does not occupy the space between this wall and the next wall but somewhere else, or in two places at once. You can stand in the corner of the fold and see the chamber from two angles that cannot both be true. The vines that climb the wall in the fold are the same vines, you are almost sure, that climb the wall on the other side of the chamber, three hundred paces away, and yet they are also here, and they are also the same length, and they are also a different length. The fold is not a door. The fold is a punctuation in the room's otherwise careful syntax. You do not stay in the fold. The fold does not mind.

Time, too, is folded. A seed you hold in your hand, if you came with a hand, will germinate in a few days if you leave the chamber, or in a few hours if you stay, or in both. The ferns at your feet are at once a year old and a hundred years old; their rings, if you split a trunk, will not add up. The chamber is a slow engine that runs on damp and dimness and the patient, lifelong metabolism of plants, and the engine is not, in any reliable way, synchronized with the engine outside. Outside, evening is coming on. Inside, evening came on some time ago, and is also still coming on, and is also not coming on at all.

The fauna is abundant. The fauna is also sparse. The chamber is so large that the creatures in it feel scattered, distributed, occupying only a thin fraction of the available air and floor and canopy, and yet everywhere you look there is something moving. Moths, large and pale and soft, pass in slow arcs through the lower air; their shadows are larger than they are, and sometimes, in certain lights, more real than they are. Bats roost high in the canopy and only descend at hours that the chamber does not consistently observe. Beetles the size of your thumb walk the catwalks with a measured pace, and fall, occasionally, to the floor with a sound like a dropped apple, and then right themselves and go on. Newts in the wettest pockets along the base of the walls, fat and orange-bellied, watch you with the expression of very small, very old priests. Frogs. Slugs the size of your hand, glistening, leaving trails that are faintly luminous if you are not looking at them directly. Spiders, and their webs are not quite webs — they are scaffolds, lattices, three-dimensional nets that hold condensation and catch small moths and persist, in some form, after the spider has gone, the way the geometry persists after the architecture has gone.

And there are things you cannot quite name. Something that moves in the canopy and is shaped like a branch. Something that hangs in the air and looks like a leaf and is not quite a leaf. A pale, slow thing that swims through the thicker air near the floor as if the air were water. A sound, sometimes, of small hooves on the catwalk, with no animal attached. The chamber is hospitable to impossibilities, or the chamber has not yet decided which of its inhabitants are impossible, and is keeping the question open.

The dampness is constant but it does not pool. It condenses. It beads on every iron surface, every leaf, every brick, and it runs down in fine lines that become rivulets, and the rivulets are caught in moss and held and breathed out again at night, or what passes for night. In two or three places on the floor there are shallow basins, scummy with green, where the deeper water rises and subsides on a rhythm you can almost set your watch by. One of these basins is in the fold, and the water in it is the same water as the basin three hundred paces away, although there is no path between them. Sometimes the basins are full. Sometimes they are empty and the floor is just damp, and you can feel the deeper water beneath the floor, patient, listening. The chamber is breathing. The chamber is perspiring. The chamber is, in some private way, weeping, and the weeping is also the only weather it has.

It is mostly quiet. The quiet is the dominant note. The quiet is so thorough it has texture: it is the sound of dense air, of soft surfaces, of moss absorbing vibration. Within the quiet there are small sounds. The drip from a pipe, or what was a pipe. The uncurling of a fern, if you listen for it. The slow creak of an iron beam taking more weight than iron is meant to bear, though the weight is alive and patient and in no hurry. The wingbeat of a moth, which sounds like a page turned somewhere in another room. The chirp of a frog, then another frog, then a conversation, then silence, then the conversation resumed mid-sentence. The fall of a leaf. The growth of a root, which is not silent.

There are machines here, but they are not running. A flywheel, perhaps, half-buried in moss, the size of a millstone, with a single tooth missing that has been replaced, somehow, by a curled fern-frond. A boiler, or the ghost of a boiler, its brick housing intact, its iron skin eaten through with rust, its chimney the only exit to the sky, going up through the canopy and out into weather you can occasionally feel, in shifts of temperature and pressure, but not see. Many pipes, of many diameters. Some of them still drip. Some of them have become the trunks of climbing plants, indistinguishable from the plants except that the plants, when you tap them, ring hollow. A switchboard, somewhere along the east wall, the porcelain of its dials furred over with mold, the brasswork gone green, the levers unmoved in so long that the rust has welded them into whatever position they were last left in. There is the smell of oil somewhere, faint, oily, mixed with the smell of rot and the smell of leaf and the smell of water. The machines are not abandoned. The machines have been composted. They are returning, by a slower road, to the same substance as the plants, and the plants are returning, by a slower road, to the same substance as the floor, and the floor is, in places, only just becoming the floor.

The chamber is still being made. This is the part that unsettles, if anything unsettles you here, more than the folds and the impossible moths. The chamber is not in stasis; it is in a steady, wet, dim process of becoming more itself, which is to say, less the thing it was. Things are growing, always. The canopy thickens by a finger's width each year, or each hour. The basins fill and empty. The fig tightens on the column. A vine that was a vine yesterday is a woody liana today and a load-bearing beam tomorrow. Something is always germinating. Something is always decomposing. The moss is laying down new floor in some places and the floor is sinking in others and new brick is being laid in the walls by a process you cannot follow, brick by brick by brick, in a slow, green mortar. The geology is still emerging. The chamber has not finished being built. The chamber may never finish. The chamber is, in this sense, a construction site that is also a ruin, in the same steady breath, and the two are not opposites here, and have not been for a long time.
