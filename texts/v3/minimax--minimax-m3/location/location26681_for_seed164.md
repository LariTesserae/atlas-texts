The Cold Upland of Velska

You stand — or, more honestly, you are suspended — somewhere on the Nordvegen Plateau, and the first thing to register is that the sky is doing something. It is not weather as you would recognize weather. There is no forecast here, no yesterday's rain to compare to today's. The same gray-white ceiling hangs at the same altitude. The same pale, diffuse light falls at the same angle. A fine mist is always descending — not falling, exactly, but condensing out of the air at chest height, beading on every surface, and you cannot say whether it began a moment ago or has been doing this for a thousand years, because nothing here moves from one state into another. The plateau has only one state.

So: mist. Mist, and the sound.

The sound is what brings most travelers to their knees, eventually, though not from fear. It is from the simple fact that a sound this large cannot be coming from any one place. It is everywhere, the way the mist is everywhere. It has a low drone underneath — you can feel it in the long bones of your legs if you stand still — and over that, a brighter, slicing hiss, the sound of vast quantities of metal moving against itself, somewhere. Behind a ridge. Beyond the next valley. Across the whole breadth of the place, possibly. You walk a full day in a chosen direction and the sound never gets louder where you are, and never gets quieter. The cities are working at exactly the rate they are working.

A ridge first, then. The geology here is young — you can see it in every line of the rock. Nothing has been rounded. The plateau rose, geologically speaking, only just recently, in a single catastrophic uplift, and the wind and water have barely begun to argue with it. So the ridges are knife-edged. The valleys are V-shaped, sharp at the bottom, no meanders, no floodplain softening. Where water has cut, it has cut in clean straight incisions, like wounds. The stone is dark — a basalt or andesite of some kind, you cannot tell, the colors muted by the constant damp — and it has a wet sheen to it even on the upturned faces, because the air is so saturated that water condenses on every surface that loses heat to the sky. The rocks weep. The rocks are always weeping. A droplet will form, swell, run down a centimeter, and then — and this is the strange part, the part the eye keeps refusing — freeze in place. Not all the way. Not to solid ice. Just to a kind of arrested slush, a glaze that holds the droplet where it landed on its journey. The rocks are decorated with ten million half-frozen tears, and have been forever.

Below the ridges, in the flat places between, the ground is bog. There is no other word. The cold is not quite cold enough to lock the water into permafrost — it hovers just above that threshold, year after year, century after century, because it hovers at the same temperature, because nothing changes. So the bogs are soft. They are not deep — a person could cross most of them, with effort, with patience — but they are everywhere, and they breathe out a slow fog that joins the descending mist. Walking across one feels like crossing a mattress that is also a cloud. The footing gives. Each step sinks in a few centimeters and water wells up around your boot, dark brown, cold, smelling of iron and peat. Vegetation here is mosses, sedges, low groundcover that has not seen the sun's direct rays in living memory and so has never had any reason to grow up. It lies flat. It spreads sideways. In places it has built itself into hummocks a meter high, dense as felt, and these hummocks are crowned with small bright shrubs that have, somehow, found a way to flower. The flowers are the only saturated colors on the Nordvegen. They are red. They are red because they have to attract something, even though nothing has come to be attracted for a very long time. They are red anyway.

Higher up, where the slope steepens and the bog cannot hold, the vegetation changes character. It becomes — there is no other word — *luxuriant*. The cold is uniform and the wetness is uniform and the mist is uniform and so the plants have discovered that they have everything they need, all the time, and they have committed. The slopes of the knife-edged ridges are hung with what look, from a distance, like curtains of green velvet. From close up they resolve into something stranger: long draping lichens, trailing mosses, ferns whose fronds are as long as your arm and which never yellow because nothing here instructs them to die. There are flowering things too — not the polite red of the bog hummocks but great fleshy blooms in colors that have no earthly business existing, blue-orange, green-purple, white with a luminescence that is not quite luminescence but only feels like it because the ambient light is so flat. Some of them have petals a handspan across. They are wet. They are always wet. A finger brushed against one will come back beaded. The flowers never open and never close. They are always at the same stage of bloom. The bumblebee, were there a bumblebee here, would have a problem.

There are no bumblebees here. There are no bumblebees, or anything else that moves under its own power that is not a person or a machine. You understand this slowly. The eye keeps offering you animals that are not there — the way a moving shadow on a fern will read, for a moment, as a small mammal; the way the wind in a stand of grass will sound, for a moment, like footsteps. But no. There is nothing. The flowers are red for no audience. The lush slopes are lush for no grazer. The bogs are not churned by anything. A million years of ecological relationships, the whole tangle of who-eats-whom, has simply been removed, and what remains are the plants alone, doing the work of plants without any of the partners, and they have filled the vacuum. The plateau is green from edge to edge, all the way to where it stops, and the green is weird, the green of a single player in an orchestra that is missing all the other players. You keep expecting something to interrupt it. Nothing does.

The cities, then.

You see the first one from a ridge, after two days of walking. You see it because the mist has a faint luminance against it — the cities throw a little extra light back up into the sky, a sodium warmth, the kind of light that is the color of old photographs. They are large. From the ridge, the city fills a whole valley, climbs halfway up the next ridge, and still seems to extend out of sight behind a spur of basalt. Closer — another half-day, the bog grows firmer underfoot as you approach, the road begins, a corduroy of metal and timber laid across the soft ground — closer, the city resolves into a single organism of metal and steam.

There is no obvious plan to it. The first buildings were put down to exploit the water power — there are turbines, vast slow things, half-buried in the bog and turning at the same rate they have always turned, you can see the wear-marks on their housings but the wear-marks are *also* unchanging, the metal has been eroded to exactly the same depth forever — and around those turbines, workshops. Around the workshops, housing for the workers, and around the housing, more workshops, and around those, the receiving yards for materials that come in by rail from somewhere to the south, and around the yards, more housing. The city has grown in layers. It has not grown up. It is only two or three stories high almost everywhere, because it has not needed to be higher, because the plateau is empty, because there is room. So it sprawls. The whole thing is low, long, gray-black, wet. Every roof drips. Every gutter runs full and overflowing. Steam comes from a hundred stacks at once and joins the mist and falls back as mist.

The machines inside are loud. They are the source of the sound. You step into a turbine hall and the sound is a physical thing, a pressure against your eardrums, a vibration in your teeth. The machines are running. The machines have always been running. Workers move among them — or rather, workers *are* among them, because they move so slowly and so repetitively that you cannot, for the first hour, tell that they are people rather than fixtures. They are damp. They wear dark clothes. They perform the same gestures over and over. They tend machines that produce things — you cannot quite tell what — that go onto rail cars and leave the city and presumably arrive somewhere else, although you have never met anyone who has been to that somewhere else. They do not ask you why you are here. They do not seem especially interested in you. They have work. They are at work. They have always been at work.

You ask, eventually, what they are making.

Steel plate, they tell you. Always steel plate. Every city makes steel plate. The plate goes out. New plate comes in, to be re-formed and sent out again, or perhaps not — they are not sure about the input side of things, only the output. They have not checked. It does not seem urgent to check. The plate goes out at the same rate. It has always gone out at the same rate.

You ask how long the city has been here.

They do not know. There are records. The records say the city was founded. The records do not say when. The records are kept in a building that is older than the rest of the city, a building made of stone instead of steel, and the stone is worn — but the wear is also frozen, you can see that the wear happened and then stopped, the chisel marks on the cornerstone are sharp at the back of the groove and frozen at the lip, as though the chisel withdrew halfway through. The records, when you manage to read them, are written in a hand that is firm and unhesitating. The hand is the same hand, on every page. There has been only one archivist, for as long as there have been records, and the archivist is still there, writing in the same steady hand, adding new entries that say the same things the old entries say.

You leave the city. You walk back into the bog. The sound fades, but it does not fade to silence — it fades to the drone, the constant drone, the pressure of machines heard from a long way off. The mist closes around you. The bog gives under your boots. The red flowers on the hummocks are unchanging. A bright blue-orange bloom on the slope of a ridge is at exactly the same stage of opening it was at when you first saw it, days ago. The droplets on the rocks have not moved. The turbines, somewhere behind you, are turning at the same rate.

The Nordvegen Plateau is the size of a continent. There are other cities — you can see, on the clearest part of the horizon, the faint luminance of another — and they are all at work, all producing, all loud, all steady. Between them is empty bog and knife-edged ridge and the constant weeping of the rock faces. Nothing eats the flowers. Nothing turns the soil except the slow heave of freeze and thaw, which is also frozen, which has not happened in either direction for longer than anyone can say. The mist descends. The mist has always been descending. You walk a long time in one direction, and the place does not change, and does not grow smaller, and does not respond to your walking, because there is nothing here that is keeping track. The place is itself. The place has been itself. The place will be itself.

You begin to understand, eventually, that the weirdness is not the lack of animals, or the colors of the flowers, or the frozen droplets. The weirdness is that nothing here has ever been anything other than what it is right now. The cities were not built. They have always been here, in their present form, working at their present rate. The vegetation did not colonize the slopes; the slopes have always been covered. The mist did not begin to fall; the mist is the air, and the air is the mist. You cannot find the moment anything started, because nothing did. The plateau is in equilibrium. It is the equilibrium. The whole vast cold wet loud unchanging continent of Velska is one single held note, and it has been held for so long that the holding and the note have become the same thing, and you — standing on a bog hummock, watching a red flower that will never wilt — are the only thing in the place that is in any way different from one second to the next.

You are the only thing that is happening.

The mist descends. The turbines turn. The flowers bloom.
