# The Verdant Deep

The first thing is the sound. You think, before you descend, that you'll hear dripping, the slow music of underground water. There is no dripping. There is a roar — layered, throat-deep, the sound of ten thousand wings and ten thousand throats working in shifts that never quite overlap. It begins before you reach the lip of the stairwell and by the time you are thirty paces down it has swallowed the sound of your own breathing, and you stop being able to hear your footsteps. You feel them through your soles, but the air is too full for them.

The stairwell is not carved. It is worn. Generations of feet, and before that generations of claws, and before that the slow relentless growth of the things the locals call *the pillars* — fibrous columns of root-stuff that have been trimmed and trimmed and trimmed until they are rungs. Some are still soft. Some bleed a thin pale sap when you grip them, and the sap is warm and almost sweet and it is the closest thing to free water you will find in the deep. You learn to lick your palms after the climb. Everyone does. No one speaks of it.

The cavern opens beneath you the way a wound opens — not all at once. You pass through a throat of compressed earth and root and then the ceiling lifts and lifts and does not stop lifting, and the floor tilts away, and you are standing on a shelf of woven fiber looking out at something that has no name in your language because your language was not built for it. The locals have a word. It means *the breathing place*. It means *the alive place*. It means, depending on who you ask, *home*, or *hungry*, or *god*.

There is no water in the Verdant Deep. There is almost no water. What water exists is held — held in the spongy trunks of the bigger growths, held in the bellies of the crawling things, held in the spore-mist that never settles out of the air. You can taste it if you open your mouth. The air is wet with intention if not with liquid. Breath condenses on every surface. The walls weep. Nothing pools. Everything drinks.

The walls themselves are not stone anymore, or only barely. They are furred. Rootlets by the million, fine as hair, lash themselves to every surface and drink from the slow trickle of mineral moisture that seeps down from above. Behind them, deeper, are older roots — trunks of woody fiber, knotted and re-knotted, so dense a man could not drive a stone axe through them in a day. They breathe. You can see them breathe. They swell and recede in a tide that has nothing to do with any moon, a tide set by some internal weather you are not equipped to understand. When the swell passes, the rootlets on the surface ripple outward from each trunk like the fur of a wet dog shaking, and a soft fragrant dust puffs into the air, and you sneeze or you don't, and either way the dust finds you.

The floor is the worst of it and the best of it. A continuous mat, dense as felt, soft as something that should not be soft. It is many things at once — moss, fungus, the woven bodies of small animals that have died into the weave, the husks of insects too large to count. When you step, it gives, and from the impression your foot leaves a thin steam rises, warm and faintly luminous. The mat is warm everywhere. The whole cavern is warm, the temperature of a held breath, the temperature of blood. You never shiver in the Verdant Deep. You never quite stop sweating either.

Light comes from the growths themselves. Not uniformly — in patches, in constellations, in slow blooms that open and close over days. Some are pale blue, the color of a sick sky. Some are deep orange, almost red, and they pulse. Some are green-white and so bright they hurt. The light does not travel far. It falls on whatever is near it and leaves the rest in a dimness that is not darkness — a dimness full of moving shapes, a dimness that hums. You learn to read by the light of the nearest bloom. The blooms are also food, or drink, or both, depending on the season. The locals wait for them. They hold festivals when a great bloom opens, and the festivals last until the bloom is done.

The fauna is everywhere. This is the part that the surface-dwellers cannot be made to believe. *There are animals*, the travelers say when they come back, and they say it the way a man says *I saw the sea*. As though it must be exaggerated. As though the telling of it is itself a kind of lie.

The insects are the foundation. They are not the insects of any field. They are the size of your thumb, your palm, your forearm, and they move in sheets — in columns, in spiraling towers that touch the ceiling and break and reform. They eat the root-mat. They eat each other. They are eaten by the things that are not insects. The air is never still because of them. It is never clear because of them. Your skin, after an hour in the deep, is freckled with them — tiny climbers, tiny grazers, tiny things with no name that simply use you as terrain. You do not brush them off. You learn not to. The ones you brush off are the ones that bite.

Above the insects, the flyers. They are not bats exactly, though there are bats. They are not birds exactly, though there are birds — strange ones, flightless except in the warm columns of rising air, with vestigial wings they use as fans and as mating displays. The flyers fill the upper cavern in layers, each species at its own altitude, each layer feeding on the layer below or above or beside. Their droppings are the rain of the Verdant Deep. Nothing falls as water. Everything falls as something else. The locals gather it. They call it *star-soil*. It is the closest thing the place has to a harvest.

On the ground, between the trunks of the great growths, the larger things move. They are shy, mostly. Six-legged, eight-legged, an uncountable number of legs. They browse the root-mat in herds, in packs, in solitary circuits that take them across the same trail for years until the trail is a groove in the living floor. Some are armored. Some are soft and pale and luminous from within. Some are plant and animal at once — rooted to a spot for a season, then walking away on legs they grew for the purpose. You do not approach these. The locals do not approach these either.

The things that are *strange* — and there are many strange things, and this place is strange past reckoning — live in the dim zones between the blooms. Shapes that assemble themselves from smaller shapes. Lights that move against the current of the insect columns. A sound, low and patient, that you feel in your teeth before you hear it, that comes from somewhere beneath even the floor. The locals say it is the dreaming of the deep. The locals are mostly right about everything, in the way that people who live in a place are right about it: not by knowing, but by being of it.

The dwellers are here. Scattered. A handful of families, perhaps thirty, perhaps forty, distributed through the upper cavern in clusters of three or four shelters each. The shelters are woven. Frame of cut pillar-rung, lashed with fiber rope, thatched with the broad flat leaves of the surface-feeders (the leaves are brought down once a season on the backs of the larger animals; it is the closest thing to a trade good the deep has). Smoke does not rise in the Verdant Deep — there is no draft, only the slow breathing of the roots — so the cooking fires are small and banked and the smoke is eaten as it rises, by insects, by the moss, by the air itself. The dwellers are small and brown and they move through the chaos of the place with an ease you will never have. They know which trunks are resting. They know which flyers are nesting. They know which blooms are about to open and which are about to close. They speak a language that is half words and half sounds that imitate the place, and when you ask them what a thing is they will sometimes answer by making the noise the thing makes, and the noise will be answered, and they will nod as if this settles it.

They have tools. They are simple. Knives of sharpened bone, of flint traded down from the surface, of the shells of the great insects. Axes with stone heads, lashed with sinew, used to trim the pillars and clear a path. Needles of bone and thorn. Pottery — small, thick-walled, fired in pits, glazed with star-soil. They do not smelt. They have no need of metal here; the place provides, in its slow and terrifying way, almost everything. What it does not provide, they trade for, in small bundles sent up the stairwell on the backs of children too young to fear the climb.

The stairwell goes up to a surface that is dry and bright and mostly dead — scrub and stone and the occasional tree leaning away from the wind. The dwellers do not love the surface. They go up to trade, to gather the dead wood, to bury their dead in the dry ground because the deep will not let a body rest. They come back down as soon as they can. The surface is too quiet for them. The surface has no warmth in it.

Things are still being made down here. That is the strangest part, and you do not believe it until you have been there long enough to see it. The cavern is growing. New roots push through the ceiling in the night, fibrous tentacles probing, finding the cracks, forcing them wider. New blooms open in places that were dim a season ago. The floor is higher than it was when the elders were young, and the elders are not old. New tunnels open — slowly, explosively, you cannot predict which — and the dwellers move to meet them, or the dwellers move away from them, and either way the map of the place is redrawn every few years and the stories they tell of the deep are always slightly behind. There are rooms no one has entered. There are rooms that did not exist a month ago. The place is not finished. It is not even close to finished.

When you sleep in the deep, and you will sleep in the deep if you stay longer than a day, the floor holds you. It warms to your shape. The insects pass over you like a second coverlet. The roar of the place softens, or you grow deaf to it, and what remains is the slow tidal breath of the roots and the patient sound from beneath, and somewhere in the dim a thing you cannot see makes a noise that is almost a word, almost your name, and you do not answer, and it does not ask again.

You wake with the bloom-light in your eyes and the taste of sweet sap on your hands and the knowledge that the place is older than the dwellers and younger than the morning and that it does not care which of you is which.

You go up the stairwell when you are ready. Most people go up the same day. The dwellers watch you go with an expression you cannot read — pity, or amusement, or recognition of something they have seen before in the faces of those who came down confident and went up quiet.

The surface is bright. The surface is dry. The surface has no roar.

You stand in it for a long time before you remember how to hear yourself think.
