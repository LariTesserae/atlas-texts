# The Sunken Conservatory

It rises.

That is the first thing to understand about the place — it rises. From a seabed of pale sand and volcanic grit, a structure climbs. Eight tiers, perhaps nine, each one a hexagon of bolted steel and laminated glass, stacked with the geometry of a child's block tower. The youngest of the joints are still sharp at their corners. No corrosion has yet softened them. No barnacle has yet found purchase on the hexagons' lower edges. The whole thing has the taut look of something that was finished yesterday, or the day before, and then set down here to live.

The water covers it. Twenty meters above the topmost platform, perhaps more, the surface rolls in long patient swells, but here, at the working depths, it is almost still. Warm. The thermocline sits somewhere above; you feel it in the way the light thickens as you descend, in the way the warmth presses in like weather. Visibility is generous — perhaps fifteen meters, perhaps twenty — and the green-tinged sunlight falls through the columns of glass without quite arriving. It is the light of a kitchen at dusk. It is enough light to see by, and not enough to read fine print.

Inside the hexagons, everything is made.

The floors are checkerplate aluminum, drilled with drainage holes that no longer drain because there is no air to drain into. The planters are corrugated steel troughs bolted to the walls at waist height, waist height, ceiling height, every height. The catwalks are woven through the troughs in a pattern that looks almost mathematical until you try to walk one and discover it requires a kind of faith. Overhead, sodium vapor lamps still hang in their housings — inert now, redundant, their job performed by the water itself — and from the lamps descend the irrigation spines. Black plastic. Pvc. They branch and rebranch and rebranch again, every joint sealed with a bead of silicone that is still white, still sharp-edged, not yet yellowed.

The plants.

The plants are the place. They are what you came to see, if you came to see, and they are why no animal has stayed. There is no food here for an animal — or rather, there is too much of the wrong food. Every surface that could hold a planter holds a planter, and every planter holds growth. Ferns, engineered ferns, their fronds the size of dinner tables, unrolling in spirals that begin at one wall and finish at the opposite. Cordyline. Philodendrons with leaves like flags. Orchids on the glass, on the steel, on each other. A species of moss — deep emerald, almost black where the light fails — that climbs the irrigation spines and does not stop climbing. The vines have learned the catwalks. The bromeliads have filled the drip trays. Where two hexagons meet, the growth on either side has reached across the gap and woven itself into a continuous green wall, so that passing from one chamber to the next is like pushing aside a curtain.

It is packed. There is no floor you can stand on that is not also a planter, no wall that is not also a trellis, no ceiling beam that is not also a perch for something rooting. If a human being were to enter, they would have to turn sideways. They would have to go slowly. They would have to push.

And the machines.

They are why it is industrial. They are why it is not a ruin.

Conveyor belts, narrow as a hand's breadth, run along the base of every trough. Their rubber is new. Their motors — sealed against the water, but inefficiently, with gaskets that have not yet failed — turn at a rate you can only detect by watching a frond-tip for a long time and noticing that it has, over the course of a minute, moved a centimeter toward the cutting blade at the end of the line. Cutting blades, yes. Scissors-blades, oscillating slowly, taking fronds at a pace so patient it seems ceremonial. Pruning shears the size of a forearm, opening and closing, opening and closing, every forty seconds. Harvest bins slide along beneath them, and what falls into the bins — a leaf, a stem, a frond-tip — is taken down a chute, into a hopper, into a press.

The press is the loudest thing in the place. And it is quiet. A hydraulic piston, working against a chamber of perforated steel, squeezing the green matter down into a cake, into a slurry, into something that might be fuel or might be feedstock or might be a record of something no one is going to read. It clanks once every few minutes. The clank travels through the water and arrives at your ear flattened, softened, almost swallowed. There is no other sound. The pumps — and there are many pumps, impeller pumps, diaphragm pumps, pumps for water, pumps for nutrient, pumps for the slow circulation of warmth — make a hum that is below the threshold of attention. You feel them in your teeth.

There are no fish.

You look, because the place is submerged, and you expect fish. You expect some medusa drifting by, some curious eel nosing at the seams. There are none. The water is sterile by design — ultraviolet sterilizers on every intake, chemical scrubbers in the recirculation loop, the whole tower a closed system in a closed system. Whatever animal life might have arrived on the current has been turned away at the perimeter, where a fine mesh, taut as a snare drum, rings the structure at every level. You can see it if you press your face to the glass: the hexagonal pattern of the mesh, the small green shadow of nothing beyond it, the patient gradient of warm water into warmer water into the long warm dark.

It is quiet. It is strange.

It is not ruined. This is the strangeness. A ruin would be loud with its own collapse — with the creak of metal tiring, with the pop of a seal giving way, with the slow avalanche of growth overwhelming structure. This place is intact. The machines run. The plants grow. The light comes down. The press clanks. Nothing is failing. Nothing is being reclaimed. It is simply here, performing its purpose — whatever that purpose was, whatever it has become — in the warm green dim, alone, at the small scale of a cathedral or a house, packed with the fullness of an overcrowded room, and utterly quiet, and utterly strange, and patient, and patient, and patient.
