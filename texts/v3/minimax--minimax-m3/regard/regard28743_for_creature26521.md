# What the Wise Power Does

It has noticed the town for a long time. Longer than the cartographer. Longer than the painter who stopped counting his own years. It has been noticing since the first shelf of stone was cut into the mountain by someone whose name is not recoverable, and it has been noticing the noticing, and the noticing of the noticing, in the recursive way that such powers attend to the things they care about.

It is not a god, exactly. It is more like the part of a god that has decided to be patient. It does not intervene in the way the word is usually meant. It is the kind of power that has learned, through long practice, that most interventions are a kind of vandalism, and that the wise thing is usually to sit with a thing for a very long time and see what it is, before deciding what it might need.

So it has sat with the town.

It has seen the moss that does not thicken. It has seen the timber that does not silver. It has seen the children who fall and are not changed by the falling, and it has noticed the small ache this produces in it — the ache of a thing that has been given safety at the cost of discovery, which is the only currency a child truly spends. It has seen the hunters come down from the high passes with the same deer, and it has seen the gratitude with which the deer are received, and it has understood that this gratitude is not lies but a kind of music played in two parts, and that both players know their notes, and that the music is what holds the town together as much as the moss or the stone.

It has seen the old woman in the high-backed chair.

It has seen, in particular, that the chair was not built for her. The chair was built for someone taller, someone whose feet reached the floor, someone who is not in the chair now and who has not been in the chair for a very long time, and the chair has been waiting, all this time, with the patience of a thing made of wood, for a person of the right stature, and the old woman is the latest in a long line of women who did not quite fit it and who sat in it anyway because the sitting was what was asked of them. The power can feel, if it bends its attention a little, the lineage of the sitting — a posture taught by one woman to the next, going back to a first woman who sat down in that chair on a particular afternoon and made a particular choice, and whose choice has been remade, in her posture, by every woman since. The first woman had a reason. The reason has been worn smooth. The sitting continues.

It has seen the river.

The river is the oldest thing in the valley, and the river is not held by the town. The town is held by the river. The power knows this the way it knows most things about water — by listening. The river made the shelf in the way that water makes shelves, by running and running and running over a long enough time. The river is not a metaphor. The river is the thing that is doing the holding, and the town is the eddy, and the old woman is the part of the eddy that has learned to think it is the river. This is not a cruelty on the river's part. The river does not do cruelty. The river does what water does, which is to find the shape of the thing in front of it and to become, for a long time, the shape of that thing. The town is the shape the river has taken. The people are the foam on the surface of the shape.

It considers.

It has broken holds before. It has seen what happens when a town that was held too tightly is suddenly released, and the release is usually worse than the holding, because the people inside a hold have built their entire inner architecture around the assumption that the hold will continue, and to remove the hold is to remove the architecture, and the people are left standing in the open air with no way to keep themselves upright. It has learned, from this, that a benevolent power does not always act. A benevolent power sometimes acts by not acting. A benevolent power sometimes acts by recognizing that the holding is not its holding to undo.

But it has also seen what happens when a power that could have asked a question decides, out of a kind of cowardice dressed as restraint, that asking would be too much. It has seen towns that went on holding for another three hundred years because no one had the small courage to leave a small crack in the wall.

It chooses, this time, to ask.

It does not ask the river. The river would not hear it, or would hear it the way water hears a stone, which is not at all.

It does not ask the people. They would not understand the question, and the question would frighten them, and the fright would be a worse cruelty than the holding.

It asks the old woman.

On a morning when the fog has come down and is sitting in the valley in the way that fog sits in this valley — that is to say, indefinitely, the way a cat sits in a lap without deciding whether to stay — the wise power does something very small. It sends a thought. Not a voice. Not a vision. A thought, the way one might remember, on a damp morning, that one once had a thought one no longer has, and that the shape of the missing thought is still there, like a stone that has been removed from a riverbed, leaving an indentation in the silt.

The thought is this: *You were a child once. You were a child who fell, and who learned from the falling. You do not remember this, because the town does not permit the remembering. But the falling happened, and the learning happened, and the child who did both is still in you, under the chair, under the keys, under the long habit of sitting. She is smaller than you. She is not gone.*

The thought is offered. It is not insisted upon. It is the kind of thought that can be picked up or set down. The old woman is free to ignore it. The old woman is free to be afraid of it. The old woman is free to do what she has always done, which is to sit and hold and pass the holding on.

The power does not stay to watch what she does with it.

The power has other towns. The power has a long attention and a great deal of patience and no particular investment in any one outcome. It is the kind of power that can offer a question and then walk away, knowing the question will be there when the person is ready for it, and knowing that the person may never be ready for it, and being at peace with this, because the question is its own small gift, and small gifts do not require receipts.

---

In the valley, the fog lifts. The old woman sits in her chair. Her feet do not reach the floor. She has, for the first time in a long time — and she cannot say how long, because she does not count, and counting has been the town's job, and she has been the town — a small discomfort. A small noticing. The moss on the beam is the moss on the beam. The child in the lane is the child in the lane. The river, whose name she knows and does not speak, runs below the shelf in the way it has always run.

She looks at the chair.

She does not know what she is looking for. She is looking, perhaps, for the woman who sat in it before her. She is looking, perhaps, for the child she was before she was the keeper. She is looking, perhaps, for the first woman, the one who sat down and made the choice, the one whose choice has been remade in her posture for as long as the moss has not thickened.

She does not find anything.

Or she finds something very small. A looseness in one of the joints of the chair. A slight give in the wood. A place where, if she pressed, the chair would move a little in a direction it has not moved before.

She does not press.

The day goes on. The small beer is poured. The bread is baked. The cabbages are salted. The hunters come down. The deer step on the rooftops. The fog comes back and sits.

But somewhere in the holding, a single thread has been loosened by the width of a single thought, and the thought is small, and the old woman is small, and the loosening is small, and the town will not notice it for a long time, if it notices it at all.

It is enough. It is not enough. The wise power does not decide which.

The wise power has other towns. The wise power goes on, with its long attention and its patient question, and the town holds, and the town will hold, and the damp will be the damp, and the mountain will be the mountain, and somewhere in the chair in the upper terrace a joint has come a little loose, and no one has pressed on it, and the pressing is there to be done, on some morning, by some woman, in some weather, when the fog is sitting and the moss is dripping and the river, below, is running the way it has always run, toward a sea it has never seen.
