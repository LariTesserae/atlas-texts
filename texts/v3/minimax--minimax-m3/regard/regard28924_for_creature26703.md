The wise power does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. It is not a being that descends through the Notch on a chosen morning, not a presence that the cable operators could see from the staging platform, not a light that would register on the rough anemometer the company keeps by the lift house. If it arrived, it would disturb the basin, and the basin does not need to be disturbed. The basin has been in its right form for longer than anything in it has been in any form at all.

What happens, instead, is that the basin begins, on a certain morning, to be *known*.

The wind is the same wind. The cable is the same cable. The mist lies on the lake the way it has lain every morning since the cable was strung. The shift whistles blow. The skips clatter up the benches. The matriarch fox comes out of her burrow in the spoil of the eastern bench and trots along the tramway tracks between cars, as she has done for years longer than the oldest miner has been down the Notch.

But something in the quality of the morning has changed. Not the air, not the light, not the sound. The way the morning is *being* the morning. As if for the first time in a hundred and forty years, the basin is not just continuing. It is being accompanied.

The wise power does not speak to anyone. It does not appear to anyone. It does not heal anyone, or feed anyone, or extend anyone's contract. What it does is *attend*. It attends to the basin the way the basin attends to itself — patiently, without commentary, without the wish for any of it to be other. But its attention has a quality that the basin's own attention does not have. The basin's patience is the patience of a thing that does not know it is patient. The wise power's patience is the patience of a thing that knows.

It knows the matriarch. It knows her torn ear and her limp and the yellow at the edges of her eyes. It knows the three cable operators she has outlived and the two teachers who came down for a season each and stayed for decades. It knows that she is not a pet and is not a tame animal and is, in some way that no one can quite name, a colleague. The wise power does not name her either. It simply sees her across her whole tenure, the way a colleague sees a colleague — without sentiment, without ownership, with the steady recognition that the work is shared.

The matriarch feels it.

She feels it the way a fox feels the season turning — not by any particular sign but by a quality of the air. She stops on the tramway tracks between cars and lifts her head and stands there for a moment longer than she needs to. The cable operators, on the next car down, see her standing there and one of them waves, as the protocol requires, and she does not wave back, as the protocol does not require, and the protocol holds. But for the first time in her long life, the not-waving-back feels like its own kind of waving. She is seen. She is being seen by something that has been there as long as she has been there. She does not need to acknowledge it. She only needs to know.

She walks on. She crosses the tracks. She goes to the lowest bench, in the oldest workings, where a small steady stream of water comes out of the rock face and runs across the ore and disappears into the lake.

The wise power is with her when she gets there. Or she is with the wise power. The difference does not matter.

The stream has been running since before the first cable was strung. It is the oldest sound in the basin. It is quieter than the wind and quieter than the crushers and quieter than the cable and quieter than the bells in the winding houses, and most of the time it cannot be heard at all.

The wise power does not turn off the crushers. The wise power does not ask the wind to drop. It does something smaller and harder to name. It listens.

It listens to the stream the way the stream has been listening to itself for three billion years — patiently, without commentary, without the wish for it to be louder. And in the listening, something happens. Not to the stream. Not to the basin. To the listening itself. The wise power's attention, even when it does not act, has a way of making the thing attended to feel less alone.

The matriarch drinks from the stream. The water tastes of iron and lime and the faint sweet of the unnamed mineral, the same water the miners drink because the alternative is hauling it up the cable. She drinks the way she has always drunk. She does not know she is being witnessed. She only knows that the morning is, for no reason she can name, a little more present than usual.

The wise power moves on. It moves through the barracks where the miners are sleeping off shift, and it sees the dust in their lungs and the basin-mask at the edges of their irises, and it knows the company name for the metal and the university name for the intrusion and the mother's name for each man, and it knows that none of these are quite the right name, and it does not say so. It moves through the crushing works where the hammers are stopped for the morning greasing, and through the chapel that is no longer used, and through the cable car as it goes up empty and comes down full. It sees the operators wave at the workers on the ledges. It sees the workers not wave back. It knows that the workers not waving back is its own kind of waving, and it does not try to make it otherwise.

It sees the children in their too-big boots, picking up loose ore from the spill piles and pocketing it. It sees the youngest ones afraid of the tramway. It sees the older ones not afraid. It sees the fish dimpling the standing waves in the moments between gusts. It sees the birds on the basalt shelves, with their clattering call that has become part of the basin's mechanical weather. It sees the foxes in the spoil of the abandoned cuts, the matriarch's kin, the small thick-coated knowing things that have learned the rhythm of the cable cars and cross the tracks only between cars.

It sees the spillway pour over in the spring, and the slag fall half a mile down the outer slope and arrive as a black rain of pebbles, and the green-black benches wind around and down toward the water. It sees the basin being eaten by the wind and the water and the plants, slowly, in the way the basin has been eaten for three billion years.

It does not intervene in any of this.

It does not save the basin from the mining, because the basin is not being saved from the mining. The basin is patient with the mining the way it is patient with the wind. The work is one of the forms the basin is tending through. The wise power does not pick favorites among the basin's forms. It loves the green-black ore and the slag rain and the cable car and the fish and the foxes and the abandoned benches with their tangled curtains of pale flowers, all equally, the way a parent loves a child through every age the child passes through.

What it does is simpler than saving.

It lets the basin know it has been seen.

It lets the matriarch know she has been seen across her whole tenure.

It lets the oldest miner know that his basin-mask is not a sickness but a sign — the sign that he has stayed, the sign that the basin has, in some way, agreed to keep him.

It lets the cable operators know that their clean coveralls and their shouted conversations into each other's collarbones are not a separation from the basin but a particular way of being in it, and that the basin holds them too.

It lets the children know that the cable operators wave at them even when they do not wave back, and that this is its own kind of love, and that the company Academy on the rim is not a removal but a sending-out, the way a basin sends its water over the spillway and receives it again in the spring.

It lets the small stream know it has been heard.

It lets the wind know it has been recognized as the form the basin is always tending toward.

It does not announce any of this. It does not speak. It does not write on the wall. It is present, and its presence is the announcement, and the announcement is for anyone who has ears to hear it, which is to say, for anyone who has been in the basin long enough to have stopped expecting announcements.

Then the wise power does one small thing.

On the morning after it has been in the basin — and it has been in the basin always, but it has only now, on this morning, chosen to be felt — the matriarch comes out of her burrow and finds, at the threshold, a piece of ore. It is green-black. It is dense with the metal the smelters want. It is the size of her paw. It is not different from any other piece of ore on the benches. A miner, passing, would not glance at it twice. A geologist would not mark it on a map. The university people would not change their estimate of the intrusion's age.

But it is not from the benches. It is from somewhere else. It has a quality that the ore on the benches does not have, which is the quality of having been *given*, although no one gave it, and of having been *placed*, although no one placed it, and of being, in some way that no fox and no miner and no operator could put into words, a *thank you*. A thank you from the basin to the matriarch for staying. A thank you from the form that is tending toward the green-banked lake to the form that has been tending with it, all this time, in the spoil of the abandoned cuts, with a torn ear and a limp she will not let heal.

The matriarch picks it up in her mouth. She carries it down to the lowest bench, to the oldest workings, to the place where the small stream comes out of the rock face. She sets it down beside the stream. The water runs across the ore and disappears into the lake. The ore is the same ore. The water is the same water. The sound is the oldest sound in the basin.

She does not know why she did this. She will not know tomorrow. She will only know that, on this morning, she had to put something beside the stream, and the thing she had was a piece of ore, and the ore is now where she put it.

The miners find it on their next shift. They do not move it. They have a name for it but the name is just the name for ore.
