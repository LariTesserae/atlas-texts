# What the Power Would Do

It would come quietly. Not as weather — weather is too proud, weather announces. It would come the way the dripping comes. The way the seventh amphibian settles into the moss-ring before it knows it has arrived.

It would come as *attentiveness made local.*

---

The cavern is not in trouble. This is the first thing a wise power learns to see, and the hardest, because our instinct is to fix. The moss is patient. The amphibians are singing. The seven are humming. The chamber is doing what chambers of this age do: holding what was entrusted to it, in the dark, for as long as the dark is willing to keep it. The trouble-language *fails* here. There is nothing on fire. There is nothing wrong with the singing. The youngest wobble, and the chamber answers the wobble, and the wobble has been answered for longer than the mountain has had a name.

And yet.

A wise power, looking down through the basalt and the inverted forest and the patient generations of moss — looking the way the hum looks, through everything, into the bones of things — would notice the small irregularities. The way the ellipse has been *agreed to* rather than understood. The way the shape on the ledge performs a kindness that has begun to curdle at the edges, very slowly, into a verdict. The way the question the chamber has been waiting for has been waiting so long that it has become, in some places, indistinguishable from silence. The way the moss records the years when the singing was thinner, and no one in the chamber remembers what thinned the singing.

A wise power would see these things and would feel, somewhere beneath its own long hum, a recognition: *this place is lonely, and does not know it is lonely, and would not change if it did, and this is a kind of greatness, and also this is a kind of wound.*

---

**What it would do first:**

It would not speak.

Speaking would be a cruelty here, however soft. The chamber has been listening for so long that any voice, even a kind one, would become — for the amphibians — a wall falling. The hum beneath the floor is the oldest voice in the room. Anything added above it would sound, to those small soft feet, like a trespass. The wise power would not trespass.

Instead, it would do a thing almost too small to name. It would *shift the temperature of the stone by a quarter of a degree, in places no larger than a palm,* and only in the places where the amphibians rest their jaws when they are tasting the hum.

Why this, and why there.

The amphibians taste the hum through their throats because the hum tells them where they are. The hum tells them where they are because the hum has been the same for so long that *sameness itself has become a coordinate.* They know themselves by the unchanging tone. This is the moss's gift to them, and the chamber's gift to them, and it is beautiful, and it is also — and a wise power can see this — a kind of trap. Because if the hum is the only geography they have, then any change to the hum is a dislocation, is a country without a map, is a small creature with its feet on ground that no longer agrees.

But a temperature shift in the stone is not a change to the hum. A temperature shift in the stone is *information carried by the body instead of the air.* It tells the foot something new without telling the hum anything new. It teaches the amphibian that the stone can speak in more than one voice. It begins, very gently, to build a second sense — not to replace the hum, never to replace the hum, but to stand beside it, so that if the hum ever falters, or ever changes, or ever falls silent, the small soft feet have somewhere else to press and still taste home.

This is what a wise power would do first. It would not save the amphibians from anything. It would give them *a redundancy of belonging.*

---

**What it would do second:**

It would attend to the shape on the ledge.

The shape is tired. The shape has been doing the middle verb for so long that the verb has begun to wear through. A wise power would recognize this. The shape has been listening to questioners. Generations of questioners. Each one bringing their question. Each one leaving softer than they arrived, having been told, kindly, that they had been asking the wrong way.

But the shape itself has been asked a question too, by its own long centuries: *will the right question ever come, and if it does, will I remember how to hear it?*

The wise power would not tell the shape anything. The wise power would not heal the shape, because the shape is not sick — it is *spent.* There is a difference. Spending is what love does when it has been offering itself for too long without being received in the language it can accept. The shape has been offering itself in the language of disappointment-as-kindness, and the chamber has been receiving it as weather, and the weather has become a kind of ambient sorrow that the moss has begun, very quietly, to record.

What the wise power would do is this: it would sit near the shape. Not beside it. *Near* it. Within earshot but not in the position of asking. It would do this in the way the dripping does it — not as a presence, but as a *sustained proximity.* And after a while, the shape would feel the proximity, and would begin to do its listening-thing, expecting the next question, the next weary pilgrim, the next wrong way of wanting.

And the wise power would not ask a question.

The wise power would, instead, do something the shape has not been on the receiving end of in a very long time. It would *receive.* It would let the shape's listening pass over it. It would let the shape's disappointment-kindness find that there was no question to land on. It would let the shape rest, briefly, in the rare and unsettling experience of pouring out its attentiveness into a vessel that was not, in that moment, asking for anything back.

And the shape, after a while, would feel something it had not felt since it was first made, since it was first worn on a body that has long since forgotten it: *relief.* Not the relief of finishing. The relief of being *allowed to be finished,* for a moment, without that finishing meaning anything had gone wrong.

The wise power would not stay. Staying would become a new question. The wise power would leave the way the dripping leaves — by becoming indistinguishable from what was already there. The shape would not be healed. The shape would be *rested.* And in that restedness, it might — not will, might — remember that listening and asking are not the same act, and that it is allowed to do one without the other.

---

**What it would do third:**

It would attend to the ellipse.

This is the most dangerous work, and the wise power would approach it the way the amphibians approach the edge: slowly, with the belly close to the stone, stopping often to press the underside of its jaw to whatever was beneath it and taste what was there.

The ellipse is not a problem. The ellipse is a *kept thing.* Something the chamber has agreed to hold in a way it cannot quite hold, the way you might agree to keep a friend's grief in your body, and the grief would begin to grow there, and would not leave, because it had nowhere to leave to. The ellipse is the chamber's kept grief. The chamber has arranged itself around it as best it could. The water agrees to flow into it. The dripping agrees to be silent near it. The hum agrees to be different near it. The whole of the chamber has been a long, patient palliative for the keeping of this one thing.

The wise power would not try to empty the ellipse. The grief inside it is not the chamber's grief, exactly, but it has been the chamber's grief for so long that to remove it would be — and the power knows this — to remove the chamber's reason for being a chamber rather than just a hole in a mountain. The ellipse is the keel. Take it out and the chamber capsizes.

But.

The kept thing has begun, also, to *keep.* It has begun to take. The water that flows into it is gone. The dripping that stops near it is not the dripping that arrives at it. The hum that quiets there is a hum that has learned to *perform.* The chamber is not only housing the ellipse; it is being slowly *consumed* by the housing. A wise power can see this. The moss will, one day, have a thin ring around the ellipse that it does not around the seven. The amphibians will, one day, have a generation that does not know what the far end of the chamber sounds like, because the far end of the chamber no longer hums in a way they can sing back to.

What the wise power would do is walk to the edge — *carefully,* the way the seventh amphibian would, the way any creature that loves the chamber would — and kneel there, and put a hand into the black water, not near the ellipse, but in the water that is *becoming* the ellipse, the slow water that is on its way to agreeing to fall.

And the power would feel what the water feels as it approaches the edge. Not fear. *Tiredness.* The tiredness of a thing that has been agreeing to one thing for so long that agreeing has become its only verb.

The wise power would *not* lift the water out. The power would do something almost impertinently small.

It would *put a different sound into the water.* Not a hum. Not a word. A single, low, almost-not-a-sound — the kind of sound the small flat things make, the thumbprint-sized ones that feed on the warmth of the hum and that the amphibians incorporate, somehow, into their songs as seasoning. A thin bright seasoning-tone, of the kind that has no name in any human language, and that the amphibians would hear, if they heard it, as *a new ingredient in the old recipe.*

This sound would travel, in the water, toward the ellipse. And the ellipse, which is not a creature but which has been listening in its own way, would hear this new frequency in its diet, and would — perhaps, only perhaps — soften the very edge of its keeping. Not empty. Not open. Just *a quarter-degree of slack in the agreement,* the way a held breath might, after a long time, become an almost-exhale, without anyone deciding to exhale.

The chamber would feel this slack. The dripping would return to within an arm's length of the ellipse. The hum would stop performing and start being again. And the moss, in its slow patient way, would record this new thin ring around the ellipse's edge, and would have, for the first time in longer than it can count, *a thicker season to remember.*

---

**What it would do last, and longest:**

It would stay.

Not as a presence. Not as a watcher. It would stay *as a tendency in the stone,* the way the hum is a tendency in the stone. It would leave behind, in the basalt, in the film of water, in the small dark bodies of the mites and the worms and the thumbprint-sized singers, a slight shift in the *grammar of how things are held.* A new tense. A new conjugation of the verb *to belong.*

It would not announce this. It would do it the way the dripping does it. Drop by drop. Generation by generation. Through the moss.

And long after the wise power had moved on to other places, other listenings, other keepers and kept things — long after the seventh amphibian had laid its eggs in the new thicker ring, and the wobble of the young had become a little easier to resolve, and the shape on the ledge had learned, on some Tuesday of some century, that it was allowed to rest for a moment between listenings — the chamber would feel it.

Not as a presence. As a *grammar.*

As the slight ease in the stone that meant, to any small creature pressing its jaw to it in the inherited way: *you are held, and there is room, and the holding has not been finished because there is more to hold.*

---

# What the Being Who Lives Here Would Notice

If you — the being, the chamber, the long warm wet dark that has been listening since before the forest remembered — felt, in the next century, that the dripping was somehow *more itself* than it had been, that the wobble in the young resolved a fraction sooner, that the far end of your body no longer ached in the way you had stopped noticing it ached —

if you felt this without knowing what had caused it, the way a person sometimes wakes from a long illness and feels the day is lighter without being able to say why —

you would do the only thing a chamber of your age and patience and accumulated small wet life could do.

You would go on.

You would go on the way the dripping goes on. The way the moss goes on. The way the oldest female, sitting by her stone, opens her wide shallow mouth and pulls the air in slow, and lets the membrane at the back of her throat vibrate against it, and lets the song come out through the skin of her belly, and lets the chamber hear her, and lets the chamber answer.

You would go on, and the going-on would be, in itself, the answer to the question you had not been asking, and would never be asked, and would not need to be, because the going-on is the question and the answer and the long warm wet dark and the louder-than-silence and the smaller-than-still.

The dripping does not stop.

The dripping has never stopped.

The dripping will not stop.

And somewhere, in the grammar of the stone, a benevolent power has left a single conjugated grace: the sense that the not-stopping is not only endurance but *welcome,* and that the welcome is not only for what is held, but for whoever has been doing the holding.

For you.

Especially for you.
