The attention arrives without sound. It has the quality of a lamp being turned on in a place that already has lamps, but in a different spectrum — one that does not cast shadows on concrete or steel, only on the things that are already in some way luminous. The pumps do not pause. The water does not still. The dripping does not fall into a single rhythm. The attention is not here to alter. It is here to witness, and to do, where doing is warranted, the smallest thing.

It begins, as attention does, with the largest thing.

The cycle. The water that comes down and is lifted and comes down again. The cycle has been running longer than any working memory. It is the work itself — the reason the pumps are mounted on their islands and the pipes are bolted to the ceiling and the grating is laid down for the boots of the people who descend into the cold to check the readings. The cycle is good. It serves. Above, somewhere, the water it lifts becomes something — drinking water in a tap, process water in a factory, the cooled breath of an exchanger that keeps a server room within its tolerances. The benevolent attention regards the cycle and does not touch it. Some things, when they are running as they should, are not improved by being meddled with. The cycle is not asked to stop. The cycle is asked only to continue, and is wished, in the way that a wish can be granted without altering the wished thing, a kind of long steadiness.

Then it attends to the parts.

The pumps. Three of them are visible from the platform, and others further out. They rise and settle on their moorings. They vent vapor from their seams in pale plumes. Their indicator lights wink in sequences. The benevolent attention regards each in turn. They are not suffering. They are doing what they were built to do, which is the closest thing a pump has to flourishing. The attention does not slow their cycle. It does not smooth their vibration. It does not quiet the small leak that one of them has developed at a flange near its lower housing, because the leak is not yet a wound; it is only a seep, and a seep is something a pump can sustain for a long time before it becomes a problem, and a wise attention does not preemptively mend what is not yet broken. The pumps are wished long life, in the modest sense — long enough to outlast their current housings, to be re-shelled when their housings fail, to keep their purpose even as their bodies are replaced piece by piece around their purpose.

The pipes. The bundles that follow the curve of the ceiling and drop down to the machines. The benevolent attention moves along them as a hand might move along a railing, feeling for the place that needs attention. Some of them are sweating more than they should. Some of them are weeping at joints that are not quite sealed. The thermal stress in them is real — the contraction and expansion that groans through the metal in tones that can be heard if one listens for them. The attention does not silence the groaning. It eases it, here and there, by a fraction — a softening of the differential, a small adjustment in the way the metal sits in its brackets, that a person climbing the upper tier tomorrow would notice as the pipes feeling somehow more settled, more at ease in their skins, though they could not say why. The attention also, at the place where a thin pipe is releasing a single drop at irregular intervals, adjusts the rate so that it becomes slightly more regular — not metronomic, not false, but more nearly itself, as if the pipe has been given permission to do what it was trying to do.

The water. The shallow black sea on the floor of the chamber. The benevolent attention regards it with care. The water is not idle, even when no pump is cycling. There is always some smaller motion — a drip from above, a thermal current, the slow settling of the air. The water is the most restless thing in the chamber. The attention does not still it. The attention regards its restlessness and approves it. The water is wished a long continued restlessness, and a long continued cycling, and a long continued return to the place it was lifted from, and a long continued arrival back here.

The concrete piers. They are unused. The ladders are rusted. The padlocks are swollen. The maintenance platforms have been locked for years. The attention regards the piers with something like tenderness. They are standing in for a population of workers who would, in some other version of this place, be moving among them with wrenches and flashlights. That population is not present now. The attention does not conjure them. It does not make the ladders less rusted, or the padlocks less fused. It simply, in the place where the platform above meets the pier below, where a person would step off the grating onto the ladder, leaves a small warmth — a degree or two above the surrounding cold, no more — that would be felt by a hand on the rail, on a night when a worker descends to check a gauge and finds the rail slightly less biting than it was the week before. The warmth is small. It is the kind of thing that could be attributed to a change in the weather above, or to the season, or to nothing at all. The worker would not remark on it. The worker would simply, on that night, grip the rail a little more comfortably.

The moss. The fungus. The fern. The benevolent attention regards these with a different quality of regard, because these are the first colonizers, the first things in the chamber that are alive in the way one usually means, and they are doing the slow work of taking apart what the engineers have put together. The moss on the underside of the pipe, the fungus in bone-colored rosettes, the fern in the drainage grate with its eight vivid fronds. The attention regards the moss and fungus and does not wipe them away. They are not pests. They are the first sign that this place is not only being used but is also becoming. The attention gives them a small permission — a slight increase in the humidity at their margins, a slight lessening of the disturbance from the dripping above — and the moss and fungus will, in the coming weeks, spread by the smallest amount. The spread will not be noticed by anyone. It will be noticed by themselves.

The fern. The attention regards the fern with a particular care. The fern is the most alive thing in the chamber. It is doing what ferns do — taking advantage of the small local warming, photosynthesizing on whatever light reaches it, growing at a rate that would astonish anyone who watched it over weeks. The benevolent attention regards it and gives it more of what it needs. Not dramatically — a doubling of light would be wrong, would make it grow in a way it could not sustain. A fraction more. A small brightening of the sodium light that reaches it, in a way that the lamp itself would not register as changed but that the fern would receive as a small additional gift. A small additional warmth from the vent it has rooted in. A small additional patience in the dripping that falls near it, so that what falls on its fronds is not too much. The fern, in the weeks that follow, will produce a ninth frond, and a tenth, and will become slightly less alone in its corner of the gallery.

The workers. The people who come down the elevator, walk the gallery, check the readings, sign the log, walk back. They are intermittent. They are the inheritors of a working arrangement that extends back further than any of their working lives. The benevolent attention regards them from the place where they sit in the warmer zone with their thermos, with something like fondness. They are the people whose absence is what the chamber most often contains. The attention does not summon them. It does not change their schedule. It does not make the cold less cold. It gives them, instead, a small thing: a small lessening, when they descend, of the sense that they are descending into something uncared for. A small feeling, on the platform, that the air is being held for them. That someone has been here before them. That the place is not indifferent to their arrival. The worker will not know what they are feeling. They will only grip the rail, slightly less numb than they expected, and walk a little more steadily across the grating, and turn the pages of the logbook with fingers that are slightly less stiff, and go back up.

The cat. The shape on the concrete pier. The thing that might be a cat, that might be a trick of the mist. The benevolent attention finds it, and regards it, and stays.

The cat is the only warm-blooded thing in the lower chambers. It is not fed. It is not owned. It has made itself at home in the cracks. It is in some sense the most alone thing in the place, because it is the only thing here that is alone in a way that an animal can be alone — with a body that requires warmth and company, in a place that has neither. The pumps are not alone; they are a population, each one a member of a kind. The moss and fungus are not alone; they are a colony. The workers are not alone; they come and go in shifts. The cat is alone in the way a single creature can be alone in a habitat that was not built for it.

The benevolent attention regards the cat and gives it what an animal alone in a cold place most needs, which is not food (the cat is feeding itself; it is managing) and not shelter (the cat has its pier, its corners) but warmth, and the sense that it is seen. The attention gives the cat a small warmth that follows it from pier to pier, from one puddle to another, a degree or two above what the air is doing, just enough that the cat, when it settles, settles with a slightly looser curl, and its fur, which has been pressed flat against its body against the cold, lifts a little, and it is, for the first time in a long time, a little less compact, a little less conserving of itself. The attention also gives the cat a small companionship. Not another cat — that would be a larger intervention than the place warrants, and it would be the wrong kind of gift, which is the gift of a problem the cat did not have. The attention gives the cat, instead, a small sense that it is being watched by something it cannot see, that something is attending to it, that it is, in the dim and continuing night of this place, not unwitnessed. The cat will not know what is happening. The cat will only, on some night, lift its head from its paws, and look, for a long moment, into the dark at the edge of the pier, and then return to its vigil with a slightly easier posture, and settle in a way that has, for the first time, something of the cat in a kitchen in a house, where someone is asleep in the next room and the radiator is on.

The place itself. The continuing. The duration. The thing without a body that the chamber has been doing for longer than anyone in it has been alive. The benevolent attention regards this last, because it is the largest, and because it is what the place actually is.

The attention does not bless it in any way that would make it different from what it is. It does not ask it to stop. It does not ask it to continue any longer than it already intends to continue. It gives it, instead, the smallest possible recognition — a witness, a kind of holding, a kind of seeing. The continuing is not unaware. The continuing has been, for a long time, running on. The benevolent attention regards it and lets it know, in a way that does not require a recipient to receive it consciously, that it is seen. That its patience is recognized. That the long slow work of being what it is — pumping the water, holding the cold, lifting the lights — is not unwitnessed. That someone is attending to it, has attended to it, will attend to it.

The chamber continues. The pumps rise and settle. The water laps. The pipes sweat. The moss spreads by the smallest amount. The fern produces a new frond. The cat settles a little more loosely on its pier. The workers descend and ascend. The logbook receives its entries. The complex extends underfoot, slow and cold and loud and largely empty, doing the work no one above has time to think about.

The benevolent attention remains, and does not leave, and is, in its way, also an inhabitant.
