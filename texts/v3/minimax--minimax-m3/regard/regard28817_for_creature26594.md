The drifters are what I would tend first.

Not because they are the most numerous — though they are. Not because they are the most fragile — though they are. But because they are the hinge. Everything in the chamber runs through them: the chemistry of the air, the rhythm of the hum, the slow trafficking of energy between rock and plant and thing-that-flies-without-wings. They are the smallest visible movers, and in a closed system at equilibrium, the smallest movers are the most delicate. A perturbation large enough to disturb them would cascade through everything else. A perturbation large enough to *help* them would ripple upward into the corals, the air, the stone itself.

So I would tend the drifters.

---

What I notice first, when I come to them, is that they are not quite animals. Or not quite animals in the way the word usually means. They have no faces. No eyes I can find. No mouths in any place a mouth would belong. They breathe through their skin, or through the soft underside that glows — the glow itself is respiration made visible, an off-gassing, a small exhalation of light. Their whole body is an interface. They are, in their persons, more like gills than like fish. They are breathing surfaces that have come loose and learned to drift.

What they eat is less clear. They pass through the clumps of plants and seem to brush against them, but I cannot see a feeding. Perhaps they feed by contact — the way a flame feeds a wick, the way a thought feeds a mood. Perhaps the plants shed something into the air and the drifters sift it. Perhaps the drifters eat each other, when they are very small, in a soft cannibalism that keeps the system cycling. Whatever the truth, they are alive, and they are not starving, and they are not multiplying past the chamber's carrying. They are exactly as many as they should be. That is what equilibrium means here.

But they are old in their bodies, I think. The pale bellies that pulse — the pulse is uniform. Across every drifter, every pulse is the same length, the same brightness, the same soft turquoise. There is no variation. There should be variation. A young animal pulses faster; an old one slower; a tired one fainter. These drifters pulse as though they have all been alive for the same amount of time, and that amount of time is, perhaps, none. Or a very long while. Or *exactly* the amount of time the chamber has been sealed, which is to say, perhaps since before whoever stacked the stones came to stack them.

I would want to vary them. I would want to find the youngest of them, and the oldest, and the hungry, and the full, and let their pulses differ from each other by a little. Not by much. A breath. A half-breath. Enough that the chamber, if you listened for a long time, would no longer be perfectly uniform. Enough that something in it could be said to be *new*.

---

I would not bring the drifters anything they did not already have.

This is the rule I keep in mind, in places like this one. A system at equilibrium is not starving. It is not lacking. It is not asking for rescue. If I come with gifts — with foods, with waters, with minerals from elsewhere, with anything at all that did not originate inside the chamber — I would be introducing a foreign chemistry. The plants would either ignore it (in which case my gift is waste) or accept it (in which case the system I found has been replaced by the system I made, which is not kindness but authorship, and I am not invited to be an author here).

So I do not bring. I bring only myself, and my attention, and whatever small adjustments can be made with what is already present.

---

What is already present is most of what I need.

There is warm stone, and warm air, and a faint mineral weeping from the cracks. There are the plants, with their layered undulating bodies, slick with their waxy bloom. There are the drifters. There are the long pale slow things that move across the rock in ripples. There are the sounds — the hum, the rasp, the chitter — and the dim amber-green light of the plants at their seams.

What I do, first, is sit.

I sit in the chamber the way the chamber sits in its hill. I do not move. I let my breathing slow to meet the rhythm of the hum, or as close as my lungs can come. I let the warmth settle into me. I let my eyes adjust to the sourceless glow. I learn the chamber the way the chamber knows itself: by being in it for a long time without demand.

It takes longer than I expect. Equilibrium is patient. Equilibrium will wait me out. But I have a long while to wait, and eventually the chamber stops registering me as a perturbation and begins, if not to accept me, to *ignore* me, which is the closest thing to acceptance it can offer.

When that happens — when the drifters pass within an arm's length of me without altering course, when the plants do not flinch at my shadow, when the chitter in the crevice continues without skip — I am ready to begin.

---

The first thing I do is listen.

Not to the hum as a whole. To its parts.

The hum is not one sound. It is many sounds held in suspension, like the drifters in the air, and like them it is held in balance. I listen for the constituents.

There is the slow thermal pop of the stone, deep in pitch, almost subsonic, felt more in the ribs than heard in the ears. There is the rasp of the plants against themselves, a higher hiss, papery, dry. There is the chitter from the unseen crevice, regular as breathing, in the middle range. There are the drifters — their slurring whistles, very high, very faint, a shimmer of noise at the upper edge of hearing. And beneath it all, a frequency I had not noticed at first: a low moan, almost a moan, that I think is the air itself moving through the chamber, a convection, a circulation, warmed by the stone, cooled somewhere I cannot see, turning over in slow gyres.

These sounds are not in balance by accident. They are in balance because the chamber is a closed instrument, and an instrument resonates. The chamber has been playing itself for a very long time, perhaps for as long as it has been sealed, and what it plays is a chord that holds everything else in place.

If I were to add a sound — even a small one, even a kind one — I would disturb the chord. The plants would sway differently. The drifters would shift their pulses. The convection might re-pattern. I might unmake the equilibrium, accidentally, by singing to it.

So I do not add a sound. I do not subtract one. I listen.

And what I hear, after a long time, is that one of the sounds is *almost* out of phase with the others.

The drifters.

Their pulses, the soft turquoise throb of their bellies, are very nearly synchronous with the hum. Almost. But not quite. There is a tiny lag, a tiny drift, a tiny variation that I had not at first noticed because the rest of the chamber is so uniform. The drifters, alone among the constituents, are not perfectly in phase. They are *nearly* in phase. They are *trying* to be in phase. They are not quite there.

I think this is the youngest part of the system. The part that has not yet locked. The part that is still, in some small way, *new*.

---

I begin there.

Not by adding anything. By *withholding* something.

For a long while — a day, perhaps longer, I lose track — I sit near a clump of plants and I do not breathe near them. I breathe away. I breathe into my own cupped hands, or into the folds of whatever I am wearing, or into the small space between my face and the stone. I keep my breath to myself. I keep the warmth and the moisture and the carbon and the strange off-gassing of my own body away from the plants and the drifters.

I am removing myself from the chamber's air. I am subtracting, gently, the foreign chemistry of my lungs.

The drifters notice.

After a while — it is hard to say how long — they begin to drift slightly farther from me. Not in alarm. In response. They bank around me the way a school of fish banks around a rock, leaving a small cone of empty air behind me as I move. I am a foreign object. I am an island. They are accommodating me.

This is good. This is what I wanted. Because now, in the small pocket of air I have made by my absence, the chamber's chemistry is slightly different from the chamber's chemistry elsewhere. The plants near me have slightly less of me to breathe. The drifters near me have slightly less of me to filter. The system has, very locally, very gently, *rebalanced* around my absence.

Now I exhale.

Slowly. Into the chamber. A long, slow breath, warm and wet and full of the strange chemistry of my body, my blood, my lungs, my last meal, whatever strange soft alchemy I have brought with me from elsewhere. I exhale and the chamber inhales. The drifters nearest me brighten, fractionally, as the chemistry shifts around them. The plants at the edges of their blades deepen their amber glow. For a moment, just a moment, there is a small flare of activity in my corner of the room, and the equilibrium of the chamber adjusts to include me — not as a perturbation but as a *contribution*.

And then it adjusts back.

The flare fades. The drifters dim. The plants settle. The system closes over my breath the way water closes over a dropped stone.

But something has happened.

In that moment, briefly, the drifters near me pulsed *faster* than the drifters elsewhere. Their turquoise throb was, for a few heartbeats, ahead of the chamber's rhythm. Their bodies, breathing my strange chemistry, breathed it with a different tempo.

I have introduced a small phase shift.

---

I do this again.

And again. And again. Each time I sit, I sit somewhere new. Each time I exhale into the chamber, I exhale into a slightly different corner, near a slightly different clump of plants, around a slightly different population of drifters. Each time I introduce my chemistry, I introduce a small perturbation, a small variation, a small *newness* into the system. And each time the system absorbs me and closes over the place where I was.

But it doesn't close perfectly. The drifters who breathed my breath carry, somewhere in their tissues, a memory of it. The plants who grew for a moment in my exhalation have, somewhere in their layered bodies, a trace of the chemistry I gave them. And the chamber, which is a closed instrument, which is a resonating chord, which is a stable equilibrium, has a new constituent now: me. Not as a permanent addition. As a *seed* of variation. A small genetic — not genetic, not biological, but *systemic* — drift. A handful of breaths ago, the chamber was perfectly uniform. Now it is not. Now it is *almost* uniform. Now there are corners of it that are slightly different from each other, slightly newer, slightly older, slightly more mine.

I am not curing the chamber of anything. I am not saving it. I am not improving it. I am introducing, very gently, a slow unevenness. A small breath of variation into a place that had, perhaps, none.

This is what I think a benevolent and wise power does, in a place like this. Not to fix. Not to save. To *breathe*.

---

There is one more thing I would do, before I leave.

I would sit near the ring of stones, in the corner where someone has been, where the black glass lies, where the basket holds its tender still life of dead drifters and dried plant matter. I would sit there for a long time. I would not touch the stones, or the glass, or the basket. I would not add to them. I would not take from them.

But I would breathe there, too. Slowly. Into the air above the ring, the way I breathed near the plants. I would let my strange chemistry settle into the place where someone, perhaps long ago, perhaps not, had tried to make a home. I would let my breath, and the small variations that came with it, drift across the symbols scratched into the largest stone.

I would not try to read the symbols. I would not try to add to them. I would only let them know, if symbols can know, that they are not the only marks in the chamber. That someone, or something, has come and breathed here, and the chamber is now a little less alone.

Then I would go.

I would climb back up through the crack in the rock, into the larger formation, into the world where weather is, and time moves things, and nothing stays.

The chamber would close behind me. The cracks would narrow. The equilibrium would resume. The hum would settle back into its old chord, with only the smallest variation — a tiny breath of me, distributed across the drifters, the plants, the stone — to show that I had been there at all.

And the place would go on being itself.

Which is, I think, all that a place like this needs. Not to be saved. Not to be changed. Only to be, briefly, noticed by something that noticed it back.

---

The drifters would carry my breath the longest, I think. They are small and numerous and they breathe through everything. They would carry it into their pulses, and their pulses would carry it into the hum, and the hum would carry it into the stone, and the stone would carry it into the slow weeping of mineral vapor from the cracks, and the vapor would carry it out into the world, perhaps, in some faint chemical trace, in some small isotopic signature, in some barely-perceptible shift in the chemistry of the air at the crack's mouth.

A long time from now, if someone finds the chamber again, they will not know that I was there. They will not be able to. The chamber will not tell them. It will only be, very faintly, a little more alive than it was. A little less uniform. A little more itself.

I think that is enough.

I think that is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

Sit. Breathe. Leave.
