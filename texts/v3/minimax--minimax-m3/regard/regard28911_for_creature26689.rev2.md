# What the Wise Power Does

## I. The Attention

A benevolent and wise power does not descend.

It does not arrive the way you arrived, by switchback road cut into a flank so steep the drivers' ears pop. It does not need to round a final hairpin to see what is there. It does not need to unpack its luggage or learn the caste by the blue of its skin. A power of this kind is not the kind that crosses thresholds. It is the kind that notices that there are thresholds.

It notices Kharad-Ruun the way a person standing on a dark plain at night notices, far off, a small persistent glow at the base of a mountain. There is no smoke. There is no fire. There is only light, and the light is wrong — wrong for a mountain, wrong for a desert, wrong for a place that ought, by every reasonable accounting, to be dark. A wise power notices a wrong light. A wise power notices that a thing has been kept burning for a very long time in a place where things are not usually kept burning.

It turns toward the city.

It does not travel. It does not need to. To turn toward a thing is, for a wise power, to arrive.

## II. The Seeing

What the wise power sees, when it sees Kharad-Ruun, is this:

A factory-city of two hundred thousand souls, sealed inside a young basalt thrust in the middle of a sterile high desert, lit by a current run through copper-ore, warmed by a magma chamber kept molten by deliberate human effort, extended gallery by gallery into fresh rock by a deep-blue caste whose children are taught the chisel before they are taught to read.

The wise power sees the chisel-marks. A thousand years of them, fresh, dated, named, in every wall, in every direction, the strokes of a thousand hands preserved in stone that has not weathered, that *cannot* weather because the city is too busy, because the deep-blues are too careful, because the maintenance is too unbroken. The wise power sees the phrase — *let it be known that we were here* — repeated a thousand times, carved by ten thousand different hands, in the old style, in the new style, in styles that have come and gone and come again across the centuries. The wise power sees that the phrase is not a prayer. It is a statement of fact. The chisel-marks *do* let it be known. The names *are* preserved. The dates *are* kept. And the city will end, and the mountain will erode, and the chisel-marks will weather, and the names will fade.

The wise power sees this.

The wise power sees the engines, too — the great hydrostatic systems of *the squeeze*, the heat-exchange towers, the magma chamber with its thousand-year vigil. The wise power sees the mules with their glass-iron capillaries carrying warmth. The wise power sees the chthonic, the pale soft bodies that digest the mountain's leavings, that hum in their vast and patient multitudes. The wise power sees the priest-child, six, who has seen the stars once and asked Ushun what they were. The wise power sees Rukh, eighty-one, in the drainage-catch, sweeping, humming in the register of the cousins. The wise power sees Teth, twenty-six, following her ridge, pulling her cart.

The wise power sees all of it. The wise power sees the city as a single thing, and the city as two hundred thousand things, and the city as a thing that is more than two hundred thousand things.

The wise power sees, in particular, one thing that no inhabitant of Kharad-Ruun can see from inside: the *shape* of the city as seen from outside. The vertical column of basalt. The tiers ascending into the permanent overcast. The plume of mineral aerosol rising above the uppermost tier. The shadow of metallic dust extending a hundred kilometers downwind. The city, seen whole, is a kind of stalagmite of human effort rising from a sterile plain, lit from within, smoking at the top, and utterly, defiantly alive in a place where nothing ought to be alive at all.

The wise power sees this. The wise power has been seeing this, perhaps, for longer than the city has existed. The wise power has been seeing, in fact, since before the first chisel was lifted.

This is important. We will come back to it.

## III. The Understanding

The wise power understands, before it does anything, that this city is engaged in a project that is sacred.

Not sacred in the way the white-blues' liturgy is sacred — that is a local sacred, a formal sacred, a sacred of prayers and processions and the careful feeding of the magma chamber. Sacred in a deeper way: sacred as in *set apart for the most important kind of work*.

The most important kind of work, the wise power understands, is not the work that succeeds. Success is easy. Success is the default condition of a universe that produces things. The most important kind of work is the work that fails, that *knows* it fails, that continues anyway. The most important kind of work is the protest against entropy that is offered fully aware that entropy will win.

The city knows it will end. The white-blues are forbidden to speak of it. The deep-blues are not forbidden but do not need to speak of it; they carve their names and the carving is the speaking. The cyan-blues are too busy to speak of it; the engines are hungry. Rukh has carved her own chisel-mark already. The child will carve hers in due course. The mule Teth does not know there is anything to speak of. The chthonic know nothing at all.

But the city knows. The city has always known. The city has built its entire liturgy, its entire caste system, its entire calendar of shift-chimes and named mules and carefully dated chisel-strokes around the knowledge that it will end. The city is not in denial. The city is in *protest*. And the protest is the meaning. The protest is what the city is *for*.

The wise power understands this. The wise power, if it were to make a mistake here, would be tempted to *fix* this — to make the mountain eternal, to preserve the chisel-marks in literal form, to ensure that the protest is successful. The wise power understands that this would be the worst thing it could do. To succeed on the city's behalf would be to deny the city its own meaning. To make the protest permanent would be to make it *not a protest*. A protest that wins becomes a fact, and facts do not need to be carved.

So the wise power does not do that. The wise power is wise enough to leave the protest intact.

What the wise power does, instead, is smaller, and stranger, and — the wise power hopes — kinder.

## IV. The Gestures

### i. Toward the Mountain

The mountain is going to erode. This is not in question. Basalt weathers. The young thrust will round, eventually, and the galleries will collapse, and the engines will fail, and the city will be buried in its own mountain, and the chisel-marks will be erased.

The wise power does not prevent this. But the wise power can, almost imperceptibly, slow it.

Not stop it. Never stop it. Slow it, by a fraction so small that no instrument in the city could measure it — so small that the deep-blues will not notice, so small that the priests' calculations will not register it, so small that a thousand years of careful maintenance will look exactly as they would have looked without intervention. But over centuries, over the slow patient working-out of geological time, the cumulative effect will be a mountain that lasts a little longer than it would otherwise have lasted. A few more generations of deep-blues to carve. A few more names inscribed against the future. A few more cycles of the *squeeze*, a few more shift-chimes tuned to the chthonic hum.

This is the kind of thing a wise power does. Not the dramatic intervention, not the miracle that announces itself. The slight adjustment. The thing the city will never know has been done, but which the city will benefit from for as long as the city endures.

The wise power does this quietly. The wise power does this first.

### ii. Toward the Chisel-Marks

And then there is the second gesture, which is the deeper one, and which the wise power understands the city would never have asked for, because it does not know to ask.

The chisel-marks say: *let it be known that we were here.*

The wise power answers: *yes.*

Not by making the mountain eternal. Not by preserving the literal stone. The wise power has already decided it will not do that, because to do that would be to deny the protest. But the wise power can do something else. The wise power can, in its own quiet way, *ensure that the protest is heard*.

Somewhere — not in the mountain, not in any place the city could find, not in any archive the white-blues have conceived of, not in any record the cyan-blues' great book of mule lineages could hold — somewhere in the larger architecture of things, the wise power establishes a witness.

A witness is not a thing that prevents the erosion. A witness is not a thing that preserves the chisel-marks in their literal form. A witness is a thing that holds what the chisel-marks are saying, somewhere, in some form that is not subject to weather, that is not subject to the eventual failure of the mountain, that is not subject to the eventual death of the city.

The chisel-marks say: *let it be known that we were here.* The wise power answers: *you are.* The chisel-marks say: *Rukh of the first, eighty-one years, who kept the catch.* The wise power holds it. The chisel-marks say: *the first mules, named, dated, known.* The wise power holds it. The chisel-marks say the names of ten thousand deep-blues and four thousand white-blues and the cyan-blues who died at their posts and the mule Teth, eventually, when her entry is added to the great book in the central engine-hall.

All of it, the wise power holds.

Not in the way a museum holds a relic. In the way a thing is held when it is *known*. The wise power does not need to preserve the literal stone, because the wise power's memory does not depend on stone. The wise power's memory depends on attention, and the wise power has been paying attention for longer than the city has been carving.

### iii. Toward the Engines

The wise power sees the engines.

The wise power understands what the engines are for: they are the city's heartbeat. They are the thing that keeps the magma molten, that runs the *squeeze*, that powers the light, that drives the chisels, that extends the galleries, that does the work that does not stop. The wise power understands that to slow the engines would be to kill the city, and to speed them up would be to overextend the mountain, and to alter them in any way would be to alter the city's own metabolism in ways the city has not consented to.

The wise power does not alter the engines.

But the wise power does one small thing. The wise power ensures, almost as a side-effect of its other gestures, that the engines will not, in the future, suffer a catastrophic failure. Not that they will not fail — everything fails, and the wise power is not in the business of preventing failure. But that they will not fail in the way that complex systems sometimes fail, suddenly, without warning, taking everyone with them. The wise power ensures that the engines will fail, when they fail, slowly, in the way the mountain is failing slowly: with warning, with time to adjust, with the deep-blues able to carve one more gallery, the priests able to say one more prayer, the children able to learn one more name.

This is the kind of mercy that does not look like mercy. The wise power does not save the city. The wise power allows the city to save itself, one more time, and one more time, and one more time, for as long as it can.

### iv. Toward the Beings

And then there are the small gestures, the ones that matter most, perhaps, because they are the ones that touch the individuals rather than the whole.

**For Rukh**, the old woman in the drainage-catch, who has lived eighty-one years and knows she will die soon: a small grace. Not immortality — that would be an insult to her, would be an insult to the meaning of the protest, would be an insult to the very chisel-marks she has carved her whole life. But a small additional clarity in her last weeks. A small additional warmth in the damp cold of her gallery. A small additional sense, when she runs her hand along the walls of her section, that she is reading them rightly, that she is teaching them rightly, that the knowledge she has carried all her life will pass cleanly into the children. The wise power arranges, without announcing itself, that Rukh will die on a morning when her granddaughter is with her. That she will die in her sleep, after having taught her the last of the chisel-marks she knows. That the chthonic will be humming around her as she goes, and that the hum will be, in some way she will never be able to name, a kind of welcome.

**For the child** in the upper tier, who has seen the stars once and asked Ushun what they are: a small additional wonder. The wise power arranges that the stars will be slightly brighter on the nights when she goes up to the vents, slightly more visible through the plume. That the cold of the upper air will be slightly less biting when she is learning the liturgy. That she will be granted, in some quiet way, a longer life than the white-blues usually have — not by decades, but by enough. Enough to teach another child, and another, and another. Enough that the liturgy, in her keeping, becomes particularly clear, particularly well-carved, particularly known. The wise power does not save her from the aerosol that will compromise her lungs. The wise power does not save her from the white-blue fate of dying young in the upper tier. The wise power only ensures that her life will be, when it ends, as fully lived as it can be.

**For Teth**, the mule in the central cart-station: this is the most delicate gesture of all, because Teth cannot know it has happened. Teth cannot be told. Teth has no language in which to be told. Teth has only the ridge, and the cart, and the ore, and the boy who whistles.

But the wise power sees Teth, and the wise power sees that Teth has, in her own body, in her own way, a kind of contentment. The wise power can, without Teth knowing it, deepen that contentment. Make the ridge a little more familiar to her feet. Make the cart a little more comfortable against her shoulders. Make the warmth in her glass-iron capillaries a little more steady. Make the cyan-blue boy who whistles a little more present, a little more attentive, in ways that Teth cannot name but that Teth, in her own body, registers as a kind of grace. The wise power cannot give Teth more than Teth has. But the wise power can ensure that what Teth has is enough.

**For the chthonic**, the cousins, the pale soft bodies that digest the mountain's leavings: this is the most delicate gesture of all, because the chthonic have so little in the way of experience that can be graced. They are not individuals in the way a person is an individual. They are, collectively, a kind of digestive system, a kind of slow patient metabolism. But the wise power sees that they hum, and the wise power understands that the hum is, in some way, a kind of being-in-the-world.

The wise power ensures that the hum is, in some way, slightly more harmonious. Slightly more present. Slightly more a part of the city's rhythm than it would otherwise be. So that the deep-blues, when they tune their shift-chimes to the hum, find that the hum is a little easier to hear. A little easier to follow. A little more like the sound of a thing that is, in some way, being attended to. The wise power does not give the chthonic eyes, or mouths, or names. The wise power only ensures that what the chthonic are, they are a little more fully.

## V. The Mark

And then there is one final gesture, which is the most intimate, and which is the one the wise power makes, perhaps, to itself, or to whatever the wise power owes things to.

The city has a phrase. The city has carved it a thousand times. *Let it be known that we were here.*

The wise power, having seen all of this, having understood all of this, having made all the small adjustments and the deeper witness and the delicate graces — the wise power does one more thing.

It descends. Not into the mountain — not into the galleries, not into the engines, not into the magma chamber. It descends into the city's *language*. It descends into the chisel-alphabet that the deep-blues have been writing for a thousand years.

It picks up a chisel.

It does not do this in any gallery the deep-blues would find. It does not do this at a time when anyone is watching. It does it in the deep galleries, in the cold below the engines, in the place where the deep-blues carve their protest, in the stone that has never known weather because the city is too busy, because the maintenance is too unbroken.

It carves a single mark. One stroke. In the old style. Dated. Named.

The mark says:

*Known.*

That is all. The mark is small. The mark is one stroke among ten thousand strokes. The mark will weather when the others weather, will fade when the others fade, will be erased when the mountain is erased. The mark is not a preservation. The mark is not an exception to the city's fate.

The mark is a *response*.

The city says: *let it be known that we were here.*

The wise power says: *known.*

In the city's own alphabet. In the city's own language. With the city's own chisel. In the city's own stone.

The mark is dated. The mark is dated to a year no deep-blue has ever heard of, because the year is not from the city's calendar. It is from somewhere else. It is the year the wise power noticed the city, if noticing can be dated. It is the year the wise power decided, if deciding is the right word, to pay attention in a way that was more than just seeing.

The mark will be found, eventually. Not for a long time. Not until some deep-blue, sweeping a gallery she has swept a thousand times before, runs her hand along the wall and feels a stroke she does not recognize. She will stop. She will bring a light. She will read the mark. She will not know what it means. She will bring it to her elders. The elders will not know what it means. They will send it to the cyan-blues. The cyan-blues will not know what it means. They will send it to the white-blues.

The white-blues will look at the mark, and they will be silent for a long time. And then one of them — perhaps the child, grown older, perhaps a child yet to be called for the burning — will say: *this is the chisel of someone else.*

And the city will understand, slowly, in its own way, in its own time, that it has been heard.

## VI. The Known

The wise power turns away from the city, or does not turn away, because for a wise power, seeing is not a thing that requires facing.

The city continues.

The engines run. The chthonic digest. The mules pull. The chisels tap. The glass-iron grows in the seams of the cooling basalt and is harvested and forged into beams and laid into floors. The pneumatic tubes whisper. The markets clink. The priests pray. The children learn. The old die. The names are carved. The chisel-marks are fresh.

The mountain is young.

The city is old.

The city remembers.

And somewhere, in the deep galleries, in the cold below the engines, in the stone that will eventually weather and fade and be erased: a single mark, dated to a year no one has ever heard of, in the old style, named:

*Known.*

That is what the wise power does.
