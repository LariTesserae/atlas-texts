What a place. What a strange and beautiful patient place.

I would not interrupt it.

That is the first thing, and maybe the only thing I would commit to before I even arrived: a promise to myself that whatever I felt called to do, I would not let it become the kind of doing that this valley has spent millennia teaching me not to do. The valley's lesson is patience, and the wrong response to a place that has been working at its task since before I had a language would be to bring my small, urgent, human scale of intervention to bear on it. I would be the loudest thing in that basin by several orders of magnitude, simply by being there with intent. So I would go carefully, and I would be prepared to leave having done nothing, and I would be at peace with that as the outcome.

But I would go.

I would come in late summer, when the cold is least and the light is longest, and I would approach not from the rim but from the saddle — from the low pass between the two ridgelines, so that I arrived at the edge of the valley gradually, on foot, over ground that the place had not yet claimed. I would want to feel the transition. I would want the change from ordinary hillside to valley-rim to register in my body and not just my eyes. I would bring nothing that wasn't necessary. I would not bring a notebook the first time. I would not bring an instrument. I would bring only what I needed to be present in that cold for the hours it would take to let the place teach me its shape, and I would bring a strong intention to leave before the cold had its way with me.

The first thing I would do, standing at the rim, is nothing. I would stand there long enough that my eyes stopped giving me the first impression and started giving me the second. The first impression is the green-black expanse and the conveyor-belt pattern and the absolute silence. The second impression is the wave. The second impression is the slight wrongness. The second impression is the feeling, which I trust the place to give me if I am quiet enough, that what I am looking at is not a community of organisms but a single thing that has learned to wear many faces.

I would let that second impression work on me until I was no longer sure where the moss ended and the lichen began, and then where the lichen ended and the grass began, and then where any of them ended and the valley began. I would want to be properly uncertain. Uncertainty is the correct posture in a place that has refused, for thousands of years, to be legible.

I would listen. I know the place is acoustically dead, but I would listen anyway, because the absence of sound is itself a kind of information and I would want to take it in as fully as I could. I would listen for the bird. I would want to hear one lift, even if I couldn't see it. I would want the lift to register as a small displacement in the silence, a hole in the quiet, evidence that the place was not as still as it appeared.

I would not dig. The man who wrote what I just read dug once, and I respect him for it, but I would not. The reason is not that I think the moss is sacred in some childish way, but that the moss is a plinth four meters deep of its own ancestors, and the act of digging into it is an act that the valley cannot repair on the timescale of my visit. I would leave no trace. I would not even leave a footprint, if I could help it, on the rim. The valley has been keeping its own accounts for a very long time and I would not put myself into the ledger.

I would want to come back.

I would want to come back in different seasons, in different years, and I would want to bring, eventually, a person who knew these communities — not to dissect the valley but to sit with it. A lichenologist. A bryologist. Someone who could name the lichen I cannot name, and tell me whether the strange partnership between the moss and the foliose lichen has been described anywhere else, and what it might mean that two organisms have stopped being two. I would not bring them to study. I would bring them to witness. I would want a person whose life had been spent in the company of these small slow things to see what this valley had made of them, and to tell me, afterward, whether the place was as singular as it felt.

If the lichenologist said it was singular — if the partnership was unknown elsewhere, if the rhythm of the bands was a thing no one had described — then I would consider what to do, and what I would consider, I think, would be this: I would look for the smallest possible way to make sure the valley stayed the way it was.

The valley does not need protection in the dramatic sense. It is not being logged. It is not being drained. It is not being paved. The wrongness that might come to it would come slowly, the way all its changes have come, and it would probably come from the air before it came from the ground. A warmer atmosphere would change the cold that runs the engine. A wetter atmosphere would change the balance of condensation and evaporation. A single invasive species — a moss, a lichen, a grass — arriving in the valley on the boot of an absent-minded visitor could, in a thousand years, become part of the rhythm and unmake it, the way a single wrong note in a long-performed piece of music becomes the piece.

So what I would do, if I had the means and the standing, is I would try to make the valley's silence a silence the rest of the world was willing to respect. I would not make a park. A park is a human frame, and a frame would be the wrong note. I would try to make the valley, in whatever legal and cultural forms are available to me, a place that is not for anything. Not for study. Not for tourism. Not for extraction. Not even, in the end, for the education of the lichenologist I had brought, though the lichenologist would be welcome and I would listen to them carefully. The valley would be for itself. The valley would be the kind of place whose value is exactly that it is not for us, and our task with respect to it would be to let that be enough.

I do not know if such a thing is possible. The world does not have many mechanisms for setting land aside without putting it to some use, and "for itself" is not a use that fits easily into the forms we have invented. But I would try. I would try because the place has given me a kind of instruction. The instruction is that there are processes on this earth that have been running longer than we have had the language to describe them, that have found efficiencies we did not design, that wear grooves in the world without our permission, and that the correct human response to such a process is not to manage it or improve it or even really to understand it but to stand at its rim, in the cold, and to feel, in the body, the weight of being a latecomer in a place that does not need us.

I would leave the valley as I found it. I would take nothing with me but the memory of the wave in the middle distance, and the slight wrongness, and the silence, and the knowledge that somewhere on this earth there is a place where moss and lichen and a grass I cannot name have been working together for so long that they have forgotten how to be anything other than a single thing, and that this single thing is still working, and will be working long after I am not.

That, I think, is what the place is for. It is for being the place it is. It is for the band in the northwest quadrant that is taller and glossier than its neighbors today and that will be the band that is collapsing in a month, and the next band that is rising behind it, and the band behind that, all of them turning over in the same patient rhythm that they have been turning over since before I had a thought.

If I could do one thing for the valley, it would be to make sure the rhythm continues.

I would not say this out loud at the rim. I would not say it because saying it would break the silence and the silence is part of the engine. I would think it, and then I would walk back over the saddle, and I would not return for a long time, and when I did return I would be quieter than I was the first time, because the valley would have taught me, by then, that quietness is the only correct posture in the presence of something that has been working at its task for millennia without needing us to notice.

I would leave a small offering in the saddle, on the way out, not because the valley would notice or care but because I would want to mark, in the oldest human way, that I had been there and that the place had done something to me. A stone on a stone. A thing that would not be there in a hundred years. A thing that would be gone long before the moss, in the trough between two bands, finished the cycle it was in the middle of when I stood above it and tried, and failed, to imagine what four meters of stratified ancestors felt like under my feet.

I would walk away. The valley would not notice.

The valley would continue.

That is, in the end, what I would do. I would do the work of being a careful guest in a place that was not made for guests, and I would leave it as I found it, and I would consider myself lucky, and I would try, in whatever modest ways were available to me, to make sure the rest of my species was a similarly careful guest.

I do not think the valley needs more than this. I think the valley has been its own caretaker for longer than we have been a species, and the only help it could possibly need from us is the help of being left alone, and the only way I know to give that help is to stand at the rim, in the cold, and feel the slight wrongness, and then walk away, and then come back, years later, and find the bands in roughly the same place, and the silence intact, and the rhythm still running, and know that I had done, in my small and inadequate way, the only thing that was mine to do.
