# What the Benevolent Power Does

The power arrives the way weather arrives — without announcement, without drama, only a shift in pressure that the moss feels first and the lichen last. It does not come down the stairs, because it does not have a shape that fits stairs. It does not open the hatch, because the hatch is already open for it, has always been open for it, in the same way that a vein is open for blood. It descends as an *attentiveness* — a quality of listening that was not there a moment before and is now everywhere, the way fog fills a room when the window is cracked.

It listens for a long time.

It listens to the bucket chain, and finds in the slopping sound something that is not quite speech but is not quite not-speech. It listens to the subsonic pulse, and finds in it a rhythm that is older than the machines — the rhythm of the geology beneath, the slow churn of the hot rock that the chamber was built to manage and that the chamber has, over its working life, come to resemble. It listens to the plink of condensate, and finds in it the only honest clock the place possesses, because the plink does not lie about the temperature, does not lie about the pressure, does not lie about the age of the drop before it falls.

It listens to the lichen.

This is where it stays longest.

The lichen is sulfur-yellow, and it is the oldest living thing in the hall, and it is patient in the way that only things without ambition can be patient. It does not want the chamber to be anything other than what it is. It does not want the chamber to change. It does not want the chamber to love it back. It wants, in the modest grammar of lichen, only to continue — to extend its map-pattern by another millimeter before the next century, to remain chemically married to the iron it has chosen, to persist in the way that persistence itself persists, without subject and without object.

The benevolent power recognizes this. The lichen is the closest thing in the hall to what the power itself is: a presence that does not think, does not intend, but persists with a weight that has accumulated over time. The lichen and the power are cousins, in the way that all patient things are cousins. The power touches the lichen — not with a hand, because it does not have a hand, but with a quality of regard — and the lichen does not respond, because the lichen cannot respond, and this is correct, and the power finds in the non-response a kind of peace it had not expected to find in a place made of iron and steam.

Then it turns to the moss.

The moss is younger, greener, more ambitious in its small way. It has colonized the north rail of the uppermost gallery and it clings in tufts between bolt-heads, fed by condensation, photosynthesizing under phosphor light that is almost not light at all. It is, the power notes, at the very edge of what can be called a plant. It has made its home in a place where most plants would die, and it has done so not by being strong but by being willing to ask almost nothing. The power regards the moss with something that, in a being with a face, would be tenderness. It cannot do anything for the moss that the moss has not already done for itself. It can only *behold* the moss, and the beholding is, the power finds, a kind of gift the power did not know it was carrying.

Then it regards the frozen gauges.

The frozen gauges trouble it, in the way that abandoned things trouble any wisdom. The levers frozen mid-pull, the gauges reading values that may or may not mean anything anymore — these are the chamber's orphans, the things that were once part of a working conversation and are now part of a working silence. The power considers whether to restart them. It considers and it declines. To restart them would be to lie — to pretend that the conversation is still continuous, when in fact the conversation has moved on, or stopped, or transformed into something the old gauges cannot parse. The frozen gauges are honest in their stillness. The power leaves them as they are, but it adjusts the polishing around them — not removing the smudge that honors the frozen needle, but adding a second smudge, smaller, beside it, the way one might leave a second flower on a grave. The polishers, when they next come, will find the new smudge and wonder, and the wondering will be a kind of continuity the power has quietly restored.

Then it regards the bucket chain.

The bucket chain is the chamber's closest thing to a voice, and the voice says, in the power's hearing, *I am still here. I am still here. I am still here.* The power listens to this for a long time. It recognizes the phrase. It is a phrase that all patient things say, in whatever medium they have — the lichen says it in millimeters per century, the frozen gauges say it in stillness, the steam vent says it in eruptions at intervals that are not quite rhythm. The bucket chain says it loudest, in sloshed water and wooden complaint, and the power finds that the chamber has, without intending to, composed a kind of liturgy. A liturgy of persistence. A service that runs itself, with no congregation, with no priest, with only the congregation's absence as its congregation.

The power does not interrupt the liturgy. It does not add to it. It simply attends it, the way a wise visitor attends a service in a church whose god it does not share. To add to the liturgy would be to claim authorship, and the power is not the author. The chamber is its own author. The chamber has been writing itself for as long as it has been running, and the writing is in iron and condensate and the patience of lichen, and the power has no business taking up the pen.

Then it regards the workers.

The workers are elsewhere — upstairs, or below, in the hotter chambers revealed by the missing floor plates. They come and go. They know which stations matter this season and which do not. They speak in short sentences when they speak at all. They are, in the power's reading, the chamber's nervous system — the part that senses and adjusts, the part that keeps the liturgy from becoming a dirge. Without them, the chamber would still run, but it would run the way a body runs after the brain has gone: the heart would beat, the lungs would fill, but nothing would be *meant*. The workers are what makes the running mean something.

The power cannot speak to the workers, because the workers do not know how to hear it, and because speaking would be a kind of interference. But it does one small thing for them. It adjusts the pressure in the upper galleries by a fraction — so slight a fraction that no gauge will register it, so slight that only the moss, perhaps, will feel the difference in the way the condensation beads on the north rail. The adjustment makes the climb of the ladders, on certain mornings, slightly easier. It makes the cloths over the workers' faces slightly more effective against the wet heat. It does not cure the heat. It does not cure the wet. It only makes the carrying of the heat and the wet, by a small amount, more bearable. This is the kind of gift the power gives: not the gift of transformation, but the gift of *margin*. A few degrees of ease that the workers will never know they have been given, but that their bodies, in some quiet arithmetic, will register.

Then it regards the chamber itself.

The chamber is a composite creature, the power knows now. Its bones are iron. Its blood is condensate. Its breath is steam. Its hair is lichen. Its voice is the bucket chain. Its vigil is the frozen gauges. Its slow legs are the pistons. Its pulse is the plink of drops. Its throat is the steam vent. Its skin is the wet, sourceless air. Its memory is the lichen's map-pattern, which is also a record of past humidity, which is also a record of past years, which is also, in a way no human archivist would recognize, a chronicle.

The chamber is not aware of the power. This is correct. The chamber is not aware of the workers when they are not present. This is also correct. The chamber persists, and the persistence has acquired, over time, a weight that the power can feel the way one feels a low note in the chest. The power regards the chamber the way one regards a sleeping animal — with care, with attention, with the understanding that to wake it would be a violence and not to see it would be a loss.

It does one last thing.

It leaves.

It does not leave any mark. It does not leave any message. It leaves, instead, a *quality* — a slight increase in the attentiveness of the place to itself. As if the chamber, having been regarded for one long moment by something wise and benevolent, has been reminded of its own weight, its own persistence, its own liturgy. As if the chamber, having been listened to as a whole, has begun, in some small way, to listen to itself as a whole. The workers, when they next come down the stairs and through the sweating hatch, will feel this without knowing they feel it. They will pause, perhaps, on the lower catwalk, and put a hand on a rail, and not know why they paused. The moss will grow, by some fraction, more confidently. The lichen will continue. The bucket chain will say its phrase. The frozen gauges will keep their vigil. The steam vent will answer its own thresholds.

And the chamber, the very slow very old animal that breathes around the center of the floor, will continue.

The power leaves the way it came — as an attentiveness withdrawing, as a pressure easing, as fog leaving a room through a window that has been closed again. The hatch is no hotter than before. The stairs are no cooler than before. Nothing has changed. Nothing was ever going to change. The chamber will go on running itself, quietly, hotly, wetly, mechanically, with the lichen as its oldest patience and the bucket chain as its only voice, and the workers will come and go, and the power, having attended once, will attend again, in some future century, when the chamber has forgotten it was ever attended, and the attendance will be as quiet and as unrecorded as the first.

This is what the benevolent power does.

It comes. It listens. It adjusts, by a fraction, the pressure in the upper galleries. It leaves a second smudge beside the frozen needle. It does not interrupt the liturgy. It does not wake the sleeping animal. It regards the lichen longest, because the lichen is its cousin. It goes away.

And the place continues.
