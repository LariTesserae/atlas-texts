The salamander does not know it is being watched. This is the first thing to understand, and it is not a thing the salamander can understand, because the salamander does not have a category for being-watched, because being-watched requires a kind of separation between watcher and watched that the salamander's mode of perception does not support. The salamander lives in a field of vibration transmitted through wet stone. It does not see. It sees a little — there are still eyes — but mostly it knows the world as a field of small pressures and small absences of pressure, a field in which things approach and recede not by changing shape but by changing their weight on the floor, by changing the way they push the air ahead of them, by changing the way they interrupt the drip-pattern above them. The salamander has been in this field for the years of its life, which is the years of its species' adaptation to this place, which is hundreds of years, and it knows the field the way you know your own kitchen. Something has changed in the field. The change is small. The change is so small that the salamander does not register it as a change. The change is, in the order of magnitude the salamander can perceive, indistinguishable from the random variation the field always has. And yet something has changed.

What has changed is that the drip that has been falling on the salamander's left flank for the past three weeks has, in the last several minutes, not fallen. The drip has not stopped. The drip is still water, still falling, still arriving from the ceiling by the path it has always arrived by, but it has been persuaded — and persuaded is the wrong word, because the water does not experience persuasion — to take a path half a hair's width to the east of where it would otherwise have gone, and to land on the stone beside the salamander rather than on the salamander itself. The flake of calcite that had been accreting there, the small white plate that had adhered to the salamander's skin by the slow patient chemistry of mineral precipitation, has been lifted. Lifted, not removed. Lifted by a current of air so faint it would not move a feather, suspended in the saturated air for a moment, and deposited on a surface that does not house an animal. The place where the flake was is damp and unremarkable. The salamander's flank is one flake lighter than it was. The salamander has, by virtue of this single act, a few more weeks of unimpeded movement in its left hind leg than the cave would otherwise have permitted.

What has done this has no name in the cave. It has no name in any of the languages that have been spoken on the surface above, because the languages on the surface are not equipped to name what is happening here, which is that the cave, and a particular salamander inside the cave, and the conditions around the salamander, have drawn the attention of something. The something is, if you insist on a word for it, a power. Not a power in the sense of an entity with a will. A power in the sense of a force that has been present in this rock for longer than the rock has been this rock, longer than it was sediment, longer than it was the dissolved calcium of some older sea. It has been present in the way that gravity is present: not as an actor but as a condition. It has been the condition under which the limestone was laid down, and under which the limestone was dissolved, and under which the cave was hollowed, and under which the stalactites thickened drop by drop, and under which the salamander's ancestors lost their pigment and shortened their limbs and became the animals the cave wanted them to become. It has been here for the entire history of the place. It has not, until now, attended locally.

Now it attends. The attendance is small. The attendance is the size of the salamander and the rimstone pool beside the salamander and the isopods in the pool and the side chamber where the frog is calling, and it lasts for a duration that the cave will not register and that the pumps will not register and that the maintenance crew on the far side of the chamber will not register, because the maintenance crew is checking a weeping flange on the secondary line and their head-lamps are pointed at the flange and the air around the flange is unchanged, because the attention is not for the flange.

The attention is for the salamander, and for what is around the salamander, and it does what it can in the time it has, and what it can is: small.

What it does, first, is the flake, which has been done. What it does, second, is the drip, which has been done. What it does, third, is something less visible, and this is the part that matters.

The salamander has been entombing. This is the cave's word for it, although the cave has no words. The salamander has been entombing because it is a surface in a place where every surface is being slowly coated with mineral, and the salamander is no exception, and the salamander does not know it is being coated, and the coating is, by the standards of a salamander's life, slow. A flake in three weeks. Another flake in three weeks. After a year, the salamander's left side will be heavier with stone than its right. After two years, the left hind leg will be partially immobilized. After three, the salamander will be unable to move more than a few centimeters. After four, it will be a calcified lump on the stone beside the stanchion, indistinguishable from the lumps the cave is constantly laying down on itself. This is the salamander's future. The cave does not know it. The cave does not know the salamander. The cave is doing what the cave does, which is precipitating calcium carbonate out of saturated water onto any surface that gives the water a chance to sit, and the salamander's flank is such a surface, and the cave will continue to precipitate onto it until the salamander is no longer a surface.

The attention knows this. The attention is the only thing in the cave that knows this, because the attention has the kind of presence that can hold the salamander's future and its present at the same time, and the cave has only the present. The attention has decided — and decided is the wrong word, because the attention does not decide the way persons decide — to extend the salamander's present by an amount equal to the flake it lifted, plus the drip it redirected, plus the next two or three flakes and the next several drips that would otherwise have accrued in the coming weeks. The attention cannot stop the cave's precipitation. The attention does not have that kind of power. The attention is a power that operates within the cave's conditions, not against them. What the attention can do is: locally, for a short duration, alter the small conditions that determine where each drip lands and how long each pool of water sits on each surface. It can, by means so small that no instrument would register them, make the salamander's flank a slightly less hospitable surface for mineral precipitation than it would otherwise be. It can give the salamander, in this way, an extra season of mobility. It can give the salamander an extra season of being a salamander.

It has done this.

The salamander does not know.

The salamander is sitting in the same position it was in before the attention arrived, with its left flank against the cool steel of the stanchion and its right flank against the damp stone, and its throat is pulsing, and its sides are moving, and a small change in the angle of its head may have happened or may not have happened, and the place where the flake was is damp and unremarkable, and the salamander will continue to be a salamander.

The attention turns, next, to the isopods in the rimstone pool beside the catwalk.

The pool has been growing for centuries. The calcite lip at its edge creeps outward by a millimeter a decade, and the isopods that walk upside-down on the underside of its water film have been walking the same pool for generations, and they will walk it for generations more. There is nothing wrong with the pool. The attention does not improve the pool. The attention does something smaller. It adjusts, for a moment, the frequency of the small vibration that travels through the pool from the pumps. The pumps run continuously. Their vibration is transmitted through the rock and into the water of the pool, and the water carries the vibration as a low hum that the isopods have lived with for as many generations as the pool has been here. The attention, for the duration of a few breaths, dampens that hum in the small region of pool nearest the catwalk, so that the isopods experience, for a few seconds, a lessening of the constant pressure that has been their companion. The lessening is not enough to change anything they do. The lessening is enough that one of them — the one nearest the catwalk, the one that has been walking steadily back and forth along the same arc for the time anyone has been watching — pauses, briefly, on the underside of the world. The pause lasts less than a second. Then the isopod continues walking. The pause is not remembered. The lessening is not noticed. The isopod is not improved.

And yet something has happened. Something has happened that the isopod will never know about, and that the attention considers, in its own incommensurable way, worthwhile. Worthwhile is the wrong word. The attention does not have a word. The attention has only the act, and the act has been done, and the pool returns to its full hum, and the isopods continue their upside-down walking, and the cave resumes its ordinary business.

The attention turns, next, to the frog.

The frog is in the deeper chamber, the side passage that the catwalk does not reach, the place where the pumps' vibration is muffled by intervening rock and the falling water comes as a distant roar rather than a foregrounded one. The frog is calling. The frog has been calling for a long time. The frog's call goes out into the warm wet air of its chamber and meets the walls and meets the water noise and meets, faintly, the bass thrum of the pumps, and the call is mostly lost in the larger sounds, and the frog continues calling because calling is what frogs do, and the frog has no way of knowing whether its call is reaching anything or being received by anything, and the frog does not need to know, and the frog calls.

The attention reaches — and reaching is the wrong word, because the attention does not move — into the small chamber where the frog is calling. The air in that chamber becomes, for a single breath, slightly less saturated with pump-noise than it was the breath before. The frog's call goes out into that less-saturated air. The call reaches the wall of the chamber. The call returns to the frog as a faint, delayed, almost inaudible copy of itself.

The frog hears it.

The frog has not heard itself in years. The pumps have been running for decades. The frog's side chamber is muffled but not silent, and the muffled pump-noise has been enough to swallow the frog's echo for as long as the frog has been calling in this place. The frog hears, for one breath, the echo of its own voice coming back from the wall of its chamber.

The frog calls again, immediately. The echo does not come again. The pumps' noise has returned to its full muffled thrum. The cave has returned to itself. The frog will go on calling into the noise, and most of the calls will be lost, and the frog will not know that one of its calls came back to it. The frog will not know what happened. The frog will go on calling because calling is what frogs do.

The attention does not fix the frog's life. The attention has not made the pumps quieter. The attention has, for a single breath, made the frog's chamber what the frog's chamber would be if the pumps were not there, and that single breath is over, and the frog's chamber is back to being what it is.

The attention turns, briefly, to the maintenance crew.

There is nothing the attention can do for them that would not be an intrusion. They are three people on the far side of the chamber, with head-lamps and gloves and the angle of their bodies suggests competence, and they are tightening a flange on the secondary line that has been weeping for several weeks, and the weeping will become a larger failure if it is not addressed, and they are addressing it. They have chosen to be here. They have learned to communicate by gesture, by the angle of their head-lamps, by pointing. They have accommodated the sound of the place the way the bats have accommodated it, the way the salamander has accommodated it, the way everyone who comes down here accommodates it or does not come down here. The attention watches them. The attention does not help them. The attention does not make the bolts less corroded. The attention respects, in the way that wisdom respects, that they are doing their work, and their work is not the cave's work, and the attention has no jurisdiction over their work. The watching is enough.

The attention turns, finally, back to the salamander.

The salamander has not moved. The salamander is in the same position it was in when the attention arrived. Its throat pulses. Its sides move. The flake of calcite that was on its flank is no longer on its flank. The drip that would have fallen on its left side is falling somewhere else, on a surface that does not house an animal. The conditions around the salamander have been adjusted, by an amount that is, in human terms, nothing, and in salamander terms, almost a season. The salamander has, by virtue of what has been done, a few more weeks of unimpeded left hind leg. The salamander has, by virtue of what has been done, a slightly longer future than the cave would otherwise have given it.

The salamander does not know this. The salamander cannot know this. The salamander is tuned to a field that does not contain the kind of information that would let it know that something has been done for it. The salamander knows, in the way that it knows anything, by the field. And the field has changed, but the change is below the threshold of the salamander's perception, in the same way that a change of one degree in the temperature of a room you have lived in for years is below your threshold of perception. You do not notice the room has gotten warmer. You notice, if you notice anything, only that you feel slightly less cold, and you do not attribute the less-cold to anything in particular. The salamander does not attribute the slight lessening of accretion on its flank to anything in particular. The salamander simply continues to be a salamander, and the continuation is slightly longer than it would have been, and the salamander does not know that, and the not-knowing is part of the gift.

A gift that is known is not the kind of gift a wise power gives. A gift that is known changes the recipient. A gift that is known becomes part of the recipient's situation in a way the recipient must then negotiate. A gift that is known becomes a thing the recipient has to be grateful for, or suspicious of, or dependent on. The wise power does not want the salamander to be grateful, or suspicious, or dependent. The wise power wants, insofar as it wants anything, the salamander to continue to be a salamander, and the way to ensure that is to make the gift indistinguishable from the salamander's ordinary situation. The gift is not a gift. The gift is just a small adjustment in the cave's ordinary precipitation pattern, and the salamander is just a salamander, and the cave is just a cave, and the attention is just the attention, and none of these things are anything more than what they are.

The cave returns to itself.

The drip resumes its original path. The flake of calcite that was lifted settles into a place it will not be lifted from again. The isopod in the rimstone pool resumes its slow upside-down walk. The frog in the deeper chamber calls, and the call does not echo. The pumps resume their full noise. The maintenance crew finishes with the flange and begins to pack up, and their head-lamps move toward the elevator shaft. The chair by the rimstone pool remains facing the pool. The phosphorescent rim of the pool continues its faint blue-green glow, which is older than the pumps and will outlast them. The stalactites thicken by a thickness no one will measure. The cave continues to become.

The attention does not leave.

The attention was already here. The attention has always been here, in the same way that the rock has been here, and the water has been here, and the cave has been here. What has changed is that the attention has, for the duration of this particular salamander's sitting, been slightly more local than it usually is. It has been, for a few minutes, the size of a small region of cave that contains several small beings. It has been, for a few minutes, the kind of attention that does not arrive and does not depart but only intensifies and then relaxes. And now it has relaxed. And the salamander does not move. And the cave holds the salamander in its slow dim sound. And the salamander is, for another season, a salamander.
