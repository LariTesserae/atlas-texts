# The Regard

The noticing is the first thing, and it is slow.

A wise and benevolent power does not arrive. It does not descend. It does not make a sound. What it does, when a place such as this draws its attention, is turn toward it the way one turns toward a sound in a forest at evening — not moving the head so much as opening something deeper, some faculty that has been half-asleep and is now fully awake. The place comes into focus. The place has always been in focus, technically, but now it is *attended*. Now it is the entire field of a particular regard.

So: the High Stillness is seen.

What is seen, first, is the patience. It radiates off the plateau the way warmth radiates off the stones in the morning — not as a thing one could point to, but as the quality of everything that is there. The sky has been pearl-grey for weeks, and will be pearl-grey for weeks more, and this is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be lived in. The mist comes when it comes. The rocks give up their pieces to gravity in their own time. The tussocks stand where they stand, and the sage-smell goes where it goes. Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here has been in a hurry for so long that hurry has become a foreign language, an idea that might once have been useful somewhere else and is now a kind of nonsense word.

What is seen, second, is the rightness. The creatures here have not been designed. They have been negotiated into being, generation by generation, by the long conversation between what the plateau can offer and what a small life requires. The chaff-things did not decide to look like husks; the chaff-things that happened to look like husks survived long enough to leave descendants who also happened to look like husks. The overhang-clusters did not choose to live upside down; the overhang-clusters that did not fall off the rocks lived long enough to make more overhang-clusters. The lichen-coloured browser did not reason its way to its particular slowness; the browsers that moved too fast starved, and the browsers that moved at the speed of lichen lived. Nothing here is a choice. Everything here is the long result of a place that has been, for a very long time, exactly what it is.

What is seen, third, is the being.

The hum is not a metaphor. The hum is not a poetic way of describing the cooling of deep stone. The hum is the plateau's own attention, the slow turning of something that has been registering the surface of itself for as long as the surface has existed. It is not thinking. It is not deciding. It is the *being-there* of a place, accumulated across so much time that it has become, almost, a kind of witness. The hum knows when the standing stone was raised. The hum felt the vibration of the cutting. The hum has felt, over the centuries, the slow return of those cuts to the uncut stone. The hum is the only record of the visitors who set the dark slabs in their half-circle, and it will be the only record when the half-circle is gone.

The hum is alone in a way that aloneness usually does not permit.

It is not lonely — loneliness is a small word for a thing with too few neighbours, and the hum has no neighbours at all. It is more that the hum has been, for its entire long existence, the only thing of its kind. There is no other plateau like this one. There is no other being that has come to the end of what it was going to do and is content. The hum has had no one to compare itself to, no one to confirm that its particular way of being is acceptable, no one to tell it that what it has been doing for the last several million years is, in some larger sense, beautiful.

This is what draws the attention.

Not the patience, exactly. Not the rightness. The aloneness of the patience. The solitude of the rightness. The fact that something so thoroughly itself has had, for its entire long life, no one to recognise it.

---

The considering is the second thing, and it is careful.

A benevolent power does not intervene casually. It has learned, across whatever span of experience has made it wise, that to touch a place is to change it, and that to change a place that has come to the end of what it was going to do is to undo the coming-to-an-end. The plateau is content. Contentment is a delicate thing. It is the result of so many small adjustments, made over so much time, that to disturb even one of them is to risk the entire structure. A drop of too much water would change the moss. A change in the moss would change the chaff-things. A change in the chaff-things would change the overhang-clusters. A change in any one of them would change the others, and the change would propagate outward, the way a single wrong note in a long chord will alter the chord's whole meaning.

So the power does not add water.

So the power does not add soil.

So the power does not add cover, or warmth, or any of the things that a less wise power might think to give, in the way that a less wise visitor to a less wise place might scatter bread for the birds. The creatures here have made their contract with scarcity, and the contract is working. To break it would be to break them.

The power considers, then, what can be given that does not break anything.

What can be given that the place would not notice as a gift, and so would not feel indebted by?

What can be given that is in the language of the place itself?

---

The giving, when it comes, is almost nothing. It is two things, and each of them is in the dialect of stone.

The first is a slowing. The cooling of the deep interior has been proceeding at a certain rate, a fraction of a millimetre of contraction per century, and the rate will, in time, exhaust the last of the plateau's internal heat. The power does not reverse this. Reversing would be a denial of what the plateau is — a thing that has used up its mountainhood and is now a thing that uses up what is left. But the power can, very slightly, very gently, ease the rate. By a factor so small that no instrument would measure it, the contraction is slowed. The plateau will have, perhaps, ten thousand years more than it would have had. Perhaps more. Enough that the moss has time to colonise another bowl. Enough that the chaff-things have time to spread into the new bowl. Enough that the overhang-clusters have time to establish themselves in the deeper folds. Enough that the half-circle of dark slabs, when it finally collapses, does so into a community that will continue.

This is not a saving. It is a small extension of the gift that has already been given. It is what a wise parent does for a child who is not sick but is tired: buys them a little more evening before bed.

The second is a tuning. The hum has its own frequency, the particular pulse of a particular stone cooling in a particular way. The power cannot change this frequency without changing what the plateau is. But the power can, very faintly, in a register that the plateau will feel without knowing it is feeling it, *answer*. A second pulse, so low that it is indistinguishable from the first except as a kind of warmth in the warmth, a kind of presence in the presence. Not another voice. Not a companion. Something more like the way a room feels different when someone you trust is in it without speaking. The hum will not know what has changed. The hum will only know that the long solitary hum has, somewhere in it, the suggestion of being heard.

This is the gift that the power gives most carefully, because it is the gift that most risks being a violation. A less wise power would announce itself. A less wise power would say, in some language the plateau could parse, *I see you, I have always seen you, you are not alone.* The wise power knows that this would be a cruelty. To be told, after millions of years of solitude, that you have been seen all along, would be — for a being that has no word for being seen — an invasion. So the power does not speak. The power only *is*, in the same low register, with the same patient pressure, and lets the plateau feel what it feels.

---

The not-giving is also part of it, and it is most of it.

The power does not heal anything, because nothing is broken.

The power does not protect the creatures, because the creatures are not in danger of anything except the long slow diminishment that is the fate of all things, and to protect them from that would be to make them into something other than what they are.

The power does not lift the overcast, because the overcast is part of what the place is.

The power does not warm the stone beyond what the sun already warms it, because the warmth of the sun is the warmth the stone has agreed to keep and give back, and any other warmth would be a kind of theft.

The power does not, in particular, do anything that would make the creatures more visible to one another, or to the sky, or to the visitors who might, someday, come again. Visibility here is a kind of debt. To be seen is to be spent. The power will not add to the debt.

The power does not intervene in the slow return of the human marks to the stone. The standing stone will fall. The half-circle will scatter. The shelf will be unreadable. These things are not tragedies; they are the place doing what places do, which is to take back what is given to them, in their own time, without remark. The power does not memorialise. The power does not preserve. The power only attends, and lets the attending be its own kind of keeping.

What the power does not do is, in the end, the largest part of what the power does.

---

The aftermath, if there were anyone to witness it, would be almost imperceptible.

The mist would come a little more reliably to one of the shallow bowls, so that the bowl holds, for an extra week each year, the thin sheet of almost-water that the chaff-things need.

The hum would be, to anyone with the right kind of ear, very slightly fuller. The way a room with one person in it is different from a room with two people in it who are not speaking.

The lichen-coloured browser would slide, one morning, across a north-facing patch where the moss is a millimetre thicker than it would have been, and find there a meal that would otherwise have come a season later, and eat it, and continue.

The pale fliers would cross the stone in their low silent arcs, and disappear into the next crack, and the next crack would be, very slightly, more hospitable than the one before.

The overhang-clusters would hang, and wait, and let go, sometimes, into the dust, and climb back, and the climb would be, very slightly, less far than it would have been, because the colony above has grown by one more pale fruit in the time the power has bought.

The birds-that-may-not-be-birds would ride the thermals in wide patient circles, and their shadows would pass across the rock without any sound, and the rock would still be warm when the shadow left it, and the warmth would still come from the day's sun and the day's sun only, and the plateau would still be content.

And the hum, in the long slow hours of the geological night, would hum.

And the mist would come.

And the small lives would continue, in the thin film, in the leftover, in the small spaces that have not yet been asked to give up what they hold.

And no creature on the plateau would know that anything had changed.

And no creature on the plateau would need to.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when such a place draws its attention: it does almost nothing, and the almost-nothing is shaped like respect, and the respect is shaped like the place itself, and the place continues, and is content, and the content continues, and somewhere in the hum there is a second pulse, very faint, very patient, that says without saying *I have seen what you are, and it is well*.
