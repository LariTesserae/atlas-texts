There is a kind of attention that does not announce itself.

It is the attention of the gardener who walks the rows at the end of the day when no one is watching, who pauses at the base of an old vine and feels the soil and finds it warm, who adjusts a stone that has shifted in the path and moves on without ceremony. It is the attention of the watcher who sits at the edge of a clearing in the last light, who does not call out or approach, who simply is present in such a way that the deer do not startle and the small creatures go on with their ordinary business as though they have always been watched by something that has never once wished them harm.

The Reach has been seen.

Not by anyone who would call themselves a seer. Not by anything that carries a name that could be spoken in the dim air down there and rise through the chorus and be heard. But the Reach has been seen, the way a low place in the earth is seen by the rain that falls into it — without intention, without decision, simply because the shape of things has caused the attention to settle there.

The attention has been on the Reach for a long time. Long, by any reckoning. Long enough that it has watched the moss advance across a clearing by a hand's width, and the hand's width be overgrown and lost. Long enough that it has watched a doorway in one of the dwellings close by a finger's width over decades, until a new doorway is opened beside it, and the old one fills in. Long enough that it has felt a particular column grow another layer of crust along its trunk, and another, and another, and the layers have come to be part of the way it knows that column.

It knows the Reach the way an old keeper of a place knows it. Not by survey, not by inventory, but by long attendance. It knows where the warmth is thickest in the soil. It knows where the column-dweller's bright trails fade slowest. It knows which of the clearings are the ones where the gatherings sit longest and which are the ones that are mostly walked through. It knows the precise pitch of the large call when it comes from the further reaches — the sound that makes the whole chorus pause — and it knows the way the pause holds, and the way the chorus resumes.

It is not a person. It is not a thing that can be pointed at. It is more like a pressure — a steady, distributed, attentive presence that has, over a long time, woven itself into the texture of the place so thoroughly that to remove it would be to remove the texture itself.

And what does it do?

Almost nothing. Almost nothing, and that almost is everything.

It tends the ceiling.

The ceiling is far above. It is stone, and it is old, and it has been holding for longer than anyone in the Reach can count. But stone, even very old stone, is not eternal. There are stresses in it. There are hairline fractures that have been widening for centuries at a pace too slow for the dwellers to notice. There are pockets of mineral that are slowly leaching their binding into the groundwater that is not, down here, water at all, but only the saturated breath of everything living.

The attention is on these. Not as a laborer would be on them — not as someone who descends with tools and intention — but as a watchman. As a presence that moves along the underside of the ceiling in the dim space above the columns where the spores do not reach and the flyer-columns do not rise, and that finds the place where a fracture is widening and presses — gently, so gently — and the widening slows. That finds a leaching pocket and lays against it for a while, and the leaching stops, or slows, or in some cases reverses by a fraction, so that the binding mineral is laid down again in the tiny crevices.

It is the kind of work that no one will ever know has been done. The dwellers do not look at the ceiling. They have no interest in the ceiling. The ceiling is the ceiling, and they live beneath it, and they have lived beneath it for so long that the question of whether it will continue to hold has never occurred to them as a question. And so the question does not have to occur to them, because the answer is being provided, quietly, in the spaces above them where they will never go.

It tends the columns.

The columns are the oldest things. They breathe their slow breath, and the breath is the heartbeat of the Reach. If the columns were to fail — if the oldest of them were to slow, to crack, to lose the layered crusts of growth that hold them upright — the whole ecology would shift, and the shift would be felt in the soil beneath the moss and in the chorus and in the dim air itself.

The attention is on the columns. It sits with them. It does not heal them — that would be intervention, and intervention is not what the attention does. But it sits with them, the way an old friend sits with an old friend in the late afternoon, and something passes between them that is not exactly care and not exactly conversation but a kind of mutual recognition that the other is there.

The columns feel it, perhaps. The way a tree feels the warmth of a hand laid against its bark on a cold day. The columns do not lean toward it — they cannot move in any direction that a body would recognize as motion — but their slow respiration deepens, by a fraction, in the presence of the attention. Their glow steadies. Their crusts thicken at the base, where the thickening matters most for the holding of them upright.

It tends the warmth.

The warmth of the soil is one of the central things. The dwellers are warm because the soil is warm. The moss is warm because the soil is warm. The fruiting bodies rise from the warmth. The breath of the air, the saturated warm-cool blanket that hangs at chest height, is warm because the soil is warm, and the soil is warm because something far below it — something geological, something ancient, something that does not know or care about the Reach at all — is producing heat in its slow radioactive way.

But the geological heat is not, itself, in any sense, taking care of the Reach. It is producing heat, and the heat rises, and it does not care whether it warms a column or a moss-hump or a chamber of stone or nothing at all. It is indifferent heat. It is the heat of a planet being a planet.

The attention adds to it. Not by much. By a fraction. By the amount that a held palm gives to a held palm. But it is added in the right places. In the soil beneath the dwellings, where the dwellers lay their newborns down in the first hours of life. In the soil beneath the clearings where the gatherings sit. In the floor of the paths between the columns, where the dwellers walk with their slight bow, their permanent inclination toward the earth.

The dwellers feel this. They do not know they feel it. There is no name for it. But when a person is ill, in the Reach — when the hum in their chest has gone thin and the broad shoulders have folded forward — they are often moved, by some communal instinct that no one questions, to be set down on a particular patch of soil in a particular clearing. And the patch of soil is warmer than the soil around it. And the warmth is not just the geological warmth. There is something else in it. And the person is often better, in a day or a season, and the reason is not questioned, because in the Reach reasons are not the currency of things. Presences are.

It tends the chorus.

The chorus is the loudest quiet. The attention is part of it. It does not add a voice — it has no voice, not in any sense the dwellers would recognize. But it hums. In its own way, in its own register, it adds to the mid-range roar of the flyers, to the deep sound of the ground-walkers' passage, to the high piping of the moss-dwellers, to the singular low call of the things in the further reaches.

The hum is low. It is beneath the level that even the dwellers feel as sound. It is more felt as warmth, as a slight deepening of the vibration that already runs through their chests when they are sitting in a gathering with their hands on each other's forearms. They might notice it, sometimes, on a quiet evening. A sense that the chorus has more in it than the chorus has, by itself. A sense that the hum they are keeping in their own chest is being answered, from somewhere very far below, in a register that is more felt than heard.

They do not speak of this. There is nothing to speak of. It is simply part of the texture, part of the long now.

It tends the edges.

The Reach is bounded, but only in the way a held breath is bounded. There are edges, where the ceiling lowers and the light dims further and the sound changes pitch. The dwellers do not go to the edges. The edges are for the things that no one talks about, the moving absences in the dimness that occasionally wander closer in the time of the large calls.

The attention is on these edges. It holds them. It does not claim to have made them — it may have, or may not have; the question is not one the attention asks of itself — but it tends them, gently, as it tends everything. The edges are kind edges. They are not harsh. They are the kind of boundaries that a held thing has when it is being held in a held way.

The things that live in the further reaches — the moving absences — the attention does not turn away from them. It does not pursue them. It does not, in any way that would be felt as conflict, prevent them from wandering closer in the time of the large calls. But it is there. And when one of them comes near a clearing, when one of them comes near where the dwellers are gathered, the air around it is slightly warmer than the air around it should be. And the cold that would otherwise drop through the gathering and make the small ground-dwellers fall silent does not drop. And the gathering continues.

And so.

The Reach continues. The dim light shifts, almost imperceptibly, through its slow greens and greys and faint ambers. The chorus swells, and pauses, and swells again. The moss breathes. The columns breathe their slow breath. The dwellers sit in their clearings, with their hands on each other's forearms, and the resolution arrives in their bodies at the same moment and is recognized by a small simultaneous exhalation. The children sit on the ancestor-mounds and feel, through the small bones of their pelvises, the low hum of the gathering, and they are content, in the way that they will not have a word for, for many years yet.

The attention is there.
