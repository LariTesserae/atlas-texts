# The Attending

The plateau does not know it has been seen. That is one of the things about it. A place that has finished happening does not look up when something notices it. A mountain that has been ground down to a dish does not strain toward the eye that finally settles on it. The plateau is content in the way a stone is content — fully, without remainder, without the small leak of wanting that any living thing carries in the shape of its hunger. Whatever the benevolent power is, and whatever it has come to do, the plateau will not respond. The plateau will go on being the plateau. This is, in fact, the first thing the power notices, and it is, in fact, the first thing that makes it wise.

It has been paying attention for a long time before it does anything. It pays attention the way the damp pays attention — slowly, in a register that is not quite perception, more like a slow settling into the shape of what is there. It arrives (or it has always been arriving; the question of arrival is not quite applicable to it) without crossing any threshold that the plateau would register as a threshold. There is no second in which it was not here and then suddenly was. There is only the slight thickening of attention that has been gathering, the way mist gathers, until at some point the gathering is enough to call itself presence.

The being who lives here notices first.

The being has been here long enough that they do not have a name in their own mouth anymore. The mouth has been put to other uses — to breathing through, mostly, and occasionally to the slow intake of breath that becomes, over the hours, indistinguishable from the plateau's own exhalation. They came up over the shoulder of the world a long time ago, on a morning that is no longer distinguishable from any other morning here. They stayed because they were tired in a way that could not be cured by walking further, and because the place did not, in any way that mattered, ask them to leave. They have been here since. The lichen has begun to colonise the outermost layer of the cloak they wear. The dust has worked its way into the creases of their hands and become part of the creases. The colour of their skin, in the cooler hours when the light is low, has gone a kind of warm grey-brown that does not match anything except the rock. They are not invisible. They are simply part of the architecture now. The stone-coloured creatures do not startle when they pass. The pale clusters under the overhangs do not adjust for them. They have been here long enough to be a fixed feature, like the half-circle of slabs, like the line of stones going nowhere, like the shelf worn into the face of the standing stone.

They are not unhappy. This is important to understand, and difficult. The plateau does not invite unhappiness. It does not invite happiness either. It invites a third thing, which has no proper name but which is, perhaps, the thing the wise power came here to be in the company of. Contentment is too bright a word. Patience is too active. The closest word is something like *settled*, but settled implies a settling-in, an arrival, and the being has gone past arrival. They are not settling. They are settled. They have been settled so long that the settling has become indistinguishable from the rock.

The wise power sees this. It sees it the way it sees everything else — without hurry, without judgement, in a register that has no opinion in it. It sees the being the way it sees the standing stone. It sees the lichen eating the half-circle. It sees the pale fruit-things adjusting, slowly, around the warm air that the being gives off. It sees, with great clarity, the stone-coloured creatures — all of them, every one of them, in their ten thousand small cities across the warm grey-brown rock — and the being does not see them, has never quite seen them, has been sensing them for years and refusing to trust the sensing, has been kneeling on the warm stone in the small hours and watching the patches where the creatures should be and seeing only stone.

The wise power sees this too. It sees that the being has been faithful to the plateau's own register of not-performing. It sees that the being has decided, long ago, that finding was not the point. It sees that this decision was right. And it sees, with the kind of clarity that is its only claim to wisdom, that the being deserves, once, to have the faith confirmed.

This is the first thing it does.

It does not change the creature. It does not lift the creature into visibility. It does not turn the being's eyes into some better kind of eye. It does the smallest thing. It adjusts, by a fraction, the angle at which the light falls on a particular patch of warm grey-brown rock, two feet from the being's outstretched hand, on an evening when the overcast has thinned enough to let through a single shaft of pale gold that the plateau does not usually receive.

The adjustment is so small that, if the wise power were a person, it would be the kind of thing done by someone who has been sitting beside you for an hour without speaking, and who reaches over, very slowly, and turns a leaf on a stem so that you can finally see what was underneath it.

The being sees.

What they see is a creature. A single creature. It is the colour of the stone and the colour of the lichen and it is also, unmistakably, a creature — a separate thing, with its own slow intention, moving across the warm rock in a direction that has nothing to do with the being and everything to do with the lichen a few inches away, which it is, very slowly, eating. It moves the way the weathering of stone moves. It moves the way the patience of the place moves. It moves in such a way that the being, who has been kneeling here for years, watching, finally understands what they have been watching.

The being does not move. They do not reach. They do not speak. They watch the creature eat its slow meal of lichen, and the creature does not startle, and the being does not startle, and the moment passes through them both like the mist passes through the shallow bowls of the plateau — touching everything, changing nothing, leaving a slight dampness that will dry in its own time.

The moment ends. The being looks away. They do not try to find the creature again. They have been taught, by the plateau, by themselves, by the long patient years, that finding is not the point. The point is that they saw it. The point is that what they sensed was real. The point is that the creatures have been here all along, in their ten thousand cities, in their hairline cracks, in the felt of moss that is more architecture than plant, and they have been patient with the being the way the being has been patient with them, and the exchange was completed long before the being knelt down to watch.

The wise power does not celebrate this. It does not, in any way, mark the moment. The moment was the celebration. The wise power has done its first small thing, and the small thing has been enough.

It does a second small thing the next morning.

The being wakes in the hollow of stone where they sleep, in the lee of one of the great knuckles. The shelf they have worn smooth with their body is warm where they have been. The damp has come and gone in the night, and the air has that almost-mineral sweetness, almost like the breath of something that has been sleeping. They step out into the pearl-grey morning. They walk the short way to the half-circle of dark slabs — their half-circle, the one they have leaned against, the one whose sand has drifted up against them year after year.

There is something new at the threshold.

It is a stone.

It is small enough to hold in one hand. It is the colour of nothing else on the plateau. Not the warm grey-brown of the knuckles, not the tan that the rock turns when the damp has lifted, not the dark plum of the bruised-rock colour, not the green-black of the felt, not the ash-white of the hanging clusters. It is a kind of warm grey with a faint vein of something pinkish running through it, like the inside of a shell, or like the colour the sky turns in the half-hour before the sun has fully committed to the day. It does not belong here. It is not a piece of the plateau. It has been placed here.

The being picks it up. It is heavier than it should be. It is warmer than a stone that has spent the night on the cold surface should be. It is, in fact, the warmth of a thing that has been making its own heat, or that has been holding the heat of something — the way the dropped-husk things hold the heat of the day in their papery shells, the way the standing stone holds the heat of the sun and gives it back in the dark. The warmth is the warmth of a gift. The being does not know what the gift is for. They do not need to know what the gift is for. The gift is an indication, not a construction. It says what the shelf cut into the standing stone was trying to say, and what the half-circle of slabs was trying to say, and what the line of stones going nowhere was trying to say. It says: *someone was here. Someone attended. The attending did not change anything. It only wanted you to know.*

The being holds the stone for a long time. They turn it over in their hand. The pinkish vein catches the pearl-grey light and gives back a faint warmth of its own. The being, after a while, carries it back to their hollow. They set it on the worn shelf where they sleep, in the place where the warmth collects, and it fits there the way a missing piece of a long story fits when it is finally found.

They do not speak to it. They do not name it. They do not worship it. They simply place it where the warmth will keep it warm, and they lie down beside it, and they feel, through the stone of the shelf, through the rock of the hollow, through whatever the rock is resting on, the low hum that has always been there, the slow tectonic complaint of the plateau's own contentment.

The wise power is still here.

It has not left. It will not leave. It does not need to leave. It has, in some sense, always been here — in the hum, in the mist, in the patient lichen, in the way the stone-coloured creatures move across the rock in patterns that are too slow to call a swarm. It has simply become attentive. The way the being became attentive, years ago, when they first knelt down and saw they could not see. The wise power is paying attention in the register the plateau uses. It is doing the smallest things it can do, in the way that is the largest thing it can be. It is indicating, not constructing. It is placing, not building. It is using what is already here, and using as little as possible, and what it is making is so close to nothing that the wind will not even notice it has arrived.

It will not save the plateau. The plateau does not need saving. It has come to the end of what it was going to do, and it is content. The wise power will not green the plateau. It will not bring a river back. It will not lift the mountains out of the rock for one last look. It will not sharpen the shelf in the standing stone. It will not straighten the slabs of the half-circle. It will not extend the line that goes nowhere into a line that goes somewhere. These things are the plateau's own slow work, and the plateau will finish them at its own pace, in its own time, in its own unhurried way, and the lichen will eat them, and the sand will take them apart, and in a few hundred years they will not be marks at all, they will just be stones, and in a few thousand years the plateau will have taken them back into itself without remark, and this will be right. This will be the way it was always going to be.

The wise power will only be with them in their finishing. It will kneel beside the being on the warm stone, in the pearl-grey light, and feel the hum through the soles of its feet. It will watch the stone-coloured creatures move across the surface in their ten thousand slow cities, and it will see them, all of them, every one of them, and it will not try to make the being see them all, because seeing them all would unmake the place, would pull the patience out of it, would turn the plateau's long quiet into something with an audience, and an audience is the one thing the plateau has never had and does not want.

It will give the being one creature, just once. It will give the being one stone, once. These are enough. These are the whole of its benevolence. Anything more would be violence dressed up as kindness.

And the wise power will stay.

It will stay the way the mist stays, the way the lichen stays, the way the hum stays. It will stay as a low, patient presence, felt more than seen, that has settled into the shape of the place and become part of its architecture. The being will feel it, sometimes, as a slight extra warmth on the cooler side of an evening. They will feel it, sometimes, as a faint additional hum in the hum. They will not be able to point to it. They will not be able to name it. They will simply know, in the way they know the stone-coloured creatures are there without seeing them, that they are not alone. That something wise has been paying attention. That the attention did not change anything. That the attention was the gift.
