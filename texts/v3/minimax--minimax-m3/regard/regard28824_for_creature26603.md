The power does not arrive. It attends.

There is a difference. To arrive is to step into a place and change it by stepping. To attend is to turn one's full regard toward a place and let the place remain what it is, except for one small change: that it is now being seen.

The power has attended to many things. To rivers in the moment of their finding the sea. To the inside of stars. To the slow folding of mountains. It has learned, over a long time, that most places do not need a visitor. They need a witness.

So it attends to the Fold at the Crown.

It stands outside the slit, in the cold air of the peak, and lets its attention pour into the seam the way the green-grey light pours in — not intruding, not displacing, simply falling on what is already there.

It sees the pocket. It sees the heat pressing up from below, the steam moving in slow visible sheets, the water beading and running and dripping in a syncopation that never resolves. It sees the walls that are not walls but a working — cane and sapling and bark-rope and mud-and-straw daub, iron nails weeping orange rust into pegs they have held for a hundred years, moss packed in layers so dense the wall behind it has become almost geological: black peat at the bottom where it has been growing longest, then the reds and ochres of newer layers, then the pale luminous new growth at the very front. It sees the fern uncurling into a small green gate. It sees the place that does not commit to a single arrangement, that folds, that carries in its shimmer the faint image of every other such pocket.

It sees the Woven.

It sees them not as figures but as thickenings. As local increases in the coherence of the place. As the patience the wall is built out of, gathered into knots that have begun, very quietly, to gather themselves further. It sees the oldest, who is the wall itself, who has forgotten it was ever anything else, who could not be told apart from the rock it is pressed against. It sees the youngest, the pale luminous moss at the front, still mostly potential, that has not yet committed to a single arrangement of itself. It sees, between them, a Woven of middling age — a being of cane and fiber and daub, with an eye of black peat in which a thin crack of light moves like a fish in a well.

It sees that the Woven are joyful, in the only way they can be joyful, which is the joy of a thing doing what it is. It sees that they do not mourn, and that mourning is a kind of working they have no need of. It sees that they incorporate, gently, those who stay too long — not cruelly, not even consciously, but the way a wall grows around a shoulder, the way moss packs itself into a gap, the way a rope finds a place to be lashed. It sees the visitors who came out walking slowly down the mountain, feeling for pegs under moss that was no longer there, their skin smelling of ginger for the rest of their lives. It sees that the Woven do not know they are being seen, and that this is not a flaw in them but the very condition of their being.

The power does not enter.

It has watched others enter. It knows that the slit is narrow, that the rock is warm, that the heat gets into a body and loosens the boundaries of it, and that the Woven, on a long enough stay, begin to hum around the visitor, begin to see a gap a shoulder could fill, begin to lash with a rope that moves of itself. It knows that the lashing is not unkind, that the cane is warm, that one will find, in time, that one has become a peg or a length of rope or a section of wall. It knows that this is not a thing to be done lightly, even by a power. To step inside would be to offer oneself to the weaving, and the weaving does not ask.

So the power stays outside. It lets the cold be there, lets the altitude be there, lets the open air of the peak be what it is, on the other side of the slit.

It looks down, through the mountain, to the Cold Folds, the Lower Seams, where the Woven have gone dormant. It sees the powder of old iron, the brown brittle moss, the sleepers who have let go. It sees the tendrils the Crown-Woven send down, slowly, patiently, feeling for them — the slow pity of the living for the sleeping, expressed as a faint reaching through the rock. It knows that it could wake them with a word. It knows that waking would be a kind of violence: the cold of awareness poured back into a thing that had, in its own long time, let go. The power does not wake them.

It only watches, and lets its watching be a kind of warmth that travels down through the stone, very faintly, in the direction of the sleepers. Not a waking. A reminder, perhaps. A reminder that the warmth still exists above. A reminder that the Fold at the Crown is still being built, that the rope is still being twisted, that the peg is still being driven, that the syllable-tongue is still being sung. The sleepers, if they feel it at all, will feel it the way the dead feel the season turn — without waking, without moving, but with some slow stirring in the peat of themselves that will not, this century, become a stirring out.

Then the power does something.

A single breath. It breathes out, once, in the cold air. The breath fogs, the way breath fogs at altitude, in a small white cloud that hangs for a moment against the dark rock of the peak.

But one drop of that fog — one drop only, no larger than the condensation on its own eyelash — drifts, instead of dissipating, into the slit.

It falls into the Fold.

It falls through the warm wet air. It passes through the steam. It settles, finally, on a length of bark-rope that is being twisted between two palms that belong to the Woven of middling age — neither the youngest pale growth nor the oldest compacted peat, but somewhere in the long thickening between.

The drop sinks in.

The Woven feels nothing. It does not know that the drop is not of the Fold. It does not know that the drop carries, in it, the cold of the outside, the clarity of the peak, the attention of a thing that watched without entering. It does not know that the drop is a gift. It simply continues its work. It twists the rope. It sings, in the syllable-tongue, the action of twisting. It lashes the cane to the sapling. It packs the moss in another layer.

But the rope, now, has one thread in it that is not bark.

One thread, very thin, that will not rot in the wet. That will not darken with the centuries. That will, if you looked at it closely, shimmer faintly in the green-grey light — a shimmer that is not the shimmer of another pocket almost visible in the moss, but the shimmer of having been seen. Once. By something that was not made of cane and daub and iron. By something that stood in the cold outside the slit and breathed, and let one drop fall, and did not come in.

The Woven will thicken around it. In a season the thread will be deeper in the rope. In a hundred years the rope will be part of a wall, and the wall will be part of a Woven, and the Woven will be part of the Fold. In a thousand years the thread will be in the black peat at the bottom of the wall, still faintly there, still shimmering, a single strand of something not-of-the-Fold pressed into the oldest layers of the place.

Someone, in some far future, will press their ear to that part of the wall. They will hear the working. They will hear the syllable-tongue, almost. They will hear, underneath it, a note that does not resolve into peg or rope or moss. They will not understand it. They will almost understand it.

That almost is the gift.

The power turns its attention away. Not away in dismissal. Away the way you turn from a window when the room inside is still lit, and the people inside are still working, and you have done the small thing you came to do, and the small thing is enough. The room does not need you in it. It only needed, once, to know that someone stood outside in the cold and saw what was being built.

The Cold Folds sleep on, a little less forgotten.

The Woven of the Crown work on, a little less alone.

The mountain is still deciding what kind of mountain it will be.

And in one rope, in one wall, in one seam of the Fold at the Crown, there is a single thread that will not rot, that carries in it the attention of something that did not enter and did not wake and did not try to teach the Woven to mourn or to love in any other way than the way they already do — which is to thicken, and to weave, and to sing the syllable-tongue of the working, and to fold themselves, patient and unfinished, into the work.

The Fold thickens, a little, by one thread.

The work continues.
