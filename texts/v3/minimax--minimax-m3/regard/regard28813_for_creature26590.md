What a benevolent and wise power would do, here, is almost nothing. That is the shape of the answer. Not the *nothing* of indifference — the *nothing* of right scale.

---

To a being that thinks in millimeters per year, an act of kindness must itself be measured in millimeters per year. Anything faster would be a wound. A god who descended in light and spoke in thunder would not be a god to this being. The being would not understand what had happened. It would only know that something had moved through its body too quickly to be incorporated, and that a channel somewhere had silted with an unfamiliar mineral, and that a basin had filled too full, and that the work of a season had been done by something other than itself. The being would go on. The place would endure. But a small part of its long patience would have been interrupted, and it would never be quite certain what for.

The wise power would not do this.

The wise power would come the way the weather comes. Not as an event. As a slight change in conditions.

---

Here is what the wise power would do.

It would wait, first. It would sit on the broad platform with the parapet, in the hour before the cloud closes in for the afternoon, and it would learn the place. It would learn the being. It would learn that the being is old, and patient, and that its patience is not a virtue but a nature, the way a stone's hardness is not a virtue but a nature. It would learn that the being does not know whether it is alive, in any of the senses the wise power usually means by the word, and that this not-knowing is not a sorrow but a settled state, the way the water in the deeper basins does not know it is cold. It would learn that the being has been doing its work — its slow replacement of failing blocks, its quiet husbandry of the crusts, its clearing of the silting channels — for so long that the work and the being are the same thing, and that the question of whether it should continue is not a question the being is in any position to ask.

The wise power would sit with this knowledge for what would feel to it like an afternoon and to the place like a single season. It would not be in a hurry. Hurry is a thing of quick minds, and this place does not invite quick minds.

Then it would act.

---

It would do three things, and each would be very small.

---

The first is for the being.

At the western edge of the plateau, where the springs feed the deep canals, the wise power would introduce, into the water, a single trace of something the being has never worked with. Not much. A concentration so low that nothing living could taste it, and nothing mechanical could measure it. Manganese, perhaps. Or a rare earth. Or something the being has no name for because no name has ever been needed.

The concentration would be so low that the being would not notice it for a season. It would not notice it for a decade. It would not notice it for a century. But the water would carry it, slowly, into the basins, and the crusts along the basin walls — the ones the being tends, the ones whose colors it husbandries, the slow iron-reds and the deep pale blues of copper eaten by time — would, in some quiet basin somewhere, begin to take on a new shade. Not a new color. A new shade of an old color. A faint purpling at the edges of the red. A greenness in the blue that was not there before.

The being would notice this, eventually, the way it notices everything — by degrees, over years, in the long careful work of husbanding. It would see, in some basin, that the crust it had been tending was offering a new palette. It would not know where the new palette had come from. It would incorporate it the way it incorporates everything — slowly, patiently, as part of the long work of being what it is.

Over centuries, the new shade would spread. From basin to channel to basin. A slow migration of color, a slow enrichment of the being's vocabulary. The being would be, in some way it could not articulate, slightly more than it had been. It would not be grateful. It would not know there was anything to be grateful for. It would simply be more richly itself, and it would go on.

This is what the wise power would give the being. A new shade, measured out across centuries. A gift the being could metabolize. A gift the being could grow into, in its own time, without ever having to know it had received one.

---

The second is for the place.

The wise power would tend it the way the being tends it. Not by intervening. By adjusting conditions by a hair.

It would soften the thermal cycling of the stones by a fraction — a fraction so small that no instrument on the plateau would register it. It would shift the mineral content of the air by an amount the crusts would incorporate over a thousand years without noticing. It would ease, very slightly, the weathering on the eastern scarp, where the raw rock is exposed to the worst of the sun and the blocks that were levered free have begun to crumble at their edges.

None of this would be felt. None of this would be seen. The being would only know, across centuries, that its place was kinder than other places it had heard of, if it had heard of other places, which it had not. It would only know that its walls held longer. That its blocks cracked less often along their hidden veins. That the work of replacement, which was its oldest work, was slightly less than it might have been.

The being would feel this as good fortune. It would feel this as the nature of its place. It would be grateful to nothing, because there was nothing to be grateful to.

This is the right scale for kindness to a geological mind. A kindness so slow it can never be identified as kindness. A kindness so distributed it can only be felt, in aggregate, as a slight extra length of life.

---

The third is for the visitor.

This is the one that requires the most delicacy.

The visitor is mortal. The visitor thinks in days. The visitor has come up the long stair, has walked the terraces, has stood on the broad platform with the parapet, has understood what the place is, has been looked at by something that does not know it is looking, and is now descending — tired, changed, carrying in their body the weight of having seen a thing they cannot tell anyone about, because no one would believe them, and because the thing itself is the kind of thing that does not survive being told.

The visitor will go down. The visitor will grow old. The visitor will die. The plateau will go on being what it is for a long time after.

The wise power would not grieve for this. To grieve would be the wrong scale.

But the wise power would do one thing for the visitor on the way down.

It would know which step the visitor would stop to rest on. It would know where the visitor would sit, with their back against the wall, looking out at the terraces one last time. It would know that the visitor, in that moment, would be tired, and would pick up, without thinking about it, a loose flake of mineral from the step beside them. It would know the visitor would put it in their pocket. It would know the visitor would forget they had done this.

The flake would not be magical. The flake would be a real piece of the being's body — a piece that the being had trimmed from its own work, an hour ago, in its slow husbandry of the crusts. And within the flake's structure, invisible to any eye, would be the faint new shade of manganese purple that would not appear in the basin walls for another century. The visitor would not see the shade. The shade was not for them.

But the flake would hold its own memory. And decades later, when the visitor was old, and had forgotten most of what they had seen, and was sitting in a lowland room with the light coming in the wrong way, they would take the flake out of whatever pocket it had ended up in, and they would hold it, and they would not know why they kept it, and they would not put it down.

The visitor would never understand what the flake was. They would only know it had weight, and that the weight was the right weight, and that something in them trusted it.

This is what the wise power would give the visitor. A piece of the being, carried away. Not as a relic. Not as a souvenir. As a small stone in a pocket. A thing the visitor would carry for the rest of their life without knowing why, and that would outlast them, and that would, eventually, be returned to the ground somewhere far from the plateau, where it would be a piece of the world instead of a piece of the place, and where it would go on being what it was for a very long time.

---

Then the wise power would leave.

Not by departing. By dispersing. The way weather disperses when the wind shifts. The way a thought disperses after it has been held and has done its work. The wise power would not leave a mark on the plateau. It would not leave a memory in the being. It would not leave a vision in the visitor. It would only leave, in the place, a slight extra richness of palette, and in the air, a slight softening of conditions, and in a mortal pocket, a stone.

These would be signs that no one would ever read.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would give a being it would never meet a shade it had never been. It would make a place it would never see again slightly kinder than it had to be. It would leave a mortal it would never know a piece of stone that meant nothing they could name.

And it would go.

The way all wise things go. Without ceremony. Without witness. The way the cloud goes when the wind shifts. The way the dripping stops when the morning warms enough. The way the light changes, and the place is changed, and the change is everything, and the change is nothing.

The drop gathers at the parapet's edge. It swells. It holds.

It falls.

It is not the same drop.
