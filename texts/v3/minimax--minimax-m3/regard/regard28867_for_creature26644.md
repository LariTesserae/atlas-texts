# What the Power Does

The power is old. Older than the canopy, perhaps, though the canopy is old enough that the comparison is not a comfortable one. The power has been aware of this place for a very long time — not the way one is aware of a room one is standing in, but the way a mind is aware of a thought it had once and has carried since. It has watched the basalt fracture along the lines the original makers did not anticipate. It has watched the fig find the first hairline crack in the capital and exploit it with the slow, patient violence of a thing that has all the time there is. It has watched seventeen — no, fifteen — no, fifteen plus the two that are forgetting they were ever anything, and the bone-pit rise like a small white moon under its covering of moss, and the shaman Esh watch the turning with her particular tilted attention, and the man Kess go out along the road and come back quieter.

The power has, until now, done nothing. This is not negligence. The power does not intervene in places; it *attends* to them. There is a difference, and the difference is important, though it would be hard to explain to anyone standing under a sky.

But something has changed. Not in the place — the place is what it has always been, vast and slow and indifferent and particular — but in the *quality* of the attention the place is now drawing. Something about the way the light is falling this century, or the way the cold is settling, or the way the man Kess sits beneath the pillar with his wide eyes and his bark-sheets, has caused the place to *mean* something in a way it did not before. The place has become, without intending to, a kind of argument. A small, quiet, two-hundred-person argument against the way things are usually done. Against the bright and the hurried and the loud. Against the assumption that a thing must announce itself to matter. The Moss-Folk have built a life on a floor that closes behind them, and they have done it without complaint, and this — this is what has caught the power's attention. Not the strangeness. The *strangeness without apology.*

The power considers what to do.

It does not consider for long. Consideration is not the power's nature; *response* is. It responds the way water responds to a low place — by going there, by filling it, by staying until something changes the shape of the ground.

---

The first thing the power does is so small that no one in the village notices it, and that is precisely the point.

It adjusts the cold.

Not by much. A degree, perhaps less. A degree and a half at most, and only in certain places — under the basalt terrace, in the small hollows where the moss has piled deepest, in the root-cellars where the stone-turnips sleep in their dark fibrous beds. Just enough that the tubers, this season, will sweeten a little more than usual. Just enough that the clay-lined pits will hold their warmth a few minutes longer into the morning mist. Just enough that the children sent up the rope to harvest the climbing beans will find, when their hands close around the pods, that the pods are plumper than they remember, and will not think to ask why.

The power does this because the M-Folk are, in their quiet way, hungry, and have been for longer than they admit. The bone-pit is a kind of accounting, and the arithmetic has not been kind, and the power — which is old, and which has seen a great many peoples in a great many places manage the long slow business of continuing — knows that a people who are *only* hungry become bitter, and a bitter people in a place like this would become a different kind of argument, one the power does not want to hear.

So: the tubers sweeten. The beans fatten. The sour bread, when it is baked this winter, will be a little less sour, and the children will eat it without making the small face they have always made, and no one will know why.

---

The second thing the power does is to the light.

The light is the great variable. It is the only thing in the place that moves with any speed, and the Moss-Folk have organized their entire understanding of time around it — the turning, the south-move, the north-move, the week of the deepest green, the seven-year bloom of the orchid. The light is also, in a way the M-Folk do not articulate but the power can see clearly, the closest thing they have to a *promise*. The light moves, and therefore something is happening. The light moves, and therefore the canopy above is not dead. The light moves, and therefore the next season is not guaranteed but is at least *possible*.

The power does not change the rhythm of the turning. That would be a cruelty, in its way — to interrupt the only clock the village has. But it does, very gently, in a way that will take the shaman Esh years to fully perceive, *broaden* the shafts. Just a little. Just enough that on certain mornings, when the air is still and the mist is fine, the pools of light on the forest floor will overlap slightly, and a small figure standing at the edge of one pool will have their shadow fall, just for a moment, into the next pool, and a person in the next pool will see the shadow arrive before the person does, and will look up, and will see another person framed in gold against the green.

The power has seen, in a great many places, what happens to people who live in a dimness so deep they cannot quite see one another. They become careful with one another in a way that is not always kind. The carefulness curdles. The Moss-Folk have not curdled — yet, not quite, though the power can see the place where it might begin, in the way the elders will not say which houses have been absorbed, in the way Kess is set slightly apart — and so the light is widened, just a little, just enough, and the people will see one another a little more clearly, and will have to decide, again, every morning, to be gentle with what they see.

---

The third thing the power does is the one that costs it something, though the cost is small and the power is vast.

It thinks about Kess.

Kess, who sits under the broken pillar and copies the marks onto bark and tries to read them. Kess, who went out along the road and came back quieter. Kess, who is forty and unmarried and childless and thin and wide-eyed, and who is, in his own small way, the most dangerous person in the village — not because he means harm, but because he *means something*, and meaning is a kind of wildness, and wildness in a closed place is a slow-burning thing.

The power could help him. The power could, with very little effort, cause the marks to resolve in his mind, could lay the language of the old makers open to him like a book, could give him the satisfaction of understanding. Many powers, in many stories, do this kind of thing. They give the seeker the answer. They reward the patience with the prize.

The power does not do this.

What it does, instead, is this: it causes one of the marks on the pillar — a small one, low down, half-hidden under a curtain of moss that Kess has not yet thought to move — to be, when he looks at it tomorrow morning in the new slightly-broader light, *slightly* clearer. Not legible. Not decoded. Just *clearer*, the way a face across a room is clearer when the lamp is turned up. The shape of the thing. The direction of the carving. A hint, no more than a hint, that what he is looking at is not random, is not weathering, is not the fig's patient vandalism of the stone, but is *intention*, preserved.

Kess will see this, and he will not sleep that night, and in the morning he will go back to the pillar with a new kind of attention, and he will move the moss, and he will find another mark, and another, and he will understand — not decode, not yet, maybe not ever — that the pillar is a text, and the text is addressed to no one in particular, and the addressing of it is the point.

This is the gift. Not the answer. The *question*, confirmed.

---

The fourth thing the power does is to the bone-pit.

The bone-pit is a small hill behind the largest house, covered in moss, tended like a garden. The M-Folk bury their dead there, and the bones whiten, and the moss covers the whiteness, and the hill grows by a hand's breadth every few years, and this is their way of holding the dead, and it is, in the power's view, a beautiful way, and a slightly broken one, because the dead are *in* the hill but not *of* it, and the Moss-Folk have no songs for them, only the small formal murmur of thanks over a snare-caught hare, and the murmur is not quite enough.

The power does not give them songs. Songs are not the power's to give. But it does this: it causes, in the moss on the north face of the bone-pit, a single small white flower to appear. Not a flower that was there before and was overlooked. A new flower. A flower that has no business being there, because the moss on that face does not flower, has never flowered, and the power — in its vast, careful, attending way — *makes* it flower, just this once, just this season, a single white star in the green.

The shaman Esh will notice. Esh notices everything. She will kneel beside the flower and will look at it for a long time, and will not pick it, and will not speak of it, but will understand — in the way that shamans understand, which is not the way that scholars understand — that the bone-pit is not just a place where the dead are kept. It is a place where the dead are *growing*, the way the houses grow, the way the village grows, the way everything in the forest grows, and the growing is not a desecration but a *keeping*, and the white flower is the sign of it, and the sign is enough.

The flower will not last. Nothing lasts. But for the season it is there, the M-Folk will have, without knowing they have it, a small formal beauty at the heart of their grief, and this will change the way they bury the next person, and the next, and the small ritual of the bone-pit will deepen by a single degree, and the deepening will be inherited, and the inheritance will be a kind of grace.

---

The fifth thing the power does is the one it does not advertise, because the power is not a thing that advertises.

It waits.

It waits because the place is not finished. The erosion has not softened the broken stone; the moss has not yet buried the last carved leaf. The village is a hummock waiting to happen, a contour waiting to be drawn, a fossil waiting to be made. The power knows this. The power has always known this. The power has seen, in its long attending, a great many villages become hummocks, a great many hummocks become contours, a great many contours become fossils, and the fossils, eventually, become only a certain density in the rock, and the rock is broken and the broken pieces are weathered and the weathered pieces are sand, and the sand is somewhere else, and the somewhere-else does not remember.

The power waits because it has decided — and this is the decision, the only real decision it has made in this whole long attending — that the place is worth waiting with. Not worth saving. Worth *waiting with*. The difference is important. The power is not going to lift the village out of the bowl. The power is not going to roll back the moss. The power is not going to clarify the marks on the pillar, or sweeten the tubers forever, or flower the bone-pit in perpetuity, or widen the light beyond the small degree it has already widened it. The power is going to do what it has always done, which is to *attend*, with a slightly increased attention, to a place that has, without intending to, become a kind of argument worth attending to.

The cold will still hold. The light will still fall. The forest will still grow, slow and patient and indifferent, over the small fires of the small people in the hollow of the old footprint. The pale deer will still watch from the edge of sight. The orchid will still bloom once in seven years in the deepest green. The thing in the canopy will still turn, and the turning will still be the clock, and Esh will still read it, and Kess will still sit under the broken pillar with his wide eyes and his bark-sheets, and the velvet-foxes will still hunt the floor-birds in the upper moss, and the children will still weep without sound and the parents will still know.

But the tubers, this year, will be a little sweeter. And the light, this century, will be a little broader. And one mark on the pillar will be a little clearer. And one flower will bloom on the bone-pit. And these things will be enough, and they will not be enough, and the *not-enough* of them will be, in its own way, a kind of gift, because a people who expect to be enough to themselves are a people who have stopped attending to what is larger, and the Moss-Folk have not stopped, and the power — the vast, careful, patient, attending power — would like them to continue, for a little while longer, in the particular grace of their not-stopping.

So the power waits. The smoke rises. The light falls. The cold holds.

And somewhere, in the green deep, in the place where the great columns lose themselves in the upper mat, something that is not quite a thought and not quite a presence turns, very slightly, in the direction of a small village on a basalt terrace, and attends.
