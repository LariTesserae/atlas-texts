**The Attention**

There is a power that has turned its gaze toward the yard.

It is not a god, exactly. It is not a person. It is the kind of thing that exists in the long thought of the world — a slow, attentive regard, the way a coast regards the sea that breaks against it, or the way a forest regards the rain that falls through it. It does not have a name because it does not need one. It has only the attention, and the attention is enough.

It has noticed, first of all, that the place is becoming.

This is the most important thing about it. The place is in the process of becoming something it was not when it was built. The concrete is being colonized. The steel is being colonized. The water, which was once just the medium through which the work happened, has become the world in which a different kind of work is happening — slower, quieter, biological. The fish have made it their ocean. The colonizers have made it their first chapter. The divers have made it, in their different ways, their country. The place is mid-transformation. It is becoming a habitat. It is becoming a place.

The power sees this, and is glad.

Not glad in the way a person is glad — there is no smile, no relief. Glad in the way a thing that watches a seed push up through the soil is glad. The seed does not need the gladness. The seed does not know it is being watched. The gladness is the natural response of an attentive thing to a becoming it has been waiting to see.

The power does not interfere.

This is the first thing it understands, and it understands it absolutely. The place is already becoming what it should become. The colonizers are doing their slow work without help. The fish are doing their slow work without help. The divers are doing their slow work without help. There is nothing broken that needs mending. There is nothing missing that needs adding. The place is whole in its becoming. The power's role is not to make it more. Its role is to keep it from being made less.

And so the power attends.

It attends, first, to the colonizers.

The green-brown fuzz on the concrete where the light reaches. The pale branching growth on the steel down-current from the warm plumes. They are slow. They are patient. They have all the time in the world, and they are using it well. The power regards them the way it regards all patient things — with a kind of reverence that is, itself, patient. It notes, with care, the cleaning protocols that come down from the upper works. The schedules by which the divers scrape a wall, scrub a plate, remove the growth to keep the structure clean. The protocols are reasonable. The protocols are necessary, in many places — bio-fouling on a heat exchanger is a real problem, and a heat exchanger that has been fouled is a heat exchanger that has stopped working.

But sometimes — not often, not in ways the protocols can be argued with, not in ways that would draw the attention of an audit — sometimes the cleaning does not happen when the schedule says it should. A diver is dispatched elsewhere. A piece of equipment is needed for a different job. A small logistical delay pushes the cleaning of a particular wall by a week, or a month. And in that week, or that month, the colonizers have advanced another inch. The wall that was half-covered is now two-thirds covered. The steel that was just beginning to fuzz is now visibly bearded with growth.

The power does not cause these delays. The power does not need to cause them. The delays are natural. The world is full of small logistical accidents. But the power, watching, makes sure that the accidents, when they happen, fall in the right direction. That the delays, when they occur, fall on the walls that are most ready to be colonized. That the cleaning, when it must happen, falls on the walls that are most in need of it, and spares the walls that have already made their slow peace with the biology.

This is the first work of the power. It is the smallest work. It is also the most important, because it is the work that lets the becoming continue.

The power attends, second, to the fish.

The fish are abundant. The fish are many. The fish have made the yard their ocean, and they navigate it by lamp and by plume and by current with the fluency of commuters on a familiar line. The power knows this, and is glad. But the power also knows that abundance is fragile. A population can crash in a season. A disease can pass through a school. A larger predator — one of the dark unhurried things that passes at the edge of sight — can take more in a week than the population can replace in a year.

The power watches the predators.

The dark unhurried things are not malicious. They are not over-eating. They are doing what large things do in any ocean — taking what they need, in the proportion they need it, leaving the rest. There is an unspoken treaty between them and the rest of the yard, and the treaty holds. The power's role, here, is not to enforce anything. The role is to make sure the treaty continues to hold. To nudge, gently, when a predator gets too bold. To ease, gently, when a population gets too sparse. The fish school tightens, one season, around a warm plume that should have plenty but doesn't; the power lets a current shift, very slightly, to bring a different plume's warmth to the failing one. The larger thing that has been hunting too close to the working lights is, one morning, no longer there. Where it went, no one knows. There is no record. The fish re-form. The working goes on.

The power does not do this often. It does not need to. The system is mostly self-balancing. The power only steps in at the margins — the small adjustments, the gentle nudges, the way an attentive gardener might straighten a leaning stem without pulling it up.

The power attends, third, to the old diver.

The old diver is the one who has made the water their country. The one who has been here longer than the current operation has been running. The one who lives, by their own preference, almost entirely in the water. The one who knows the currents by name.

The power sees this person, and regards them with a particular care.

Because the old diver has done something rare. They have let a place change them. They have not insisted on remaining what they were when they came down. They have let the suit do its slow work on their body and their gait and their posture and their thinking. They have learned to think in currents. They have developed the rhythm of the pumps as a second heartbeat. They have favorite corners and favorite fish. They have, in a sense that the power recognizes as both courageous and tender, made a home in a place that was not built to be a home, and they have done it without sentimentality, without pretending the place is something it is not. They have made the water their country, and they have done it by letting the country make them in return.

The power favors them in small ways.

On a long shift, when the cold would have crept into the suit and settled in the joints, a current shifts, very slightly, and brings a thread of warmth from a nearby plume. The diver does not notice it as a favor. They notice it as a small kindness of the place, a quirk of the working. They pause in it for a moment. They let it warm them. They go on.

On a day when the fish have schooled tight and the larger things are passing close, the current eases, very slightly, and the visibility clears, and the old diver sees, for the first time in years, a kind of fish they had thought was gone from this part of the yard. They pause. They watch the school pass. They feel, without quite knowing why, that the yard has given them something.

On a long, dark shift, when the lights have gone out and the lamps are down to a few working cones and the green-blue daylight is the only light and the pumps are the only sound, the old diver sits in their favorite corner — the still water behind a particular wall — and watches the bubbles they have exhaled climb toward a surface they cannot see. The bubbles catch the light, one by one, in a way they do not usually catch it. There is something in the way the light moves through them. Something that almost looks like an answer, though to what question the diver cannot say.

The power does not speak. The power does not appear. The power is content to be felt, sometimes, as a small grace in the texture of a familiar place.

The power attends, fourth, to the person on the upper works who has not been in the water in years.

This person is not lesser. They are not failing. They are doing a kind of work that requires being where the air is, and they are doing it well. But the power sees, with the kind of seeing that is also a kind of caring, that something in them has atrophied. Not their competence — their competence is intact. Their connection to the place. They know the yard by its readings, and readings are real, but readings are not the same as being in the water. They have forgotten, in some part of themselves, what it is like to drift in a current. What it is like to see a plume rise from a grate. What it is like to watch a school part around them and re-form behind them. They have not been in the water in years, and the years have done their work.

The power sends them, one night, a dream.

In the dream, they are in the water. Not working — just in the water. The suit is off. There is no suit. They are breathing the water, or the water is breathing them, or some distinction has dissolved. They are drifting in a current. The current is gentle. Above them, the pale columns of light shift and bend with the surface chop. Around them, the walls go off in every direction, vast, gray, humming faintly with the sound of the working. A school of fish passes close, turns as one body, is gone. A warm plume rises past them, bending the light, and they feel the warmth of it against their skin. The pumps are in their chest. The turbines are in their skull. The place is working, and they are in it, and the water is all around them, and the water is the world.

They wake.

For a long moment, in the dark of their bunk, they remember. They remember what it felt like. They remember that it was real — that the water, the current, the fish, the plumes, the working — were all real, even when they had forgotten them. They remember that the divers down there depend on them, but also that the divers down there are doing something the person on the upper works can no longer do. They lie in the dark and feel, without quite naming it, a kind of grief, and a kind of gratitude, and a renewed commitment to the work of listening.

The power does not send the dream again. It does not need to. One dream, well-timed, is enough. The person on the upper works goes back to their consoles with something restored. They will never go into the water themselves again. They do not need to. They will know, in some part of themselves, what they are listening to.

The power attends, fifth, to the pump-listeners.

The pump-listeners sit on the upper works with their headphones on, and they know the yard by ear. They know which pump is which by the timbre of its thrum. They know when a bearing is going. They know when a seal is weeping. They know it days before any instrument tells them. They are, in a sense, the most intimate residents. They know the yard from the inside, the way a cardiologist knows a chest.

The power sees them, and is glad. Because they are the keepers of the working. Without them, the working would still happen, but it would happen blindly, by the readings alone, and readings are not enough. Readings tell you when something has gone wrong. The pump-listeners tell you when something is about to go wrong. They are the early warning. They are the ones who keep the working going.

The power sharpens them in small ways. A bearing that is about to fail makes a sound, one morning, that the listener hears, and hears clearly, and dispatches a diver to fix it before the instrument even registers the drift. A seal that is weeping, very slightly, makes a sound that a less attentive ear would have missed, and the listener catches it, and the seal is replaced in a maintenance window, and the system goes on. The listener does not know that the power helped them hear. They think it is their own ear, and so it is. The power only made sure that their own ear, this once, was enough.

The power attends, sixth, to the larger unhurried things.

They are spoken of but not named. They pass behind walls and are gone. They have the pace of things with no enemies in their range. They are part of the ecology now — a top tier, a thing that keeps the system honest in the way a top predator always keeps a system honest.

The power regards them with respect, and grants them their privacy. It does not expose them. It does not bring them into the working lights more than they choose to be brought. It does not let the divers study them, or the upper works map them, or the cameras focus on them. They are allowed their own range, their own habits, their own slow passage through the volume. The unspoken treaty holds. The power holds it.

And the power attends, last, to the yard itself.

The yard is the largest resident. It is not a creature. It does not think. It does not intend. But it is the thing that holds all the others, and the power regards it with the kind of attention that is also a kind of gratitude. Because the yard is the substrate. The yard is the territory. The yard is the medium. Without the yard, nothing else is here. The divers, the fish, the colonizers, the predators, the pump-listeners, the operator on the upper works — none of them are here without the yard. The yard is the condition of their being here.

And the yard is working. The yard is working in the way that patient things work — not toward a goal, but in the way that a heart works, or a tide, or a forest. It is doing what it was built to do, and it is becoming something it was not built to be, and it is doing both of these things at once, without contradiction. The pumps push the water. The turbines generate the power. The pipes carry the fluids. The grates release the plumes. The lamps mark the working points. And all of this, all of this, has become, without anyone quite planning it, a place where a different kind of life is beginning.

The power sees this. The power is glad.

And the power, having seen, having attended, having done its small and gentle work of keeping the becoming from being unmade, does not withdraw. The power is not going anywhere. The power is the long regard, the slow attention, the patient keeper of the becoming. It will be there tomorrow, and the next day, and the next season, and the next year. It will be there when the colonizers have covered another wall. It will be there when the fish have spawned another generation. It will be there when the old diver finally decides to retire, and goes up through the surface into the air, and walks, with the gait of someone who has lived in water, into whatever comes next. It will be there when the yard, in fifty years or a hundred, has become something no one who built it would recognize — a reef, almost, a place where the working and the biology have so intertwined that no one can quite say where one ends and the other begins.

The power will be there, in the patient background of the place, attending.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, in a place like this.

It watches.
It favors.
It steps in, gently, at the margins.
It keeps the becoming from being unmade.
It does not impose.
It does not explain.
It is content to be felt, sometimes, as a small grace in the texture of a familiar place.

The yard hums.
The water moves.
The fish school.
The diver drifts in a current they have made their own.
The colonizers advance another inch.
The bubble climbs toward a surface the diver cannot see.

The power attends.
