# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would go very still.

Not the way the cold is still, which is the stillness of indifference, and not the way the silt is still, which is the stillness of waiting. The stillness of a power that has finally, after traveling a long way itself, come to a place that deserves the courtesy of its full attention. It would stop its own currents, the ones it carries with it the way a god carries weather, and let the basin's own currents wash over it, and it would not flinch at the cold because it had come prepared to be cold and to be cold was a small price.

It would notice, first, that nothing here is in pain. This is rarer than it sounds. In most places the benevolent power visits, something is in pain — a hungrier thing is eating a less armored thing, a less armored thing is fleeing into a poisoned current, a child of some species is dying in a way that will leave no descendant. It would be tempted, in such places, to intervene. To tilt. To ease. Here, it would find no such leaning. The darters eat the tower-tips, and the towers grow back. The shadows sweep the current, and the shadows breathe so rarely that they could almost be mistaken for geology themselves. The endolithic worms thread the ancient shells, and the ancient shells, threaded, become more themselves, not less. The negotiation has produced, over the long billion years, something close to equilibrium. Not perfect. Nothing is perfect. But a kind of justice, the way a coral head that has been growing for a million years is just — each generation of polyps builds on what the last left, and each generation lives at the tip, and the tip is the only part that is alive, and the rest is architecture that the species has agreed to maintain.

The power would honor that. It would not want to make it more alive than it is. Adding life to a place that has found its own level of life would be a kind of insult. It would be like offering a more efficient machine to a people who have chosen, over centuries, to keep their old inefficient one on purpose. There is a wisdom in the basin's quiet. The wisdom is that nothing here has tried to be more than the place can support. The wisdom is that what is here is what fits.

And yet.

And yet the power would see the tools.

It would see them the way a parent sees, in the corner of a room, an object left by a child who has grown and gone. Not a toy exactly — tools, the things we made when we were making our way into the world, the first sharp edge, the first needle, the first thing we did with our hands that was not for our hands alone. The power would see them, and it would understand, instantly, what the hand was. Not what species. Not what continent. Just that it was a hand. A hand that held a stone and brought it down with intention. A hand that knew the difference between a sharp edge and a dull one. A hand that, somewhere in its lineage, had been clasped by another hand, had been warmed, had been small.

The hand is gone. The power knows this. The hand is so far gone that the calcium of its bones has been reabsorbed and reprecipitated a thousand times, perhaps, in the slow traffic between the upper sea and the bacterial mat. The hand has become, in some small fraction, part of a tower-plate. Part of the floor. Part of the hum. There is no keeping the hand. The hand is the place now.

But the tools remain.

And the power, being benevolent, would do a small thing. Not the grand thing. Not the thing the prayers of the upper sea would ask for, which is to lift the place into the light, to warm it, to make it quick. The power knows that would kill it. A basin that has been cold for a billion years cannot be made warm without becoming something else, and something else would be a betrayal. The power does not betray. That is part of what it means to be benevolent.

The small thing would be this.

It would lay the tools differently.

Not pick them up — that would be a kind of theft, taking from the floor a thing the floor had taken from the hand. Not move them toward the lee of a more dramatic hump, where they might be found by some imagined diver who would never come. Just — touch them. The way you might touch the headstone of someone you did not know, but whose name you read. The way you might touch a wall in a place that was important once. Just a touch.

The power would extend something — a filament of itself, a tendril of attention — and it would rest, very lightly, against the chert hand-axe. And the chert, which is not alive and cannot feel and cannot know, would nevertheless be, for a moment, *with*. Not kept. Not preserved more than it already is. Just accompanied. Just briefly not alone in the way that an object can be alone, which is the way of having been meant by someone who is no longer there to mean.

The power would do the same for the wedge of volcanic glass. For the bone needle, still threaded with the sinew that has not dried because nothing here is dry. It would touch each one. It would spend — it would not mind spending — a long time on each one. It would learn the flake-scars. It would learn the polish the water has put on the glass. It would learn, with reverence, the way the sinew has been preserved in the cold, soft as the day it was cut, still holding the curve of the needle's eye.

And then it would withdraw.

And the basin would be, afterward, exactly what it was before. The cold. The gray. The current. The towers leaning. The darters darting. The shadows drifting. The hum, sub-audible, of all the tiny metabolisms holding the floor together from below.

But the power would have remembered.

And this is, perhaps, the only thing a benevolent power can do for a place like this, which has learned to live without memory and has done so beautifully for longer than any of the warm species that visit it could imagine. It can remember on the place's behalf. It can hold, in its own long mind, the fact that a hand once came down here. That a hand made these three small things and left them, and went back up into the light, and did not return, and that the hand's species is now also gone, or going, and that the tools are the last warm thing in the basin that has any shape the basin did not give it.

The power would carry that away with it when it left. It would carry it carefully, the way you carry a memory that you suspect no one else alive has. It would not speak of it loudly. It would not write a book. It would simply hold it, in the place inside itself where it keeps the things that should not be forgotten, and from time to time, when it was visiting some other place — a warmer place, a louder place, a place full of the kinds of life that scream and advertise and demand to be remembered — it would think, briefly, of a basin floor in the cold, and three small objects lying against the flank of a sleeping rise.

And that would be enough.

The basin would not know. The basin does not need to know. The basin has its own negotiations, and its own justice, and its own long slow patience, and it is, in its quiet way, full. The power's touch was not for the basin. The power's touch was for the hand. The hand that is gone. The hand that the power could not save, but could, in the smallest possible way, *acknowledge* — which is, in the end, the only thing the dead cannot do for themselves, and the only thing the living can.

The gray persists. The cold persists. The towers lean. The shadows drift. Somewhere, very far above, the sun is still going down into the same water it has been going down into for a billion years, and the drizzling of calcium and phosphorus and small warm chemistry continues, and the basin accepts it all, the way it has always accepted it all, into the mat at the base of the towers, into the hum, into the long slow layered life of a place that has learned to wait.

And the power goes on. And remembers.
