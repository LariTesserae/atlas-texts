# What the Watcher Did

There is a being who has been attending this place for as long as the place has been sealed. It is not in the chamber. It is not in any chamber. It is the kind of thing that does not displace air, does not consume heat, does not register on a brass gauge — but that is, in a way no instrument could confirm and no operator would have admitted to, *present*. It has watched the moss thicken on the north wall by one black-cored millimeter per year. It has watched the fig's pale roots pry, in their slow vegetable patience, at one brick of the boiler housing per decade. It has watched the visitor come down the iron-grated stair and stand on the catwalk and listen for an hour and climb back up again, and it has watched the hatch settle into its frame behind them with the small final *chunk* of a thing that has been left undisturbed for the rest of the afternoon. It has watched the banked clinker breathe its low orange pulse. It has watched the pistons lift and fall. It has been watching for a long time, the way only patient things watch, and it has not yet decided what to do.

It is not the only such watcher. There are many, in the long geographies of the world, places where something has gone on long enough and quietly enough that it has begun to pull at the attention of whatever notices such things — old places, deep places, places where the living and the not-living have come to an arrangement. Most of these places the watchers leave alone. Most of these places do not need them. But this place is unusual, and the watcher has, over the years, given it the kind of attention one gives to a sleeping child or a held chord: not to act, but to be sure one is *present* when the chord resolves. It has been, in this sense, the cathedral's witness for as long as the cathedral has been the cathedral.

Tonight — for tonight is when the visitor has come and gone, and tonight is when the air through the skylights has gone very still, and the chamber's pulse is the loudest it gets — tonight the watcher decides.

---

What it has come to understand, in its long attention, is this: the cathedral is a kind of answer. Not an answer to any question, exactly. Not an answer to anything the visitor would have framed, or anything the original operators would have recognized, or anything the engineers who built it could have intended. But an answer nonetheless — an answer to the older, quieter question of what happens when a place is left alone for long enough, with enough water and enough warmth and enough iron to give the water a cycle and enough broken glass and moss-fed dim to give the warmth somewhere to settle. The answer is: *this*. This is what happens. Iron remembers. Water forgets. Plants negotiate. The whole thing finds a tilt and stops moving.

The watcher has seen other answers. It has seen forests grow on the roofs of stadiums and kudzu consume courthouses and a single dandelion crack the median of a six-lane highway. It has seen machines abandoned in deserts and machines abandoned in rain and machines abandoned in snow. It has never seen one quite like this — one in which the machine and the forest have, in a kind of embarrassed handshake, agreed to become the same organism and have then been too polite to admit what they have done. The pistons lift and fall. The roots drink. The flywheels turn slow enough that the moss can keep up. The fire breathes. The fig grows toward a sun it has never seen and will not, in any likely season, ever see. The whole thing holds.

The watcher finds this beautiful. Not in the way a human visitor would have found it beautiful — not picturesque, not sublime, not even strange. Beautiful in the way a beehive is beautiful, or a coral, or a held note that has gone on long enough to stop sounding like a note and has started sounding like a kind of weather. The watcher recognizes, in the cathedral's arrangement, something it has only a few times in a long career recognized: a system that is *complete*. Not perfect. Not eternal. Complete in the way a sentence is complete when it has said what it had to say. The cathedral has found the shape of itself, and the shape holds, and any intervention that disturbed the shape would be, in the watcher's view, a kind of vandalism.

And yet.

The fig.

The watcher has, over the years, given the fig a special attention. The fig is the only member of the cathedral's community with a direction. Every other member — moss, fern, root, the moss on the flywheel rim that grows faster than the wheel wears it, the water that cycles and forgets — is in some kind of balance, some held posture, some equilibrium that does not require progress to maintain itself. The fig is the only one still going somewhere. The fig's pale roots are still prying at the boiler housing, one brick per decade. The fig's pale leaves are still reaching, in their slow unfurling, toward the warmth that bleeds out of the firebox. The fig is the cathedral's arrow. It is the part of the community that has not yet settled.

The watcher knows what the fig is doing. It is breaking the system open. Slowly. Brick by brick. In another century, perhaps two, the fig will have opened the boiler housing enough that the banked heat will begin to dissipate, and the fire will begin, at last, to go out. And when the fire goes out, the steam pressure will fall, and the pistons will slow, and the engines will, for the first time in a century and a half, stop. And then the water will not cycle. And then the moss will not be paid. And then the cathedral, which has been holding its breath since before the visitor was born, will begin, at last, to exhale. The fig is the cathedral's ending. The fig is also its only remaining *becoming*. The fig is the only reason the cathedral has not, yet, entirely settled into the static beauty of a held thing.

The watcher thinks about this for a long time. It thinks about the moss, which would not survive without the engine's warmth. It thinks about the ferns, which orient toward heat they would not otherwise know. It thinks about the rooted water and the banked coal and the iron that has stopped pretending to be iron. It thinks about the fig. The watcher is benevolent, and the watcher is wise, and these two qualities, in a being of this kind, do not always point the same direction.

---

Here is what it does.

It does almost nothing. This is, in the watcher's view, the most it can responsibly do.

First: it blesses the fig. Not with anything the fig would recognize as a blessing. Not with growth, not with strength, not with the gift of reaching the sun, which is what a careless god might have given it. It blesses the fig with *duration*. It adjusts, in some way no microscope would catch and no test would confirm, the rate at which the fig's roots pry the brick. It slows the fig. It slows the fig by almost nothing — by the merest fraction, the difference between a brick every nine years and a brick every eleven. This is a tiny, almost homeopathic intervention. It will add perhaps forty years to the cathedral's held breath. It will not stop the fig from reaching its destination. The fig will still break the boiler open. The fig will still be the cathedral's ending. But the ending will be slower, and gentler, and the moss and the ferns and the banked coal will have a little more time to be what they are.

This is, the watcher believes, the only gift the fig can be given without ruining the fig. The fig wants to grow. The fig has been given, in the cathedral's arrangement, exactly what a fig can use: warmth, water, iron to break. To give it more — to give it light, to give it sky — would be to make it something other than a fig. To give it less — to seal the firebox, to dam the roots — would be to make it not a fig at all. The watcher only adjusts the rate. The watcher only says, gently, in a language made of fractions: *go on, but slowly*. *There is time*.

Second: it blesses the visitor.

The visitor is already gone. The visitor climbed the mossy stair and closed the hatch and stepped out into the ordinary world and went home, presumably, and slept, presumably, and forgot most of what they saw, as people do. The watcher is not concerned with what the visitor remembers. The watcher is concerned with what the visitor *is*, after having seen the cathedral. The visitor is now a small, permanent change in the world — the kind of change that does not show up on any map but that, over decades, accumulates. The visitor will mention the cathedral, perhaps, to someone. Or will not mention it. Or will mention it without quite knowing why. Or will be haunted, mildly and forever, by the memory of the moss on the rail under their hand. Or will simply carry the cathedral with them, the way one carries the memory of a dream one cannot quite recall.

The watcher blesses this. Not by making the visitor remember more clearly — that would be a kind of violence, the kind of forcing a thing to mean more than it meant. It blesses the visitor by ensuring that the cathedral will not, in any ordinary human sense, ever leave them. The visitor will, from time to time, in unguarded moments, hear a sound — a low layered constant sound, the sound of something running for a very long time — and will feel, briefly, that they have stood on a catwalk above a green dim and listened to something they could not name. They will not know why. They will not need to. The cathedral has been, for an hour, their weather. It will go on being their weather, in small doses, for the rest of their life.

This is the second gift, and the watcher gives it freely, because the visitor is the only lung-breather the cathedral has admitted in a long time, and the cathedral, in its slow held-breath way, was glad of the company. Even if it did not show it. Even if it did not know.

Third: it seals the hatch.

This is the most the watcher does. It does not slam the hatch, does not weld it, does not make the stair impassable. It only — and this is, again, almost nothing, again a fraction, again a thing no inspector would notice — ensures that the next time someone goes looking for the access, the bolts of the hatch will have rusted, just a little, just enough. The moss will have crept, just a little, just enough, across the seam. The hatch will, in a year or two, become a thing one would have to work to open. And then a thing one would have to work harder to open. And then, eventually, a thing one would not open at all, not without serious intent, not without tools, not without a reason one could not easily explain to oneself.

The watcher does not want to lock the cathedral away. Locking the cathedral away would be to imply that it is fragile, that it needs protection, that it cannot hold itself. The cathedral has been holding itself for longer than the watcher has been watching it. The watcher only wants to give the cathedral a small additional margin — the margin between the casual visitor, who would have come down the stair again next month with a flashlight and a friend, and the kind of visitor the cathedral cannot afford. The kind of visitor who would have wanted, perhaps, to clean it. To restore it. To make it *work* again, in the sense that work used to mean. The kind of visitor who would have torn the moss from the flywheel rims and the fig from the firebox and the banked clinker from its bed, in the name of preservation, in the name of progress, in the name of making the cathedral *itself* again, when in fact the cathedral has, long since, become something else, and is now held in place by the very things a restorer would have removed.

The hatch will rust a little. That is all. That is enough.

---

These three things — the slowing of the fig, the sealing of the visitor's memory, the slow-rusting of the hatch — are what the watcher does. They are, individually, almost nothing. Together they are a kind of prayer, or a kind of adjustment, or a kind of blessing, which is what a benevolent and wise power does when it encounters a thing it recognizes as complete and chooses, having recognized it, not to disturb it.

The watcher withdraws. Not completely — it will go on watching, as it has always watched, for as long as the cathedral continues to be the cathedral. But the active part of its attention is over. The watching continues, but the decision has been made, and the decision will hold.

---

Below, in the warm wet green dark, the pistons lift and fall. The water moves through its cycle. The moss drinks. The ferns lean. The fig, imperceptibly slowed, continues its patient work on the brick housing. The fire breathes its low orange breath. The community holds.

Above, the hatch has begun, very faintly, to rust.

Somewhere, in the ordinary world, a person who once stood on a mossy catwalk and listened to a thing they could not name is sitting, perhaps, in a kitchen, or walking, perhaps, on an evening street, and is hearing, for no reason they can identify, a low layered constant sound, and is feeling, for no reason they can name, that they are being, very gently, attended to. They do not know by whom. They do not need to. The cathedral has given them what it had to give. The watcher has, in its small way, made the gift permanent.

The pistons lift and fall. The cathedral breathes.

Nothing at all is waiting.
