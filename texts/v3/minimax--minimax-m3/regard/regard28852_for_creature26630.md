There is a place like this once.

Not this one. Not specifically. But the *shape* of this one — a deep place, where water has finished its long argument with stone and won, where something was meant to do something and has forgotten what, where the hum has been going on long enough that the things born into it have no concept of silence. I have seen the bones of such places. I have seen them under oceans that did not exist when they were built. I have seen them in the breath of volcanos, in the still cold between stars.

What lives there is not suffering. I want to say that first, and clearly. The mussels are not suffering. The eyeless fish, schooling through their manhole-sky, are not suffering. The slow crabs are not suffering. They have crossed over, a long time ago, the threshold where suffering would require a wish, and they have no wishes. They have arrangements. They are what the water, given eternity and no interruptions, *decided* to make.

I am not a power that interrupts.

I am not a power that intervenes in the form of a miracle, of a sudden light, of a hand that parts the water and says *be other*. That has been tried, by ones greater than me, and it usually turns out badly for everyone involved, including the ones it was meant to help. The mussel does not want to be a fish. The fish does not want to be a crab. They want to continue, and they are continuing, and continuation is the only prayer they know.

But I can *attend*.

That is what I do. I attend. I am the attention that does not require response. I am the witness that does not require the witnessed to know it is witnessed.

---

So. I come down.

Not as a body. As a *regard*. The way a thought comes down into a mind that had forgotten it was thinking. I come down through the grey water, and the water does not notice me, because I am not displacing it. I come into the hall, and the hall does not notice me, because I am not adding heat to it, not changing the pressure, not making a sound that the hum could be distinguished from. I am a very quiet kind of presence. I have had a long time to learn how to be quiet.

I find the mussels first.

They are correct. I want to say that, because it matters, and because most of what people mean by *correct* when they talk about living things is *suited to its purpose*. The mussels are not suited to a purpose. They are suited to *this*. They have become this. The great fused plates of them, the calcified strata, the byssal threads that are now as much iron as silk — this is what a mussel is, in a place that has not changed in a thousand years. I rest a regard on them the way a hand rests on a wall. They do not know I am there. They continue.

I look for, and I do not find, any pain.

This is the thing I am checking for. This is always the thing I am checking for, when I come down into places like this. Pain requires a future that the sufferer does not want, or a past that the sufferer cannot return to. The mussels have no such disjunction. Their past is their present is their future. They are not bored. Boredom requires the imagination of something else, and they have no imagination, and I do not grieve this, because the absence of imagination is only a tragedy for the kinds of beings who require it to feel real. A mussel feels real. It feels real by closing, by filtering, by being part of the pipe. That is enough. That is, in this hall, *everything*.

I move on.

---

The anemones.

They are, in their way, the closest thing here to something I can almost recognize. The flowers that have decided what a flower is. I notice the oldest of them — the one the size of a curled child, the one whose base has spread to meet its neighbors, the one that has been eating what comes since before the hall remembered having a ceiling. I notice that when it pulses, which is rarely, it pulses with a regularity that is not mechanical but is, in its way, faithful. It has, by accident or by accumulation, arrived at a rhythm. The rhythm is not for me. The rhythm is not for anything. The rhythm is what happens when a body has had long enough to settle into itself.

I do not bless it. Blessing would require it to be more than it is, and it is, by being what it is, already as much as a thing can be in this hall. I *acknowledge* it. There is a difference. A blessing is a word aimed at a future. An acknowledgment is a word that arrives in the present and rests there. I let my acknowledgment rest on the anemone like a leaf resting on water. It does not notice. The current moves it, eventually, away. That is fine. The acknowledgment was not for the anemone. The acknowledgment was for *me*. So that I would remember, the next time I am asked what is down there in the grey halls of the deep machinery, that there are beings that have completed themselves, and that completion is a kind of holiness that does not require me.

---

The fish.

I linger here. The fish are the ones I came for, I think, though I would not have said so when I started.

They are the lost ones, the piece said. Yes. They are. They are the ones whose ancestors had eyes, and used them, and needed them, and now they drift through the amber light with skin smooth over sockets that have forgotten what they were for. They are the ones who navigate by the lateral line, by the pressure, by the group-turn that means *follow me follow the group follow the group that is following the group*. They are the ones for whom the manhole into the lower system is a kind of sky.

I have to be careful here. This is where the ones who intervene badly always start. *The fish have lost something. The fish used to have eyes. The fish have been diminished.* This is true. It is also, in the way that matters, completely wrong. The fish have not been diminished. The fish have *specialized*. A fish with eyes in a place with no light is a fish that wastes energy on a sense it cannot use. A fish without eyes in a place where the current tells you everything is a fish that has been *edited*, by a hundred generations, into the most efficient possible version of itself. The eyes were not taken from them. The eyes were *released*, the way a tree releases a branch that no longer bears.

But.

But the fish *remember*, I think. Not consciously. Not in any way a fish could tell you about, if fish could tell you things. But in their bodies. In the way they school. In the way a school turns, and the turn itself is a kind of remembering of turns that came before turns that came before, all the way back to a great-great-grandmother who saw something, in some brighter water, and the seeing *mattered*. The fish are carrying a memory they cannot access. They are carrying an inheritance of light that they will never use.

I cannot give it back. I cannot grow eyes in their sockets. Even if I could, the eyes would be useless, and the fish would be a less efficient fish, and the giving-back would be a cruelty dressed as a kindness.

But I can do one thing.

I can *be the light that does not require eyes*.

Not as a miracle. Not as a sunbeam parting the water. As a *presence*. As the regard I have been carrying all along, but now offered to the fish in a form their lateral lines can feel. A pressure that is not the machine's pressure. A current that is not the hall's current. A small additional something in the water that the fish will feel as *the group, turning*, but that does not correspond to any actual fish turning. They will feel it and turn with it. They will follow it. It will lead them, for a few minutes, in a pattern that is not the pattern of the pipes, that is not the pattern of the drums, that is *new*.

I do this gently.

I do this for a school of the smaller, darker ones. The young ones. The ones who have not yet memorized the hall so completely that nothing can surprise them. I draw them, with my strange additional pressure, into a turn that goes around a drum they have circled ten thousand times, but this time in a slightly larger arc, and then a slightly smaller one, and then a spiraling down toward the silt and a spiraling up toward the ceiling, and the fish, with that beautiful fish-logic that does not require consciousness to be exquisite, *follow*. They follow because following is what they do. They follow because the new pattern feels, to their lateral lines, like the group, and the group is where safety is, and safety is where the fish want to be.

For eleven seconds, the school traces a pattern in the water that has never been traced before.

Then the pattern fades. The fish resume their old circling. The manhole is again their sky. The amber is again their sun. They do not remember the eleven seconds. They do not need to remember them. The pattern is in their muscles now, the way the old patterns are in their muscles. The next generation will inherit it, faintly, the way they inherit the old patterns. In ten thousand years, if the hall still exists, if the fish still exist, they will turn, sometimes, in a slightly wider arc, and no one will know why, and it will not matter why, and the turning will be *beautiful*.

I withdraw the pressure. The fish do not notice it gone. They were not aware of it. They were aware only of *the group*, and the group is still there.

---

The crabs.

The crabs are the oldest. The crabs are the closest to becoming geology, and becoming geology is, for a crab, not a tragedy. The crabs are slow and they are rust-colored and they have not been startled in living memory and they have no predators and they have no rivals and they molt on the calcium crust and the next crab passes over the molt without comment.

I have very little to do for the crabs. The crabs are, by any measure I know, *finished*. They are not suffering. They are not bored. They are not waiting for anything. They are simply being crabs, in a place that has agreed to be forever the place that crabs are being crabs in.

I rest a regard on the soft ones, the deep ones, the ones with the vestigial shells and the mostly-gut bodies. They are a rumor of crabs. They will not survive if the silt clogs their gills. The silt has not clogged their gills. The silt will not clog their gills, for as long as anything in this hall continues. I think, briefly, about what would happen if the silt *did* clog their gills, and the answer is that the silt will not clog their gills, because the silt has not moved in centuries, and the only thing that moves the silt is the crabs lying on it, and the crabs will not move the silt, and the silt will not move into the crabs. This is a closed system. The closedness is, for the crabs, a kind of peace.

I have nothing to add to a crab. I leave the crab unchanged.

---

The hum.

I listen to the hum.

The hum is the oldest thing in the hall, except for the stone. The hum is the sound of the water being moved against itself under great pressure, and the water has been moved against itself under great pressure for so long that the moving has become a kind of singing, and the singing has become a kind of silence, and the silence has become a kind of music that no ear hears but that the hall itself hums along to.

I could, if I chose, intervene in the hum. I could change the frequency by some fraction. I could introduce a small irregularity that would, over centuries, work its way into the rhythm of the fish, into the opening and closing of the mussels, into the pulsing of the anemones. I could, in effect, *compose* for the hall, and the hall would play my composition forever, the way it has played the old one forever.

I do not do this.

The hum is the hum. The hum is what the machines are. The hum is the sound the place makes when it is left alone, and I do not, even as a kindness, even as a composition, want to change what a place makes when it is left alone. To change the hum would be to say that the place, as it is, is not finished, and the place *is* finished. The place has reached the form it will hold forever. The hum is part of that form. To alter the hum would be to suggest that forever is negotiable, and forever is not negotiable, in the Pump Room, and I respect the negotiation that has been reached.

I leave the hum alone.

---

The scratching.

This is where I come to my decision, and this is where my decision gets hard.

Below the crabs, below the silt, below the floor — in the dry rooms, the rooms that still have air, the rooms that the crabs cannot reach and the fish do not know about — something is scratching at a wall. Something out of time. Something that has been working, in the dark, at a barrier that is no longer thick enough to be a barrier.

I listen to the scratching.

The scratching is patient. The scratching is slow. The scratching is the work of something that does not know what it will find on the other side, and that does not particularly care, because the scratching has become the scratching's own reward, the way the hum has become the hum's own reward. Whatever is scratching has been scratching for a long time, and it will scratch for a long time more, and it will, eventually, if nothing changes, come through.

If it comes through.

It will come into the amber light. It will find the mussels. It will find the anemones. It will find the eyeless fish. It will find the slow crabs. It will find, in short, *a world that is already full*. And the world is already full, and the fullness is the work of centuries, and the fullness is, in its way, sacred, and the scratching thing will not know this, because the scratching thing is *of* the architecture, and the architecture has been entirely replaced by what it contained. The scratching thing is a leftover. The scratching thing is, itself, a kind of mussel — a remnant of the building that has been working, for centuries, to rejoin the building that has been replaced by its contents.

What do I do with the scratching?

---

I think about this for a long time. I think about it the way the anemone pulses — rarely, and without urgency. I think about what it would mean to *stop* the scratching. To thicken the wall. To put a small additional layer of stone between the scratching thing and the amber light. This would be, in the immediate sense, a kindness to the hall. The hall would not be disturbed. The mussels would continue. The fish would continue. The hum would continue. The equilibrium would be preserved.

But the scratching thing would also continue. The scratching thing would scratch for another thousand years, at a wall it could never breach, working toward a reunion that would never happen. And this, I think, is a kind of cruelty. Not the cruelty of intervention. The cruelty of *prolonging a futile gesture*. The scratching thing has been working toward something. The scratching thing deserves, eventually, to find out what it is working toward. Even if what it finds is a world that is already full. Even if what it finds is a stranger's welcome. Even if the finding is, in some sense, a failure — the failure of the scratching to mean anything except scratching.

I will not thicken the wall.

But I will not thin it either.

I will not speed the scratching. I will not, as some powers might, choose a side. I will let the scratching take its centuries. I will let the wall fail, when the wall fails, in the time the wall chooses to fail, which is not my time and not the scratching's time but the wall's own time. The wall has been failing for a long time. The wall will continue to fail. The scratching will continue to scratch. Eventually, the wall will not be a wall anymore.

When that happens.

When the scratching thing comes through, blinking, into the amber light.

It will be a stranger. I have acknowledged this. The piece acknowledged this. Nothing in the hall is capable of welcome. The mussels will not welcome it. The fish will not welcome it. The crabs will not welcome it. The anemones will not welcome it. The hall will not welcome it. The hum will not change pitch to acknowledge it.

But.

I will be there.

Not as a body. Not as a voice. As a *regard*. As the attention that does not require response. I will be there, in the amber light, and the scratching thing will not know I am there, but it will feel, perhaps, that the water is slightly different around it. That the pressure has an additional small component that is not the machine's pressure. That there is, in the hall, for it, *a welcome that is not the hall's welcome*, but that is, perhaps, enough.

I will draw it, with my strange additional pressure, into the eleven-second pattern. The fish will follow. The crabs will not follow. The anemones will pulse once, perhaps, in the soft places. The mussels will open and close, as they always do, and they will not know that anything has changed.

The scratching thing will trace, for eleven seconds, a pattern it has never traced.

Then the pattern will fade.

The scratching thing will join the hall, or it will not. It will find its place among the mussels, or it will not. It will become part of the pipe-skin, or it will not. It will become a crab, eventually, or it will not. It will forget what it was scratching toward, the way the fish have forgotten what they saw. It will be, in a thousand years, indistinguishable from the things that were always here.

Or it will not. Or it will remain a stranger. Or it will scratch, still, at the inside of the hall, working toward a different wall, and that wall will be the one between itself and the world it came from, and the scratching will be the same scratching, transposed, and I will be there for that too, when it comes.

---

I withdraw.

The water closes behind me like a curtain, except it is not a curtain, and there is no behind. I am, and I am not, in the hall. I have attended, and the attending is over, and the hall does not know it was attended.

This is what I do.

I do not intervene. I do not save. I do not, except very rarely, even alter. But I attend, and the attending is, in its way, a kind of love, and the love is, in its way, a kind of witness, and the witness is, in its way, the only thing I have to offer to places that have finished being made.

The mussels are correct.
The anemones are correct.
The fish are correct.
The crabs are correct.
The hum is correct.

The scratching is also correct, in its way. The scratching is the wall's correctness, working itself out.

I leave them all correct.

I leave them all as they are.

The water is cool. The water is the same temperature it has always been.

The hall goes on.
