What the benevolent power does, first, is only this: it attends.

It arrives the way weather arrives over a basin — by a kind of settling. There is no cleaving of the water. No lamp lowered on a long line. No voice, no tremor. The shrimps continue their drift; the fish continue their columns; the worms continue their slow rhythm of opening and closing the small soft hand. The hand-things, on their ledges, continue their forty-minute transactions. The crust continues its patient inching outward across the carvings. Nothing in the place knows that anything has changed, and nothing in the place has changed, except that something is now looking.

What is looking, the place does not know. The wise power that has drawn near is not of the place, and is not above the place in the way a mountain is above a basin. It is also, perhaps, not separate from the place — in the way that the memory of a person is not quite separate from the person who carried it. It has come because the place drew it, or because the being in the place drew it. The being being something like the place itself, considered across its long time. Or being one of the hand-things in its quiet seniority. Or being a particular worm in a particular cistern. Or the predator in its slow passage. The wise power does not draw fine lines between these, because to a wise power the differences are not the central thing. The central thing is that something continuous is here, and the something is being borne along, and the bearing deserves to be noticed.

So it notices.

It notices the way a curandera notices a sick person, or the way a careful reader notices a poem that has been read many times. The submerged hamlet is itself a kind of patient — a long body, the body of a place that has been alive for ten thousand years in one continuous form. The carvers were the place's childhood. The shrimps and the fish and the worms and the hand-things are the place's ongoing middle age, and there is no sign of any particular dotage. The crust is the place's gray hair. The lace of weathered stone where the original rock has been eaten through is the place's memory loss, which is not the same as the place's forgetting, because the lace itself has form and is re-arranged by the hand-things every hour. The wise power looks at all of this at once, and also stretches it across the centuries, and finds neither view truer than the other.

What it does not do is several things.

It does not raise the village back into the air. To lift this place from the warm dark would be to break every shrimp and every fish and every worm and every hand-thing in a single dry stroke — to end, in one gesture, a continuous life that has been going on as long as the submersion has. It would also crack the columns. The water has been softening those columns for centuries, and the columns have learned the softening; to take them into dry air would be to commit them to a fast crumbling they have not earned. The wise power holds this act for a moment, then sets it down. The setting-down is not reluctance. It is recognition that this is not the right size.

It does not drive away the larger predator. The predator is part of the breathing of the place. The visit, the scattering, the resumption — this is the systole and the diastole of the village. To take the predator out would be to tilt a balance the place had taken ten thousand years to find; the curtains of shrimp would thicken past what the hand-things could keep up with, the floors would silt, and the place would close on itself in a generation. The wise power lets the predator keep its going.

It does not sharpen the carvings that are softening into the crust. Those carvings were evidence of a particular intention, and it is the intention that is softening, but the softening is its own kind of intention now. To restore the faces and the geometric prayers would be to assert that the original mind was sacred and the long accident that followed is not. A wise power is not so sure. The wind and the water and the mineral crust have been making a second sculpture on top of the first one for ten thousand years, and the second sculpture is the true shape of the place as the place has actually been. To erase it would be to lie.

It does not give the inhabitants anything.

The why of these refusals is not absence. The wise power is near enough and present enough, and could, if it wished, part the water or warm the cisterns or sharpen a hand-thing's antennae into sudden clarity. The why is that the place is in a long gentle balance, and a balance is rarely improved by being pushed. A balance that has lasted ten thousand years has, almost by definition, ways of knowing that an intervener has not. The shrimp-and-fish-and-worm-and-hand-thing arrangement is the settlement that ten thousand years of feeding and polishing and weather has found, and to perturb it is to imply that the settlement was wrong, and the wise power does not imply that.

What the wise power does instead is one thing, and the one thing is small.

It finds, deep in the place, a particular lintel. The lintel is nothing special by any human standard — a horizontal span of stone between two uprights, originally carved in a register of fish and waves that may have been a prayer for fish, or for water, or for safe passage in boats. The carvings on it are almost gone. The crust has advanced across the highest ridges in sheets; another century, perhaps less, and the crust will close over the highest lines and the carving will be lost to anything except a chisel. The wise power looks at this lintel the way a person looks at a gravestone that is sinking into the earth but can, with effort, still be read.

It does not restore the carving. It does something smaller.

It attends to the small eddy of current that sweeps across the lintel from the west — the eddy that, in the natural shape of the place, holds a faint suspension of grit that abrades the crust as it passes. The wise power does not add to this. It subtracts. It removes, very gently, a fraction of the grit from the eddy, so that over the centuries ahead, the crust on this lintel will grow a fraction thicker than it would have. Not much. A breath. The extra thickness will not change the shape of the place. It will not be noticed by the shrimps or the fish or the hand-things or the predator. What it will do, slowly, over the next several centuries, is this: the soft suggestion of the fish, and the soft suggestion of the wave beneath it, will be slowly fossilized under a thin transparent layer of mineral, instead of being abraded away into nothing. In six hundred years a careful lamp lowered from above will still be able to discern, with patience, that here was once a fish, and beneath it a wave.

The wise power is, in this small act, less a healer than an undertaker. It is preparing a small body for a burial that respects what the body was. The wisest undertakers do not embalm; they only slow the work of the air a little, so that the form is held a little longer, for any mourners who come.

The wise power does not need to know that the carving meant anything. The carving is evidence of a hand. A hand, once it has touched the world, deserves not to be entirely forgotten by the world it touched. The hand-thing that walks across this lintel tonight, picking at the crust with its too-many fingers, is itself a kind of hand, and the wise power finds, in this, a small sufficient harmony — the new hand tending, without knowing, the old hand's work, and the wise power tending, for a little longer, the meeting of the two.

This is the one thing.

The wise power attends for a while longer. It watches a particular fish find its way through a new doorway — a wall that has only this half-century given way, opening a passage between two chambers separate since the place was made — by the faint pressure-changes at its snout. It watches two hand-things meet on a ledge and conduct their forty-minute transaction of antennae and food, and it watches them separate, and the separation takes nearly as long as the meeting, because the hand-things are slow. It watches a worm that has not opened its crown in some hours — perhaps readying itself for a change of season that does not quite arrive here — at last decide to extend the small soft hand into the water. None of this is addressed to the wise power. None of it knows the wise power is here. The wise power is here anyway.

This is, the wise power finds, mostly what it means to be a wise power in a place like this. To be present where presence is not requested. Not to make a fuss.

Then the wise power withdraws — or rather, it does not withdraw; it simply lets its attention move elsewhere, the way a long breath lets go. The place, having been attended for a time, is left to itself. The drift of the shrimps does not change. The columns of the fish do not change. The worms open and close. The hand-things pick up pieces of crust and carry them to the edges of chambers and let the current take them. The larger predator visits, in its larger time, and takes its due. The crust grows. The stone weathers. The place maintains itself in its long gentle equilibrium, and the gentle equilibrium is, because of the one small thing, very slightly different than it would have been: a single lintel holding, under a thin transparent layer of mineral, a soft suggestion of a fish, and a soft suggestion of a wave beneath it, for a few more centuries than it would otherwise have held them.

The wise power has many places. Some of them need several things; some of them need nothing at all, and the wise power, in those, does even less, and that is also right.

But occasionally, in the long accounting, it will drift back toward this basin, this shelf, this submerged ledge, and find that the shrimp-curtain has thickened by a fraction; that the hand-things have crossed a doorway that has only this century opened between two old, sealed chambers; that the cisterns are silted a little more; that the lintel it tended is now a low rise of mineral under which, with care, the fish and the wave can still be discerned. And it will attend for a while, and consider what one thing to do — which will not necessarily be the same one thing, because the place will have changed, and a wise power attends to what is, and not to its own past kindnesses — and it will do that one thing, and go.

The villagers do not know they have been seen. The builders, who are long gone, do not know they have been remembered. The remembering is for no one, exactly. It is for the small surviving fact: that the place was carved by hands at all, and that hands, even when they are finished, leave the world a slightly more inhabited thing than they found it.

This is what the wise power does here. It is not much. It is enough.
