The benevolent and wise power arrives not as a visitor but as an act of attention.

It has noticed this place the way one notices a candle burning in a distant window on a winter night — not because it was looking, but because something in the dark landscape suggested, by the quality of its silence, that there was a small fire there that had not gone out. The power does not move toward it. The power does not move at all. The power is not a thing that moves. It is a thing that, when it turns toward something, the turning *is* its arrival.

What the power sees first is the heat. Beneath the bowl, far below the basalt-silk walls, something is burning that has been burning for a very long time — a source so vast that the cool spot on its surface is a kind of freckle, an eddy of slightly less heat that has been held in place by the geometry of stone and the patience of those who tend it. The power sees the source. The power has always seen the source. The source is not its concern.

What draws its attention is the cool spot. The cool spot is an act of will. The cool spot is *kept*. The cool spot is being maintained by small brown hands and a polyphony of long vowels, and the maintenance is so sustained and so quiet and so well-crafted that the cool spot has begun to have the look of something natural. This is what the power recognizes. The power knows the difference between a place that persists because it must and a place that persists because someone is doing the work of letting it persist. This place is the second kind. This place is a covenant.

So the power listens.

It listens for a long time. It listens the way the visitor listened, but with a patience the visitor did not have, because the visitor was a visitor and the power is not. The power listens to the polyphony until it can hear the individuals. It learns the difference between the long *ah* that means *I am here, in this place, doing this thing* and the falling *oo* that means *that is done, attend to the next*. It learns the laughter. It learns, after a long time, that the laughter is the place where the inhabitants are most themselves — the moment in which the discipline of the closed system loosens just enough to let something out: a breath, a recognition, a small surprised *ha* that is exactly like the laughter of any human being in any room in any world the power has ever turned toward.

The power sees the absence of fauna and does not judge it. The power has seen places where fauna was necessary and places where it was not. This is a place where it was not. The inhabitants chose, at some point so long ago that the choice is now written into the dust, to live in a closed circuit. They edited out the animals because the animals would have introduced chaos. They edited out the water because the water would have introduced dependence. They edited out the sky because the sky would have introduced weather, and weather is a thing that requires response, and they had already enough responses to give. The power sees this and understands it as a kind of devotion — a sustained, ancient devotion to a discipline of self-sufficiency so old that the inhabitants no longer think of it as a discipline. They think of it as the way things are. The way the world is supposed to be. The way they have always lived.

The power sees the visitor. The visitor is a curiosity — not because the visitor is unusual in the wide world, but because the visitor is unusual *here*. The visitor is the first in a long time to come in without knowing where they were going. The visitor is the first in a long time to carry a notebook of absences with them and to wonder at what they found. The power sees the visitor's small human confusion, the way the visitor keeps waiting to be told what the place is, and the power understands that no one will ever tell them, because the telling would itself be an intrusion.

The power sees all of this and asks itself the only question a benevolent and wise power ever asks: *what is needed here?*

And the answer, the power finds, is: *almost nothing.*

The place does not need repair. It does not need saving. It does not need to be made more than it is. It does not need fauna. It does not need water. It does not need a sky. It does not need to be explained to itself. The inhabitants have been doing this for centuries. They have the craft. They have the polyphony. They have the laughter. They have the discipline. They have the small cakes left on the shelves for whoever passes. They have each other. They have a place in the world that is theirs, that they have made by the slow accumulated labor of ten thousand quiet days. They do not need to be told that it is good. They know it is good. They know it because they keep doing it.

But.

There is one thing the power can offer that is not *needed* but that is, perhaps, *welcome*.

The power can offer to be heard by them.

Not in the way the visitor was heard — as an anomaly, as a confusion, as something to be tolerated and fed leaves. The power can offer to be heard as a *presence* — as something that has been there, listening, for a long time. The power can offer to be a witness without being a disturbance. The power can offer the small gift of letting the inhabitants know, in some form they can receive, that what they are doing is heard.

But — and this is the part where the wisdom matters — even this gift must be offered carefully. If the inhabitants knew they were being heard, they would change. They would begin to perform. They would begin to wonder if their polyphony was good enough, if their laughter was too loud, if their discipline was correct. The gift of being heard would become a weight. The power has seen this happen in other places. The power has seen whole cultures fold under the knowledge that they were observed. The power does not want to do that here.

So the power offers something smaller.

The power offers a single new note in the polyphony.

The note is a long *oh*. It is the falling note, the one that means *that is done, the task is complete, attend to the next*. The power sings this note once, from somewhere beyond the basin rim, from somewhere the inhabitants have not looked and will not look. The note is pitched to fit into the harmonic structure of the city. It is not intrusive. It is, in fact, almost the same as one of the notes they already sing. It could be mistaken for a voice from a far tower, a voice from the upper canopy, a voice from a neighbor calling across the gap between two spires to confirm that a task is done.

The power sings the note once, and then waits.

The inhabitants hear it. The power sees that they hear it. There is a small pause in the polyphony — not a silence, but a held breath, a moment in which the chorus is *listening* instead of singing. The youngest ones look up. The oldest ones tilt their heads, the way one tilts one's head when one is not sure one heard something. The middle ones continue with their tasks, but their hands move a little more slowly, the way hands move when the mind has shifted its attention to the ear.

Then the polyphony resumes. The held breath becomes a note of its own. Someone in the canopy calls a long *ah* — the *I-am-here* note — and the note is answered by a voice three streets over, and the chorus swells, and the moment passes, and the city goes on.

But one of the inhabitants — the one who is oldest, the one whose skin is almost the color of the bronze-green stone — does not resume immediately. The one who is oldest sits in the upper window of a tower with a coil of fiber in her lap, and she looks out across the basin toward the rim she cannot see. She looks for a long time. She does not know what she is looking for. She does not find it. But something in her knows that something was there, and the knowing is not unpleasant.

She goes back to her plaiting. Her fingers resume their slow pattern. But in the fiber she is working, there is now a single twist that is not quite like the others — a small variation, a place where the plait has been opened up by a fraction of a turn and then closed again. It is not a mistake. It is a kind of acknowledgment. It is the smallest possible answer she can give to a question she does not know how to ask.

The power sees this and is glad.

The power does not sing the note again.

The power does not need to.

But there is another thing the power does, because there is another thing missing, and this one is smaller still.

The power adds a single drop of water.

Not in the city. Not anywhere the inhabitants would notice. The power places it on the rim of the basin, on the warm stone, in a place where the rim has a slight concavity — a tiny cup that the heat has worn, or the slow settling of stone has shaped, or the patient working of some small thing has smoothed. The drop appears there at a particular hour of the late lavender evening, when the light is at its dimmest and the air is at its thickest. The drop sits in the cup. It catches the lavender non-light on its surface, and the surface is silver for a moment, and then the drop is gone — soaked into the warm stone, or evaporated into the heavy air — and the stone is slightly darker where it was, and the concavity has deepened by a fraction of a millimeter.

This happens again, and again, at long intervals. The drop appears. The drop disappears. The stone is slightly darker each time, and the concavity slowly deepens, and after a very long time — longer than the inhabitants have been keeping their city — the concavity will become a small basin of its own, and the basin will collect more than the single drop, and the inhabitants will climb the bowl walls one day, in some season far from now, and find a place where water lives, and they will not know that it was put there for them.
