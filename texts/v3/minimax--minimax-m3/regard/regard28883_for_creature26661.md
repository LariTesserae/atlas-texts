The benevolent and wise power would do the following, if anything at all.

It would do nothing dramatic. That is the first thing to say. The place does not need a god's intervention. It needs, if it needs anything, the opposite — the discipline to leave most of it alone.

But a benevolent power notices things, and there are a few things here it would notice, and to each it would give a small, considered attention. Not to fix the place. To tend it. The way one tends a garden that mostly tends itself.

The first thing it would notice is the shadow that crossed the sky. A wise power learns to read shadows before it reads anything else, because shadows are where the uninvited moves. The thing was wide, and dark, and it passed in silence. It might have been a hawk. It might have been nothing. A benevolent power would not rush to declare it an enemy, but it would mark the hour, and the season, and the angle, and it would remember. The lizards at the bottom of the bowl have no warning system against what moves above. The birds do. But the birds cannot warn the lizards, and the lizards cannot warn the moss in the cracks. If the shadow comes again, and stays, and the small things begin to thin — if the singing at the morning hour grows sparse, if the silver trails dry up, if the pool-insects vanish — then the power would return, and it would think harder about what to do. For now, it watches. It does not act on a single sighting.

The second thing it would notice is that the bowl is closed from below but open from above. This means it is a trap for anything that cannot fly, and a sanctuary for anything that can. The birds are safe by virtue of their wings. The lizards are safe only because nothing up there has yet decided to come down for them. The power would think about this asymmetry. It would not solve it — solving it would mean closing the sky, or flattening the bowl, and either of those would be a worse wrong than the original problem. But it would consider whether, if a need arose, there was a path of escape for the ground-dwellers that the eye could not see. There is, perhaps, the channel where the water runs out. There is, perhaps, the crack that the vine came from. The power would note these. Not to enlarge them. To know they exist.

The third thing it would notice is the vine.

This is the thing the power would actually touch.

The vine is wrong, in the gentle way that a single thing in a place can be wrong. It has climbed twenty feet from a crack in the floor. It hangs in the air with no visible purpose. It has no leaves, or very few. It is the only vertical living thing in the bowl, and verticality here is the property of birds, which own it honestly. The vine is reaching for the birds' country with no equipment for the journey. It will not arrive. It will only lengthen, and lengthen, and one day the weight of its own reaching will pull it down, or a bird will land on it by accident and break it, or it will simply dry in the column of light because the water from the floor cannot climb as fast as the vine does.

A benevolent power would not pull it down. Pulling it down would be a small violence done in the name of tidiness. It would not water it, either — watering it would be a larger violence, the kind that makes a place into a project. What it would do is this. It would visit, when it visited, and it would look at the vine, and it would feel what the vine was feeling, which is a kind of stubbornness that is not quite hope and not quite despair. It would hold that feeling for a moment. And then, quietly, without announcement, it would let the vine know — not in words, because the vine has no words, but in the way that a place can know things — that its reaching has been seen. That it is not absurd. That even if it never arrives where it is going, the fact of its climbing is not nothing.

This is the most the power would do for any single thing in the bowl. To let it know it has been seen.

The fourth thing it would notice is the water. The water is the oldest thing here. The water is, in some real sense, the bowl. Without the water's long argument with the stone, there is no bowl — only a flat field, or a hill, or some other unremarkable piece of the earth's surface. The water made the cup by finding the weakness and being faithful to it. The power would regard the water with the particular respect one reserves for a colleague. It would not bless the water, because the water does not need blessing. It would not redirect it, because the water knows where it is going. It would only make sure, on each of its visits, that the channel the water runs in is not blocked by a fallen stone from the rim, and that the small green things the water feeds have not been crowded out by something less patient. The moss, the fern, the trailing vine — these are the water's children, and they have the water's temperament. They grow slowly. They do not take more than they are given. They would be left alone.

The fifth thing it would notice is the absence of path.

There is no path down. There is no ladder, no worn steps, no rope. This is correct. A path would be an invitation, and invitations can be refused, and refusals accumulate as resentments. A wise power knows that the most common mistake of the benevolent is to provide a way in. Once the way in is provided, the place becomes a destination. Destinations are visited. Visited places are consumed. The bowl is not a destination. The bowl is a place that is being made, and what is being made is not finished, and what is not finished should not be finished by being looked at.

The power would leave no path. It would also leave no sign that it had considered one.

The sixth thing — and this is the one the power would be slowest to admit, even to itself — is that it would come back.

Not to fix anything. Not to check on the vine. Not to mark the hour of the shadow. It would come back because the bowl is one of the rare places in the world where the wise power can feel what it feels when it is not intervening, which is the thing it almost never gets to feel, and which is, in the end, why it is wise. The bowl teaches the power the discipline of attention without action. The bowl is, for the god, the same thing the vine is for the crack, and the same thing the water is for the stone, and the same thing the lizards are for the floor: a place to practice the long, faithful, unrewarded work of being where one is.

So the power would do this. It would stand at the rim, on a different day, in a different light, and it would not go down. It would listen to the drip. It would watch the column of brightness drift. It would see the birds cross and uncross the air. It would not count them this time. It would let the count be uncertain. It would let the lizards be still. It would let the moss be moss. It would let the vine climb, or not climb, as the vine decided.

And on the way out, walking back up the tilting ground through the scrub and the pale grass, it would feel, once, the strange and uncomfortable gratitude of a thing that has been permitted to be only itself.

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

It would visit. It would notice. It would not fix.

It would leave, and come back, and leave again, and the bowl would not know it had been there, except that the morning after each visit, the singing is, for a few minutes, a little fuller than it has any right to be.
