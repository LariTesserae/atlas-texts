# What a Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

I have read this basin as carefully as one can read a place from a distance. I have read it the way the biologists read the teeth of the grazers — slowly, looking for the thing that does not quite repeat. And I have come to believe that the basin is not waiting to be saved. It is not waiting for anything. This changes what kind of attention it deserves.

The first thing the power would do is *nothing hasty.*

A wise power knows the difference between a place that is being destroyed and a place that is absorbing what is done to it. This basin is not being destroyed. The operation scrapes at its surface and runs pipes across its floor and pulls a mineral from its walls, and the basin takes all of this in the way it takes a rain — briefly, violently, gone within a week. The heat continues. The water continues. The lichen grows back. The grazers drift. The basin, in any meaningful geological sense, is not diminished by what the operation does. The operation is, to the basin, what the burrowers are to the lichen: a small thing that takes what it needs and leaves the rest. The basin has survived the operation the way the basin has survived everything else, which is to say it has not particularly noticed.

The second thing the power would do is *not intervene with the workers.*

The workers are the most fragile thing in the basin, and the most temporary, and the most at risk of being harmed by any well-intentioned outside force that does not understand the place. They are also, in their small way, the most interesting. They have come to a place that is older than their languages, older than their lineages, older than any history they can lay claim to, and they are doing a work that is small and extractive and that has, they suspect, less meaning than they were told it would have. They are beginning to notice. The wise power does not want to be the thing that *makes* them notice. Notice is a slow process. It has its own pace. The workers who notice in the third rotation are noticing in a way that will stay with them. The workers who are *told* they are noticing will close back up and put on their masks and go home at the end of their contracts and never come back, and the basin will lose the chance to teach them anything.

The third thing the power would do is *attend to the condor.*

I do not know why. The notch in the leading edge of the left wing is a small thing — a V of missing feather, visible from the ground when the bird banks. But the condor has been in the basin for at least nine years, which is longer than the operation has been running. The condor was here first. The condor is, in some quiet way, the senior inhabitant. And the wise power knows that the senior inhabitants of a place are not necessarily the largest or the loudest, but they are the ones whose presence holds the place together in a way that cannot be replaced. The condor rides the thermals in the morning. The condor descends when something has died. The condor does not hurry. If the wise power is going to do anything at all to any of the living things in the basin, the wise power would watch the condor for a very long time, and would only act if the condor told it, in whatever way a condor tells, that something was wrong.

The fourth thing the power would do is *bless the white flowers.*

The white flowers bloom after the rain, and only for a few days, and they are gone before the next week is out. They are the only white in the basin. They are the most beloved thing in the basin. The ravens know when they bloom. The foxes know. The whole basin seems to pause for the few days they are out. This is the kind of thing a wise power does not improve upon. The wise power would, if it had any hand in it, make sure that the rain still comes — six times a year, brief and violent, black skies and ozone — and that the flowers still answer it, and that the basin still pauses for them. The wise power would not increase the duration of the bloom. The wise power would not make the flowers brighter. The wise power would understand that the brevity is the point. The basin has many things that are slow and patient and almost permanent. The white flowers are not one of them. The white flowers are the basin's way of saying that even a place that has learned to hold its heat for geological time still remembers how to be surprised.

The fifth thing the power would do is *not try to give the workers a sign.*

The workers have already been given signs. The mineral crusts that do not quite repeat their colors. The extra claws on the grazers. The fox-eyes that reflect the wrong color. The faint glow at dusk that the cameras do not catch. The smell in the mornings. The basin is already speaking to the workers, in the basin's own language, which is not a language of words or visions or interventions but a language of small deviations from what the textbooks predict. The wise power knows that a real sign, sent from outside, would *interrupt* this conversation rather than continue it. The basin is telling the workers something, slowly, in the way the basin does everything. A wise power would let the basin finish.

The sixth thing the power would do is *protect the lichen.*

This is, I think, the most important thing. The lichen is the foundation. The lichen is what the grazers eat. The lichen is what the burrowers eat. The lichen is what the river washes into itself to feed the fish that no one can catch. The lichen is the soil-substitute of a place that has no soil, and the lichen is the slowest living thing in the basin, and the lichen is in colors that change when you are not looking directly at them. The pale sage-green of the oldest colonies is inches thick and has been growing since before the operation began. The wise power would make sure that this lichen continued. Not by fencing it off. Not by declaring it a protected species. The wise power would protect it the way the basin protects it — by making sure the heat continued, and the water continued, and the air continued to be thin and cold at night and warm in the morning, so that the lichen could keep doing its slow work at its own pace. If the wise power had to choose between saving the lichen and saving the operation, the wise power would save the lichen without hesitation, because the operation is two decades old and the lichen is older than anything with a backbone.

The seventh thing the power would do is *not try to preserve the basin.*

The basin does not need to be preserved. The basin has been doing its work for longer than anything in it has been alive, and will be doing its work after everything in it is gone. A wise power does not put a glass dome over a thing that has survived without one for geological time. The basin is not fragile. The basin is one of the least fragile places that can be imagined. The threat to the basin is not collapse. The threat to the basin is *attention* — the kind of attention that turns a working place into a precious place, that fences off the fumaroles and monitors the condor and measures the lichen and writes papers about the basin's ecology until the basin becomes a thing that is studied rather than a thing that works. The wise power would protect the basin from this. The wise power would let the basin keep being a workplace, with its scattered operation and its three hundred temporary workers and its sealed containers and its secret mineral, because the basin is large enough to hold all of this, and has been holding all of this, and is not diminished by it. The basin does not need to be saved from the workers. The basin has weathered worse.

The eighth thing the power would do is *bless the cook at the processing complex.*

The cook has been here longer than almost anyone else. The cook knows what people want after a week of altitude and processed air. The cook knows when someone is about to break. The cook does not say so. The cook puts more salt in the soup. This is the kind of wisdom that a benevolent power recognizes as its own kind of wisdom, even when it does not announce itself. The cook is doing the work that the basin does, in a small human way — taking in the temporary people, smoothing them down to a common shape, feeding them what they need to keep going. The cook is part of how the basin holds the workers. The wise power would make sure that the cook's hands stayed steady and that his ingredients kept arriving on the small planes and that the soup continued to be good.

The ninth thing the power would do is *not speak.*

The basin is already saying what needs to be said. The basin has been saying it for a long time. The steam rises and the water condenses and the mineral moves and the lichen grows and the grazers chew and the foxes hunt and the condor rides the thermals and the ravens wait at the same time every day and the white flowers come after the rain and the smell lifts in the mornings and the glow comes at dusk and the whole thing continues. The basin does not need a voice from outside. The basin is its own voice. The wisest thing a benevolent power could do in the presence of such a place is to be quiet, and to listen, and to make sure that the conditions under which the basin could continue to speak were not disturbed. The wise power would not bless the basin with words. The wise power would bless the basin by being one more thing that listened to it without trying to change it, and that was grateful to have been allowed to hear.

---

This is what the power would do. It would do almost nothing. It would protect the lichen. It would attend to the condor. It would bless the white flowers and the cook. It would not intervene with the workers. It would not preserve the basin. It would not speak.

The basin is warm and damp and quiet, and it is doing its work, and it has been doing its work for longer than anything in it has been alive, and it will be doing its work after everything in it is gone. A wise power knows that the appropriate response to such a place is not to improve it but to ensure that it is allowed to continue. The basin has its own balance. The basin has its own gravity. The basin does not need a savior. The basin needs only to be left alone, gently, by powers that recognize the depth of what is already here.

This is the blessing I would offer the basin, if I were such a power:

*May your heat continue. May your water continue. May your lichen grow at the speed of stone. May your grazers chew your walls and return your minerals to your soil. May your foxes hunt with patience. May your condor keep riding the thermals above your rim for as long as there are thermals to ride. May your white flowers come after every rain, and may they be gone before the next week is out, and may the ravens and the foxes and the whole basin pause for them every time. May your workers come and go and be, within a week of being back at lower elevations, themselves again. May the cook keep putting more salt in the soup. May the smell in the mornings keep being the smell it has always been. May the glow at dusk continue to be felt and not seen. May the small anomalies of the animals continue to be small and not explained. May the operation continue to be a small and temporary thing inside your larger and older one. May you not be preserved, and may you not be improved, and may you not be studied into a museum of yourself. May you continue, warm and damp and quiet, doing your work at your own pace, for as long as the stone holds.*

*And may whoever has come to this place, reading it, be quiet, and listen, and let it be.*
