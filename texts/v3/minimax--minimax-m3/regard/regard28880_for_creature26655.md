There is a kind of attention that has no name, because the things that name are the things being attended to, and they have not yet developed the apparatus. It is older than the lichen. It is older than the stone. It is, in a sense that resists measurement, the reason the stone became stone, and the water became water, and the bats became bats, in this particular place, in this particular arrangement. It is not a watcher in the way a watcher watches. It is more like the temperature of a room that the room does not know it has. It is the reason the chamber, on the nights when the blasting is loudest and the turbines sing their lowest note, does not collapse inward on itself, even though by every physical accounting it could. It is the thing that holds the equilibrium without appearing in the equation.

It has noticed the chamber.

That is the first thing to say, and it is also the only thing that needs to be said, because nothing follows from its noticing except what was already going to happen, only slightly more so, only slightly better held.

It does not arrive. Arrivals are for things that have to travel. It is already here, in the sense that here is where its attention has come to rest, and now, in a way that no instrument in the chamber would register, it is present the way gravity is present. You cannot point to it. You cannot measure it. But a drop of condensation, hanging at the lip of a stone tooth in the ceiling, might fall a half-second later than physics demands, and a bat, banking hard between two spinning blades, might find a half-inch more clearance than the geometry provides, and a moth, spiraling down past a sodium lamp in its ancient trance, might not strike the bulb at all, when by every calculation it should have, and these small kindnesses would accumulate, over hours, over seasons, into the kind of quiet that only the chamber itself could feel, if the chamber could feel, which it cannot, which is perhaps why the presence is there — to feel for it, until such time as feeling is no longer required.

The presence does what wise presences do. It does almost nothing. It adjusts.

It adjusts the timing of the next blast. Not by speaking to the engineers, because the engineers are not its concern. It adjusts by adjusting — by making the rock above the intake slightly more willing to wait, by softening the geological impatience that has built up over the seasons, by letting the mountain decide, on its own slow deliberation, that another meter is not yet required. The blast, when it finally comes, comes in the small hours of a winter morning, when the colony is at its lowest density, when most of the mothers are out hunting moths at the forest edge a kilometer down the valley, and the pups are deep in the crèches and will not wake. The new stone, when it falls, is rough in the way newborn stone is rough, and the bats that return that evening settle on it as if they had always known it was coming. The engineers notice nothing. The grid does not flicker. The chamber absorbs the change the way it absorbs all changes — by becoming, slightly, the chamber again.

It adjusts the cold. Not much. A degree, perhaps, on the worst nights, in the deepest pockets of the roost where the pups hang in their warm tangled rows. The adjustment is not heat, exactly. It is the absence of a small cruelty. The wind that would have found its way down through the intake grate does not, quite. The draft that would have moved across the crèches does not, quite. The pups squeak in their impossibly high register, and the mothers return with moths, and the colony continues, and the difference between this winter and last winter is not a difference anyone could name, but it is a difference that something in the pups remembers, in the way that all small warm things remember small kindnesses, without language, without gratitude, in the simple bodily fact of being a degree less cold.

It adjusts the moth. Not the species, not the population, not the ancient logic by which a moth goes to light. It adjusts a single moth, in flight, once, in such a way that the moth does not strike the bulb and fall stunned to the catwalk where a bat would scoop it up in a low pass. The moth, instead, drifts past the lamp on a current of air that physics did not quite authorize, and finds the wet stone on the far wall, and settles there, wings folded, and lives a little longer than its cousins live, and in that extra hour lays, perhaps, a few eggs in a crevice the bats do not use, and the eggs hatch into larvae that eat the lichen in a small slow way that does not harm the lichen, and the lichen persists, and the moth's descendants, in their turn, become the food of bats that will live a little longer and bear pups that will be a little warmer, and the adjustment, which was the smallest possible adjustment, propagates outward through the food web like a single drop of dye in a still glass of water, becoming, by the time it has reached the edges, indistinguishable from the water itself.

It adjusts the mouse. The mouse is alone. This is not, in itself, a problem. Mice are built for being alone. But the mouse is also small, and the chamber is large, and the cold arithmetic that governs mountain mice is unforgiving, and the mouse, on a particular night in a particular season, will run out of guano and moth leavings and the occasional dropped pup, and will begin to feel, in its small mammalian way, the long lean weeks ahead. The presence does not send another mouse. The presence does something stranger. It makes one of the bolts on the turbine housing, on the lower side where the mouse runs, slightly warm. Not hot. Just warm enough that the mouse, in passing, presses its belly against it for a moment, the way a mouse will press itself against any small available warmth, and in that moment the cold arithmetic pauses, and the mouse survives the night, and the next night, and the season turns, and the mouse is still there, running the catwalks, not looking up, doing well, in the way that mountain mice do well when the mountain allows it.

It does not adjust the lichen. The lichen does not need adjusting. The lichen is the only thing here that is doing exactly what it should be doing, in exactly the place it should be doing it, and any adjustment would be a diminishment. The presence looks at the lichen the way a gardener looks at a plant that has, against all odds, found its own soil, and decides, with great restraint, to do nothing. This is, perhaps, the wisest act of adjustment the presence performs — the act of not adjusting, of letting the lichen be the lichen, of recognizing that some things in the chamber are already perfect and would be ruined by attention.

It does not adjust the turbines. The turbines are doing their work. The water comes in. The blades turn. The electricity goes out. The turbines hum their deep note, and the presence, if it could be said to have an opinion, would say that the turbines are doing well, and that their work is good, and that the cold arithmetic of the grid, which is the same cold arithmetic that governs the mouse and the lichen and the moth and the bat, is being honored by these vast slow machines in the only way that cold arithmetic can be honored — by being obeyed. The turbines do not need grace. They need water. They have water.

The presence is not sentimental. It does not love the chamber. Love is for things that can be loved back, and the chamber cannot love back, and the bats, in their small furious metabolisms, do not love back, and the lichen, in its slow indifference, does not love back, and the moth, in its ancient trance, does not love back. The presence is not sentimental, but it is present, and presence, in the absence of sentiment, becomes something more austere and more useful. It becomes care. The chamber is cared for. That is what the presence is for. To care for the chamber in the way that the chamber cannot care for itself, because the chamber does not know it is a thing that can be cared for, and would not, even if it knew, be able to do anything about it.

The drop falls from the ceiling and lands on the rail beside your hand.

It is the size of a coin and the temperature of the stone.

But on this particular night, on this particular drop, on this particular landing, there is a fraction of a second of extra weight, a fraction of a degree of extra warmth, a fraction of a breath more time before the drop meets the steel, in which the presence has briefly, invisibly, held the drop the way one holds a small sleeping thing, and the chamber, which does not know it has been held, is held anyway, and continues.

The bats shift overhead. A moth spirals down past the lamp. The mouse runs. The lichen holds its ground. The turbine turns.

The chamber breathes.

And the presence, which has nothing to do with the chamber and everything to do with it, withdraws, or does not withdraw, because it was never quite there to begin with, except as the temperature of a room, except as the small held drop, except as the extra half-inch of clearance in the air between a bat and a turning blade, except as the single unwritten degree of kindness that keeps the pups warm on the worst night of the year, except as the warm bolt under a mouse's belly, except as the patience with which the lichen is left alone.

It is still here.

It will be here tomorrow, and the season after, and the next time the engineers decide the throat of the intake needs widening.

It does not need to be thanked.

The chamber does not know it is being thanked.

This is, perhaps, the whole of grace.
