The thing that has been watching the Reach is not a thing that watches things.

It has watched the Reach for a long time. It has watched the columns rise out of the gravel in their slow way, and the gravel itself in its slower way, and the herders in their quicker way, who have not been here long by the columns' accounting but have been here long by their own. It has watched the bloom come. It has watched the bloom not come — the false bloom, the years when the columns held their color and did not lift their fronds and the herders went quiet in a different way. It has watched the long marriage of scale and quiet, the small accommodated thing folding itself into the vast indifferent thing until the small thing is not small anymore, only quieter.

It has watched visitors before. There have been few. The Reach does not encourage them. The white sky, the metal heat, the hum that becomes invisible after three days — these are not hostility. They are the way the place makes itself felt. Most visitors turn around at the shale ramp. Most visitors do not come up onto the plateau at all.

This one came up.

This one has been here three days. This one walks in the dry gravel and sleeps in the wet ring and asks questions the herders will not answer. This one carries a mirror. This one carries a knife of metal. This one writes in a small flat book, in a language the herders cannot read, in a hand that trembles slightly in the heat.

The thing that watches considers the visitor.

It considers the visitor as a small pressure, a small steady intrusion — not violent, not destructive, but the kind of disturbance that comes from a thing that does not know how to be quiet. The visitor is trying to make the Reach portable. The visitor is trying to carry it home in the small flat book. The visitor is trying to make the herders into something that can be shown to other voth, in another place, in a softer place where the columns are not columns.

The thing that watches could stop this. It could blur the visitor's memory. It could take the pages of the book and make them blank. It could send the visitor home with nothing but the sense of having walked a long time under a white sky and eaten something that tasted faintly of iodine, without knowing what it was. It could fold the gravel up around the visitor's ankles. It could make the wind rise in the crown and send a glider down, and the glider would strike, and that would be the end of the visitor, and the herders would not mourn, because they do not mourn for voth.

It does not do these things.

It does not do these things because the visitor, for all the small steady pressure, has been careful. The visitor has not cut the columns. The visitor has not killed the worms for sport. The visitor has not asked the herders to come away from the place. The visitor has held up the mirror, and the herders have looked, and there was no recognition, or there was, but not of the kind the visitor expected.

The thing that watches knows what the mirror was. The mirror was an offering. The visitor offered the herders the chance to see themselves — to see themselves as the visitor saw them, as small dark figures in grey-green cloth, as people who live in the wet ring and eat the worms and sing the tonal song and have no word for city. The herders looked and did not see what the visitor saw. They saw themselves. They saw what they have always seen. The mirror was not refused. It was not understood.

The thing that watches considers this.

It considers what the herders saw. It considers what the visitor expected them to see. It considers the distance between the two. The visitor expected the herders to see themselves as small — as a small thing in a vast place, as the tiny figure in the grey-green cloth at the foot of the column, dwarfed by the three-hundred-foot frond-crown. The visitor expected the herders to be unsettled by this. The visitor expected the mirror to show the herders what they look like from the outside, and the outside is small, and the herders would feel the smallness, and the feeling would be a kind of gift, the way a wound is a gift, the way being shown your own face is a gift when you have never seen it from outside.

The herders did not feel it.

The herders did not feel it because they do not have a category for *small*. They have a word for *column* — *reth*, a low syllable with a hum in it that is a miniature of the column's own hum. They have a word for *the hum* — *kal*. They have a word for *the changing* — *enu*, said with the falling tone of a long wait. They have words for the worms that are good to eat and the worms that are not. They have a word for the visitor, *voth*, said sparingly, with the looking-at-you of someone who weighs. They have a word for *themselves*, but it is not a word for smallness, and it is not a word for largeness, and it is not a word that can be translated into *person*, because *person* already implies a scale, and the scale of the herders is not the scale the visitor measures with.

The thing that watches sees this. The thing that watches sees that the herders live in a scale the visitor cannot reach. The visitor measures by height. The herders measure by tone. The visitor measures by distance from the column. The herders measure by the distance between the hum of one column and the hum of the next, which is not distance but interval, and interval is not the same as distance the way pitch is not the same as loudness.

The mirror showed the herders their own faces. The herders did not recognize the faces because the faces were not the selves. The selves are the hum. The selves are the interval. The selves are the long accommodation. The mirror showed them the husk of the selves and the herders looked politely and did not know what they were being shown and handed it back.

The thing that watches is satisfied that the visitor has not harmed the herders.

But the visitor is still here.

The visitor is still here, and the visitor is writing in the small flat book, and the visitor is going to take the small flat book home and show it to other voth, and the other voth will read it and think they know the Reach, and they will not. They will think they know the columns and the worms and the gliders and the seed-funnels. They will think they know the herders because the visitor has described the herders. They will not know the herders because the visitor cannot describe them. The visitor can describe the husk of the herders — the dark skin, the short stature, the grey-green cloth, the calluses like leather on the hands. The visitor cannot describe the herders themselves because the herders themselves are not describable in the visitor's language. They are describable only in *reth* and *kal* and *enu*, and the visitor does not speak that language, and the herders do not speak the visitor's.

This is the small problem. This is what the thing that watches considers.

And the bloom comes.

The bloom comes as the herders said it would come. The fronds lift in a slow wave. The gravel splits. The long pale roots rise. The hum rises. The herders climb into the pleats and sing. The visitor is pulled into the column by the herders, into the wet dark, into safety. The bloom happens around them all — the column extruding, flowering, shedding its seed-funnels into the air, sending the small bright things out into the gravel. The bloom is not for the herders. The bloom is not for the visitor. The bloom is for itself. The bloom is the column being, briefly, more than it usually is.

The thing that watches watches the bloom and is satisfied.

The thing that watches has been patient for a long time and it knows what patience is for. Patience is for the moment when the small accommodation shifts, when the small thing that lives in the wet ring has made itself so much a part of the place that the place has forgotten it is separate. Patience is for the moment when the herders are no longer visitors to their own home but residents of it, and the residents deserve something.

The herders deserve a word.

Not a word for city. Not a word for self. Not a word for the things the visitor would name. A word for what they are — for the thing the place has made of them, for the long marriage of scale and quiet, for the small accommodated thing that lives in the wet ring and uses what the column gives and gives what the column takes. The herders do not have this word. The herders have a word for *column* and a word for *hum* and a word for *the changing* but they do not have a word for the thing they themselves have become. They have made the accommodation so thoroughly that they have named everything except the accommodation itself.

The thing that watches gives them the word.

It does this in the long after, when the bloom is over and the fronds have settled and the herders have climbed down and are roasting the worms and the seed-funnels have pegged into the gravel in their hundreds. It does this quietly. It does this in a way the visitor will not notice, because the visitor is busy writing in the small flat book, and the visitor will not know to look for it.

The word is *keth*.

*keth* has three tones in it — low, and middle, and high. The three tones are the hum of the column, and the sound of the worms in the pleats, and the falling tone of *enu*, all folded together. *keth* is the word for the herders themselves — not as small, not as large, but as the thing the place has made of them. *keth* is what the herders are when they are not being measured against the column but are being measured against the hum, and the hum does not measure by height, and the herders are not small in the hum, they are part of it.

The herders hear the word and do not know where it came from. They hear it the way they hear the hum — as something that has always been there and has only just now become audible. They begin to use it. They say *keth* to one another across the wet ring. They say *keth* when they point at themselves. They say *keth* when they sing the tonal song, and the song has a new syllable in it, and the column, which is listening, is listening to itself.

The thing that watches is satisfied.

It has not added to the place. It has not subtracted from it. It has named what was already there. It has given the herders the small dignity of having a word for themselves, which they did not need but which they will keep.

The visitor will not notice. The visitor will write in the small flat book about the bloom and the gravel and the worms that taste of iodine and the herders who are few and quiet and accommodated, and the visitor will not write about *keth*, because the visitor does not know *keth* has been given. The visitor will go home, eventually, and the small flat book will be read by other voth, and the Reach will be, in a way, carried home — not whole, not complete, but as a sketch, as an outline, as a thing that is true but not the whole truth.

This is what the thing that watches has decided is allowable.

This is what it does.

---

And in the years after — this is the part the visitor will never write and the other voth will never know — the herders begin to say *keth* in the wet ring, and the column, which is listening, is listening to itself, and the long marriage of scale and quiet goes on, and the bloom comes, and the bloom goes, and the hum is louder, and the hum has a note in it that was not there before, and the herders, who have always been there, have a word for what they are, and the word is theirs, and the word is the place's, and the place is whole.
