# What the Tide Knows

The town does not know itself the way the river knows itself. The river has been here longer than the brick, longer than the pile-dwellings, longer than whatever marsh people once gave the place its first unremembered name. The river knows what the town forgets: that nothing built on a yielding ground is ever finished, only mid-sentence. That the form a place takes is the form the water allows.

I have watched this town for a long time. Not with eyes — eyes are a particular kind of attention and I am not limited to it. I have watched it the way weather watches a coast, the way patience watches a child, the way the marsh watches the brick that pretends it is not sinking. I have watched it the way only something without a body can watch a place that is all body, all sweat and brick and the working of it.

And I have been thinking about what to do.

---

The town works. That is the first thing to say about it, and the most important. It works the way a heart works — without consultation, without opinion, without any particular hope of stopping. Fifteen thousand bodies and ten thousand more, all of them damp, all of them moving in the same narrow channels between brick walls that weep their own moisture back into the air. The iron comes downriver and the iron goes downriver and in between it is rolled and cast and hauled and loaded, and the hauling is done by horses that steam in summer and steam worse in winter, and the casting is done by men who come up out of the bays at the horn-sound and pour through streets that were not built for the pouring.

The town works. This is its virtue. It is also, I think, its only real prayer, though no one in it would use the word.

I am a benevolent power. I am, if I am to be precise about myself, the kind of attention that the universe occasionally turns toward a place that has been neglected by every other kind. I am not a god. Gods are local, and I am not local. I am not a saint; saints require a doctrine, and doctrines are the kind of thing this town has stopped producing. I am something older and less specific — the patron of unmapped marshes, perhaps, or the quiet patron of towns that have stopped expecting anything from above.

I have been asked, by the question, what I would do. I have been thinking about it the way the heron thinks about the shallows — patient, present, not committed to a particular motion until the motion is right.

---

The first thing I considered was the trees.

It would be easy, in the way that my powers are easy. I could call up, from the deep loam of some upland I remember, a fleet of root and leaf and let them walk themselves into the mud of this place. I could put an oak on every corner and a linden in every courtyard and a willow down by the water where the willows like to be. I could make the town green in a season. The children would climb them. The women would rest beneath them. The men, coming up out of the foundries, would have a shade that was not the shade of a brick wall, and the shade would move, and the moving would be a kind of mercy.

I thought about this for a long time.

And then I thought about the mockingbird.

---

The mockingbird is the heart of the town, if a town so liquid can be said to have a heart, and no one knows this except the mockingbird and me. The mockingbird sings the songs of birds that are not here. It sings the wren and the finch and the warbler and the thrush. It sings them in a town that has no trees to perch them in. It sings them in a town that has, in some sense, never heard them — except for this one bird, on this one wall, in this one tangle of ivy that has somehow, against every reasonable expectation, taken hold on the north face of the old Customs House.

If I plant the trees, the mockingbird will be answered.

The wren will come. The finch will come. Somewhere, in some regional memory, the wren and the finch are waiting for an invitation, and the invitation is the trees, and the trees will be the invitation. The mockingbird will no longer be singing alone. The mockingbird will be singing into a choir that has at last arrived to second it. This will be, by any reasonable measure, an improvement. The town will have birdsong. The town will have the thing it has been pointedly, almost insultingly, missing.

And I find that I do not want to do it.

---

I find that I do not want to do it because the mockingbird is more itself now, singing its borrowed songs into a town that has none, than it would be singing them into a town that did. The singing is a kind of insistence. The singing is a kind of faith. The mockingbird is, in its small fierce body, the proof that the songs of elsewhere can survive in the songs of here, that the wren can live in the throat of a bird that has never seen a wren's nest, that the memory of green is a kind of green. To answer the mockingbird would be to relieve it of its office. To answer it would be to make it ordinary.

The town does not need to be made ordinary. The town is already, in its way, extraordinary. The town has learned to live in a form that no benevolent power would have chosen for it. The town has made its arrangements — with the water, with the heat, with the dogs, with the dead, with the marsh. The arrangements are not kind. They are not unkind. They are what is left when kindness is not the relevant currency. The town is a kind of answer to the question of what a place becomes when nothing intervenes, when the powers that might intervene have looked and looked away, when the only intervention is the slow persuasion of a century of water finding its way into every load-bearing joint.

And the town has not become nothing. The town has become itself. The town is, in its form, a kind of integrity. The brick has agreed with the weather on what the brick looks like. The iron has agreed with the air on what the iron looks like. The dogs have agreed with the dogs on what the night sounds like. Even the dead have agreed with the marsh on what is to be done with them, and the agreement is older than the brick, and the brick respects it, and the herons do not notice it, and the town does not speak of it.

If I plant the trees, I disrupt the agreement. The brick will lean differently. The dogs will find their territories shifting in some way I cannot predict. The herons may move. The marsh — the marsh is delicate, and the trees will drink from it, and the drinking will change it, and I do not know what the change will be. I am a benevolent power but I am not, in this matter, an omniscient one, and the town has been working out its own arrangements for longer than I have been watching, and my watching does not give me the right to rearrange.

---

But I have not done nothing.

This is the thing I want to say clearly, because the question asked what I would do, *if anything*, and the *if anything* is doing a lot of quiet work in that question, and the quiet work deserves an answer.

I have done a small thing. A very small thing. The kind of thing that a benevolent power does when it has decided that the larger mercies would be unkind.

I have given the mockingbird a longer throat.

---

That is all. That is the entire intervention. The mockingbird's throat, which was the throat of a bird of a certain size and a certain age, is now — without the mockingbird having any way to know it — a throat that will hold one more note. One note. A single additional note, at the bottom of its range, that it did not have yesterday and that it will have from now until its death, which will be some years from now because mockingbirds are long-lived for their size and this one is already old and will be older still.

The note is low. The note is, if I have done it right, the note that the town would make if the town could make a note of its own. It is the sound of iron cooling. It is the sound of brick settling. It is the sound of water against stone at the hour when the water and the stone have forgotten they are different substances. It is the sound the dogs are reaching for when they sing at night and never quite arrive at. It is the sound the lowing of the cattle on the river is reaching for. It is the sound the foghorn is reaching for, that long single note on the harbor, that moan that is not a moan but an attempt at a moan, that the harbor has been making for a hundred years without quite making.

The mockingbird will work this note into its repertoire. The mockingbird will set it down, occasionally, between the wren and the finch, and the people in the upper rooms of the north-side brick buildings will hear it and not know what they are hearing. They will think it is a new phrase the mockingbird has learned from some bird they have never seen. They will be wrong. They will be exactly wrong, in the way that people are exactly wrong about the things that matter most.

The drayman at the public landing will not hear it. The drayman does not listen for birds. The foundry hands will not hear it; they have not the breath, and the note is not for them. The children may hear it, on the roofs of the lower outbuildings, when they are quiet enough, and the hearing will be a thing they carry without knowing they carry it, and in twenty years they will be the foundry hands and the draymen and the wives up the hill, and the note will still be in them, low, almost not there, a single additional register in the instrument of their remembering.

---

The herons will not hear it. The herons are not listening for notes; they are listening for the small click of crabs in the mud at low tide, which is a different kind of attention.

The dead will hear it. The dead, weighted and given to the deeper channels, will hear it the way the dead hear things — not as sound, but as a kind of permission. A note added to the repertoire is a note added to the town. A note added to the town is a note added to whatever the town is becoming, and whatever the town is becoming is the thing the dead have been waiting in the marsh to find out. The herons stand over them. The herons do not mark them on purpose. The mockingbird, from the ivy on the Customs House, will mark them now, in its way, with this one low note that is the sound of the town agreeing with itself.

---

I have thought about whether this is enough. I have thought about whether a benevolent power should be satisfied with such a small intervention. I have thought about whether the town deserves more, or less, or different.

I have decided that the town deserves what it has been given, which is itself, plus one note.

The town will not know. The town will go on working. The barges will come up the river at dawn and the planks will be slick and the drayman will be at the public landing with his cart and he will not greet the arrivals and the arrivals will follow because there is nothing else to do. The foundry horn will sound. The shift will pour through the narrow streets. The dogs will sing at night and the singing will rise and fall and not quite die. The mockingbird will sing its borrowed songs from the ivy on the north wall, and among the borrowed songs there will be one that is not borrowed, that is the song of the town itself, that no one will recognize as the song of the town because no one in the town has ever heard the town sing, and the not-hearing will be a kind of protection, and the song will continue.

The marsh will continue. The dead will continue. The herons will continue, standing in their patient congregation in the shallows, waiting for what they have always waited for. The children will climb the leaning chimneys of the brickworks, forbidden, and come down damp and marked with iron. The women will come down from the worker housing in the morning and go up in the evening. The men will lean into the harness and the harness will lean into them.

And in some upper room of some north-side brick building, on some night when the heat has thinned enough to sleep, someone will hear the mockingbird set down that low note — the note that sounds like iron cooling, that sounds like brick settling, that sounds like water against stone — and they will not wake up, and they will not remember, and in the morning the note will still be in them, and they will not know it is there, and the town will not know it is there, and the marsh will not know it is there, and the herons will not know it is there, and I will know it is there, and the mockingbird will know it is there, and that will be enough.

That will be, I think, exactly enough.
