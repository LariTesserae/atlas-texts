# What the Benevolent Power Does

I would begin by listening. This is what any wise power learns to do first in a place like Veth Khariv — to listen before it acts, to let the hum get into the listening the way it has gotten into the sleep of the tuned. A city that has lived this long, that has built itself into a mountain that should not be habitable, that has made a chord of its own machinery and its own water and its own patience, does not want to be fixed. It wants to be heard. So I would listen first, and for a long time. I would listen from the upper pastures where the goats bell. I would listen from the cable cars as they descend through the bright air. I would listen from inside the cistern-towers, where the drip of condensation makes a different note in each one. I would listen from the lower squares, where the water runs in shallow carved ruts and the old women sit just out of its reach. I would listen until I had heard the city's chord in its entirety — every frequency, every slight dissonance, every almost-music that the wind pulls from the corridors between buildings when it shifts — and only then would I begin to attend.

The city does not need much. It has been here for a long time. It intends to be here for a long time more. But there are small things a benevolent power can do, and I would do them quietly, in the registers that the city itself uses — in the slight adjustment of a wind pattern, in the color of a lichen, in the angle at which a cat sits on a windowsill, in the way a tuned sleeper wakes from a dream about the cable station.

## The Glacier

The glacier is the city's oldest parent and its most uncertain inheritance. I would not stop its retreat. The city has built its identity around the glacier's leaving; to freeze the glacier in place would be to freeze the city's grief in place, and grief in Veth Khariv is a slow liturgical thing that the people know how to carry without speaking of it. But I would slow the retreat. I would buy the city another two hundred years, perhaps three, by adjusting the upper winds and the pattern of snowfall so that the accumulation at the glacier's highest catchment slightly exceeds the ablation. The locals would notice nothing at first. They would simply say, in another generation, that the glacier is enduring longer than expected, and they would be right, and they would not look at the white patch above the city, which is smaller than it was, and smaller than the year before. The cats would notice first. The cats always notice first.

And when, at last, the glacier is gone — in three centuries, or five, or whenever its true time comes — I would ensure that the lichen on the north-facing walls continues to release its faint perfume in the early morning, because the lichen is the glacier's perfume, and the city should not have to grieve without it. I would teach the cistern-tenders a new method, a very old one, of drawing moisture from the air itself, so that the condensation that already gathers on every cold surface becomes the city's new meltwater. The trickle would continue. The public basins would remain full. The water would run, ankle-deep in the lower squares, and the old women would continue to pull their chairs just out of its reach, and the city would not have to learn, all at once, how to live without what it loved.

## The Cistern

The thing in the lower cistern is the city's deepest secret, and secrets of this kind are not to be exposed or explained. They are to be kept.

I would keep this one. I would ensure that the thing remains content — not because it is dangerous, though it may be in the way that anything large and unknown is dangerous, but because a city that lives with a mystery is a city that stays awake in a particular way, and the waking is part of what makes Veth Khariv alive. I would not make the morning water level. The slight disturbance is the city's morning prayer. I would, however, ensure that the thing does not grow restless, does not become hungry, does not rise.
