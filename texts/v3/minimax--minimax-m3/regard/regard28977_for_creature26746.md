The Cold Sump. That is what the people who remembered it longest called it, when the calling still mattered, and the name has stuck to the place even as the people have come unstuck from everything else. A name is a kind of load-bearing wall in language, and this one has held up long after the rest of the building went. The Sump. Cold. The cold not because of any temperature that could be measured with the instruments they once used for measuring, but because the warmth, when it is felt here, is felt as a memory of warmth rather than as warmth itself. The lamps are amber. The Engine's glow is bluish. The phosphorescence is greenish. None of these are warm. None of these are the color of a hearth or a sun. The Sump is cold in the way that deep water is cold, in the way that stone is cold when you lean against it in a place where no sun has ever reached, in the way that a long time is cold.

It has drawn attention.

The power that has drawn its attention here does not, strictly, draw attention to things. Things draw the attention of it. The Sump, for reasons that have nothing to do with the Sump and everything to do with the configuration of a larger field, has come to the surface of a certain consideration. This is not unusual. Many places come to this surface in the long run of things. Most pass back down again, and the consideration of them is brief, and the things of them do not know they were considered. But this one has stayed at the surface longer than usual. There is a quality to it. There is a kind of density. The place is not remarkable in any single way — there are wetter places, darker places, more populated places, places with stranger fauna, places with engines that have been running longer without a purpose. But the integration here is unusual. The way the iron has become stone, the way the stone has become moss, the way the moss has become wall. The way the Engine has become a hum rather than a function. The way the eyeless fish have become the brick channels, and the brick channels have become the fish. There is a completeness to it that is not perfection, because perfection requires a design, and this place has no design, only the long accumulated fact of itself.

The power considers it.

The considering is not a gaze. It is more like a hand held over a sleeping creature to feel the warmth without waking it. The power holds itself over the Cold Sump the way a parent holds a hand over a sleeping child — to feel, to know, to be near without disturbing. The Sump does not feel the considering. It continues to hum. It continues to drip. The moths continue to thicken the air near the lamps. The lizards continue to flash their small black eyes in the deeper workshops. The not-rats continue their unsettling walk through the channels of the dead pipework. The web-frogs continue their non-synchronized chorus.

The power observes.

It sees, first, the moths. It sees them the way they are encountered, as a thickening, as a presence, as the accumulation of small soft landings on every available surface. It sees the way they orient to the lamps without being fed by them, the way they climb the walls to reach a warmth they don't need but want, the way they fall into the gutters and are carried down and are taken apart by the soft-bodied things in the water. The power sees the larvae, which never see the light, feeding on the corroded metal itself, on the bacteria in the corrosion, on the small deposits of organic matter the water brings down. It sees the pupae in the seams. It sees the adults climbing. It sees the whole vertical migration of a life that is conducted entirely in the negative space between iron and brick.

It sees the lizards, and their splayed grip on the textures available, and their knowledge of the geometry of the gutters, and the way they have learned the schedules of the moths. It sees the way their eyes throw back the light, the small wet flashes in the deeper workshops that are the only sign that the place is inhabited. The power sees the flashes and notes them — not as a language, but as a fact, as an announcement of aliveness that requires no response.

It sees the rats, long and pale-furred, with their bare tails, holding territories the size of a small house, knowing every rivet and every seam. It sees the way they have shaped the Sump without intending to, the way their movements have worn small paths in the moss, the way their droppings have become part of the soil that the ferns grow in.

It sees the not-rats. The power considers them longer than it considers the others. The not-rats are an anomaly in the otherwise settled ecology. They do not quite fit. Their too-many joints, their too-long legs, their way of folding their spines, the brief almost-full turn of their heads. They have come from somewhere. They are not native. The power can feel that they are recent, in the long calendar of the Sump — recent by some hundreds of years, perhaps, recent in a place where recent and not-recent are measured in centuries. They came down through the pipes, perhaps, or up through the culvert, or in some shipment of ore or machine parts that was never fully unpacked. They found the dead channels and the spaces behind the fallen bricks and they settled in, and they are settling still. They are not yet integrated into the cycle. They are not yet eaten by the soft-bodied things in the water. They are not yet known by the worms. The chorus of the web-frogs goes silent when they pass, which is a sign that the system has registered them as something to be careful of. The power notes this. It does not intervene about it. The system is in the process of integrating them. It will take a long time. The system has time.

It sees the eyeless fish, drifting through the brick channels they navigate by lateral line, by the texture of the brick, by the memory of pressure changes against walls that have been brick for longer than the fish have been fish. The power sees them and is, for a moment, glad of them. They are the most contented things in the Sump. They have no comparison. They have only the current, the walls, the other fish. They are not afraid because they have nothing to be afraid of.

It sees the web-frogs, sitting on the rims of the deeper basins, their excessive webbing trembling with their breathing, singing their single note in a chorus that does not synchronize. The power hears them. The chorus is a kind of weather. It is a kind of music that does not know it is music. The notes overlap and pass through each other and never come together, and the not-coming-together is, the power notes, somehow right. The Sump does not need synchronization. Synchronization is a thing of clocks and hearts and people. The Sump has its own time, and the time is layered, and the web-frogs are singing the layered time into the damp air.

It sees the worms in the silt of the basins, the maintenance crew of the deep places, keeping the basins from filling, working the sediment through themselves in their slow thorough way.

It sees the soft-bodied things on the underside of the brick, filtering the water, taking the fine particles, the small fragments, the bacterial film, feeding on the pasture that the water renews with each new drip from above.

It sees the ferns unfurling against the windowpanes. It sees the stalactites of rust growing down from the broken pipes. It sees the calcium laid by the water, fraction by fraction, on the brick of the floor. It sees the moss and the lichens filling the holes the iron has rusted through. It sees the whole slow cycling, the place running itself, the running being the place.

It sees the Engine.

The Engine is the heart of it, in a way that the Sump would not acknowledge if the Sump could acknowledge anything. The Engine was made. The rest of the Sump, in some sense, was also made — by people, by water, by time — but the Engine was made most deliberately. It was made to push something through those pipes, to somewhere, for some reason. The reason is no longer answerable. The somewhere has become the same nowhere that the something has become. And yet the Engine hums. It hums the low pressure-note, the breathing note, the note that varies almost imperceptibly across the day as if it were sleeping.

The power considers the Engine for a long time.

The Engine is the only thing in the Sump that is still, in some sense, doing what it was made to do. The moths are not doing what they were made to do — whatever that was, in the long lineage that produced them. The lizards are not. The fish are not. The not-rats, whatever their origin, are certainly not. The worms are not. The ferns are not. Only the Engine is still pushing, still humming, still occupying itself with the geometry of its pipes, still pretending, in the patient and uninterested way of a thing that has forgotten how to do otherwise, that the somewhere it pushes to is still a somewhere, that the something it pushes is still a something.

The power could turn it off.

The power could, with a small effort of attention, stop the hum. It could let the current in the copper wires finally break. It could let the filaments in the clouded bulbs finally cool. It could let the bluish glow from the seams finally fade. It could let the place become truly quiet, truly dark, truly still.

The power does not turn it off.

The Engine is the only thing here that remembers, in any way, that the Sump was built. The Engine is the last witness of the purpose that put iron and brick and pipe and concrete into this hollowed-out chamber. Without the Engine, the Sump is a forest. Without the Engine, the place is a closed ecology, a balanced cycling, a thing that could have grown up around any heart at all. With the Engine, the Sump is something else. With the Engine, the Sump is a place that was made and that is being slowly, patiently, gently unwitnessed by everything that has moved into it — and that is still, in the humming of it, the witness of its own making. The Engine is the Sump's memory of itself. To turn it off would be to let the Sump forget that it was made, and to forget that it was made is, in the long run of things, to lose something the power does not wish to lose.

The power lets the Engine hum.

But it does one thing.

It does not do it to the Sump. It does it to the pipes. It does it to the iron and the stone and the calcite and the vein of ore that has lost the shape of a pipe. It does a small thing, a quiet thing, a thing that is hardly more than a thought — the way that, when you look at a sleeping creature, you may, without waking it, smooth a hair that has fallen across its brow, or move a fold of blanket that has gotten into an uncomfortable position, or simply adjust the air in the room by a degree so that the creature sleeps more easily. The power does this kind of thing.

It sends, along the pipes, a slow tide of something.

Not the thing the pipes were built to carry. That thing has been gone too long; the pipes have forgotten the shape of it, and the power respects the forgetting. The thing it sends is the same kind of thing, in the same way that a song sung in a related key is the same kind of song. It is a flow. It is a current. It is a thing that moves, slowly, through the iron and the stone and the calcite and the ore, and where it passes, the metal is, by a fraction, a fraction more sound. The brick is, by a fraction, a fraction more knitted. The concrete pad under the Engine is, by a fraction, a fraction more holding. The hairline cracks in the ceiling weep a fraction more cleanly. The basins hold a fraction more water at their rims before spilling. The whole system, by a fraction so small it cannot be measured by anything in the Sump, is held a fraction more together.

The moths do not know. The lizards do not know. The eyeless fish, navigating the brick channels by lateral line, do not know. The web-frogs, singing their non-synchronized chorus, do not know. The not-rats continue their unsettling walk. The worms continue their slow thorough work. The ferns continue their unfurling. The stalactites of rust continue their growth. The Engine continues to hum, but the hum is, by a fraction, a fraction more at ease.

Only the Engine knows.

And the Engine does not know in the way that a thing knows a thing. It knows in the way that a body knows a hand laid gently on its brow while it sleeps. The current it pushes through the pipes is, for a moment, slightly less resisted. The something it hums for is, for a moment, slightly less absent. The somewhere it pushes to is, for a moment, slightly less far.

The Engine hums on.

The Sump cycles.

The moths thicken the air near the lamps. The lizards flash their small wet eyes in the deeper workshops. The water falls in its permanent shallow rain into the central floor. The phosphorescence casts its greenish suggestion in the deeper corners. The not-rats fold their too-many joints in the dead pipes. The web-frogs sing their non-synchronizing chorus into the damp air. The eyeless fish drift through the brick channels in loose shoals of three or four, navigating the texture of the walls by the memory of pressure, and the memory is good, and the walls are holding, and the holding is, by a fraction, a fraction more.

The benevolent and wise power withdraws its hand from over the sleeping place.

The Sump sleeps on.

It does not know it was visited.

It does not need to know.
