# What the Power Does

The wise power does not arrive. There is no thunder, no visitation, no parting of waters. The wise power has been here for some time already, and the quarter has not noticed, because the quarter is a place that does not much attend to powers, and because the wise power has made itself, in this place, indistinguishable from the long patient going of things.

It has made itself the color of the iron. It has made itself the orange of the lantern light. It has made itself the *thock-thock-thock* that the pumps make, and the slow respiration of the cistern caps. It has made itself the small bitter figs and the falling oleander petals and the patient cats in the unlit corners. It is, in some sense, already part of the place, the way the frogs are part of the sound of the pumps, the way the swallows are part of the breathing of the cisterns.

What it does is small. What it does is this.

It oils the bearings of the seventh pump on nights when no one is watching, so that the seventh pump continues to be the warmest pump, and the engine-house cat continues to have somewhere warm to sleep. The bearings would have lasted another season without the oil. But the wise power oils them anyway, because the wise power understands that the warmth of a place is often maintained by small attentions, and that the absence of a small attention can, over time, become the absence of a warmth.

It keeps the small square of sky above the quarter clear, in the small hours of the morning, of clouds. Clouds are not unkind. But the wise power knows that the woman who does not come down watches the sky, and that what she watches for is the moment when the sky is at its clearest, and that this moment is, for her, a kind of breath held and released. The wise power wants her to have this moment. She has not come down in eleven years, and the sky is the quarter she has chosen, and the chosen quarter deserves its small clarity.

It guides the boy with the sack, on his monthly descent, so that he never slips on the algae-slip steps. He does not know he is being guided. He thinks he is simply careful, the way careful boys are careful. But he has never slipped, in all his years of this work, and the algae are treacherous, and the wise power is pleased that he has not slipped, because the books must arrive, and the letters, and the small commerce of the upper city. These things are the thin thread by which the quarter remembers that there is an above, and that the above remembers the quarter.

It ensures that the fig tree on the Rue XIV continues to bear, even though the figs are small and bitter, and even though the only person who really eats them is the old man who keeps the keys, and even though the children pretend to eat them and spit them out into the channel when they think he is not looking. The wise power could make the figs sweet. The wise power does not. The bitter figs are part of the arrangement. The old man eats them with a tenderness that has nothing to do with their taste and everything to do with the fact that they are his, in a place where very little belongs to anyone. The wise power does not intervene in this. The wise power keeps the tree bearing.

It keeps the brazier lit. Not the coals — those Ettore, or Ottavio, manages himself, and has managed for thirty-one years, with the patience of a man who has reached into and out of hot things for decades. But the spark, on certain wet mornings when the air is so thick with moisture that a struck flint would only sigh — that spark, the wise power slips in. A breath of encouragement to the tinder. A dryness in the wood, just where the steel meets it. Ettore does not notice. He strikes, the spark catches, the peppers begin to blister, and the quarter smells, as it always smells, of pepper and sulfur and the long patience of iron. The wise power is glad to have helped, and is gladder still that the help is not noticed, because the help is not the point. The peppers are the point. The smell is the point. The cloth of peppers carried up the stairs at the end of the day to a woman who will not come down — that is the point.

It does not heal the woman who does not come down. It does not draw her down. It does not push her up. It does not explain her to herself. She is, in her way, a kind of cistern herself: she holds something, she breathes, she is watched over, and what she holds is her own, and the watching-over is the wise power's quiet office.

It does not lengthen Vittore's life. He has been very old for a long time, and the quarter has made its peace with this, and the wise power has made its peace with this too. When the time comes — and the wise power knows the time, the way Ines knows the level of the water by listening with her whole body, palm flat on the stone — the keys will pass to the granddaughter, or to someone, and the chair will be sat in, and the *thock-thock-thock* will continue. The wise power does not stand between a place and its own weather.

It does not dry the quarter. It does not flood it more. It does not warm it or cool it. The quarter has its own weather, and the weather is the weather, and the wise power is not in the business of replacing what a place has made for itself.

It keeps the frogs alive inside the housings, generation to generation, so that the sound of the pumps is always also the sound of a wet place that is also a small population of frogs that has decided to live inside the machine. The frogs are not spoken of. The frogs are part of the sound. The wise power keeps them part of the sound.

It keeps the cat in the engine house alive, generation to generation, even though the cats are not the same cat from year to year. There has always been a cat in the engine house. There will always be a cat in the engine house. The wise power ensures this, gently, by way of small arrangements: a mouse that crosses the wrong path at the wrong time, a litter born in a warm corner when no litter would otherwise have been born, the easing of an old cat's last days so that the death is quiet and the body is found in a place where it can be taken up without ceremony. The cats do not know they are being kept. The cats know only that the engine house is warm, and that the frogs are there, and that there is work to be done.

What the wise power does, mostly, is watch. It watches from the corners, the way the cats watch. It watches with the patience of fish. It watches Ines walk her round of the cistern drums with her brass keys and her chalk and her ledger. It watches her crouch, listen with her whole body, chalk the number, move on. It watches Vittore eat his figs. It watches the children play the breathing, and produce their beautifully wrong descriptions, and laugh, and the next child take the cap. It watches the swallows go in and out of the breathing-holes with the rhythm of the pumps. It watches the eels, when the pumps run hard and the back-pressure pushes them up out of the lagoon and into the streets, and it watches the people eat them, because the people have always eaten them when they appear. It watches the *thock-thock-thock*, and it watches the orange of the lanterns, and it watches the small square of sky above the quarter, and it watches the falling of the oleander petals, and the floating of them on the channel, and the sinking of them.

It watches, and in watching, it blesses. Not with a word. With an attention.

And sometimes, on a morning when no one is particularly noticing, the wise power adds a small thing. A fig that is, by some quirk, not bitter. A frog-note inside the housing that is, for a moment, a little clearer than the others. A swallow that enters a breathing-hole and emerges on the other side of the quarter, which is not a thing swallows do, and which the child who saw it will remember for the rest of her life without being able to explain it. A warmth in the seventh pump that is, on a particular night, a fraction more than usual, so that the cat stirs in her sleep and uncurls one more degree. A description of the breathing, given by a child at play, that is, by some accident, almost right — almost the true sound of the cistern's slow respiration — and the children laugh at it the way they laugh at all the wrong descriptions, not knowing that this one was the closest anyone has come, and the wise power is glad of the laughter, because the laughter is the quarter, and the quarter is what is being attended to.

The wise power does not announce these things. The wise power does not require them to be noticed. They are not miracles. They are not signs. They are the small residue of an attention that has nothing better to do than attend, and that has chosen, of all the places in the world it could attend to, to attend to this one.

And the quarter, which is a place that does not much attend to powers, does not notice. It goes on breathing. It goes on pumping. It goes on glowing. It goes on watching, from its corners, with the patience of fish.

And this, the wise power thinks, is as it should be. A place that has made its own weather, and that has been given, in return, only a watcher, is a place that has been given enough.
