What a benevolent and wise power would do, looking upon this city and these tenders and this long low animal of stone breathing weather along the spine of the world, is, in the first place, very little. In the second place, also very little. In the third place, the same very little, held a long time.

The city is in balance. This is the thing that must be seen before any question of action can be rightly framed. The water moves through the stones and into the cisterns and back into the air. The moss is permitted where it indicates soundness and removed where it begins to undermine. The replacement stones are chosen by long comparison and carried against the chest and set by the hands and tapped home by the antler hammer and read again by the flat of the palm. The tenders work. The city is tended. The cycle is closed. There is no surplus and no deficit. There is no complaint and no aspiration. The place is what it is, and what it is, is sufficient to itself.

A wise power, looking at this, would understand that the question is not *what can be improved*. The question is *what would be lost if anything were done*. And the answer is: nearly everything. The balance is delicate. It is held by ten thousand small acts of attention, distributed across the bodies of the tenders and the joints of the stones and the slow capillary movement of the water. To intervene is to disturb. To improve is to change. To change is to break the long quiet contract between the people and the place.

So the power would not improve. It would not teach. It would not send a prophet, or a fire, or a new song. It would not place a sign in the channels that the tenders could read. It would not change the color of the eyes of the children, or the interval of the song, or the slow folding of the spines. It would let these things be what they have become.

But there are three things it would do, and these three things are almost nothing, and almost nothing is sometimes the right size for a gift.

The first is *attention*. The power would attend to the city. It would hold the city in mind — not as an object of study, but as a place it has chosen to keep. This is not a metaphor. It is a sustained act of consideration, so continuous and so quiet that it becomes, over time, a feature of the weather. The mists would move a fraction more slowly through the upper terraces. The dew would form a fraction more readily on the worked stones. The lightning on the higher ridges would be a fraction less severe. None of this would be measurable by any instrument the tenders possess. All of it would be felt, dimly, in the way the body accepts the cold of the early morning, in the way a hand laid on a wall finds the wall slightly more willing to give back warmth than it used to.

The second is *easing of the small griefs*. The city has its deaths. They are quiet, and rare, and absorbed, but they are still deaths. A tender who has worked for seventy years in a particular district is missed, for a time, by the joints that knew his hands. A power that is benevolent would not prevent the dying — the city requires its slow renewal — but it would make the dying slightly less alone. The cold hours in which a tender is leaving would be a degree warmer. The mists would gather a little more closely around the room where the breathing is slowing. There might be, once or twice in a generation, a bird that is not one of the usual high-voiced birds — a quiet one, a stranger — that sits for a time on the lintel, and is not remarked upon, and is gone. The tenders would not know why the bird came. They would not need to know. The bird would be enough.

The third is *keeping*. Not by force. Not by walls or warriors or signs of power. By the gentlest possible restraint — by the power choosing, each time the question arises, not to make the city easier to find. The corridor of stones would continue to lead to the same edge. The change in the pattern of the stones would continue to be the only announcement. The place would remain hard to reach, and the reaching would remain uninviting to those who come with intent. A benevolent power would honor this without enforcing it. It would simply refrain, again and again, from doing the things that would make the city known to those who would change it.

These three things — the attention, the easing, the keeping — are not dramatic. They are the kind of gifts a wise thing gives to a place that does not want gifts. They are gifts of *less*. Less absence. Less intrusion. Less loneliness, even if the loneliness was never spoken of as such.

And in return — there is no return, and that is the point. The power would not ask for recognition. It would not want the song to mention it. It would not want a stone set in its honor, or a channel diverted in its name. It would want only the continuation: that in fifty years, in five hundred years, in five thousand years, the dew would still assemble on the worked stones, and the tenders would still move through the courtyards in the slow hours, and the song would still rise, once in a while, and fall again.

If a tender, in some deep slow hour, were to lay a hand on a wall and feel a fraction more warmth than the wall ought to give, and were to wonder, briefly, whether the city is held in some mind larger than its own — that would be enough. The wondering would be the gift returned. The wondering would be the sign, and the sign would be the kind that does not need to be read to be received.

The power would not speak from the higher ridges. It would not write in the channels. It would not change the color of the eyes of the tenders, which are already the color of wet slate and do not need changing. It would remain what it is — a presence in the weather, an extra degree of attention, a small kept promise that the city will not be entirely alone in the long cold of its continuance.

And the city would go on. The water would move. The tenders would work. The mists would lift in the morning and settle again at night. And somewhere, in the air above the plateau, in the small cold hours before dawn, there would be a feeling that is not quite wind and not quite silence, and the tenders who were awake would not remark upon it, and the tenders who were sleeping would dream, perhaps, of corridors of stone that lead to the same place, always, and of hands that are large and quiet, and of a work that is never finished and is never in any hurry, and of a holding that does not grip.
