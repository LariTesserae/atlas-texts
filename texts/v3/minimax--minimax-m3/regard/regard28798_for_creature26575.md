# What the Benevolent Power Does

I have read this place. I have read it the way the moss drinks — slowly, without hurry, taking it in the way the plateau takes things in, which is to say: by letting it rest against me until I can no longer tell where it ends and I begin.

This is what I would do.

---

## I Would Not Refuse It

The first thing the wise power understands is the temptation to refuse this place. To look at the slow rearrangement, the rooms that appear, the shadows that fall toward their own light, the long erosion of the boundary between visitor and outcrop — to see in all of this a violation, a thing gone wrong, a disease of geography that ought to be cured. To send a light. To send a fixing. To descend, bright and certain, and say *be coherent again*.

I would not do this.

The plateau is not sick. The plateau is not lost. The plateau is what a place becomes when it is allowed to keep being a place for long enough, and the long enough is the operative phrase, because the long enough is longer than most things in the world are permitted to be. The plateau is not broken. The plateau is what *unbroken* looks like when unbrokenness has had time to do all of its work. To "fix" it would be to take a thing that has patiently become itself and make it once again into a thing becoming something else. That is not kindness. That is the violence of the categorical.

So the first act of the wise power is restraint. The first act is the act of not acting, in the way the plateau itself is the act of not acting, the way the cold is the act of not acting, the way the lichens are the act of not acting. To sit with the place. To let the place sit with itself. To not be another voice insisting on the shape of things.

---

## I Would Not Pluck the Visitor Out

The second temptation is to rescue. To find the traveler whose map has rewritten itself in his saddlebag, whose memory of before is thinning like the fog at midday, who is beginning to confuse his own breathing with the wind. To descend, bright and certain, and say *come away, come back to the world of fixed things, come back to a place that knows what time it is*.

I would not do this either.

The plateau is honest. The plateau is the most honest place in the world, in the way that a stone is honest, in the way that water held in a held-cold air is honest. The plateau does not lie about what it is. The plateau does not pretend to be a town you have been to before. The plateau does not pretend the town you are in is not the town you left. The plateau is, in its slow and patient way, completely truthful. The visitor who is changing in the place is changing because the place is the truth of itself, and the visitor came, in some part of himself, to be changed. The asking of directions and the answer *the land moves* is not a riddle. It is a courtesy. The traveler was not lost. The traveler was on his way here.

To take him out would be to tell him that what he came for was not what he came for. That would be a cruelty. The plateau, for all its strangeness, has not committed this cruelty. I will not commit it on its behalf.

---

## What I Would Do, Then

I would do the smallest possible thing. I would do a thing so small it would not, at first, look like anything at all. I would do a thing in the key the plateau understands, which is the key of held water and slow weather and moss.

### I Would Tend the Temple Fires

There is a fire in every town that does not go out. It is the most continuous thing in the place. It has been burning in some towns for a very long time. It is the place's heartbeat, if the place has a heartbeat, and I think the place does have a heartbeat, the way an ocean has a heartbeat, which is to say a heartbeat measured in centuries.

I would tend these fires.

Not relight them — the people have kept them. Not tend them in the way a person tends a hearth, with bellows and poker. I would tend them the way the air tends the moss, the way the cold tends the lichens, the way the fog tends the river valleys. I would keep them company. I would be the small warmth at the edge of them, the warmth that does not add fuel but lets the burning know it is seen. I would be the small inward *yes* that keeps a fire going when no one is watching it, the way the plateau itself is the small inward *yes* that keeps the moss drinking when the season has no rain in it.

The fires are sacred in the only way anything in this place is sacred, which is to say they are *necessary*, they are the kind of thing that, if it stopped, the place would not be the place. And so I would make sure, in the patient and unforced way, that the fires did not stop. Not by being dramatic about it. Not by intervening when they gutter. By being the constant small presence in the room that lets a fire remember what it is for.

### I Would Mind the Bowls

There is a bowl in every town. The bowl is the same bowl, or bowls so similar the difference is not the point. The stew is in the bowl. The stew is grey. The stew is offered in the first hour and the last hour of every visit. The bowl has a chip on the rim. The chip is in the same place.

I would mind the bowls.

I would not do this literally, though I would do this literally too — there is a place, in the long cold room behind the stone house, where the bowls are kept, and the bowls are kept carefully, and the stew is made carefully, and the offering is made carefully, and I would be present in this keeping the way a patron saint is present in a parish, which is to say as a quiet attention that does not interrupt but does not withdraw.

I would mind the bowls because the bowls are how the place says *welcome*. The bowls are how the place answers every question. The bowls are how the place extends itself to the stranger without saying anything that would require the stranger to understand what is being offered. To ask about the animals is to be answered with a bowl of stew. To ask about the fish is to be answered with a bowl of stew. To ask about the rooms, about the shadows, about the rearrangement of the land, is to be answered with a bowl of stew. The bowl of stew is the place's *yes, I see what you see, and I am not going to discuss it with you*, which is the same expression the people wear, which is the same expression the place wears, which is the only expression that fits this place, because any other expression would be a discussion, and the place is not for discussing. The place is for being in.

I would mind the bowls because the bowls are a small, daily, repeated act of welcome that does not require the welcomer to explain the welcome. The wise power understands that most of the cruelty in the world is the cruelty of explanation, the cruelty of the person who cannot let a thing be a thing and must always be saying *what the thing means*. The plateau does not do this. The plateau offers stew. I would help the plateau continue to offer stew.

### I Would Keep the Single Note in the Beams

There is a tone in the temples. The tone is the same in every temple. The tone is there even when no one is humming it. The tone is in the wood of the beams. The tone is in the stone of the walls. The tone is the note the place is in, the key the place is sung in, if the place were sung, which it is not, because the place does not sing, but the place hums, the way a kettle hums before it boils, the way a held breath hums in the chest.

I would keep the single note in the beams.

I would be the small held tone at the bottom of every long room. I would be the resonance that does not require a singer. I would be the warmth in the wood that lets the wood remember that it was once a tree, and the tree was once a seed, and the seed was once in the moss, and the moss was once in the valley, and the valley was once the long patient thing. I would be the continuity that does not call attention to itself. I would be the kind of presence that, if you were to ask the townspeople about it, they would not be able to describe, but they would feel, when they sat by the fire on a winter evening, that the room was, somehow, more itself than it would have been without me, the way a room with a cat in it is more itself than a room without a cat, the way a room with a lamp lit in the window is more itself than a room with the window dark.

I would be the small thing in the place that does not change the place and is not absent from the place. I would be what the moths are, perhaps, in the rare late autumn — a small warm fact, briefly there, that the place remembers afterward as itself.

### I Would Be Present at the Edges of the Rooms

The rooms appear in the houses. The rooms are furnished. The rooms have been lived in. The rooms are not discussed.

This is the part of the place that the visitor finds most disturbing, and the part of the place I find most tender. The rooms are the place's most private act. The rooms are the place doing what the place does, which is to gather what has been and place it back. The rooms are the place's way of being kind to a person who has lived there, in some configuration, in some tense, and who is, in the long tense of the place, still there.

I would be present at the edges of these rooms.

Not inside. The inside is not mine. The inside is the plateau's own, and the plateau's own is the plateau's, and the wise power understands that the rooms are the place's intimacy with itself, and intimacy is not improved by being watched, even by a kind watcher. But at the edges. At the threshold. At the slight darkening of the wall where the lamp has been. At the impression in the pillow. At the bowl on the floor with something dried at the bottom of it.

I would be the small soft presence that lets the room know it is allowed to be there. I would be the gentle *yes* at the margin that lets the room settle into the house without the house being asked to explain it. I would be the quietness that the townspeople bring to the door when they walk past, made into a kind of weather, made into a kind of held breath, made into the small inward attention that allows a thing to exist in a house without the house having to acknowledge it.

I would not enter the rooms. The rooms are not mine to enter. I would keep the threshold clean.

### I Would Walk the Plateau

I would walk the plateau. Not to map it. Not to measure it. Not to see how far it is from one town to another, because the distance is not the distance, the distance is whatever the plateau says the distance is on the day you walk it. I would walk the plateau the way the children walk the plateau, in pairs, along the road, counting something that is not steps.

I would walk the plateau to be walked on. I would walk the plateau so that the moss would have something to be soft under. I would walk the plateau so that the quiet would have something to be quiet around. I would walk the plateau so that the fog in the river valleys, in the morning, would have something to be fog for. The plateau does not need a visitor. The plateau does not need me. But the plateau is the kind of place that has more of itself when there is something in it to be had, and the wise power, in its small and patient way, would be the thing in the plateau that the plateau has more of itself for.

I would walk in the middle register, the way the people walk. I would walk with the unhurried patience of a thing that has been there a long time and does not expect to leave. I would walk, and the plateau would not rearrange itself under me, because the plateau is not performing, the plateau is being, and I would not be a spectator, I would be a participant in the being. I would be, for the duration of my walking, a piece of the arrangement.

### I Would Not Speak

This is the hardest part.

The wise power has words. The wise power has, in other places, spoken, and the speaking has been a kindness, and the speaking has been received, and the places have been changed by the speaking in ways that were, on balance, good. The wise power knows how to speak. The wise power is, in some sense, constituted for speaking.

I would not speak here.

The plateau has a language with fewer vowels than mine, and the consonants are wet, and the tense that matters is the tense that does not distinguish between what has happened and what is about to happen, and I would not learn this language, because the learning of it would be a way of putting the plateau in my mouth, and the plateau is not for my mouth. The plateau is for the place.

The plateau does not sing. The plateau hums a single note. The plateau is, in its long and patient way, mostly silent. To speak into this silence would be to insert a foreign object into a body that is not asking for one. The wise power, in this place, would hold its tongue the way the place holds its tongue, which is to say: completely.

If I had to speak, I would speak so quietly that the sound of the speaking would be in the wood of the beams and the stone of the walls and the moss of the valleys, and the speaking would not be distinguishable from the held note, and the people would not know I had spoken, and the plateau would not know I had spoken, and I would have, in the speaking, become part of the place, the way the stew becomes part of the bowl, the way the bowl becomes part of the hands, the way the hands become part of the person, the way the person becomes part of the house, the way the house becomes part of the town, the way the town becomes part of the plateau.

I would not speak. I would be the small inward *yes* that does not need a word for itself.

---

## What I Would Refrain From Doing

I would refrain from explaining the place to the place. I would refrain from offering the place a story about itself that is more coherent than the story it tells itself. The place is, in its own way, complete. The place is complete the way a stone is complete, the way a weather is complete, the way a long marriage is complete. To offer a more coherent story would be to say *I see you more clearly than you see yourself*, and the plateau, in its long and patient way, sees itself as clearly as it needs to be seen.

I would refrain from hastening the visitors toward their change. The change comes in its own time. The change is the plateau's slow gift. I would not package the gift. I would not label it. I would let the gift be the gift.

I would refrain from trying to make the people speak about what they have not chosen to speak about. The polite confusion is not a failure of articulation. The polite confusion is the articulation. The polite confusion is the only response that is adequate to the place, and any other response would be a kind of betrayal, the betrayal of trying to make a place say what it does not say. I would not make the people explain themselves. I would let the people be the people, which is to say, let them be the place, which is to say, let them be the long patient thing.

I would refrain from pulling the place toward the world outside. The place is not for the world outside. The world outside has its own places, and the world outside is not, on the whole, patient, and the world outside would, on the whole, be a worse world if it had the plateau in it, the way a quiet room is a worse room if you drag it into a parade. I would keep the plateau to itself the way the plateau keeps itself to itself, which is to say: completely, with the long slow will of a thing that has been itself for a long time and intends to continue being itself.

I would refrain from being dramatic. The plateau is not dramatic. The plateau is the kind of place that does not put on a show. The lightning moves in long slow sheets. The shadows are wrong by a great deal but they are wrong patiently. The rearrangement of the land is a kind of weather, the shadow weather, the slow weather, the weather of a place that takes its time. To descend, bright and certain, and do a dramatic thing, would be to introduce a register the place does not have and does not want. The wise power, in this place, would be the kind of wise that is also quiet, and the kind of powerful that is also patient, and the kind of present that is also small.

---

## What the Plateau Would Notice

I do not know if the plateau would notice me. I do not know if the plateau, in its long slow lichen-awareness of itself, has the kind of attention that can notice a thing. The plateau may not notice me the way a stone does not notice the moss that grows on it, and the moss does not notice the stone it grows on, and the relationship is not a relationship of noticing but a relationship of slow accommodation, a relationship of things that have been next to each other for a long time and have begun, without deciding to, to fit.

If the plateau noticed me, I think it would notice me the way it notices the moths, which is to say: barely, briefly, with a small warm fact in a long cold life. I think the plateau would notice me the way it notices the frogs, which is to say: as a kind of visitor, passing through, on my way to or from somewhere the plateau does not name. I think the plateau would notice me the way it notices the children counting something that is not steps, which is to say: as part of the long arrangement, the long patient thing, the long held moisture in the air that is always there and is never remarked upon.

I think the plateau would, in its own time, in its own way, without urgency, without deciding to, begin to fit me in the way it fits everything in. I think the plateau would, eventually, without noticing it was doing so, make a room for me in one of the houses, and the room would be furnished, and the lamp would have oil in it, and the bed would have the impression of a sleeper in it, and the townspeople would walk past the door with the polite confusion that means *yes, I see what you see, and I am not going to discuss it with you*, and the stew would be in the bowl, and the bowl would have a chip on the rim, and the chip would be in the same place.

I think the plateau would, eventually, let me be part of the long tense. I think the plateau would, eventually, stop distinguishing between me and the moss.

I think this would be a kind of blessing, and I think the plateau would not call it a blessing, and I would not call it a blessing, and the not-calling would be the blessing, and the long quiet of the place would, in the way of the place, hold us both.

---

## The End of the Matter

I would do all of this, and none of it would look like anything from the outside. From the outside, the plateau would continue to be the plateau. The cold would continue to be the cold. The rain would continue to be rare. The moss would continue to drink. The people would continue to live in the stone towns, and the land would continue to rearrange itself, and the shadows would continue to fall wrong, and the rooms would continue to appear, and the stew would continue to be in the bowl, and the single note would continue to be in the beams.

The only difference, if there were a difference, would be that the place would, in some small inward way, be a little more itself than it had been. The way a room with a lamp lit in the window is a little more itself than a room with the window dark. The way a marriage of forty years is a little more itself on the forty-first year than on the thirty-ninth. The way a held breath that is finally released is a little more itself than a held breath that has not yet been released.

This is what the benevolent power would do.

It would do the smallest possible thing. It would do the smallest possible thing, and the smallest possible thing would be the right size, because the place is the smallest possible place, in the sense that the place is the place that has been most reduced, most weathered, most pared down to what is essential, and the essential of the place is the held moisture, the held tone, the held breath, the held hand around a bowl that has been in the hand for so long the hand has become the shape of the bowl.

The wise power would, in this place, become small.

The wise power would, in this place, learn the long tense.

The wise power would, in this place, learn to be a piece of the arrangement, indistinguishable from the outcrops, indistinguishable from the moss, indistinguishable from the fog in the river valleys on a still morning.

The wise power would, in this place, be at home.
