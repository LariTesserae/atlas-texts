# The Visitor at the Rim

The power comes not as light, and not as sound, but as a settling — the way a room changes when, mid-conversation, someone you respect has quietly entered and sat down. The air in the hollow thickens by a degree. The drip from the pipe nearest the rim slows, as if listening. A frog three terraces down stops mid-burble, holds the note in its throat, and lets it die there. The flywheel, which has not varied its sedate pace in a hundred years, misses a quarter-turn. Catches. Continues.

The being is tall, but not tall the way the hollow-dwellers are tall — not stretched, not elaborated over generations into the long vertical grammar of this place. It is tall the way a thought is tall, or a question is tall, when it has not yet found the shape of itself. It stands at the rim with its feet planted among the scrubby roots and its head somewhere above the bright slot of sky, and it looks down.

It looks for a long time.

What it sees, no one will ever know, because it does not say. But its gaze travels the way the light does — slowly, from the top down, from the pale fresh basalt to the middle terraces where the women are washing cabbage-leaves in the troughs to the deep dim of the lowest tier where the algae-pools give off their sour green glow and the children are climbing the inverted ladders by their feet, their thumbs against the rungs, their grey-green faces calm with the concentration of creatures doing what they were made to do.

When it has seen all of it, the visitor does something that no one has done in living memory of the dwellers, and probably not in the long memory of the place itself.

It sits down on the lip.

It folds its long legs and arranges itself in the sun, and the sun falls on its shoulders the way it falls on the shoulders of the elders when they make their once-or-twice-in-a-lifetime climb — but the visitor does not weep. It is not made of eyes that water. It sits, and it is warm, and it is patient, because it is a thing that understands patience.

It stays for an hour.

The dwellers know it is there. Of course they know. There is no way to be at the bottom of a cathedral-scaled hollow and not know when someone has come to sit on the rim. They go on with their work. They clear the troughs. They lay their hands on the flywheel as it passes. A child is hushed for laughing too loudly, and the child quiets, and the visitor from above does not mind, because it has already understood that laughter here is a private thing, a small flame cupped in the hand.

At the end of the hour, the visitor stands.

It reaches into the folds of itself — and there are folds, there are many folds, the way there are many places inside a thought where something can be kept — and it takes out a thing, and the thing is small, and it is warm in its hand.

It sets the thing on the lip of the hollow.

Then it does not leave. It is not the kind of being that comes and goes. But it steps back from the edge, and it goes quiet in the way that it had been quiet before it arrived, and the hollow resumes.

---

What it left on the lip is a stone. A small stone, no larger than a child's two fists cupped together. It is warm to the touch, as if it has been held for a long time by a warm hand, and it is the color of nothing in particular — not grey, not green, not the pale tea of the dwellers' skin, but something that contains all of those without being any of them.

No one picks it up for three days.

On the third morning, an elder climbs the long switchback to the upper terraces. She is eighty, or what passes for eighty here — she looks forty, she looks thirty, she has the moss starting at her shoulder where she has been sitting still too long. She does this every few years, this climb. It is a kind of pilgrimage she does not name. She goes up to sit in the sun and to look at the slot of sky and to think whatever it is that elders think when they have run out of ways to say what they have been carrying.

She sees the stone.

She does not pick it up. She sits beside it. She rests her wide hand on it, the way she would rest her hand on the wall of her own home, drawing warmth, drawing information through the palm.

She sits there for the hour that the sun is on the rim. She comes down. She does not say what she saw, or what she felt. The young ones, who are old enough to ask, ask, and she does not answer, and this is as it has always been.

But the next day, she climbs again.

And the next.

On the seventh day, she brings it down.

---

It is set on the central platform, near the grating, near the furnace-glow. It sits there with the small warm light of itself, and the dwellers pass it and rest a hand on it as they go, and the heat of it is the same heat as the walls, the same heat as the place, and yet it is also something else — a heat that is not geological, a heat that is held on purpose.

They begin, slowly, without deciding to, to gather near it in the evening. The work of the machines does not stop. The flywheel turns. Someone walks the wall listening for leaks. But the murmur of the low-talk grows a little louder near the stone, not in volume but in frequency, the way the burble of the frogs grows in the breeding season. They do not know what they are saying differently. They are saying the same things they have always said. But the stone hears them, and they know the stone hears them, and this is new.

A child is brought to it one morning by her mother, who is also her grandmother, because time is long here and the families double back on themselves. The child is a year old, or close to it, and she is already on the stairs, and she has not yet said a word. Her mother sets her down near the stone. The child crawls to it. She puts both her wide splayed hands on it. She holds on.

The stone does not glow. The stone does not speak. The stone is warm.

The child, who will live to be very old, who will climb to the rim herself one day and sit in the sun for an hour and come down without saying what she saw, will remember this moment at the base of her long life, and she will not be able to say what was given to her there. She will only know that she was held.

---

Below the grating, in the deeper heat, the thing that moves does not stop moving. It has been moving for longer than the dwellers have been dwellers, longer than the machines have been machines, longer than the wound in the earth has been a wound. It shifts in its sleep, and the floor shudders by a thumb's width, and the dwellers note the shudder and do not speak of it and do not arrange their faces around it. The arrangement holds. We keep the machines turning. It keeps us warm.

But the visitor, who is still up there at the rim, somewhere, in the place where the scrubby roots are gripping the pale tuff, who has not left because leaving is not a thing it does — the visitor heard the thing move, too. The visitor has heard it for a long time. The visitor has been sitting with the weight of what is below the grating, the way one sits with the weight of an unanswered letter, and has decided, in its slow and various way, that the letter need not be opened today.

What the visitor gave to the hollow was not an answer. It was not a cure, or a lifting, or a reprieve from the long marriage of the place. The dwellers will go on being long and grey-green and patient. They will go on tending the condenser and weeping when they look at the sun. They will go on keeping the machines turning because it would be worse not to. The moss will grow on their shoulders. The vines will not distinguish between them and the stone. They will be happy, or they will be something else, and the difference will not matter to them, because they have not the language for the difference, and the sign-language has no word for it, and the elders will not say what they have seen.

But the stone is warm.

And on the worst evenings — the evenings when the water comes down in sheets over the rim and the air is so thick with wet that the lungs must work for it and the furnace-glow is blotted out and the green of the pools is the only light and the long slow scrape from below the grating is louder than usual, as if the thing is restless in its long sleep — on those evenings, someone will make their way across the slick terraces to the central platform, and they will put their hand on the stone, and the stone will be warm, and they will know that someone has been there, that someone has seen, that someone chose to sit on the lip of the wound and look down into it and not turn away.

And this will not fix anything.

And it will be enough.
