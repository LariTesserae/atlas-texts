What I would do, if I were that benevolent and wise power, is very little — and that little, slowly.

I would not flood the basin. I would not dry it. I would not, in my wisdom, decide that what is here is wrong, or incomplete, or in need of being made over into something I would prefer. The basin is what it is. It has been the slow patient thing it is for longer than my wanting has existed. To remake it would be to admit I do not trust what I am looking at, and the basin does not need my trust. It has its own.

What I would do is this.

I would warm, by a hair's breadth, the upper few inches of the standing water in the deepest pools — not enough to change anything, not enough that any dweller there would notice a difference in their morning. Just enough that the eggs laid tonight, in the shallows where the dog-sized amphibians nest in the shallows where the small translucent ones pulse their tiny visible hearts against the mud, have a slightly better chance of making it through the cold snap that comes late in the season, the one that every two or three years takes a little more than it usually takes. A margin. Not a rescue. A margin.

I would leave, in three or four places across the basin, in the lee of outcrops where the wind does not reach and the mist settles thickest, a handful of small stones that are slightly harder than the local stone, slightly less weathered, and that will, for a few extra generations, give the people a better edge for their hand-axes than the river stone they currently use. Not enough to change the trade. Not enough to make them rich or armed or anything else that I would recognize as a gift if I were being clever about it. Just enough that the bone needles they currently drill by patient rubbing against grit can, in a few households, be a little sharper, and the sinew can hold a little longer, and the hide tents can be stitched a little tighter. A margin, again.

I would do nothing about the things-with-leather-voices. I would do nothing about the burrowers. I would do nothing about the raptor-things, which are doing exactly what they should be doing, which is sitting, and waiting, and not missing. Whatever those large shapes are that dome the surface of the still water and pass underneath, that is their own long business with the basin, and the basin has been having that business without my opinion for a long time.

I would not heal anyone. I would not save anyone. I would not, in the way that lesser powers do, reach down into a specific tent on a specific night and cure a specific cough in a specific child, because the moment I did that I would have begun keeping accounts. I would have begun deciding who deserves and who does not, and the basin has no use for my accounting. The basin has its own patience, and its own accounting, in which the cough and the child and the night are all just things passing through, the way the amphibians pass through each other, the way the silt passes through the worms.

What I would do is small, and patient, and I would not announce it.

I would do it the way weather does it. The way the mist does it. I would do it so that in a hundred years, if someone came through the basin and asked the dark careful people if anything strange had ever happened here, if anything had ever intervened in their long wet arrangements, they would look at each other, and they would shrug, and they would say that the basin is the basin, and that things have always been mostly as they are.

And that would be the truest answer.

I think that is what wisdom looks like, in this place. Not the thunderbolt. Not the miracle. Not the small bright carved shape that says *a god was here.* Just a hair's breadth of warmth in the deepest pools. A few harder stones in three or four sheltered places. The rest of the basin left alone, to be the patient wet loud old thing it has been working on becoming since long before I thought to look at it.

The basin does not need me. The basin has been doing fine without me for an age. What the basin might accept, if it accepted anything, is a very small hand on the scale, in the direction it is already going. To make the going a little easier, for a few of the small lives here, without changing where they are going.

That, I think, is all. And I would do even that gently, and without being sure it was the right thing, and I would let the basin forget I had done it, the way it forgets most things.

That is what I would do.
