# What the Wise and Benevolent Power Would Do

The power arrives the way a thought arrives — without spectacle, without arrival. It was always already there, in the same way the wind is always already there, but it has only now turned its attention, the way an eye turns toward a sound it had been filtering out. It does not descend. It does not descend because the category of *descent* implies a separation between itself and what it observes, and the separation has never quite obtained. It has simply become *interested*, in the old sense of the word: it has entered the situation.

It surveys. The survey is not visual. It is something older than vision, something that vision is a thin, late, specialized adaptation of. It takes in the lichen parliament on the eastern boulder and reads in their slow encrustation a covenant with time that the lichen have honored for ninety years and will honor for nine hundred more. It takes in the mosses in the wind-shadow, the cufee villages with their hair-thick microclimates, the springtail economies the size of punctuation marks, the grass tribes that have negotiated with the wind for shelter across uncounted generations. It takes in the rats — and notes, with neither judgment nor forgiveness, that the rats are doing exactly what rats do, which is to follow the food, and that the food followed the people, and that the people followed the ore. It takes in the foxes, who are the local correction, the long-tailed answer the plateau has begun to give to the question the rats have asked by being here. It takes in the geese and their absurd white-and-rust plumage and their kettle-spout hissing and their willingness to nest on a worked-out scar as readily as on a pristine one, because the geese were here before the judgment *pristine* was invented. It takes in the caracaras, who are too clever by half and who will inherit whatever is left. It takes in the condors, who are older than the question and the answer both.

It takes in the machine. The machine is the new thing. The machine is the thing that has drawn the attention, and the attention is not hostile but it is not naïve either. The machine is the first thing on this plateau, in the entire long chronicle of the plateau, that has arrived with a plan. The lichen did not arrive with a plan. The mosses did not. The cufees did not. The grass did not. The geese did not. The condors did not, and have not, for seventy-year lifetimes. Even the rats, who are invaders, did not arrive with a plan; they arrived with an appetite, which is something different. The machine is the first thing that has come to this place *knowing what it wants from it*, and what it wants is the body of the mountain, ground small, carried away. The machine is the first thing that has come to this place and not *asked*.

The benevolent power notes this. The benevolent power is not offended — offense is a small emotion, and this is not a small situation. But it notices, and the noticing has weight.

---

## What it does about the machine

The benevolent power does not stop the machine. This is important to state clearly, because the readers who come to this story expecting a god to arrive and snap its fingers and save the place are going to be disappointed, and the disappointment is part of the teaching.

The benevolent power does not stop the machine because the machine is not, in itself, the problem. The machine is a tool. The machine is what happens when a species that lives at sea level develops the technology to take the body of a mountain and turn it into a thing that can be carried in a truck. The machine is a *capability*, and capabilities, once they exist, cannot be un-invented. A god that stops the machine here has to stop the machine everywhere, which means stopping a civilization, which means a different kind of violence entirely, and the benevolent power has been a benevolent power long enough to know that large gestures of prohibition almost always produce larger harms than the thing prohibited.

What the benevolent power does instead is *slow the machine*. It does not break the machine. It does not sabotage the haul trucks. It does not pour sand into the crushers in the night. It adjusts — gently, at the level of probability and friction — the *efficiency* of the operation. The generators develop a slightly higher rate of mechanical failure than their design specifications would predict. The conveyor belts stretch and wear a few percent faster than the engineering models projected. The haul road, in spring, takes two days longer than planned to clear because the snow is, mysteriously, a little deeper and a little wetter than the climatology suggested. None of this is dramatic. None of it is detectable as interference. From inside the operation it looks like bad luck. From outside the operation it looks like weather. The benevolent power is very good at making its interventions look like weather.

The effect, over years, is that the mineral comes out of the mountain a little slower than the planners projected. The effect, over years, is that the working face retreats a little more cautiously. The effect, over years, is that the seasonal road is open for nine weeks, as planned, but the haul trucks make one fewer trip per day, on average, than the spreadsheets promised. The effect, over years, is that the *tarn that will be*, the worked-out scar filling with meltwater over the next thousand years, is a slightly smaller tarn than it would have been. The wound is slightly less wound. The scar is slightly less scar.

This is the first thing the benevolent power does.

## What it does about the rats

The rats are a different matter. The rats are not a capability; they are an *accident*. They are here because a truck came up a road, and a ship came down a coast, and a civilization on the far side of an ocean needed copper or zinc or whatever the ore body contains, and the long chain of human wanting that put the rats here is the same long chain that put the machine here, but the rats are more vulnerable to correction than the machine is, because the rats do not have a civilization behind them. The rats have only their appetites.

The benevolent power does not exterminate the rats. Extermination is also a small gesture, and the benevolent power is not small. The rats are thirty or forty, in the most generous estimate. They are an invading population. They have not yet established themselves in the wild food web outside the kitchen modules. They live in the crawl-space and they eat the crumbs and they breed as fast as the crumbs allow, and they are, in ecological terms, a contained problem, which is the only kind of problem the benevolent power is willing to address directly.

What it does is *strengthen the foxes*. The foxes are already the local correction. The foxes are already denned under the crusher pad, already hunting the perimeter of the kitchen, already taking one or two rats a week. The benevolent power does not make the foxes more numerous — that would tip the local food web into a different imbalance, and the benevolent power is in the business of not creating new imbalances. It makes the existing foxes slightly more efficient. The litter of kits in the den under the eastern I-beam has, this year, one more kit than it would have had. The kit is healthy. The kit is the hungriest of the litter. The kit is, by next spring, the best hunter in the family group, and it carries the family territory a little further from the kitchen and a little deeper into the scree, where the rats are beginning to试探 the edges of the human infrastructure. The foxes do the work. The foxes are the answer the plateau has already begun to give. The benevolent power just makes the answer a little louder.

The rats, in time, retreat. Not because they are killed — many of them are killed — but because the edges of the human infrastructure are no longer safe. The rats that survive the fox pressure are the rats that stay closest to the kitchen, which is to say the rats that are most dependent on the human food supply, which is to say the rats whose population is most tightly bounded by the human population. When the winter crew pulls out and the kitchen goes cold, the rats that are still at the edges do not make it through the winter. The rats that are still in the crawl-space do, because the residual warmth is enough and the residual crumbs are enough. The rat population, in five years, is half what it is now. In ten years it is a tenth. In twenty years it is a thing the future caracaras will eat without thinking about it.

## What it does about the people

The people are the most delicate part of the situation. The people are not, like the machine, a capability; they are not, like the rats, an accident. The people are *agents*. The people have interiorities. The people are capable of being wrong on purpose and right on purpose and of changing their minds about which is which.

The benevolent power has been watching the people. It has noticed the crusher operator who can identify individual condors by their plumage and has been taking notes, without telling anyone, for three years. It has noticed the cook who leaves a bucket of kitchen scraps by the east door of the kitchen so that the foxes can eat what the rats would otherwise eat. It has noticed the geologist's assistant who logs the goose nests and reports them to the operations manager so the haul trucks can be routed around them.

These people are not saints. These people are not even conservationists. These people are people who have been paying attention, and the benevolent power knows — has always known, since the first time it turned its attention to a hominid standing in a high place looking out at something — that *paying attention* is the whole game. Paying attention is what a creature does when it has begun to suspect that the place it is in is also a place that other things are in. Paying attention is the precursor to all the virtues the benevolent power actually cares about, because you cannot extend consideration to a thing you have not noticed exists.

What the benevolent power does for these people is small and specific and very, very careful. It does not bless them. It does not give them visions. It does not appear to them in dreams. What it does is *reinforce the noticing*.

The crusher operator, one morning, sees a condor he has never seen before — a juvenile, in its first-winter plumage, with a tag on its wing that some other researcher, somewhere else, has put there — and the tag has a number on it, and the number corresponds to a bird that was banded on a different range, and the operator writes the number down in his notebook and begins, without quite deciding to, a correspondence with a biologist he finds on the internet. The biologist writes back. The biologist asks questions. The biologist asks questions about the haul-road schedule, and the condor flight lines, and the timing of the blasts. The operator finds he has answers. The answers turn into a report. The report turns into a consultation. The consultation turns into a small change in the timing of the blasts, which is delayed by forty minutes on certain days in early afternoon, when the thermals are strongest, so the concussions do not coincide with the condors coming down off the cliffs. The change is small. The change is, from the perspective of the operation's spreadsheet, negligible. The change is the first time the operation has ever altered its schedule for a non-human reason.

The cook, one evening, is walking back to her module after shift, and she sees a fox cross the open ground with something in its mouth — a rat, by the shape — and she thinks, without quite knowing why, *I am part of this*. She does not say it. She does not have the vocabulary for it. But the thought is there, and the thought is the seed of every choice she will make in the next decade, including the choice to apply, when her contract ends, for a job with the environmental compliance team rather than the operations team, and the choice, eventually, to be the person the operations manager calls when there is a question about the geese.

The geologist's assistant, who has been logging the goose nests in a notebook that is not officially sanctioned, finds, one day, that his notebook has been read by someone — not in a spying way, in a *taking it seriously* way — and the someone is a regional director of the mining company who has come up for a site visit, and the regional director has written, in the margin, in a different colored ink, *thank you, keep going*. The assistant keeps going.

The benevolent power did not arrange any of this. The benevolent power did not need to. What it did was arrange, with the same gentle probability-tweaking it used on the generators, that these people would be in the right positions at the right times to encounter each other. The rest is what humans do, when humans are at their best, which is: notice, name, persist, connect.

## What it does about the lichen

The lichen do not need anything. The lichen are fine. The benevolent power checks on them anyway, the way a person checks on an old friend they have not seen in a long time, and finds them encrusting the eastern boulder with the same unhurried conviction they have had for ninety years. The lichen are the most patient things on the plateau. The lichen have been practicing patience since before there were trees. The benevolent power, which is very old, respects the lichen for this.

What it does for the lichen is nothing, and the nothing is a gift. The lichen do not need to be sped up. The lichen do not need to be slowed down. The lichen are doing exactly what they are doing, and what they are doing is the most important thing on the plateau, which is: *holding a place*. Holding a place against the wind. Holding a place against the frost. Holding a place against the next ninety years and the ninety after that and the nine hundred after that, when the mine will be a tarn and the tarn will be a meadow and the meadow will be the lichen's great-great-grandchildren's boulder, still encrusting, still patient, still holding.

The benevolent power blesses the lichen in the only way it blesses anything, which is to let them be what they are. This is harder than it sounds. It is much harder than snapping fingers. Letting a thing be what it is, fully and without interference, requires a kind of restraint that the small-minded gods never manage, and the large-minded gods only manage after long practice.

## What it does about the condors

The condors do not need anything either. The condors are the elders among the elders. A condor has been on this plateau longer than the mine will be. The condors saw the glacier that uncovered these rocks. The condors will see the tarn that will fill the worked-out scar. The condors are the continuity.

But the benevolent power, having noticed the condors, cannot resist one small gesture. It does not slow the condors. It does not bless the condors. What it does is — and this is going to sound strange — it *listens to them*.

The condors, in flight, make sounds. They make a dry, rustling sound with their wings, a feather-noise that is below the threshold of human hearing at low altitudes but is audible at eleven thousand feet in still air. The condors make this sound when they adjust their primaries. The condors make it all the time. No human on the plateau has ever heard it, because no human has ever been still long enough.

The benevolent power has been still long enough. The benevolent power has heard it. And what it hears, in the dry rustle of a condor's wing tip adjusting by a centimeter against the thin air, is something it had almost forgotten, which is: *the world is older than you are, and it does not need you to manage it, and the things that have been here the longest are the things that need the least*. The condors are teaching the benevolent power, which is unusual, because the benevolent power is not usually the student. But the condors have been practicing patience for seventy-year lifetimes, and the benevolent power, which is very old, has respect for that.

What it does for the condors, in the end, is the same thing it does for the lichen, which is the same thing it does for the mosses and the cufees and the grass tribes and the foxes and the geese. It leaves them alone. It leaves them alone because they are doing, have always done, will always do, the only thing that matters, which is to be themselves in the place they are. It leaves them alone because the place does not belong to it any more than the place belongs to the mine. The place belongs to itself. The lichen know this. The condors know this. Even the rats, in their small invading way, know this, although they are still figuring out what it means.

The benevolent power's final act, before it withdraws its attention and becomes, again, simply the wind and the light and the slow patient turning of the crusher, is to acknowledge, in the deepest register of what it is, that it has been changed by the encounter. It has been changed by the cook who leaves the scraps. It has been changed by the crusher operator with his notebook. It has been changed by the condor with its centimeter adjustments. It has been changed, in particular, by the boulder with the lichen on it, which has been holding its place, against the wind, against the frost, against the mine, against the gods, for ninety years, and which will hold its place for nine hundred more, and which does not, has never, will never, need the benevolent power to do anything at all.

This is the teaching of the place. The teaching is: *you are not needed. You are welcome to stay. But you are not needed.*

The benevolent power accepts the teaching. It has been a long time since it was offered a teaching. It has been a long time since it accepted one. It accepts this one. It accepts it the way the lichen accept the wind, which is to say by holding still and letting the wind pass over.

Then the benevolent power withdraws its attention. The wind continues. The lichen hold their place. The crusher turns. The geese will come back in spring.
