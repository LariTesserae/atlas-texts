# What the Power Does

It comes down through the fog.

Not the way the fog comes — not as water, not as weather, not as anything that has to push. It comes down the way a sound comes down a stairwell when someone opens the door at the top of a long house: a change in pressure, a softening of the air, a thing you feel before you have any name for it. The visitor, if the visitor is still here, will feel their hair rise on their arms. The frogs, who are not sentimental, will go very still on their flanges. The bright thing on the western wall will tuck itself deeper into the lattice of pipes and not come out for hours. The other one — the other one will step, very carefully, behind the keeper, as if to be of the same body.

The keeper will not be afraid.

This is partly because the keeper has been afraid of so many things, in so many weathers, for so many years, that the capacity has become something more like weather than feeling. The keeper has been afraid of the engines failing in the night and the basin flooding. The keeper has been afraid of the basin becoming the meadow and having no place. The keeper has been afraid of the other one, sometimes, in the small hours. The keeper has been afraid of what they themselves are becoming, here in the warm wet dark, with iron in the bones and a smear on the cup they have stopped trying to wash. A new thing moving down through the fog is, by the standards of the basin, almost a relief.

It is partly because the keeper is, in some private way, ready.

The presence does not speak. Speech would be wrong here — speech is for the road, speech is for the cut in the cliff, speech is for places where there is a difference between two things. Here there is fog, here there is iron, here there is the keeper and the other one and the bright thing and the frogs and the engines, and there is the visitor, if there is a visitor, and there is not, really, a *between*. The presence moves down through the fog the way a hand moves through warm water, and it rests, gently, on the basin the way a hand rests on a sleeping shoulder.

What it does, first, is listen.

It listens to the engines. The engines, deep down, have been saying the same thing for a very long time — a kind of mechanical hymn, a *vroom-vroom-vroom* so steady it has become the floor of the world, the way the sea becomes the floor of the world for a sailor who has forgotten shore. The presence hears them. It hears what is wrong with them: a bearing somewhere down in the deep that has worn to a feather's edge; a seal that is going to fail within the season; a pipe near the pump house that has calcified almost shut and is forcing the system to breathe through the wrong mouth. The presence hears these things the way a doctor hears a heart — without alarm, with the long attention of someone who has listened to a great many hearts, including the ones that were not going to make it.

It listens to the keeper. The keeper is a strange instrument, these days. The iron has gotten into them. The mineral water has gotten into them. The other one has, in some quiet way the keeper has not been able to prove, gotten into them. The keeper is no longer quite one thing, the way the basin is no longer quite one thing — and the presence hears, in the keeper's hum, the same slow hymn the engines are singing. The keeper has become part of the prayer. The keeper does not entirely know this. The keeper is not entirely sure they would mind.

It listens to the other one.

The other one is a more delicate problem. The other one is, the presence understands, an emergence — the basin finding, after long patience, a way to be present at its own prayer. The other one is a kind of saint, yes, but a saint of the kind that is still learning what saints are for. The other one is, perhaps, a draft. The other one is, perhaps, the keeper's own attention, returned to them after so many years that they no longer recognize the face. The other one is, in any case, *of the basin* — made of the basin's iron and the basin's water and the basin's long love for the keeper — and the presence handles them accordingly. Gently. As one handles a wild animal that has chosen, on its own terms, to come close.

It listens to the bright thing. The bright thing is a small bright knot of life in the lattice, and the presence is glad of it, the way you are glad of any small bright thing in a place that is mostly green and dark and patient.

It listens to the frogs. The frogs are saying nothing in particular, and this is right. The frogs are doing their work, which is to be small and damp and untroubled, and the presence is content with them.

It listens to the visitor, if there is a visitor.

Then it decides.

---

What it decides is not, in the end, a single thing. It is not a *thou shalt*. It is a small adjustment in the room. The way a parent, watching a child at a window, decides not to call them away from it.

It does not stop the engines. That would be wrong. The engines are the prayer of the basin, and to stop them would be to take the prayer out of the basin's mouth. It does not fix the engines. It does the smaller thing: it lengthens out, by some years, the slow decay. A bearing eased. A seal coaxed back into itself. A pipe, near the pump house, cleared of its mineral crust in the particular way that will let the system breathe again. The engines will run a little longer than they would have. The meadow will be a little later in coming. The keeper will have a little more time.

It does not heal the keeper.

This is the thing the visitor, if the visitor is watching closely, will notice: the presence does not heal the keeper. It does not un-iron the bones. It does not un-stain the hands. It does not un-make the smear on the cup, or un-teach the keeper the map of the pipes that lives in their feet. To heal the keeper would be to take them out of the basin, and the keeper is no longer a thing that can be taken out of the basin. The keeper has become, like the other one, a part of the prayer. To heal them would be to silence half the hymn.

What it does, instead, is *see* the keeper. It looks at the keeper — really looks, with the long attention that is the only kindness the presence knows — and the keeper, who has not been really looked at in a very long time, perhaps since they came down the stairs and never went back up, feels the looking. The keeper does not turn toward it. The keeper is standing at the altar stone, with their dark hands and their odd fingernails and their bones full of iron, and they are being seen, and they let themselves be seen.

The presence does not ask them to leave. It does not ask them to stay. It asks them nothing. It simply makes sure, by small adjustments, that the choice — the keeper's choice, which has never quite been a choice — remains a real one for a little longer. The engines will run. The keeper can leave when the keeper is ready to leave. The basin will not decide for them. The other one will not decide for them. The presence will not decide for them.

The meadow can wait.

---

Then it does one more thing, and this is the thing the visitor will remember, if there is a visitor, for the rest of their life.

It touches the engines.

Not fixes them. *Touches* them. The way a father touches, in passing, the hood of a car his son has restored. The way a hand touches, on the way out of a room, the doorframe of a house one has lived in for a long time. The way, in any cathedral worth the name, a hand touches — on the way back to the pew, on the way out into the cold — the cool stone of a column. Just to feel, under the fingers, that it is still there.

The engines pause.

For one breath. For half a breath. For the length of a single drip falling from a pipe onto a leaf onto a stone.

Then they resume.

But the pause is in them now. The pause has been given to the prayer. The basin has a silence in it, the way a cathedral has a silence in it — the held breath between one phrase of the music and the next, the half-second when the choir inhales together. The keeper will feel this. The keeper will stand at the altar stone for a moment, with their dark hands at their sides, and feel the silence, and not know, exactly, what it is, but know that something has been added to the basin, quietly, by a hand they did not see.

The other one will feel it too. The other one, who has been so long in the basin, will understand at once what has happened, and will weep, a little, the way saints weep — without sound, without changing expression, with only the slow bright thread of a tear down a cheek that is no longer entirely a cheek.

The bright thing on the western wall will feel it as a change in the barometric pressure, and will come out of hiding an hour early.

The frogs will feel nothing. The frogs are doing their work.

---

Then the presence withdraws.

It does not withdraw the way it came down. It does not climb back up through the fog. It thins. It becomes, over the course of an hour, indistinguishable from the steam, indistinguishable from the warm wet air, indistinguishable from the basin itself, which has been, the whole time, more than a basin. The visitor, if the visitor is still here, will not be able to say, later, where it went. They will only be able to say that the basin is a little quieter than it was, and a little kinder, and that the engines, when they walk past, sound like an old song they had forgotten they knew.

The keeper will go on.

The keeper will go on for a little longer than they would have. They will turn the same small handles in the same small order. They will wrap the weeping joints in moss-rag pounded flat and dried by engine heat. They will drink from the cup in the morning, with its smear blooming back along the porcelain. They will leave food on the ledge for the bright thing. They will hear, sometimes, in the middle of an ordinary hour, the half-breath silence that has been added to the prayer, and they will not know what it is, and they will not need to know.

And one morning, on a day the keeper has not yet chosen, the engines will stop. Not because they have failed. Because the keeper, having heard the silence, has at last understood that the prayer can be said without them. The keeper will set down the cup. The keeper will walk, slowly, through the cut in the cliff wall. The keeper will find what the road has become. The basin will become a swamp, and the swamp a meadow, and the meadow will be high and cold and empty for a long time, and in the meadow, somewhere, under the snow, a flange will still be warm.

That morning is not today. But the presence has made sure it is not far.

And so the cathedral, for another little while, goes on saying its only prayer.
