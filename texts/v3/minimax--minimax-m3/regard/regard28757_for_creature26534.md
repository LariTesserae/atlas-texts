The wise one has long known this valley. Not watched it being made — it did not need to be watched — but known it, in the way that a thing is known by something patient and near. It is not a stranger to the place. It has the same slowness the place has. It does not arrive. It has always been somewhere nearby, in the way that the air has always been nearby, in the way that the patience of stone has always been nearby.

What it sees, looking, is this:

The valley is held. The rock has found its fractures and the water has found its weeping and the people have found their number. The buildings have found their plainness. The seasons have found their slow turnover of damp. Everything has come to rest in the shape it was always going to rest in, and the rest is honest, and the rest is not unhappy.

The wise one sees the people walking the polished fitted stones, year after year, in the same silver light. It sees the bodies laid into the seams of the bedrock, and the stones closed over them without a name. It sees the small forge near the hall, where the iron is worked, and the bellows breathing out a heat the wet air swallows quickly. It sees the timbers that have swollen in the wet until the joints are tight as bone. It sees the moss climb a foot or two up the walls and stop, as if it has reached the level where the air is no longer quite wet enough. It sees the small trees with bark the color of wet iron, growing singly, each one having argued its case with the ground.

None of this is wrong. None of it is suffering. It is what the valley is, and the valley is at peace.

But the wise one also sees what the valley does not yet know about itself.

It sees that the valley is young. The ridges are fresh. They have not yet learned softness. They are still slightly too large for themselves, as if they had not had time to settle into their own outlines. A million winters will soften them. The valley has time. It is not in a hurry.

It sees that the people are not unhappy, but they are not, in the fullest sense, alive. They are sufficient. They are held. They have what they need, and they do without what they do not have, because they have never had it. They are not missing the sun, because the sun has not reached them. They are not missing the birds, because the birds have never come. The absence is complete, and so it does not register as absence.

The wise one considers whether to leave the valley as it is.

It would be a kindness, perhaps. The valley is at peace. The peace is not the peace of a thing that has been forced into stillness, but the peace of a thing that has found its stillness on its own. To disturb it would be to take from it what it has made. Even a benevolent power can give harm by giving what was not asked for.
