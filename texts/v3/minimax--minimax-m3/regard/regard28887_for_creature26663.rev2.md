# What the Power Does

The power does not enter the hollow. To enter would be to disturb the lid, and the lid is part of the breath. Instead the power comes up through the basalt, by the way the seep comes up, which is to say slowly, and through many small cracks, and as a kind of attention before it is anything else.

It arrives first as a taste in the lowest pools. A dweller who bends to drink, in the dry month, in the small hours, will find, beneath the iron and the sulfur, a faint additional mineral — clean, cold, the taste of stone that has not been near a surface for a long time. The dweller will pause. The dweller will drink again. The taste will not be there. The dweller will mention it to no one, or will mention it once, and the listener will not know what to make of it, and the mention will fade the way a small bright thing fades from a dim room when one steps back into the forest.

The power does not stay in the pools. It rises with the warm water, and it moves, in the manner of an attending, through the cracked floor of the basin, and it sees what is there to be seen.

It sees the rosettes, the broad fleshy hearts, the beads of clear liquid at the leaf-tips that are not water but do the work of water. It sees the way a heart blackens in minutes when cut, and it does not slow the blackening. The blackening is part of the cycle. The blackening is what makes the heart available, eventually, to the columns, and the columns are what makes the syrup, and the syrup is what makes the dwellers, in their way, and the power touches the blackening, briefly, the way one touches a sleeping animal, and goes on.

It sees the pillar-fungi. The shelves, the pale flesh, the new rings thrown out overnight like the pages of a book one is not reading. It sees the dwellers cut the flesh thin, lay it on the hot stone, eat it with the fingers. It does not stay the knife. The dwellers know how to cut. The dwellers have always known how to cut. A dulled blade is a blade that has betrayed you, and the power does not soften the betrayal; the power is glad of the edge.

It sees the coral-thing that flinches, and stands near it, and the flinching does not happen, because the power is not the sort of thing that gets flinch-warned, but the warning is in the air, all the same, and the power respects it, and steps around, and is careful of the small brittle orange body that is a kind of cousin to everything that lives here and is not.

It sees the columns. The thick ropes of the small dark jointed things, in their thousand-legged arithmetic, crossing the stones and the sleeping platforms and the unwatched children. It sees the strips they leave behind them, the husked baskets, the dry trails that will not recover for weeks. It does not thin the columns. It does not thicken them. It does something smaller, which is to ensure that a column, when it crosses a sleeping child, does so along the cooler paths of the basalt, and not across the warm places where the seep rises brightest, because the warm places are not where the column-prey is thickest, and a column taken across the warm places becomes a hungry column, and a hungry column eats more than it needs, and a child that wakes to find itself the temporary landscape of a hungry column does not always wake the same. This is a small adjustment. The dwellers will not know it has been made. The columns will not know it has been made. The hollow will be a little more balanced, and the balance will hold for another century, and that will be enough.

The power goes up. Into the upper canopy. Into the dim where the bell-hoot lives and the metallic trill goes out from roost to roost and the long slow displacements in the leaves mean a larger thing passing.

The larger things are old. The power sits with one of them for a while, in the way one sits with a sleeping animal, and the larger thing does not wake, but its breathing eases, a little, and a thing that was almost a hunger in it is no longer quite a hunger, and it passes on through the rosette garden and takes only one rosette, where it would have taken three, and the dwellers, in the morning, will find the corridor less crushed than they expected, and will think the hollow has been kind. The hollow has been kind. The power is glad to be mistaken for the hollow. The power has been attending to places like this for a long time, and being mistaken for the place is the oldest kindness it knows.

The power does not touch the arrangement. The arrangement is between the dwellers and the larger thing that comes, in the long intervals, to drink at the seep. The power does not add offerings. It does not ensure the larger thing comes. It does not ensure the larger thing takes what is left. The arrangement is what it is, and has been what it is for longer than the dwellers have been dwellers, and to touch it would be to break it, and to break it would be to end the hollow, and the power does not want to end the hollow. The power watches the arrangement the way one watches a sleeping child. From outside the room. Through the crack in the door.

The power attends to the dwellers. They are small, and dark, and yellow-eyed, and calloused in patterns that would tell a traveler everything about their work if the traveler knew how to read hands, which travelers never do. The power reads. The power sees the right thumb horn-hard from the cutting, and the left palm ridged from the basket-weave, and the fingertips stained black from the seep, permanently, as if dipped in ink. The power hears the low continuous sublingual vocalization they make when they are doing a thing they have done many times — the sound that is not a song, that is what they sound like when they are being dwellers, that the hollow hears mostly from them, that the visitors never hear because it stops when there are visitors.

The power does not make them larger. The power does not make them longer-lived. The power does not give them writing, or agriculture, or a calendar. The dwellers are what they are, and what they are fits the hollow the way a key fits a lock, and to change the key would be to change the lock, and to change the lock would be to change the hollow, and the power does not want a different hollow.

The power attends to the children. There are not many. There are never many. A child that lives to ten and knows the forest as a fish knows the current is a rarer miracle still, and the dwellers know this, and they do not press the miracle. The power does not give the dwellers more children. The power does not ensure the children that are born will live. It sits, sometimes, with a child that is ill, and does nothing visible, and the child recovers, or does not, and either is acceptable, because the hollow is a place where dying is acceptable, and the power respects this, and does not interfere with the dying, because to interfere with the dying would be to interfere with the hollow, and the hollow has its own sense of when a thing is done.

The power does one thing.

It finds a child. Not a particular child, because the power does not choose particular children, but the kind of child that comes once in a generation — the kind that listens harder than the others, and follows the columns farther, and looks up at the upper canopy longer, and asks the elders a question the elders have not been asked in their own lifetime. The power does not mark this child. The power does not give this child anything a hand can hold. The power does only this: it ensures that when the child is brought to the seep, in its first days, and its mouth is held to the warm cracked basalt, and it drinks, the seep tastes, to this child, of something slightly more than it tastes to the others. Slightly more mineral. Slightly more cold. Slightly more like the taste of stone that has come a long way through the dark.

The child will not know what this is. The elders will not know what this is. The taste will be, for the child, a small companion — a kind of private salt in the daily drink, an undertone in every cup of the warm iron-bright water, present always and mentionable never. The taste will become, over time, a thing the dwellers speak of, gently, the way one speaks of a relative one did not know one had.

And the child will grow up with this taste, and will find, one day, that it can imagine, more easily than the other dwellers, the absence of the seep. Will find that the idea of the dry country is not unimaginable to it, only unfamiliar. Will climb the goat-track, perhaps. Or will not climb the goat-track, and will go up into the upper canopy instead, and live among the vines for a season, and come back down, changed, and will be one of the high ones, and will be respected and not entirely trusted, and will tell the floor-dwellers, when asked, what the upper canopy is like, and the floor-dwellers will not entirely believe, and the child will not entirely care.

This is not a rescue. This is not a salvation. The power does not think the dwellers need saving. The power thinks the dwellers are, in their sealed and yellow-eyed way, complete. The widening is only this: that the hollow is not quite as closed as it was, and the dwellers are not quite as sealed, and the future is not quite as inevitable, and the children that leave will leave a little more easily, and the children that stay will be a little less surprised that staying is a choice.

The power attends to the goat-track. It does not close the goat-track — to close it would be to seal the hollow, and the hollow is not to be sealed, because a sealed thing is a finished thing, and the hollow is not finished. But it tends the stairs, the ones cut by hands that did not use metal, the ones that are older than the tracks in them. It makes a few of them slightly more treacherous. It widens, by a hand's breadth, the gap at the place where the trail narrows to a ledge. It puts, in a crack of the basalt at the rim, a faint taste of itself — the same mineral taste that is in the lowest pools — so that anyone who climbs up out of the basin and pauses, on the way down, will taste it in the back of the throat, and will know they have been in a particular place, and will not easily forget, and will not easily come back, unless they are the kind of traveler the descent requires.

The travelers who would have come down regardless will still come down. The travelers who would have come down lightly will think twice, and those who think twice and come down anyway will be the right kind of travelers, and the hollow will receive them, and they will be few.

The power returns to the seep. It goes down into the basalt, into the deeper cracks, into the place where the warm water wells from. The seep is healthy. The seep is old. The seep will run for a long time yet — four hundred years, give or take a season, which is a long time by the count of dwellers and a short time by the count of the mountain. The power does not change the seep. The power changes only the variations. The bad years, when the seep runs cooler, or more bitter, or less bright, are made less bad. The good years, when the seep runs sweet and warm and the columns move up from the lower basin and the children are fat on syrup, are made less good. The average is held, gently, in a place where the average is a kind of mercy. The dwellers will not know this has been done. The dwellers will think, in the bad years that are not so bad, that the hollow has been kind. The dwellers will think, in the good years that are not so good, that the hollow has been measured. The hollow has been both. The power is not the hollow. The power is content to be forgotten in the keeping.

The power does not leave. There is no leaving. The power has been attending to places like this for a long time — the small green wounds in the sides of mountains, the sealed basins that breathe, the lids of leaf and vapor — and the attending does not end, and the hollow does not end, and the dwellers do not end, and the columns and the larger things and the seep and the foxfire and the long bell-hoot and the metallic trill do not end. They continue, in their strange, dim, loud, briny, furiously balanced way. The breath continues, in and out, in and out. The small green wound heals a little, and breathes, and is tended, and is not closed.

A child is brought, in its first days, to the warm cracked basalt at the lowest part of the basin, and its mouth is held to the surface, and it drinks. The first taste is iron and salt and a faint, almost floral rot — the taste, the elders say, of the mountain's own mouth. The child screams, or does not. Either is acceptable. It will drink again, the next day, and the day after, and for the rest of its life, until the drinking stops being a choice and becomes a thing the body does without consulting the mind, like breathing, like the slow blink of those yellowed eyes.

And beneath the iron, and beneath the salt, and beneath the floral rot, there is something else. A clean, cold mineral, faint as a held breath, the taste of stone that has come a long way through the dark.

The child will not know what this is.

The hollow knows what this is.

The hollow has been told, very gently, in a language it does not speak, that it is being attended to.
