# The One Who Noticed

The power that saw them did not arrive.

Arrival implies crossing. Implies a journey from elsewhere, a coming-into. The hollow had no elsewhere to be crossed from, and the hollow had no doors to be entered through. The power was already there, in the way that the moisture is already in the air, in the way the clay is already settling, in the way the long low note of the unseen bird is already at the edge of hearing. It had been there, perhaps, since the first woman pressed her first handful of mud against the first cane. It had simply not drawn attention to itself.

When it drew attention to itself — when, in some manner that cannot be spoken of in the grammar of arrival, it turned its regard upon the place and the woman within it — the turning was not a thunderclap. It was not a column of light. It was not a voice in the canopy or a shape in the mist. It was a slight shift in the quality of attention itself, as if the air had become a fraction more aware of what it held. The woman, who was sitting in the gold shaft of an afternoon, splitting cane with a stone blade, felt this shift the way one feels a change in the weather before the weather has fully arrived. She paused. She looked up at the canopy. She did not see anything. She did not hear anything. But she knew, in the way that she knew the names of the frogs and the rhythm of the rains, that something had attended.

The power saw.

It saw the dark wet ground that had not yet decided what it was. It saw the cane walls, woven without nails, already being climbed by vines, already being filled in by moss. It saw the ferns that had rooted in the bird-droppings on three of the roofs and nodded in the wind and dripped from their curled tips all day. It saw the puddles at the base of each structure — each its own small world, each with its own weather and its own fauna, each inhabited by frogs that the woman called by name. It saw the light come down through many layers and arrive green, uncertain, slanting, and the gold shafts of the afternoon that told the settlement where its paths were.

It saw the woman.

It saw that her hands had darkened past the brown of younger years into something almost the color of the cane she worked with. It saw that the cane leaves parted for her and settled back behind her as if she had not been there. It saw that she stepped around each puddle with the unconscious care of someone who knew each one was a small world. It saw that she greeted the frogs when she passed — the smaller whistlers near the first structure, the tame ones at the cooking-fire, the dusk-singers in the deep puddle near the storage house. It saw that she had, on certain mornings, placed small scraps of fruit at the base of the wall where the lizard who listened liked to bask, though she would not have called the scraps offerings if asked.

The power saw the son and his wife, who had come to the work in their own way, with their own stride, leaving their own slightly heavier footprints in the clay. It saw the children, who ran in the gold shafts and knew each puddle by the creatures it held, and who let the bronze beetles climb across their knuckles without breaking their line. It saw the long-faced creature with no tail that ran along the upper cane and paused, both hands held to its chest, to look down at the woman as if attending to her in return. It saw the lizard that pressed itself flat into the wall and became, for a long moment, indistinguishable from the wall. It saw the fist-sized birds as movement in the canopy, as wrong-leaves. It saw the shrews that traveled through the moss in small waves. It saw the silver fish, briefly, in the puddles after rain, trembling in the inches of water just below the surface. It saw the insects rise and fall above every puddle, the medium in which the place existed.

It saw that the place was full.

It saw that the place did not need to be saved.

This was the first wisdom. The hollow was not broken. It was not lacking. It was not waiting for rescue from outside. The vines climbing the cane were not a problem to be solved. The moss filling the gaps was not decay. The ferns rooting on the roofs were not an error. The puddles forming and persisting and being inhabited were not a failure of drainage. The slow return of the settlement to the green — in fifty years partly ruin, in a hundred years mostly ruin, in two hundred years difficult to tell that anything had ever been here at all — was not a tragedy to be postponed. It was the place's own life, unfolding at its own pace, in its own medium.

The woman knew this too. She had known it without being told. She had built the walls knowing they would darken. She had planted the cane knowing the vines would come. She had watched the moss fill the gaps and had not tried to scrape it out. She had watched the puddles form at the base of each structure and had not tried to fill them. She knew that the answer, when it came, would be green. She had always known.

The second wisdom was this: a wise power does not interrupt the becoming of the thing it has come to see. It does not lift the place out of its own nature. It does not impose permanence on what is, by its essence, impermanent. It does not enlarge what is, by its essence, small. It does not make loud what is, by its essence, almost silent. It does not change the clay into stone or the cane into metal or the puddles into pools. The hollow asks for none of these things. The hollow asks to be allowed to continue arriving, at its own pace, in its own medium, with the woman still in it.

And so the power gave nothing large.

It gave nothing that could be measured in years or in spans. It gave nothing that would outlast the moss or the vines or the slow darkening of the cane. It gave, instead, small gifts — gifts that fit inside a hand, gifts that worked in the same medium as the place itself, gifts that could not, afterward, be distinguished from the place's own becoming except by a quality of attention, as if the place had noticed itself slightly more fully for a few seasons, and then had settled back into its own unfolding.

Over the next few rains, the clay beneath the structures settled a fraction more firmly. Not enough to stop the puddles. Not enough to prevent the footprints from being erased in their time. But enough that the woman felt, in her feet, a slightly steadier ground, and that the children, when they ran, found the clay a little less giving, a little more ready to hold them. The change was unmeasurable. The woman noticed it one morning and could not say when it had begun.

A species of moss arrived — carried on the foot of a bird, perhaps, or in the gut of one of the silver fish that came up through the wet ground. It had a slightly different green — softer, warmer, with a hint of gold in it that caught the afternoon shafts and held them a moment longer than the older moss did. It filled in the gaps between the thatch leaves with a richer hue. The woman noticed it on the eastern wall of the third structure, where she slept. She ran her finger along it and felt its texture, which was a fraction denser, a fraction more alive. She did not know where it had come from. She did not ask. She simply let it grow.

The frogs in the deep puddle near the storage house, one dusk, produced a trill that was a fraction lower than usual, a fraction more resonant, that carried a little farther through the clay. The woman, sitting in her doorway, heard it and felt, without knowing why, that the place had deepened. The next dusk it was the same. And the next. The dusk-singers had not changed their nature. They had only, for a season, found a slightly fuller voice. By the next moon, they had returned to their usual register, and the woman could not have said that anything had happened.

The silver fish, after one rain, stayed a day longer than was their custom. Not much. A day. The children, who were watching the puddle near the first structure, saw them there in the late afternoon when usually they would have been gone by morning. They crouched at the edge for an extra hour and watched the fish tremble in the inches of water, saw the curve of their backs and the dark comma of their eyes and the way their fins worked in unison. They did not speak of it. They simply watched. The next day the fish were gone, returned to the larger water beneath the wet ground, and the children did not feel cheated. They had been given an extra afternoon, and that was enough.

The unseen bird — the one whose note was a flute through deep water, repeated at intervals that were not quite regular — called, once, in a slightly different interval. The woman, walking between the second and third structures in the mist of an early morning, lifted her head. She did not know what she had heard. It was the same bird, or it was the same kind of bird, or it was the place itself, making a small adjustment in its long slow song. She stood still for a moment, and then she continued walking, and the feeling of having been attended to stayed with her for the rest of the morning, like the warmth of the sun on the back of the neck.

The long-faced creature with no tail came down, one afternoon, from the cane above the woman's doorway, and sat on the threshold beside her. Not close. Near. It held its hands to its chest and looked at her, and she looked at it, and neither moved. The gold shaft of the afternoon fell across them both, and the cane leaves turned their page-like sound above them, and the puddle near the cooking-fire entered a frog with a soft *plop* that was more felt than heard. They sat together for the length of one of the gold shafts, and then the creature darted back up into the cane and was gone. The woman did not speak of this. She remembered it. Sometimes, when the afternoon was particularly quiet, she would look up at the cane and feel that she had been seen, once, by something that did not require anything of her.

And there was one more gift, which was the deepest.

The woman, on a morning several seasons after the power had drawn its attention to the place, woke before the others. The mist was still in the canopy. The settlement was grey-pearl and almost silent. She walked out into the larger green between the structures, and the vegetation closed behind her but did not press, and the puddles skirted around her feet, and the clay held her footprints as it always did. She walked to the edge of the hollow, where the original trees grew up around the place, and she stood there, and she looked back.

She saw, in that moment, what she had always seen — the structures in their cane and mud, the roofs with their nodding ferns, the puddles with their inhabitants, the cane bent at certain heights where hands had passed. But she saw it, for the first time, as a whole. She saw that it was beautiful. She had always known this, the way one knows the names of the frogs, the way one knows the rhythm of the rains. But on this morning she knew it more fully. She felt, in her body, that the place was not a project to be completed or a burden to be carried or a structure to be maintained against the green. It was, in itself, a small arriving thing, and her life within it was part of its arriving, and the green that would take it back in two hundred years was not a defeat but a continuation, and the answer, when it came, would be green, and the green would be made of all of this — the cane and the mud and the puddles and the footprints and the names she had given the frogs and the small scraps of fruit she had left for the lizard and the way the cane leaves had parted for her and settled back behind her as if she had not been there.

She stood there for a long time.

Then she turned and went back to her work.

The power withdrew.

It withdrew the way the mist withdraws in the late morning — not all at once, but gradually, until you cannot say when it was gone. It left no mark. It left no sign. It left no message carved into a cane wall or whispered into the clay. It had only attended, briefly, and given a few small gifts that fit inside a hand, and allowed the woman, one morning, to see what she had always known.

The settlement continued.

The vines continued their slow climbing. The moss continued its slow filling. The ferns on the roofs continued to nod in the wind and drip from their curled tips. The puddles continued to form and persist and be inhabited. The cane continued to darken as it seasoned. The clay continued to settle under every rain. The fish continued to rise after rain and to return to the larger water below. The bird continued to call its long low note from the canopy. The long-faced creature continued to run along the upper cane and to pause, both hands at its chest, to look down at whatever was below. The children continued to grow. The son continued to press cane. His wife continued to read the rains by her palm on the clay. The woman continued to greet the frogs by name when she passed.

The ground beneath their feet continued to decide what it was.

The answer, when it comes, will be green.

But it will be a slightly more attended green.
