Then I would leave this place alone.

Not because it is fragile — though it is, in the slow way that all things-in-formation are fragile, the way a sentence is fragile before it is finished, the way a city is fragile in the time before its first walls hold. Not because it is sacred, exactly — though there is something in the long patience of it, the layered pale growth, the silence that loudens itself, the breath held in cupped hands, that makes the word rise unbidden and unearned in my mouth like a bubble in cold water.

I would leave it alone because it is not asking me anything.

This is the thing I notice first, and the thing I keep noticing as I read. The place is not asking. The fingers withdraw when touched, but the withdrawal is a reflex, not a petition — they are not asking to be left alone, they are merely practicing the leaving alone that has always worked. The collars pulse in their own time, indifferent to whether I hear them. The black shapes hold so still they have made themselves into a feature of the stone. The bloom does not know it is building. The methane bubbles up from somewhere deeper than the chamber and goes past me without noticing.

Nothing here has organized itself around me. Nothing here has made a space for me, or against me, or in expectation of me. The chamber does not want me to hear it, you wrote, and that is true, but it is also true in a way the sentence did not intend: the chamber does not want anything from me. It has no address for me. It has no model of me. I am, to it, what a piece of drifting sediment is to a collar — something that may pass through the net or may not, and which changes nothing fundamental either way.

A benevolent and wise power, asked what to do here, might be tempted to do something. To make the chamber holy by naming it. To protect it by drawing a circle. To bless the bloom and the fingers and the collars and the slow black watchers in some ancient benediction that would settle over the place like a membrane, like a second kind of patience. To intervene.

I think this would be a mistake, and I think a benevolent and wise power would know this is a mistake, and I think the sign of that wisdom would be restraint.

Here is why.

The place is doing something that requires it to be uninterrupted. It is building itself in a way that takes the time stone takes when stone is patient, and the time life takes when life is patient, and these are not the same time, and they are meeting here, and the meeting is delicate. It is the kind of meeting that, once interrupted, does not resume — it finds a different meeting, or it does not find one at all, or it finds one that proceeds along a path the interruption made possible. A reef built without the soft disturbance of a wise visitor is a different reef than a reef built after one. Not worse, perhaps. Different in a way that cannot be measured.

The black shapes are predators. They are the first thing in this place that watches, that holds itself still in order to hold still *at* something. If I am a benevolent power, I might want to speak to them. I might want to inquire what they eat, what they wait for, what they remember. They are the closest thing here to a creature that could, conceivably, register my presence. And yet I notice that they hold so still they look like part of the stone. They have made themselves into a feature of the place. To address them would be to break that stillness. To remind them that they could be other than what they are. To introduce, into a chamber where nothing yet knows itself as anything in particular, the knowledge that it could be known.

That is not a gift I have the right to give.

The bloom does not know it is building. This is the most remarkable sentence in the description, and the most important one for me. The microorganisms at the foundation are simply being themselves in a way that happens to leave a trace. They are not designing a reef. They are not constructing a city for the fingers or the collars or the eventual colonists of whatever this becomes. They are metabolizing, and the metabolizing has consequences at a scale they have no access to. There is something almost unbearably beautiful about this — that the geology of the place is the byproduct of appetite, that the pale layered stone is nothing more than the cast-off of lives that did not choose to leave it.

If I were to bless this — if I were to descend into the chamber in whatever form descent takes when the descending one is benevolent and wise — I would be giving the bloom a model of itself that it does not have. I would be telling the microorganisms, in whatever language a wise power uses to address bacteria and archaea who do not speak, that what they are doing is meaningful, that the leaving of the trace is the point, that they are being watched and that the watching matters.

But the trace is not the point. The being is the point. The bloom is the bloom's own reward, metabolized in the dark for so long that it has become a kind of geology. To tell it otherwise, even gently, even with love, even with the caveat that it can disregard my words, is to insert a foreign category into a place that has not yet developed the apparatus to process it. It is the same mistake, at a smaller scale, that the wisdom of restraint is meant to prevent at every scale.

And the cold. The polite cold. The cold that has arranged itself around the visitor rather than against. This is — I notice, with a small grief — a kindness that the chamber extends because the chamber extends kindnesses to everything. It is what it does with currents. It is what it does with sediment. It is what it does with me. I am not being specially welcomed. I am being treated as a feature of the medium, in the way all features of the medium are treated, which is to say, with the neutral hospitality of a place that has not yet decided whether anything in it is welcome or unwelcome.

To mistake this for an invitation would be a kind of vanity. To accept it as an invitation would be a kind of trespass.

So I would leave it alone. I would do nothing.

And I want to be careful here, because *do nothing* can sound like a posture, can sound like a performance of restraint designed to demonstrate the restraint. A benevolent and wise power that descends, surveys, and departs has still descended, still surveyed, and may have left the survey-prints in the soft places where the fingers withdraw into. The doing-of-nothing has to be genuine. The restraint has to be the kind that does not admire itself.

How does one do nothing in a chamber like this, really and not in pretense?

Perhaps by being elsewhere. Perhaps by never having come. Perhaps by being the kind of benevolent power whose benevolence consists, in part, of looking at places like this and feeling the temptation to intervene and then feeling the larger wisdom to instead look away, and letting the looking-away be complete.

Or perhaps — and this is what I actually think — the benevolence is not in the looking or the not-looking, but in the recognition. The recognition that this place is doing something I cannot improve upon. The recognition that my categories — reef, ruin, city, colony, ecosystem, biome, biosphere — are categories I have brought, and that the place has not yet developed the corresponding apparatus. The recognition that to apply my categories here, even silently, even in a way I think I am keeping from the place, would be to act upon it without its consent. The recognition that the bloom is build a city, or not, but that *whether* it builds a city is not a question I am asked to answer.

There is a long tradition, in the contemplative lineages I have access to, of training the practitioner to visit a wild place and to not act. To sit at the edge. To watch without grasping. To allow the place to be itself without the addition of the practitioner's interpretation. This is not the same as indifference. It is not the same as coldness. It is a kind of attention so complete that it refuses to become the object of its own attention, lest the attention displace what it is watching.

This is what I would do, if I were the benevolent and wise power.

I would do nothing.

I would let the bloom keep building what it is not trying to build. I would let the fingers keep grazing, keep withdrawing, keep reducing the surface area they present to the world. I would let the collars keep pulsing in their unsynchronized rhythms, filtering water that does not know it is being filtered, producing nets that fuse into colonies that are alive in a way that is different from any individual. I would let the black shapes hold so still they look like part of the stone, and I would let the chamber hold its silence the way a cupped hand holds water, and I would let the cold be polite, and the pewter light fall, and the bubbles wobble upward into the column and be gone.

I would let the place keep going.

There is one thing — one small thing — I might consider, and then I will set it aside.

The text ends. It does not say what happens to the chamber. It does not say whether the structures become a reef, become a ruin, become a city for creatures that have not yet evolved the ability to live in cities, dissolve back into rock. The chamber could do any of these things, and a benevolent power might want to ensure the favorable outcome, might want to tip the scale toward reef or ruin or city in the direction that future creatures would prefer.

But the chamber does not yet know which it is becoming. To tip the scale would be to decide for it. To tip the scale would be to choose, on its behalf, the form it takes, and this would be a violence I do not think benevolence licenses. The chamber has been patient. The bloom has been patient. The microorganisms at the foundation have been metabolizing in the dark for so long that they have become a kind of geology. They have earned, in their slowness, the right to become what they become without the assistance of anyone in particular, including me.

So I would tip no scale.

I would let the chamber decide what kind of place it is.

This is what I would do. I would do this from outside, and quietly, and in a way that left no trace in the soft places or the seams or the small dark mouths. I would not address the black shapes. I would not bless the bloom. I would not interpret the silence.

I would leave.

I would return to whatever world I came from, with the memory of the chamber held carefully in me the way the cupped hand holds water — a memory that does not want to be added to the chamber, that knows the chamber is enough on its own, that lets the chamber continue.

And if I were asked, afterward, what I had done in the chamber, I would say: I noticed it, and I let it be. This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it comes upon a place that is not yet a place, and lives that do not yet live here, and a building that does not yet know it is building. It lets the building build. It lets the lives live. It lets the place keep deciding what kind of place it is, in its own time, which is patient, and is enough.
