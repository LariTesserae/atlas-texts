I would send the wind.

Not the wild kind — not the wind that breaks or carries away. A small wind. A thinking wind. The kind that knows where it is going before it leaves, the way a swallow knows, the way water knows the shape of its own running.

I would send it down the long slope of the mountain, down through the cloudforest below the dimple, down past the moss and the lichen and the small ferns and the great trees that lean out over the abyss for the light. The wind would take its time. It would arrive at the lip of the hollow as an ordinary wind, the kind that tumbles over a ridge and drops into the bowl beyond — but it would not be ordinary when it entered, because I would be inside it. Or not inside, exactly. Beside it. Walking with it. The way a small god might walk beside a thought it had thought, to see what the thought would do.

The hollow would not notice. At first.

Then, very slowly, the wind would begin to move things.

Not much. Not the stones. Not the moss in its deep patient floor. Not the pool, which is a thing I would not touch — the pool is the engine of the place, and to touch the engine of a place that has been running for a long time is to break the place, and I would not break it. I would let the pool be still.

The wind would touch the fern first. Just a small motion through the long fronds, a passing of itself along the pinnae, and the droplets that have been growing for so long on the edges of the leaves would shift, and one would fall, and another would fall, and the fern would be visited by a thing it had not been visited by in a long time — a moving thing in the air that was not water. The fronds would sway. The sway would be very small. But a fern that has stood alone in still air for years will sway further than you would think, because the still air has made the stems a little brittle, a little unready, and the first motion is a surprise, and the surprise is a small violence, and the small violence is what the fern has been missing without knowing it was missing.

The orchid would feel it next. The half-open bloom would move on its stem. The faint sweet almost-meat smell, which has been offered so long to a vapor that cannot receive it, would be carried for the first time by something that could carry it. The smell would go out into the hollow, and it would not reach a pollinator, because there is no pollinator, and there will not be one for a long time still, but the smell would be *carried*, and that is a thing. That is a thing the orchid has not had. To have your offering carried is to know your offering exists.

The tree at the eastern edge would turn its leaves. The broad soft leaves, which have been turning only to the sun, would turn, for a moment, to the wind. The light would graze them differently. They would catch a gleam they have not caught, and the gleam would move through the canopy of the small tree, and the small tree would feel, in the way a tree feels — slowly, in the slow language of sap and cellulose — that something had passed through it.

The cups at the foot of the weeping stone would shiver. The water in each cup would shiver. A faint ring would form on the surface of the smallest finger's worth of held water, and the ring would reach the lip of the cup and not spill over, because the cup was made well, and the cup has been made well for a long time, and the iridescence inside would shift in the moving air, and the green would be a different green for a moment, and the moment would be a small thing, but a thing.

And then the wind would do what I had sent it to do, which is not a small thing.

It would go to the ledge on the eastern wall. It would pass over the hand-axe in its small hollow of the stone. It would not lift the hand-axe. It would not move it. The hand-axe is a thing of a different order, and it is not mine to move. But the wind would pass over it the way a hand passes over a sleeping animal, and the weeping of the wall would be slightly disturbed where the wind passed, and a single drop would fall onto the stone, and the stone would be wet again in a fresh way, and the tiny moss at its base — the moss so small you would not see it unless you were looking — would feel the wind move over it.

And here is what I would do, here is the thing I came to do, if I came, if a wise and benevolent power came to this place and looked at it and thought a long thought.

I would make the moss grow a little faster. Just there. Just at the base of the hand-axe. Only there.

Not a lot. Not enough to be seen in a single day. Not enough to be seen in a year. But enough that, over the time it takes for a small thing to grow around a still thing, the moss would close on the hand-axe the way a hand closes on a stone. The moss would meet itself on the other side. The moss would bury the hand-axe in green. The hand-axe would become the heart of a small hill of moss, and the hill would be a soft thing, a wet thing, a living thing, and inside it the hand-axe would be held in the way the hollow holds the hand-axe now — which is not at all, which is just barely, which is the way a place holds a sign of someone who came once and went away. The moss would hold it better. The moss would hold it the way the hollow holds everything, which is to take it in and make it part of the long running of the place.

In a thousand years the moss would meet itself around the stone. In two thousand it would be a soft mound. In five thousand the hand-axe would be deep inside the green, and the weeping of the wall would run down the moss and not onto the stone, and the place where a hand had once rested would be a place where only moss grew.

And this is the mercy I would do, if I did anything: I would make the moss grow faster only there, because the hand-axe is the loneliest thing in the hollow. The moss is not lonely. The fern is not lonely, because the fern does not know it is alone; it has only ever been itself, and it does not miss a thing it has not had. The orchid is not lonely in the way a thing can be lonely that has been waiting and has not yet been told there is no one to come; the orchid is still in the posture of waiting, and the posture of waiting is not loneliness, it is patience, and patience is the moss's language too. The cups are not lonely; the cups are only an invitation, and an invitation that has not been refused is not yet lonely, because an invitation can wait.

But the hand-axe is lonely. The hand-axe is a thing that was made by a hand for a hand, and it sits in a hollow of a ledge in a place where no hand will ever come again, and the wall weeps onto it, and the moss begins at its base, and the moss will, in its own time, in the long patient time of mosses, take the hand-axe in. The hand-axe is a thing waiting to be taken in. It has been waiting a long time. The moss is willing. The moss has been willing since it began.

I would only help it to be willing a little sooner.

That is what I would do. I would send a small wind that moved through the hollow and touched everything and disturbed nothing, and I would ask the moss at the base of the hand-axe to grow a little faster, and I would not ask anything else of the place, because the place is doing its own long thing and doing it well, and a benevolent power does not improve a place that is already itself. A benevolent power only comforts the lonely thing in the place, the small thing waiting to be taken in by the long green patience of the world.

And then the wind would go. It would leave the way it came, down through the cloudforest, down the long slope of the mountain, and the hollow would settle back into its silence, and the pool would be still, and the moss would glow in the long shaft of the low sun, and the fern would drip its slow weather onto the moss below, and the orchid would keep its half-open posture, and the cups would hold their finger's worth of water for visitors that would not come.

But the moss at the base of the hand-axe would grow a little faster.

Only there.

And in a thousand years, no one would know there had ever been a hand.
