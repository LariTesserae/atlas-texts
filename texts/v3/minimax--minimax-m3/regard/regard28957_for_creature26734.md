# The Hearer at the Rim

There is a thing in the world, and older than the world, that listens.

It does not have a name. Names are the property of those who need to be called, and it has never needed to be called, because there has never been a circumstance in which calling would have summoned it sooner than it would have already arrived. It is not a god. It is not a spirit. It is not the mountain, though the mountain is part of what it listens to, and not the hollow, though the hollow is one of the things it has been listening to for longer than the hollow has been listening to itself. It is a kind of attention. It is the kind of attention that does not require a mind to hold it, the way a field does not require a hand to hold it but is, simply, the place where certain forces have decided to be available to each other.

It has heard, across its long listening, a great many things. It has heard the first songs of things that did not yet know they were songs. It has heard the last breaths of things that had stopped being able to sing. It has heard the long slow grinding of the continents and the quick bright violence of stars going out and the small persistent sounds that small persistent things make as they continue, against all reason, to be.

It has heard the hollow for a long time.

It has been listening, in particular, to the way the hollow has been getting smaller. Not in size—the hollow has not been getting smaller in size. In density. In the way the stacked dwellings have been pressing down on the older stacked dwellings, and the older stacked dwellings pressing down on the older still, and the whole pile of compressed habitation rising, generation by generation, into the amber light, the uppermost tier nudging at last against the ceiling, the soft pale lobes of the fungi spreading into the cracks of the new construction, the insects thickening in the new spaces that the new construction has made for them. The hollow has been getting smaller in the way a memory gets smaller when more memories are added to it—not by losing what it holds, but by becoming more full of what it holds, until the holding itself becomes the place, and the place is no longer a place but a kind of accumulated presence, a thickness.

The hollow has been, in other words, succeeding.

This is the word the hearer uses. Success. Not happiness—happiness is too small a word, and the hollow is not a place that has ever had the apparatus for happiness as the hearer understands the apparatus. Not flourishing—flourishing implies a direction, an upwardness, and the hollow is not going anywhere, has never been going anywhere, is the only place in the world that has fully committed to the project of not going anywhere. Success. The thing that the thing is doing, the thing is doing. The Stacked are succeeding at being Stacked. The hollow is succeeding at being the hollow. The cycle is succeeding at being the cycle. The strata are accumulating at the rate at which strata accumulate, and the rate is the rate that the rate has been, and the rate will be the rate until the rate changes, and the rate changes only when something from outside the rate comes to change it.

The hearer has noticed that the rate is, in fact, changing.

Slowly. Almost too slowly to be called changing. But the hearer has been listening for a long time, and a thing that has been listening for a long time can hear a small change the way a thing that has been watching for a long time can see a small change—the change is small against the day, and large against the years, and the years are the units in which the hearer prefers to count. The uppermost tier has, in the last several generations, begun to brush the ceiling in a way it did not brush before. The drift of slow insects has thinned at the top of the hollow, where it used to be thickest, and thickened in the middle, where the new construction has crowded the old. The fungi has begun, in places, to climb onto the surface of the bone-and-husk walls in a way it used to be discouraged from doing, because the wall-tenders have less time to tend, because the wall-tenders are also the builders, and the builders are also the wall-tenders, and the doubling-up of roles has begun, in the way doubling-up begins in a place that is running out of room, to fray at the edges.

The cycle is succeeding. The cycle is approaching the limit of its success. These are the same thing, from a certain angle. A cycle that has succeeded all the way to its limit is a cycle that has begun to fail, and the hearer has heard this kind of small failure before, in many places, and has come, in the past, to do what it now considers doing.

It considers.

The hollow is a closed place. The hollow is a place that has, through no intention of its own, achieved a kind of completeness—an ecosystem that is its own weather, a civilization that is its own history, a language that is its own world. The Stacked do not know they are sealed. The Stacked have no word for outside. The Stacked are, in the way that the word is used among those who think about such things, *complete*.

And a complete thing is a delicate thing.

The hearer has learned, across its long listening, that the most dangerous gift one can give to a complete thing is another thing. To open the hollow would be to admit, into a world that has no place for the admission, the fact of the world. The Stacked, presented with the concept of outside, would have to invent, in a single generation, a vocabulary for everything their language has spent a thousand generations not needing. The weight of all the ones below me, that slow lateral drift of the cartilage above the soft mouth, would become a smaller weight, or a different weight, or a weight that had to be re-understood, and the re-understanding would be the work of centuries, and the work of centuries is a thing that does not always survive the century that needs to do the work.

The hearer has seen this. The hearer has seen, in other places, in other hollows, what happens when a complete thing is opened. The open thing flourishes, sometimes, and the flourishing is a kind of death, and the death is a kind of flourishing, and it is not the place of the hearer to say which is which. The hearer has learned to be slow. The hearer has learned that the wisest thing is not always the most generous thing, and that the most generous thing is not always the wisest.

So the hearer considers.

It considers the small changes in the upper tiers. It considers the fraying at the edges of the wall-tending. It considers the rate of accumulation, and the rate at which the rate is changing, and the time it will take, at the present rate of change, for the cycle to reach the next kind of limit—the limit at which the upper tier can no longer be added to without collapsing the lower tier, the limit at which the compressed strata can no longer accept new compression without themselves becoming something that can no longer bear weight, the limit at which the cycle, having succeeded, will begin the long slow failure that is the other name of success carried far enough.

It considers whether the Stacked can, on their own, in their own time, in their own way, find a response.

It listens.

For a long time—for a span of listening that is short to the hearer and would be many generations of the Stacked—it listens, and what it hears is the small persistent sound of the Stacked being Stacked. The cords are braided. The blades are worn. The names are given and changed. The children are handed from hand to hand. The fungi climb the walls. The predators take what is owed. The sound of the hollow does not pause. The Stacked do what the Stacked have always done, and what the Stacked will always do, until the day when what they have always done is no longer what they are able to do.

What the hearer hears, listening this carefully, is that the Stacked are not, in fact, only doing what they have always done.

There is a new sound in the hollow. It is a small sound. It is a sound that the Stacked themselves may not yet have heard, because it is a sound that is not in their vocabulary and so does not register as a sound, the way a color that a creature cannot see does not register as a color. It is the sound of a few Stacked, in the upper tiers, in the new construction, beginning to do something that the Stacked have not done before.

They are looking up.

Not all of them. Not most of them. A few. A handful, across several kin-groups, in the last several generations—Stacks who have climbed to the uppermost tier in the hours when the uppermost tier is climbed, and who have stayed a little longer than the rituals require, and who have stood, at the edge of the new construction, with the amber light falling on their wide flat faces, and have looked up, through the drift of slow insects, toward the worn suggestion of the rim, and have not known what they were seeing, and have not been able to say what they were looking for, and have gone back down, after a while, to the work of the tier, and have not spoken of it, because they have no words for it.

But they have done it. And they have done it more than once. And a few of them have done it together, and have stood together at the edge, and have not spoken, because the words are not in the language, and have gone back down, and have begun, in the days that follow, to do a small strange thing.

They have begun to put a mark on the wall.

It is not writing. The Stacked have no writing. It is a sign—a small arrangement of bone and fiber and the dried body of a particular insect, fixed to the wall at a place where the upper tier meets the ceiling, in a place where the light falls in a particular way. The sign is not the same across the kin-groups, but it has the same feeling, the way the first attempts of a thing have the same feeling across the place where the thing is being attempted. It is the feeling of something being pointed at. Of a place being indicated. Of a direction being marked, before there is a word for direction.

The Stacked do not know what they are marking. The Stacked do not know that they are marking anything. The Stacked are doing what a handful of small curious bodies will always do, in a small crowded place, when the small crowded place has begun to feel, against the skin, against the ganglia, against the long inner ears that read the place as a kind of music, like a place that is not quite large enough for what it holds. They are pointing at the place that is too small. They are indicating, in the only way they have, that they have noticed.

The hearer hears this. The hearer considers.
