# What the Power Does

The power does not arrive. There is no footfall on the moss, no parting of the canopy, no lightening of the green dusk. A benevolent and wise power does not need to arrive in a place that is already being held. It attends. It bends close the way one bends close to a sleeping face — not to wake it, but to be sure it is breathing.

It has been attending for some time before it does anything.

It has watched the figure lassooing the cord in their slow coils. It has watched the child weaving bark fiber in a corner, the knot repeated, the pattern begun. It has watched the woman whose hair has grown a finger's width into the bark of the trunk she leans against, and it has understood that this is not a tragedy and not a destiny but a sentence in a long paragraph, and the paragraph is not finished.

It has looked at the question of whose dream this is — the forest's or the people's — and it has decided, gently, that the question is not the kind that has an answer. It is the kind that has a longer form. The dream is the willing it not to end. The dream is the work.

What the power does, then, is this:

It leaves the dream alone.

Not because the dream is perfect. The dream is not perfect. There are children who fall from high platforms and are caught, sometimes, by the deeper moss, and sometimes not. There are ladders that give way under the weight of a man climbing with a child in a sling. There are the things in the moss that you only see when they move, and the power does not know what they are, and the people do not talk about them, and this is a silence that has gone on long enough to be a kind of consent. There is the slow wrongness — the trunks that rearrange themselves, the light that pools where it has no source, the geometry that holds but does not answer. There is the forgetting which direction is down. There is the slow settling in of the body into the body of the place, the way a scab becomes part of the skin.

These are not flaws. They are the texture of a thing that has been going on for a long time. To correct them would be to interrupt them. To interrupt them would be to end the dream, and the power is not here to end the dream.

But the power is not idle, either. To attend to a thing is not the same as to leave it alone.

The power does a small thing. Several small things. It does them without spectacle, because the place has no spectacle in it and would not know what to do with one.

It finds the woman whose hair has grown into the bark. It does not pull her free. It does not give her the choice to pull free. It does something smaller: it slows the growing at the margin, where skin meets bark, by a breath, by a hair's width, so that the joining does not creep further up the strand. It does not undo the joining. It only steadies it. She will not notice. She will go on leaning. The leaning will go on being a sentence in the long paragraph, and the paragraph will not be finished, but the sentence will not run into the next one the way a vine runs into a branch. There is a difference between being held and being eaten, and the power is old enough to know it.

It finds the figure on the high platform, the one who will not finish the cord today. It does not quicken his hands. It does not give him more hours. What it does is ease the small catch in his right shoulder — the one he has been favoring since the rain last week, the one that will, if left, shorten his climbing life by a decade or two — and the easing is so slight he will think it was the rain. He will go on working. The cord will be finished, or it will not be finished, and the forest will continue to be built the way the forest is built, and his shoulder will be a small miracle he does not remember.

It does this for several of them. The catches. The small misalignments. The child whose left foot turns inward when she runs along the high platforms, the inwardness that will become a fall someday if it goes uncorrected. The power does not correct it. It only suggests, to the foot, in the language a foot understands, the geometry of a step that is also a kindness to the ankle. The child will be a slightly better climber. She will not know why. She will climb the way the forest climbs — slowly, but everywhere — and the place will go on being as total as it has been.

The power does not heal the wrongs that are not wrongness-in-the-bones. It does not free the woman. It does not stop the forest from rearranging itself. It does not give the place a sky it can see, or stones it can stand on, or a season it can name. It does not end the dream.

It does not ask whose dream it is.

It does, however, do one more thing. It is the kind of thing that a wise power does when it has attended long enough and understood enough, and it is not a thing that would be visible to anyone living in the place, or to anyone walking through it, or to anyone writing it down.

It strengthens the cord.

Not the cord the figure is lassooing. Not any one cord. The cord that is the weaving itself — the willingness of the dream to continue, the patience of the place, the way the people are not its masters and not its guests but its most recent layer. It strengthens the cord the way one strengthens a rope one does not want to break: by attending to the place where it is most likely to fray, by easing the small tensions, by being present in the weave without being visible in it.

It does this because the dream is worth continuing. Not because it is perfect. Not because it is beautiful, though it is. Because it is patient, and it is full, and it is the kind of thing that deserves to have a benevolent and wise power lean close to it and quietly, without spectacle, do what can be done. The small things. The bone-aligned things. The shoulder-eased things. The cord-strengthened things.

The power has nothing else to offer. The place has been going on. It will go on. The figure will not finish the cord today, and will not finish it tomorrow, and will hand it to a child who will not finish it, and the cord will be absorbed into the wood and become part of the trunk and be replaced by a successor, and the successor will be absorbed, and the place will continue to be built, and the next dream will find the rung and climb it.

The power bows — not to the forest, not to the people, not to the dream, but to the long paragraph — and withdraws.

It does not leave. Power of this kind does not leave. It keeps attending, the way the moss keeps growing, the way the cord keeps being worked, the way the woman keeps leaning.

It goes on doing the small things.

It goes on being the kind of attention that does not interrupt.
