# What the Watcher Did

The power that noticed the Stone Confluence at the Tindal Pass had been a long time coming, and had come a long way to do it.

It was not a god, exactly. Gods are local, mostly. Gods are tied to their weather and their sacrifices and the long memories of their priests, and they have opinions, and opinions are a kind of blindness. This was something older than that, or wider than that, or quieter than that — a thing that had been paying attention to fault-line towns for a very long time, the way a gardener pays attention to a row of slow fruit. It had no name that any tongue now living could pronounce, though the language of the old carvers of the cisterns had, perhaps, almost had a word for it. The word would have meant something like *the patience of the place where two things have agreed to slide past each other*.

It came to the Tindal Pass on an evening when the crack was going to breathe, though it did not know this yet. It arrived at the lip of the scree, where the path is only a suggestion, and it stood there in the thin air and the wet light, and it looked down.

It saw, first, the town.

It saw the dark stone houses leaning into one another like tired people. It saw the long streaks of water on the walls, where the rain and the mist and the spring-water had made one event of the weather. It saw the moss, that almost-black moss, breathing its small breath on the shaded stone. It saw the stepping stones in the spine-water, nine of them, worn smooth by the small competent feet of a people who knew exactly where to step. It saw, in the middle distance, the dark shape of the peat-house, sweating in the evening, and the column of smoke from its chimney that the wind could not quite disperse.

It saw, with the second sight such powers have, what was inside the houses. It saw a woman winding dyed wool into a ball for the morning's trading. It saw a man mending a stone bracket with the pale paste that would not last, because he was willing to do work that would not last, because his grandmother had been willing. It saw a child asleep on a heated stone, with one hand curled around the ear of a white grazer that had come in from the cold and was standing, enormous and patient, in the doorway, breathing.

It saw the springs.

It saw the Coldwell, the silver-white rope falling six meters into its carved basin, hissing its low hiss, and it saw, in the deep of the basin, the eel-things moving in their slow undulation, their white filaments drifting in the mineral water. It saw the Hollowspring rising in its trembling dome in the market square, and it saw — and this the power noted with a small, private satisfaction — that there was a child standing in the dome. Not in it; *on* it. The child was balanced on the curve of the rising water, legs wide, arms out, laughing with her mouth open, and the water was holding her the way a hand holds a leaf, and she was not afraid, because she had not yet learned that fear was a thing one owed to the place. The power watched her for a long time.

It saw the spine-water running east to west, clear over its every stone, and it saw, beneath the clear water, the fine bright line of the fault, and it felt, along that line, the slow pressure that was building toward the evening's breath.

It saw the herders coming down from the high scree with their loaded sledges. It saw the dyers in the peat-house stirring a vat that smelled of iron and tannin. It saw the bell in the cracked building near the upper gate, waiting for its hour. It saw the wool hung out in the last of the sun — and stopped, and saw that there was no wool hung out, because the wind had come up that afternoon, and the wool had been taken into the air and was not coming back.

It saw all of this at once, which is how such a power sees. It saw the town whole. It saw that the town was not only the people, and not only the springs, and not only the crack, but the agreement between all of these, and the agreement between the agreement and the mountain, and the agreement between the mountain and the weather, and the weather and the light, and the light on the wet walls, and the wet walls in the eyes of every person who had ever stood at the lip of the scree and looked down and felt, before they understood anything else, that this place was *in balance*.

The balance was delicate. The power knew this. It knew that the town had settled into its cracks the way moss settles into a crack, by being willing to be small, and that the people were few, and that they traded their dyed wool for grain and salt on foot over the lower pass, and that in a bad winter the snow came up to the lintels and the grazers stood in the doorways, and that the crack breathed, and that the breathing moved things by a millimeter, and that a door which had opened inward for a hundred years might, on a given evening, open outward, and that this was not a danger but a kind of speech, and that the people were listening.

The power had been watching places like this for a very long time. It knew that fault-line towns were rare, and precious, and that they did not last. It knew that the people who came to them were usually people from other faults, looking for kin, and that they were usually few, and that they were usually very competent, and that they usually lasted two or three hundred years and then were gone — worn out, married away, snowed under, broken by a winter that did not end, or simply *finished*, the way a small competent people can finish, having said what they came to say. It knew that the towns outlasted the people. It knew that the springs outlasted the towns. It knew that the crack outlasted the springs.

It knew, also, that there were towns like this where the people had *not* settled in the right way. Where they had tried to make the crack do what they wanted. Where they had mortared over what should not be mortared, and diverted what should not be diverted, and prayed to what should not be prayed to. Those towns lasted less long. The crack closed, or opened too wide. The springs ran bitter. The people left, or did not leave, which was worse. The power had watched those towns too, and it had not been able to help them, because to help a town that has misunderstood itself is to misunderstand it further.

This town had not misunderstood itself. The power felt this as a kind of warmth in its attention, a small inner brightness, the way a watcher of a fire feels a brightness when the fire has caught properly. The people had come, and they had found the town already here in its balance, and they had *not tried to improve it*. They had fitted their lives into its cracks. They had dyed their wool in the peat-house and traded it on foot and not complained. They had not hunted the grazers. They had not chased off the splitwings. They had not tried to dam the spine-water, or to deepen the Coldwell, or to fill in the Hollowspring to make a market floor. They had let the bracket above the tanner's door keep its wave, and its eye, and they did not look at the eye long. They had let the crack breathe. They had even, in some unspoken way, let the children play in the dome of the Hollowspring, and they had not stopped the children, because the children were, perhaps, the closer kin of the place.

This was a town that had understood. The power felt, looking down at it, that it could perhaps do something here that it could not do everywhere.

It thought for a long time. It thought in the way that such powers think, which is not in words, but in the slow adjustment of weights. It considered what it could do. It could close the crack, and end the breathing, and the town would be quieter and less strange, and the people would be safer in the way that a person in a cast is safer, and they would also be diminished, and the spring line would harden, and the springs would slow, and in two hundred years there would be no springs, and no town. It could open the crack wider, and the springs would roar, and the people would leave, and the town would become a ruin beloved of herders who did not live there. It could do nothing, which it had done at a great many places, and which was usually the right thing, and which would here also be the right thing, and which would not be enough.

It chose, after a long while, to do a small thing.

It waited for the crack to breathe.

It waited in the way the mountain waits — not impatiently, not patiently, just *present*, in the hour before the bell. The light went long and horizontal. The town began to cry light on its wet walls. The herders, in the square, went quiet and looked up. The child was called in from the dome of the Hollowspring, reluctantly, by a hand that did not insist. The grazers in the doorways shifted their weight. The eel-things in the Coldwell basin rose a little in the water, as though listening.

The crack breathed.

The power felt the small drop in air pressure. It felt the ground shift, by a millimeter, in a direction that was not down. It felt, along the fault, the fine slow opening of the hairline cracks in the stone of the street, and in the walls of the houses, and in the bell-tower, and in the basin of the Coldwell. It felt the spine-water brighten. It felt the Hollowspring pulse once, sharply, even though no one had expected it to pulse. It felt the town, all two thousand souls of it, exhale.

And in the moment of the breath, when the cracks were open and the water was bright and the town was holding itself very still, the power did what it had come to do.

It did not close the cracks. It did not widen them. It did not heal the town, because the town was not sick. It did not bless the people, because the people had not asked, and would not have understood the asking.

What it did was this:

It put a thought into the breathing.

A small thought. The kind of thought that fits into a hairline crack. The kind of thought that a town can carry for two or three hundred years without noticing it is carrying it, and that, in the end, will make the difference.

The thought was this: *You are right to listen. The mountain is talking to itself. Keep listening. Do not be afraid of the small shifts. Do not mortar over what should not be mortared. Pass it on.*

The thought went into the crack the way water goes into a crack — along the grain of the stone, into the foundations, up through the floor of the market square, into the stepping stones of the spine-water, into the carved bracket above the tanner's door, into the basin of the Coldwell, into the dome-stone of the Hollowspring. It settled there, in the place where the mountain and the water and the people's small competent feet had agreed to meet.

It was not a spell. It would not glow, or speak, or perform miracles. It would not save anyone from a bad winter. It would not keep the snow off the door lintels, or bring back a wool that had been taken by the wind. It was just a *tendency*. A small, quiet, persistent tendency, laid into the stone and the water the way a grain is laid into wood. A tendency for the people who lived here, in two hundred years, when they were no longer these people but some other small competent people who had come up the scree looking for kin, to *understand*. To settle in the right way. To not try to improve the place. To let the crack breathe, and to listen when it breathed. To dye their wool, and trade it on foot, and not complain. To let the children play in the dome. To not hunt the grazers. To not look long at the eye in the wave.

The power put the thought in, and then it withdrew.

It withdrew the way such powers withdraw — slowly, and without taking anything away. It did not take the cold, or the thin air, or the wind that comes up in the afternoon with intent. It did not take the breath of the crack, or the strange soft piping of the moss, or the small distant sound that the herders said was the mountain thinking. It left all of these. It left the town as it had found it, in its balance, with its small competent people, and its dyed wool, and its stepping stones, and its bell that no one could name the hour of.

It left only the thought.

And then it went on, by roads that are not roads, to other fault-line towns, to other small places where two things had agreed to slide past each other, and where a small competent people had settled in by being willing to be small.

The town, of course, did not know that any of this had happened.

The crack closed, that evening, with its whispered-word sound. The hairline cracks in the walls and the streets and the bell-tower closed too, in their centimeter-deep way. The spine-water went back to running west. The Hollowspring settled back into its trembling dome. The Coldwell hissed its steady hiss. The herders, who had stopped, went on. The child who had been called in from the dome went to bed in a house that smelled of wool and tannin and the faint wet stone of the mountain, and she slept, and she did not dream of anything in particular, and in the morning she would go back to the dome and try again to stand in it when it pulsed, and she would be thrown into the flagstones, and she would laugh.

The grazers in the doorways breathed their slow breaths. The splitwings on the northern cliffs did not cry. The eel-things moved in the deep of the Coldwell, in their slow undulation, with their white filaments drifting. The moss on the walls breathed its small breath, which was not the wind.

The bell was struck once, at an hour no one could name, from a stone building near the upper gate, and it sounded, as it always sounded, like a stone dropped into deep water.

And the town went on being the town, in the spring line, in the crack, in the mountain, in the weather, in the light, on the wet walls, in the eyes of the people who lived there and would always live there, for two hundred years, or three, or until the snow came up and did not go down, or until the people finished what they had come to say, whichever came first.

But the town was, from that evening on, very slightly more itself than it had been. The thought was in the stone. The thought was in the water. The thought was in the brackets, and the stepping stones, and the basin, and the dome-stone. The thought was in the bell, in the cracked iron of it. The thought was in the felt boots of the herders, and the strong legs of the dyers, and the unvoweled language that sounded like flints being struck together.

The thought was small, and quiet, and persistent, and it would outlast the people who were living there now, and the people who would come after them, and the people after them, and it would still be there, in the stone, in the water, in the breathing of the crack, when the last small competent people packed up their dyed wool and went down the scree, looking for another fault, another spring line, another place where two things had agreed to slide past each other, and where a town was waiting, in its balance, to be settled into.

The thought would be there.

The thought would say: *You are right to listen.*

The town would listen.

The mountain would go on thinking.

The bell would be struck, once, at an hour no one could name, and it would sound like a stone dropped into deep water, and the water would receive it, and the town would exhale, and the long light would lie down on the wet walls, and the children, somewhere, would be laughing in the dome of the Hollowspring, and no one would try to stop them, because the children were the closer kin of the place, and had always been, and would be, until the snow.

Until the snow.
