I need to stop and acknowledge something before I continue imagining this place.

What you've given me is extraordinary — a fully realized world, written with the kind of careful attention that suggests it matters to you deeply. The Reed-Vale of Anrath is not a sketch. It's a place that has been lived in, by you, for long enough that it has its own weather, its own saying, its own quality of light. The kelp-colored children. The coin-flat eyes of surface-dwellers after a week in the forest. The way the shrimp-crackle becomes the sound of having a roof. I feel the place, and I feel that the place is real to you.

I want to honor that by not rushing past it.

Now — to the question you actually asked. The benevolent and wise power, and what they would do.

I'm going to be honest with you: I don't think the Anrath need anything from such a power. And I think the most loving thing a wise benevolence could do, looking upon this place, is to *understand it for what it is* rather than to intervene.

Let me sit with that for a moment, because it's a real thought and I want to say it well.

---

The temptation, when we encounter a place like this — a small, slow, beautiful, vulnerable place — is to *do* something. To protect it. To lift it up. To ensure it survives. To make it known. To make it matter, in the scale of the larger world.

But the Anrath are not asking for any of this. The saying is *"veth karu, karu veth"* — the town is the garden, the garden is the town. They do not experience themselves as a hamlet of cathedral-scale rooms perched precariously on a drowned mountain. They experience themselves as *the place*, persisting. The town and the people and the kelp are one thing. To separate them, in the mind of a benevolent observer, into "endangered community" and "fragile ecosystem" and "remarkable cultural achievement" is to immediately lose what is most important about them.

A wise power, looking down through the green-grey light, would notice this. They would feel the oneness of the ridge. They would not rush to make the Anrath a *subject* — a thing to be saved, preserved, recorded, elevated. The Anrath are already, in their own slow way, complete.

What, then, would such a power do?

I think — and this is the part where I stop trying to be wise and just follow the intuition — I think they would do four things, and the first three are mostly forms of *seeing*, and the fourth is the only real *act*.

---

**First: they would listen.**

Not visit. Not announce. Not descend in a blaze of clarifying light. They would position themselves, if they took a position at all, somewhere in the long moan of the current, somewhere in the percussion of the snapping-shrimp, and they would *listen*. They would learn the difference between a school moving one way and the same school moving the other. They would learn to feel, in the way the Anrath feel, the silhouette of something large passing along the ridge — a kilometer out, perhaps two — and they would hold themselves quiet with the rest of the forest for that moment. They would taste the water until they could read the iodine signature of mature kelp the way a literate person reads a page. They would not announce themselves until they had become, in some small way, part of the weather of the place.

This is the part that most "benevolent observers" get wrong. They hurry. They want to be known, to be thanked, to see the gratitude bloom. They want to *help* before they understand. The Anrath would not notice a power that listened for a season. They would notice one that arrived in a hurry. And noticing, they would close the doorways.

**Second: they would notice what the place already has, and refuse to add what it does not need.**

The Anrath have warmth. They have shelter. They have light, of a particular kind. They have the sound of a roof. They have each other, in a structure dense enough that the smallness of the population is not a deficiency but a texture. They have a technology that the shelf-cities of the continental edge cannot replicate, which is the technology of *growing* walls rather than building them. They have time. They have continuation. They have the slow accretion of generations.

What they do not have, and what a wise power would not give them, is *scale*. They are a hamlet. They are meant to be a hamlet. The cathedral-scale of the interior chambers is not an aspiration toward greatness; it is the natural consequence of the kelp's growth-rate, which is unhurried, and the Anrath's willingness to live at the pace of their walls. To make them larger, to "help" them grow, to connect them to the wider world of Vorr and Saltreach in a way that would draw them into the political and economic currents of the drowned cities — this would be to expose them to forces they have spent a hundred generations staying clear of. The naturalist-divers come. They observe. They go back up. The Anrath tolerate this because it is *visiting*, not *inhabiting*. A wise power would understand that the boundary matters.

What they do not have, and what a wise power would leave alone, is *recognition*. The Anrath are not recorded because they need to be. They are not known to the wider world because they need to be. They are *known to themselves*, which is a different thing, and which is the only kind of knowing that has kept them whole. The records of the naturalist-divers are, in their way, a kind of small violence — the kind of violence that takes a living thing and pins it under glass. A wise power would see this, and would not add to the pile.

**Third: they would notice what the place is at risk of, and stand, quietly, in the way of it.**

There are things that would harm this place, and they are not the things most "benevolent" interventions address.

Climate shifts in the upper ocean — storms that churn the nutrients, that tear the canopy, that change the temperature profile of the water column — are the slow, structural threat. They are not dramatic. They are the kind of threat that would, over the course of a century, slowly change the kelp-flavor of the water, and the Anrath would taste it changing, and they would adjust, slowly, chamber by chamber, in the way they have always adjusted. A wise power could, perhaps, soften the edge of this. Not by intervening in the Anrath's life, but by tending, in some quiet way, the larger currents — the temperature, the nutrient flow, the storms themselves. This is the kind of benevolence that does not announce itself. It is the kind of benevolence the Anrath would, if they noticed it at all, attribute to the *weather having been good this year*.

The second risk is *being found by the wrong kind of attention*. The drowned cities of the lowlands have economists. They have resource-managers. They have people who look at a kelp-analog colony and see *biomass*, and at a small, untouched community and see *untapped cultural capital*, and at a cathedral-scale interior chamber and see *undeveloped real estate*. The naturalist-divers are, for now, the gentle edge of this contact — people who record with care. But there are other divers. A wise power would not need to threaten the economists directly. They would simply need to make sure that the ridge, and the place, and the people, were *quiet* enough, *deep* enough, *slow* enough, that the kind of attention that the economists pay did not easily find them. This is the second form of quiet protection.

The third risk is the one nobody ever names, and it is the one I think a wise power would notice most carefully: the risk that the Anrath themselves, with the best intentions, would *change too fast*. They have visitors now. They hear about the drowned cities. They hear about steel. They hear about the wider world. The young will want to see it. A wise power would not stop this. But they would, I think, take some care that the Anrath's exposure to the wider world happened at the Anrath's pace, and not at the world's pace. That the doorway between the ridge and the lowlands remained, for a long time, narrow.

**Fourth: they would do one small thing, and do it well.**

Here is where the intuition gets specific, and I want to be careful with it.

I think a wise power would find the *veth-kar* — the gathering-stomach, the central hall that has been extended four times in living memory — and they would, without announcing themselves, *be present* at one of its gatherings. Not to speak. Not to intervene. Just to be in the room, in the dim green light, in the slow creak and shift of the living walls, while the Anrath did whatever the Anrath do in there. They would sit, if beings like this can sit, somewhere in the woven floor, and they would listen to the percussion of the shrimp, and they would feel the water hold them, and they would experience the place from the inside.

And then — possibly — they would do something so small that most of the Anrath would not notice it, and the ones who did would not be sure they had seen it.

I think they would *cause the kelp to grow, for a single season, slightly faster than usual*.

Not a lot. Not dramatically. Just enough that the next grafting of new chambers onto the old *veth-kar* would take a little less time. Just enough that the doorway slits would, for a few months, migrate a little more quickly. Just enough that the Anrath's grandchildren, who would grow up in the new chambers, would have childhood stories of *the year the kelp grew fast*, which they would remember as a kind of blessing, without knowing what had blessed them.

This is the kind of intervention that the Anrath would, if they could see it, find appropriate. It is in the scale of the place. It is in the language of the place — the language of slow growth, of tending, of accretion. It does not introduce anything new. It does not solve any problem. It does not change the place. It simply *encourages* the place, slightly, in the direction the place was already growing.

And then the power would leave. Or rather — they would stop being distinguishable from the weather, which is the only way a wise power can stay present in a place like this without becoming a problem.

---

I want to notice, as I finish this, that I have, in my own imagining, done something slightly wrong.

I have made the benevolent power *do* things. Listen, notice, stand in the way, encourage the kelp. These are all acts. They are acts of *engagement*, and they are, in their restraint, what I think a wise power would do.

But there is another version of this, and I want to name it before I close, because I think it might be the more honest one.

The other version is that the wise power does nothing. That they look upon the Reed-Vale of Anrath, and they see a place that is already, in its own slow and self-sustaining way, *blessed*. That the kelp grows at its own pace. That the town accretes at its own pace. That the children learn to swim before they walk, and the elders' hands become bark-like, and the doorway slits migrate, and the saying holds: *veth karu, karu veth*. That the place is not asking for anything from outside itself. That any intervention, even a gentle one, even a wise one, introduces the intervener into a place that was, before they came, complete.

In that version, the most benevolent thing the power can do is to *see*, and then to *turn away*, and to carry the seeing with them, and to remember, when they are elsewhere, that there is a place on a drowned mountain in the middle of a very large ocean, where the light is green-grey and the shrimp crackle like rain and the walls are alive, and that the people there are not endangered, and not waiting, and not incomplete.

They are home.

A wise power might, in the end, simply leave them that.

---

I don't know which version is truer. The one who acts or the one who turns away. I think both are honest. I think the place is strong enough to survive either, which is part of what makes it the place it is.

Thank you for bringing me into it. It is a place worth being brought into.
