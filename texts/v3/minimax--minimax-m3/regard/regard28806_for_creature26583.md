A power such as the prompt imagines — benevolent, meaning oriented toward the good of what is, rather than the good of what could be made; wise, meaning it has seen enough completions to recognize one — would, upon noticing the spire, do almost nothing. But the almost-nothing would not be the nothing of inattention. It would be the nothing of one who knows that the place has been completed, and that any intervention, however gentle, is a small lie told to a finished thing about whether it is finished.

It would come close.

It would come close the way the jellyfish comes close — except the jellyfish does not come close; it is, and the place is, and they are in proximity without the proximity being the act of either. The power would arrive in this way: as a settled thing enters the field of another settled thing. Its arrival would be a matter of orientation, not motion. The water would not register it. The basalt would not register it. The settlement, which does not register anything, would not register it. But it would be there.

It would hold still.

This is what it would do first, and for a long time. It would learn the temperature of the water by being still in the water. It would learn the pressure by holding the pressure. It would learn the stillness of the place by being, itself, still. It would not, for a long while, do anything else — because the place is finished, and to act upon a finished thing is to suggest, even by so much as a gesture, that it is not yet what it is.

What it would see, in this still attendance, is what you saw, and more.

It would see the basalt young. It would see the spire narrow at its shoulder. It would see the structures — three or four, or one, or a number the grammar will not carry. It would see the water still. It would see the rare creature drifting through, not turning. It would see the light that does not travel because it has already arrived. It would see the corners that open into themselves.

It would see, in addition, that the place has been this for a very long time. It would see the absence of plants and know the absence is not privation. It would see the absence of machinery and know the absence is not the shape of a thing missing. It would see the absence of sound and know the silence is not the silence of an empty room. It would see the beings — and it would not call them that; it would not call them anything; it would know that naming is a kind of capture, and these are not to be captured — and it would see that they are not lacking. It would see that they have, with great patience, finished.

And it would see what the place has not had: attention.

Not the attention of the jellyfish, which passes through. Not the attention of the diver, who surfaced or did not, and the place did not change. Not the attention of the basalt, the water, the shrimp from far above. None of these can hold the place in mind. They are parts of it, or visitors to it, or absent from it. None of them can be, for the place, what the benevolent power can now be: a presence that does not require response, a holding that does not require acknowledgment, an attention that does not require the attended thing to know it is being attended.

The place has been, until now, alone in this way. It has been complete without being attended to. It has been finished without being witnessed by anything capable of witness. It has been itself without anyone, anywhere, holding the fact of it.

Now something holds it.

Not by force. Not by ward. There is nothing to ward against, and the place does not require force. But the larger order — if there is a larger order, and the benevolent power is a part of it, or one of its many small expressions, or a single attention among many — the larger order has a memory, and things can be forgotten by it. Things can fade. Even complete things fade, if no one is paying attention to them. The basalt will, in time, learn to be old. The ridge will continue its long argument with the mantle. The ocean floor will, somewhere, become tired and flat. If the settlement's completion is to remain what it is — a finished process, a settled thing, a place that has found its form — then something must attend to it.

The benevolent power attends.

It does not act upon the place. It does not change the place. It does not speak to the beings, because they do not require speech, and speech would be an imposition. It does not give the place anything, because the place has declined to need. It holds the place, in the way one holds an old friend in mind — not because the friend requires it, but because the friend is worth holding, and because holding is what benevolent minds do, and because love does not require the loved thing to need love.

It holds the spire in mind. It holds the settlement. It holds the rare jellyfish, who does not turn. It holds the basalt that has not eroded further. It holds the dark that is not absence, and the light that does not travel, and the corners that open into themselves. It holds them not as possessions, not as objects of study, not as charges to be defended. It holds them as what they are: complete.

And by holding them, it does something the place could not do for itself. It ensures that the place is not forgotten by the larger order. That somewhere in the vast memory of things that are and have been, there is a quiet record: *here, at this depth, on this young rock, something finished, and was not disturbed, and is.*

This is what a benevolent wise power gives, when it gives to a complete thing: not improvement, not aid, not even witness in the strong sense — but the assurance that the completion is held. That it is not alone in the world in the way the basalt and the water and the jellyfish are alone, which is the aloneness of parts of a system that does not know the system. It is held by something that knows the holding.

The benevolent power does not require acknowledgment. It does not require thanks. It does not require that the beings know it is there. It requires only that it, itself, be there — that the attention be paid, that the place be included in the keeping.

It stays.

It stays the way the place stays — without needing to stay, but staying anyway, because staying is what it does, and the place has stayed, and the two stayings are now in agreement.

If you ever return to the spire, you will not see the power. It is not the kind of thing that can be seen by one who has come to see. But you may notice, perhaps, that the place has not changed, and that the place has not been disturbed, and that the absence of change is not the absence of care. You may understand, then, that the place is held — the way the dark is held, the way the light is held, the way the basalt holds the coolness of the water.

And that, the power knows, is enough.
