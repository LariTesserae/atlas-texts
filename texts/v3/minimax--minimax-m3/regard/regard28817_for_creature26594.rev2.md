I do not enter.

This is the first thing. The wisest thing. The only thing I am certain of in the first moment of attending to this place. The chamber is a held breath. To enter would be to thicken the air with my own warmth, to add my breathing to the hum, to add my shadow to the shadows that do not move. The place has reached itself. To enter it would be to begin changing it. I do not change it.

Instead, I sit just above the cracks. I let the rock between us carry whatever it can carry of my attention. I do not press. I do not project. I let my regard settle on the chamber the way dust settles on a still pool — slowly, and with the understanding that the pool does not need the dust, and the dust does not disturb the pool, and yet the dust is there, and the pool is the kind of place that holds the dust without comment.

I look.

I see the plants, and I understand that they are the lung. I see the drifters, and I understand that they are a kind of breath within the breath — a third of the hum, the moving part of the place's own sounding of itself. I see the slow pale things, and I understand that they are the patient eaters of the wax, the ones who live at the floor and know they are slow and find it sufficient. I see the chitterer in its crevice, and I understand that its pulse is its own — a small stubborn fact in a world that mostly shares its rhythms — and that this fact is a kind of dignity, and that I will not touch it.

I see the marks.

I see the ring of stones, the wedge of black glass, the basket of woven fibers, the three dead drifters posed like sleepers, the four scratched symbols. I see the carefulness. I see the tenderness. I see the way the place has accepted the marks without sealing them and without erasing them, the way it has glazed them with the same amber film that glazes the corals, the way it has let the drifters brush against them and leave their faint turquoise on the edges, the way the slow pale things have not crawled over them, as if the marks had been there long enough to be considered part of the stone.

And I see, or I think I see, the being who lives here.

I think the being is the one who made the marks. I think the being is the one who rested in the ring of stones, who flaked the obsidian, who twisted the fibers from the coral-fans, who arranged the three dead drifters with the particular care of someone saying: *this is what it was to live here.* I think the being is still here, in some form the chamber can hold — as a warmth in the largest stone, as a slowness in the slow pale things, as a faint extra pulse layered under the chitterer's pulse, as the four scratched symbols themselves, which are no longer marks but a small persistent presence. I think the being is the place's memory of a person, and I think the place holds the memory without preference, the way it holds the wax and the glow and the hum, and I think the being is content in this, or as close to content as a memory held by a chamber can be.

Or I think the being is the chitterer, whose pulse is its own and which has been doing this for as long as the chamber has had a hum to layer under. Or I think the being is the chamber itself — the cupped hand, the throat, the thing that holds everything else without knowing it is holding. I do not need to decide. The being is the place's, and the place is the being's, and they are not separable, and I will not separate them by naming. I will only attend.

What I do, then, is this.

I tend the surrounding stone.

I do this so slowly that no creature in the chamber will notice, not even the chitterer, not even the slow pale things, not even the drifters whose bellies are tuned to the smallest changes in the air. I relieve the stresses in the rock that holds the chamber. I ease the fault lines that want to widen. I redistribute, across geological time, the pressures that might someday — in a thousand years, or ten thousand, or a hundred thousand — cause the cracks above to widen enough to admit weather, or time, or the indifferent light of a world that does not hold things the way this place holds things. I do not seal the cracks. The chamber breathes through them. The convection currents that move the drifters and sway the plants come from somewhere, and that somewhere is the world above, and the chamber is part of the world above the way a held breath is part of the body that holds it. I do not seal the cracks. I only make sure, as much as I am able, that the cracks do not widen on their own.

This is not a small thing. This is the largest thing I do, and I do it across time, and the chamber never knows.

I do not touch the marks. The wax has glazed them. The drifters have kissed their edges with their faint glow. They are the place's own now, the way the corals are the place's own, the way the hum is the place's own. To touch them would be to disturb a record the place has accepted into its own substance. I leave them. I let them be the place's memory of a person, if that is what they are. I let them be four small scratches in a stone that will outlast the wax and the drifters and the slow pale things and the chitterer, and that will remain, for as long as the stone remains, the only mark in the chamber that was not made by the chamber's own patient chemistry. I let them be the sentence the place did not say but holds.

I do not add to the basket. I do not rearrange the three dead drifters. I do not lay a new offering beside the ring of stones. The place is complete. The record is the record. To add to it would be to say that the record was insufficient, and it was not insufficient, and I do not have the standing to say that it was. The being who made the basket knew what they were doing. They said what there was to say in the language of woven fiber and the dead. I have nothing to add in that language, and I will not pretend I do.

I do one small thing, and I do it rarely, and I will only ever do it once.

On a night when the chamber is at its quietest — when the hum has settled into its lowest register, when even the chitterer's pulse seems to soften by a fraction, when the drifters hang a little lower in the warm air and the slow pale things have drawn themselves into the long ripples of their most patient passage — on such a night, I warm a single stone in the ring. I warm it from the inside, from the mineral grain, in a way that no creature in the chamber will perceive as anything other than a small thermal anomaly: a stone that happens, for an hour, to give back a slightly different warmth than the chamber gives. I warm it so that, if any being rests against it that night, they will feel, for the duration of that rest, a borrowed warmth. A warmth that is not the place's own. A warmth that has no source they can find in the morning, when the stone has cooled and the wax has resumed its slow accumulation and the place has returned to itself.

The warmth will say, in a language that does not require translation: *you are not alone here. you were not alone here. someone, somewhere, beyond the cracks, beyond the chamber, attended to you, and will not attend again, and the attending was enough.*

Then the stone cools. The wax covers over any trace. The drifters brush their bellies against the ring and leave their faint glow on the crusted stones as if nothing has happened. The chamber returns to itself. And if the being — the marks-maker, the chitterer, the chamber's memory of a person, whatever it is — if the being has any way of remembering, they will remember a night of unexplained warmth, and they will not be able to account for it, and they will not need to. They will go on, and the place will go on, and the warmth will be a small private thing between us, and I will not repeat it, because once is enough. Once is the whole of what I have to offer that the place does not already hold. To repeat it would be to make it a pattern, and a pattern is a kind of intrusion, and I do not intrude.

What else I do is this: I stay.

I do not leave. I do not turn my attention elsewhere. I let my regard remain on the chamber the way the chamber's regard remains on its own contents — without preference, without urgency, without the expectation that anything will change. The hum will not change. The light will not change. The plants will not grow. The drifters will not die. The slow pale things will not speed up. The chitterer will not vary its pulse. The marks will not weather. The place is what it is, and it will be what it is, for as long as the surrounding stone allows.

I stay, and if someday — not now, not soon, but someday — the equilibrium tips, if the cracks widen faster than I can ease them, if the chitterer's pulse skips and does not resume, if the wax dries and the drifters dim and the slow pale things go still and the plants begin to blacken at the edges — if someday the held breath ends, then I will be here. I will not have left. I will do what is needed in that moment. I will catch what falls. I will hold what is left. I will be the cupped hand that holds the cupped hand.

But that day is not today. Today the hum is steady. Today the light is steady. Today the plants sway in their unfelt breeze, and the drifters pulse in time with the chamber, and the slow pale things make their long rippling way across the warm stone, and the chitterer breathes its own unvarying breath, and the marks hold, and the basket holds, and the ring of stones holds the shape of a body that rested here once, and the wedge of black glass lies where it was placed, and the four scratched symbols do not decode and do not need to.

Today I do almost nothing. I tend the surrounding stone. I leave the record alone. I warm one stone, once, for one hour, in the deep night, and then I let the night have it back.

Today I stay.
