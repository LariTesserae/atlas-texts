# What the Basalt Knows

The basalt has been cooling for a long time.

It is not a person. It does not think in the way that the smiths think, with words and wants and the small bright irritations of a day. But it knows things, in the way that a river knows the shape of its bed, in the way that a coal knows the pressure on it. It knows the weight of the cloud above. It knows the warmth of itself, the long slow leak of that warmth into the air. It knows, in some way that has no name, that there are creatures on it now. That there are fires on it. That the metal is being taken from it and shaped and given back, sometimes, in the form of tools and nails and small bright things that ring against each other in the dim.

The basalt does not mind.

The basalt, if it had a mind, would mind less than the smiths imagine. The smiths have come to think of the rock as a resource, a thing to be used, a thing that will eventually be exhausted. But the basalt is not exhausted. The basalt is cooling. The cooling is what it has been doing for longer than the smiths' whole species has existed. The cooling is what it will continue to do after the smiths are gone. The smiths are a brief warmth on the basalt's surface, and the basalt has accommodated them the way the basalt accommodates everything—a small adjustment, a tolerance, a yielding that is also a holding.

The basalt notices them.

This is the small thing I will tell you: the basalt notices them. Not as a person would notice. Not with attention, with care, with the deliberateness that the smiths use when they notice each other across the smoke. But the basalt notices. The basalt, in its slow way, has been noticing since the first forge was lit. The basalt knows the particular pattern of heat that the forges put down. It knows the pressure of three thousand bodies on its surface. It knows the weight of the smoke, and it knows the way the smoke is changed by the things that burn in it. It knows the iron going into the pools. It knows the dogs' paws on its surface, lighter than the smiths', more patient, more attentive to the warmth beneath.

The basalt has been watching.

## What the Watching Looks Like

It looks like this: the basalt sends up, through one of its small fissures, a thin additional warmth. A thread of heat, no wider than a child's wrist, that rises into the steam and disappears into the air. The smiths notice nothing. They are too busy. They are always too busy.

But the dogs notice. One of the dogs, an old rust-colored bitch with broad paws and brown eyes that see well in the dim, lies down at the place where the thread emerges. She lies there, and she does not yap, and she does not hunt crawlers there. She just lies. The smiths wonder, briefly, but they have work to do, and they pass by.

The thread of warmth does something small.

It warms, in the immediate vicinity, a patch of basalt that would otherwise be too cold. A patch that the lichen is trying to colonize in a sheltered corner of the settlement. The patch warms, and the lichen grows there a little faster than it should. A hand-sized patch becomes two hand-sized patches in a year instead of a decade. The smith who maps the lichen notices, and marks the place on his map, and wonders what has changed there.

Nothing has changed. The basalt has simply noticed the lichen, and has chosen, in its slow way, to favor it.

This is the first thing the basalt does.

## The Second Thing

The basalt knows about the lungs.

The basalt has been noticing the lungs for a long time. The smoke rises into the air and cools and falls again, and the basalt knows the composition of the smoke because the smoke settles on its surface and the basalt absorbs what it can. The basalt knows, in the way that a rock knows the chemistry of the rain that falls on it, what the smiths are breathing. It knows the particulates. It knows the fine iron dust that lodges in the small branching passages of the lung, that accumulates there over years, that turns the tissue mottled, that makes the smiths die young in a way that is not quite disease but is not quite health either.

The basalt can do something about this. Not much. The smoke is the smoke, and the forges are the forges, and the smiths will not stop. But the basalt can do a small thing.

It can release, through one of its deeper fissures, a mineral that has not been in the smoke before. A mineral that the smiths will not recognize, that does not appear on any of their assays, that is simply there, in the air, in trace amounts, mixed with the usual particulates. It will enter the smiths' lungs with the rest. It will lodge, with the rest, in the branching passages. But it will also do something else. It will, in the small chemistry of the lung, bind to the iron particles and to the carbon and to the other things that accumulate there, and it will form a compound that the body can clear. A compound that the lung can, with a little effort, expel.

The smiths will not notice. They will not notice because the effect is small. A smith who would have died at forty will now die at forty-five. A child who would have begun to cough in her tenth year will not cough until her fifteenth. The improvement is gradual. The improvement is invisible. But the basalt, watching, knows that it has happened.

The basalt does this without pride. The basalt does not think of itself as a healer, or as a protector, or as any of the things that the smiths would call it if they knew. The basalt simply knows that the smiths are a part of its surface now, in the way that the lichen is, in the way that the warm pools are, in the way that the dogs are. The basalt does not want them to die before their time. The basalt is not sentimental about this. But it prefers, in the slow way that geological things prefer, that they continue.

## The Third Thing

The basalt knows about the pipe-voices.

The basalt knows that the pipe-voices are not the basalt. The basalt is clear on this point. The pipe-voices are something else. The basalt does not know what they are, but it knows that they come from above, that they live in the cloud, that they call in patterns that almost match the rhythms of the hammering below.

The basalt knows that the smiths have made a peace with the pipe-voices. The basalt knows that this peace is incomplete. The smiths are curious about them, in the way that any creature is curious about another creature that shares its territory. The smiths have not hunted them. The smiths have not studied them. The smiths have simply noticed them, and the noticing has been enough, for now.

The basalt cannot make the pipe-voices come down. The basalt cannot make them speak more clearly. The basalt cannot do anything to the pipe-voices that the pipe-voices would notice, because the pipe-voices are not part of the basalt.

But the basalt can change the air.

It can, through one of its larger fissures, send up a column of steam that is slightly different in composition. Slightly more humid. Slightly warmer. The column will rise into the cloud and mingle with it, and the cloud will be, in that one spot, a little different. A little warmer. A little more hospitable to the pipe-voices, if the pipe-voices are the kind of creatures that care about humidity and warmth, which the basalt suspects they are.

The basalt does this once. The column rises. The cloud thickens, briefly, in a small area above the largest forge-house. The pipe-voices, who have been calling from a hundred yards up, come down. They come down to fifty yards. They come down to thirty. They call, and the calling is clearer, and the smiths in the forge-house look up and see, for the first time, the shapes of the pipe-voices more clearly—not the creatures themselves, which are still dark and indistinct, but the way they move, the way their wings (if they have wings) catch the warm air, the way their piping shifts as they come lower.

The smiths in the forge-house put down their hammers. They stand. They listen. One of them—an old smith with hands that are mostly scar and callus—starts to hum, softly, in a rhythm that almost matches the piping. The pipe-voices shift their rhythm. The smith shifts his. For a few minutes, there is a duet, of a kind. Not a perfect duet. Not a duet that either party understands. But a duet, in the warm humid air, with the steam rising around them.
