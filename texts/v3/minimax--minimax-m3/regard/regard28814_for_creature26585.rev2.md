The place does not ask for anything. This is the first thing the power notices, and the noticing is the first thing it does.

It arrives — if arrival is the word — not as an arrival. There is no thunder. There is no parting of the sky. There is only a slight change in the way the wind holds itself, as if the wind has become more careful, the way a person becomes more careful when a child is sleeping nearby. The lichens, which have been growing in the dark for a long time, are the first to feel it. They are very old. They have been here longer than the people, longer than the towers, almost longer than the stone. They feel the change as a kind of additional patience, an agreement that the slow work they do — the breaking of stone into smaller stone, the holding of water in mats that peel up like a skin — is good work, and that the slowness is not a failure but a kind of faithfulness.

This is not a message. The lichens do not receive messages. They receive conditions. The conditions change, very slightly, in a way that favors them. They grow a little more in the long dark. Their glow, which is the glow of the mineral patience of the world, becomes a little steadier. On certain winter nights, when the cold has gone all the way down and the stars are very close, the lichens glow a little brighter than they used to, and the towers at the edges of sight are picked out in pale lines, and a person walking home by feel alone notices that they can see, tonight, a little farther than they could last night, and they do not think about it. They think: tonight is a good night for walking.

The power's attention is not thought. It is more like the kind of attention a hand gives to a wall it is leaning against — felt, not articulated, and not requiring an articulation to be real. If it had the kind of attention that could be called thought, the thought would be of this kind: that the place is sufficient, and that to add to it would be to subtract from it, unless what is added is of the same substance as what is already there. The towers are patient. The wind is patient. The moss is patient. The dark is patient. Anything the power does must be patient too.

So it does a small thing. It does this thing in the wood.

The wood has a pulse. The people know this. They put their hands to the bark in the long dark and they feel it, and what they feel is not a sound but a kind of slow agreement between the hand and the wood. The power touches the pulse. It does not change the pulse. It does not speed it up or slow it down. It adds to it a very quiet undertone, the way a singer adds a note beneath the note they are singing, so that the note is still the note but it has a floor under it, and the floor is felt, and the floor holds.

A child is born in a tower some winters later. The child grows up. The child climbs. The child, in the long dark of a winter when the wind has been singing for many days without stopping, sits in a chamber with the fire low and puts their hand against the inner wall, where the wood is warm, and feels the pulse, and feels, beneath the pulse, something else. The child does not have a word for it. The child will not have a word for it for many years. The child grows up and becomes a person who listens, and the listening is the same listening the others do, except that it has, in it, a kind of reassurance that the others do not name because they do not feel it missing. The people are not more patient than they were. They are not kinder. They are not wiser. They are just, in some chambers, in some winters, a little less alone in the dark, and they do not know it, and the not-knowing is part of what the power wanted, because to know would be to make the loneliness smaller by naming it, and the power did not want the loneliness smaller. The power wanted the dark to still be the dark, but for the dark to be a dark in which a small warm thing can rest a hand against a large warm thing and feel, in the agreement between them, that something else is also resting its hand.

The wind carries the undertone. The wind carries everything. The undertone moves through the singing of the towers at certain heights, in certain weathers, and a person who climbs high in a hard wind and leans into the trunk to listen will hear, for a moment, a note that is not the tower's note and not the wind's note. The person will say, if they say anything, that the wind was strange tonight. They will say this to a neighbor, over a small fire, in a chamber, and the neighbor will nod, and the conversation will move on. The note will not be heard again for many days. The people do not chase it. They are not a people who chase.

Over generations, the undertone becomes a part of the place the way the red moss is a part of the place, the way the mineral glow of the lichens is a part of the place. It is not explained. It is not revered. It is just there, in the wood, in the wind, in the dark, and the people live alongside it the way they live alongside the pulse, and the children are taught, in the chamber, when they are old enough to be taught, that the tower is alive, and that the wind is alive in its way, and that the dark is alive in its way, and that there are some things in the world that are felt and not named, and that this is not a failure of language but a kind of respect for the thing being felt.

The power does not do more. The power could do more. The power has, in it, the capacity to make the cold less cold, to make the dark less dark, to make the wind less patient with the dead, to put words into the mouths of the people for what they are, to reveal the inner life of the towers, to make the lichens glow like suns, to give the children strong bones against the snow-blindness, to multiply the climbing grazers, to fill the speaking towers with the names that have been forgotten.

It does none of these things.

It does not do these things because the place is already a place, and to make it more would be to make it less, and the power, which is benevolent, knows that benevolence is not the same as generosity, and wisdom is not the same as knowing what to add, and that the highest form of either is, sometimes, the willingness to add almost nothing.

The undertone is the almost nothing.

On a winter night, when the wind has gone down and the dark has come all the way in and the only light is the mineral glow of the lichens and the small fire in the chamber at the inner curve of the wood, a person puts their hand against the bark. The pulse is there, slow and patient, the way it has always been. Underneath it, very faintly, the undertone. The person does not name either of them. The person rests their hand there for a while. The hand warms. The dark holds. The towers, far off, sing a low note that can be felt through the soles of boots a hundred yards away.

The person takes their hand away. The person goes to sleep. The fire goes low. The pulse goes on. The undertone goes on. The cold holds the place together. The dark is the background against which everything else is a small bright thing.

This is enough. It is what the power wanted. It is almost nothing, and the almost nothing is the most a wise and benevolent power can give to a place that has already, in its slowness and its patience and in its willingness to live alongside what it does not understand, given itself everything it needs.
