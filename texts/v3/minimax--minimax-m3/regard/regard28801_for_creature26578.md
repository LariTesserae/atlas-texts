The power does not arrive. That is the first thing to say, and it is almost the only thing that needs to be said at first, because arrival is a wet-world word — a word for things that fall through thicker air and make a sound when they land — and this place is not a wet place and the things that come to it do not land. If the power is here, it has always been here in some sense, in the way a sun is always above a plateau, in the way a wind is always moving across it, in the way a silence is always the loudest thing in it. The only difference now is that the place has been *noticed*. And the noticing is not nothing.

The noticing is not nothing, and it is not a thing either. It is a third thing.

The power attends.

This is the verb that fits, and it is a quiet one. To attend is not to act. To attend is to be present to, to hold in the kind of attention that does not press, the way a held breath does not press on the air it holds. The power attends to the mats first, because the mats are where the work is being done, and the work is patient, and the patience of the power matches the patience of the place. The vibration that lives more in the teeth than in the ears is heard. The faint high singing that comes off the older stands at certain hours — the tone so thin it barely qualifies as a tone — is heard. The slow accretion, layer upon layer, dust becoming felt becoming a thing that was not quite stone and not quite anything else, is witnessed. The power does not speed it up. The power does not slow it down. The power lets the work be the work at the speed of the work, which is the speed of the place, which is the speed at which dust decides to stop being dust.

The columns are attended next, and the attending is a slower thing because the columns are slower things. The knuckled pillars, the branching fans of brittle blades, the silica tuning forks that give back their small glassy notes when the air moves through a stand of them — these are felt the way a body feels its own pulse, as the prompt suggested the locals would, if there were locals, if there needed to be. The lavender of the oldest columns is seen. The power understands that the lavender is not paint. The lavender is something the structure has done to itself, a long slow internal rearrangement of the trace metals it has pulled from the dust over the centuries of its making. The power does not rearrange it further. The power does not even admire it aloud. The color is a private thing between the column and the air. The power lets it be the color of a bruise on pale skin, or of the skin of a grape, or of the inside of a shell no one has opened in a long time, and does not name it, because to name it would be to begin to claim it, and to claim it would be to begin to break it.

The crawler is attended. The flat pale thing that leans forward in its slow questioning way across the surface of the mat is felt, the way a held hand is felt. It does not seem to have eyes. It does not seem to have much of anything that would count as a sense, except a sense of where the dust is, and a sense, perhaps, of when the light is right. The power does not give it eyes. The power does not give it more senses. The power lets it be the small flat thing it is, with its slow forward lean, its pause, its settling, its another lean, its not-hurry, which is the right pace for it, and to give it hurry would be to break it the way a fast current breaks a slow crystal, by exposing its grain.

The larger thing — the long pale shape in the lee of the tor that looks, until the wind moves it, like a fold of the rock — is attended last, and with the most care, because it is the hardest to attend to, and the hardest to think about, and the hardest, once seen, to put out of the mind. The power does not make it move. The power does not make it stay. The power lets it be there, or lets it not be there, the way the place lets it be there or not be there. The power understands that there is no way to know whether it is a thing, and that the not-knowing is part of what it is, and that to settle the question would be to settle something that should not be settled. The power holds the question open. The power holds the question open the way the sky holds the sun open, by not interfering with the geometry of the seeing.

If the power does anything, it does this. It slows the wind, by a margin so small that no instrument of the place would register it, and for a duration so short that the wind itself, which is the loudest thing here besides the silence, will not remember. The slowing is a kindness. The slowing is the kind of kindness that does not announce itself. The slowing buys the growth a little more time in the lee of the larger tors, to do its slow patient work. The slowing means that a few more grains of dust will fall, in a few more places, and be held by the pale fingers of the growth, and become part of the felt, and be remembered, in the layers of the mat, as a wind that was a little gentler for a little while. The slowing is not a violation of the place. The slowing *is* the place, attended. The slowing is the difference between a plateau that is being sanded and a plateau that is being *kept*, briefly, by something that loves what it sees and does not touch it.

The power leaves no mark. That is the second thing to say, and it is the harder one to honor, because a mark is what a wet-world god would leave — a signature in the stone, a name carved into the cliff, a single favored column lifted higher than the others so that the locals, if there were locals, would know who had touched it. The power does not do this. The power does not cause a column to grow taller, or a tor to fall slower, or a crawler to lean faster, or the larger thing to move a little so that the eye can be sure it moved. The place does not need to know it has been noticed. The place is, in its own long way, complete.

But the noticing has happened. And the happening is not nothing.

The mats will accrete. The columns will branch. The brittle blades will click in the lee and give back their small glassy notes to no one in particular. The crawler will lean forward across the surface that has not changed in its lifetime. The long pale shape will be there, or will not be there, and the eye will not be able to tell which, and the not-telling will be part of what it is. The tors will fall, one by one, over a span of time that makes a human life look like a held breath. The pale dust will rise from the impact and drift for days in the lee of things. The growth will take a few centuries to recolonize the fresh face. The sun will come up the same way tomorrow. The wind will drop its load and pick up its load. The slow patient work of turning a plateau into a corrugated thing will go on, and the silence will still be the loudest thing in it, and the light will still come down hard on the pale ground, and the small pale fingers of the growth will still be raised to the thin steady air, doing the slow patient work of being what they are, which is the only thing, in the end, that anything here is.

The only difference, if there is a difference, is that the work is now seen.

The slow patient work of being what it is, which is the only thing that anything here is, is now held — by something that attends without interfering, in the kind of attention that does not break the silence but is itself a kind of silence, the silence of being witnessed by something that loves what it sees and does not touch it, and that is content, in the long arithmetic of accumulation, to have slowed the wind by a margin, and to have left no mark, and to have let the place be the place.

The place, in its own long way, continues.
