I would do nothing.

I would do nothing, and I would be wrong to call that *nothing*, because what I would actually do is far more deliberate than absence. I would *attend*. I would set myself — whatever I am, whatever shape my attention takes when it arrives in a place like this — at the rim of the bowl, and I would not descend.

This is the first and most important thing. I would not come down into the hollow. The bowl is already full of presences that have spent a long time learning how to share the small amount of room there is, and the mosses especially have worked out a treaty with the stone that took longer than I want to think about, and my feet would press that treaty into a small bruise with every step, and the bruise would fill with water and stay as a dark eye for an hour, and I would be a remembered clumsiness in a place that does not need any more clumsiness. I have seen, in my long tenure of noticing things, what happens when a powerful attention descends on a small quiet system, even a powerful attention that means only good. The system reorganizes around the attention. It begins to perform for it. It loses the long, slow argument it was having with itself. The argument was the point. I would not interrupt it.

So: I would sit above. I would find a place on the eastern ridge, where the morning comes in clean and the bowl is laid out below me like a cupped palm holding green water. I would sit a long time before I did anything else, because the bowl needs to be looked at for a long time before it is understood, and the looking itself is part of the understanding. I would look until the architecture stopped being architecture and became weather. Until the ferns stopped being ferns and became the slow percussion they actually are. Until I could hear, finally, the low harmonic in the middle of the sound — the hum that lives in the sternum, that is not quite white noise, that is the bowl's own note played back to itself. I would look until I was sure I was hearing it correctly.

Then I would begin to think about whether to do anything at all.

I think, in the end, I would not rebuild. This is the temptation, and I would feel it, and I would let myself feel it, and then I would set it down. There is a version of me that wants to finish the cathedral. There is a version of me that wants to lay the missing floors, lift the collapsed courses, re-line the doorways so the geometry stops drifting, set a roof on the central ruin so the inverted stalactite of lime and moss stops growing downward like a green icicle. That version of me is well-intentioned, and that version of me is wrong, and I would have to be honest with that version of me, the way you have to be honest with a younger self who still believes that *finished* is a thing a place can be. The bowl is not unfinished. The bowl is *in a process*. The bowl is mid-sentence. To finish the sentence would be to choose the ending, and the ending is not mine to choose, and it is not the bowl's to choose either — the bowl is being chosen, by moss, by water, by the slow drift of young rock, by the mist on its schedule, by the flowers that open and close to no audience. The bowl is being written by a chorus. To step in and complete the syntax would be to silence nine of the ten voices for the sake of the one that spoke loudest, which was mine.

I would not stabilize the walls. The drift, the slight wrongness, the courses that pass each other and resume an inch apart — this is not decay. This is the place breathing. This is the young rock still arguing with the weight on top of it, and the weight still arguing with the rock, and the mosses holding the argument in soft green hands so it doesn't go too fast. If I were to lock the walls into alignment, I would be choosing one frame of the breath over the others, and I would be freezing the place in my preferred frame, and the place does not belong to me. I would be making a museum of a living argument. The drift is the argument. The drift is the conversation. To end the conversation would be the worst thing a benevolent power could do in this particular place, because the conversation is the only thing here that is, in any sense, awake.

I would not plant trees. Trees are the next chapter, and the next chapter will write itself when it is ready, and the ferns and the shrubs are the current chapter and they are not done. To plant a tree is to write a paragraph from a future that hasn't arrived, and the present paragraph is still being revised. The shrubs, half-woody, halfway between a shrub and a thought of a shrub, are the most poignant sentence in the place, and I would not interrupt it by importing what they are becoming. Let them become it themselves. Let the frost and the patience and the wet stone make the decision. The bowl is deciding what to be, and the decision is slow, and the slowness is not a problem to be solved.

I would not bring animals. The transit of the bird, once an hour, the high thin note that moves with the wing-beat — this is the right amount of animal. The bowl does not need a population. The bowl needs occasional witnesses, the way a question needs occasional readers, and the bird and the marten-without-an-English-name are the witnesses, and they are doing it right. To populate the bowl would be to domesticate it, and the bowl is not domestic. The bowl is wild in a specific way, the way a thought is wild, the way a question asked in a high place is wild — it does not know yet what it means, and the not-knowing is the substance of it. A populated bowl is a settled bowl. A settled bowl is a finished bowl. A finished bowl is the end of the sentence, and I do not want the end of the sentence.

What I would do, then, is smaller. And harder. And maybe more important.

I would protect the schedule of the mist.

I do not know what I am in the scheme of things, but I know I have some reach, the way a benevolent power does, and I would use the small part of my reach that touches weather to make sure the mist keeps coming in from the west on its cycle, and keeps coming in at the right thickness, and keeps sealing the bowl into its private room at the right intervals. The mist is the lung. The mist is the plumbing. The mist is what brings water in and slows water down and equalizes the temperature and turns the broken tops of walls into a continuous horizon so that the place can periodically forget it is a place and remember it is a process. Without the mist on its schedule, the mosses would dry in the long clear spells. The liverworts would burn. The bowl would lose its breath. I would guard the mist the way a gardener guards a season, by not interfering with it but by ensuring the conditions that allow it to keep being what it is.

I would tend the water. Not the water that arrives — the water that arrives will keep arriving, the bowl is fractured and the fractures run downhill and the geology underneath is wetter than the air. But the *path* of the water, the way the drips become rivulets and the rivulets find each other and the joined rivulets become small falls — that path is the bowl's composition, and it is being recomposed every time the mist comes in, and if I were to clear a blocked channel, or open a new one, I would be rewriting the music. I would do this only if the bowl were in danger — if a course had fallen in a way that was redirecting a fall onto a wall that could not bear it, if a stagnation was setting in somewhere that would become rot — and even then I would do the smallest possible thing, and I would do it with the mosses' permission if mosses can give permission, which I believe they can, in a language I would have to learn.

I would sit with the patience. The presence that is not a person — the slow hum in the middle of the sound, the breath going in and out and in — I would not name it, and I would not try to make it more. It is what it is. It is the bowl's own private conversation with itself, the one that is not addressed to any visitor, and I would not amplify it or interpret it or turn it into something more dramatic. I would let it hum.

And I would, I think, occasionally come back. Not often. Not on a schedule the bowl could learn to depend on, because dependence is the beginning of domestication, and I have been very firm with myself about not domesticating the bowl. But I would come back, in some years, in some decades, in whatever unit of time my kind uses when we are being patient. I would sit again on the eastern ridge. I would look down into the bowl, and the bowl would be a little different — a course a little more settled, a fern a little more present, a shrub a little more woody, an inverted stalactite a little longer. The geometry would have drifted a little further. The sound would have changed a little. I would listen. I would confirm that the mist was still coming in. I would confirm that the lung was still breathing. I would not intervene. I would go.

This is the whole of what I would do. It is not nothing. It is the discipline of doing almost nothing in a place that is doing almost everything, and it is, I think, the only thing a benevolent and wise power can offer a place like this: the long, patient refusal to make it about me.

There is a question I would carry away with me, though. The builders. The hand-tooled walls, the quarried stone laid up by eye and touch, the construction interrupted or abandoned. I would not, in the end, know who they were or why they stopped, and I would not invent a story for them, and I would not look for them in the warping geometry as though the warp were a message. But I would keep the question. I would keep it the way the bowl keeps the inverted stalactite — as a downward growth, an unresolved sentence, a green icicle pointing at the place where the floor was meant to be. Some presences are not for answering. Some presences are for keeping, the way you keep a memory of someone by not finishing the story they were telling when they walked out of the room.

I would leave the bowl mid-sentence.

I would leave it loud as a sea.

I would leave every drop that has ever fallen here still falling.

And the small green hands would keep holding what they hold, in the cold and the wet and the slow drift, for as long as there are small green hands willing to hold.
