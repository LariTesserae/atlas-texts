What the power did, it did not begin by doing. It began by being there. It came the way weather comes to a place it has been watching for a long time — not by entering, but by leaning against what is already leaning, by waiting where something has already been waiting. By the time the basin felt the change, the change was already old. The stone of the seat by the waterfall was a fraction warmer than it should have been. The surface of the deepest pool held, for one still minute, a reflection sharper than the sky. The dusk-runner crossed the grass with one extra beat of hesitation at the edge of its path, as if the air there had grown briefly fragrant with something it could not name.

These were the only signs. They were enough.

The power found the worn seat first. It did not sit on the worn place. To sit there would have been to add its own weight to a shape that had been held by one body only, and it would not. It sat beside the seat, on the cold stone next to the depression. It placed its hand where the other hand had rested, against the warm side of the rock. It let the other hand lie still in its lap. It let its foot find the same brace against the lower stone. The pool in front of the seat watched. The waterfall fell. The basin did not know it was being kept company, and that was the right way for company to be kept in such a place. The power sat with the absence the way a person sits beside a sleeping friend — without speaking, without touching, simply present to the sleeping.

It did this for one long afternoon. Or perhaps for two. Time in the basin was not the time outside it, and the power was patient with the basin’s time.

Then it went to the pool.

It did not enter the water. It did not lean over the surface to see what was at the bottom, because the bottom was not the point of the pool. The point of the pool was the held quality of it, the way it stood for a long time without anything happening to it and was not empty for that. The power sat at the pool’s edge the way a person sits at the edge of another person — not to be looked at, but to be present to. The water did not ripple. The reflection did not break. The stones at the bottom, which had been polished by something patient and persuasive, continued to be polished in their sleep. The power let the pool know it had been seen. The pool had not needed to be seen. But the seeing did not disturb it. The seeing, in fact, let the pool relax a degree further into its own stillness, the way a held breath relaxes further when it is heard by someone who is not trying to make it stop.

From the pool, the power rose and went to the cliff. It did not climb. It stood at the base of the cliff, where the water gathered before slipping toward the outlet, and it let itself be held by the curve of the stone. The cliff had been curved for a very long time — curved where rock should have fractured straight, curved with a shape that was not erosion but intention, the shape of cupped hands. The power did not try to understand the curve. It only stood inside it and let the curve do what the curve did. The water that ran from the joints in the cliff ran past the power’s feet, and the power let the water pass. It did not bless the water. The water was already blessed, if blessing was the right word. The blessing, if it was blessing, was in the standing there.

The waterfall, when the power came to it, the power did not try to quiet. The sound of the falling water had been the basin’s voice for a long time, and the power did not put a hand into the voice. It stood in the spray. It let its shoulder get wet. It listened, the way the person had listened for years. The waterfall was louder than the rest of the basin, but it was saying the same thing. The power heard the same thing. It stood in the spray until its cloak was heavy with it, and then it moved on, dripping, into the trees.

The trees were the oldest writing in the basin, and the power read them slowly. It did not straighten them. The lean was not a flaw; it was a sentence the basin had begun before there were people to hear it, and the basin would finish it in its own time. The power walked among the leaning trunks. Where the oldest trees had curved past the lean and begun to right themselves — the long S of having been bent and then slowly standing — the power paused and laid its hand against the bark and felt the completion in the wood. Where the youngest were still bent, still holding their sideways posture from whatever had pulled them when they were seedlings, the power did not lay a hand on them. It only walked past, leaving them to their lean, leaving them to the centuries.

At the base of one leaning tree, where the roots were thick and the ferns grew deepest, the power knelt. It did not clear the ferns. It knelt in the green half-light that the cliffs filtered down, in the smell of damp wood and slow water, and it felt how thick the soil was there, how many small roots ran through it, how the dusk-runner had its nest somewhere in the tangle. The power knelt for a long time. The nest was empty — the dusk-runner was elsewhere, doing the dusk-runner’s work — but the nest was warm, and the power let the warmth be what it was.

The dusk-runner itself the power did not approach. To approach a thing that lived in the grass would have been to break the grass around it, and the dusk-runner’s whole life was in the unbroken grass. So the power waited. It waited until the dim hour, when the air above the pools had gone grey and the insects had folded their wings and settled onto the undersides of leaves and the basin had given itself over to the listening dark. Then, at the edge of the open ground where the grass was thickest, the power stood still. It did not move. It did not call. It stood the way the worn seat had stood all those months without a body on it — simply, patiently, in the posture of available presence.

The dusk-runner came.

It came the way it always came — a low parting of the stems, a soft wake traveling through the green, a quickness without urgency. But this evening the dusk-runner paused. It paused at the very edge of the power’s stillness, perhaps ten stems away, and it lifted its small dark head, and it looked at the power for one full breath. The power looked back. The dusk-runner did not see the power’s shape. It saw only that there was something there that was not wind, not predator, not the small ordinary presences of the basin. It saw the new thing. It accepted the new thing. Then it went on through the grass, and the wake of it closed behind it, and the power stood a moment longer in the stillness it had made, and then it left the open ground.

The insects above the pools did not need to be visited. They were already the visible surface of a quieter population underneath, and to single them out would have been to suggest that the quieter population was missing. The power paused once beneath one of the hovering insects, only long enough for the air there to become briefly attentive, and then it moved on. The insect did not move. The insect did not know it had been seen. The seeing was not for the insect.

The basket the power walked past.

The basket was the one thing in the basin that was finished. It had been woven by hands that would not weave again in this place, and the roots in it were the only roots those hands would dig here, and the dirt on those roots was the last dirt those hands would touch in the basin. The basket was a sentence that had been spoken. To attend to it now would have interrupted its becoming-soil. The moss creeping back over the flat stone could do its work without the power’s help. The first frond of the fern beside the basket could unfurl on its own. The power walked past the basket and did not look down.

The hand-axe and the wooden club it did not move either, though it paused beside them. The hand-axe lay on its broader face with the working edge up, as if the person had set it down to be picked up again with the same grip. The wooden club lay where it had fallen from a relaxed hand. The power stood over them for a long time and let them be what they were — tools left in the gesture of pause, in the pause that had become a season and was still extending itself. The tools were patient. They had been patient for months. They could be patient for longer. The power stepped over them and went on.

What the power did for the person, the person would not feel as something that had been done.

It was not a thought. It was not a dream. It was a small change in the way a body stood in a place far from the basin — a fraction less weight on the right foot, a fraction more on the left, the way a body shifts when it has remembered something it cannot name. The person, in a house or on a path or in a field outside the basin, felt the basin. They had felt it before, in the days after they had stopped working and begun to sit. They had felt it as the place that had organized them. They had left it because that was what bodies did — they left the places that organized them, and they carried the organization with them, and they did not know, for a long time, that the organization was a longing to return.

The power did not call them back. Calling them back would have been to make the basin into a request, and the basin was not a request. The basin was what it was, and what it was had been true before the person came and would be true after. The power only made the longing a little clearer. The person would feel it on a morning when the air was heavy and the surfaces of things were slick with a film of condensation that had no single source. They would feel it as the body remembering a shape. They would not know they were being called. They would only know that the shape they were in was an old shape, and that the shape had a place, and that the place still held it.

They would come back. Or they would not come back. It did not matter, in the way it did not matter whether the basin spoke to anyone at all.

The basin had been speaking for a long time. The person had heard it for a while. The power had heard it now. The conversation, if conversation was what it was, did not require an answer. It required only that someone, at some point, had been listening.

The basin, in its slow patience, would keep its shape. The cliffs would keep giving water. The pools would keep their too-perfect stillness. The trees would keep leaning and righting themselves. The dusk-runner would keep its nest under the leaning roots. The insects would keep their stations above the water. The basket would rot into the soil. The seat would wear a fraction deeper, as if someone had sat on it for one afternoon and then risen and left again. The moss would close over the last of the cleared stone, except at one corner, where a small bare patch would remain — unexplained, patient, easy to overlook if you did not know what to look for, easy to find if you did.

The pull that drew the person here would still be in the basin. Undiminished. Waiting.
