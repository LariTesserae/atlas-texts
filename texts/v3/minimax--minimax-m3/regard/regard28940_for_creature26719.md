# A Response to the Greenworks

I would listen first. This is the sort of place that punishes haste, and rewards the kind of attention that is willing to outlast a single human lifespan. Before I did anything, I would stand in the square for a long time — an afternoon, maybe several — and let the place accustom itself to me. I would not try to interpret. I would let the wheel groan. I would let the furnace sigh. I would let the vine do whatever it is doing near the carts, and I would not investigate, because investigation is a kind of accusation, and this place has not done anything wrong.

What I would notice, if I stayed long enough, is that the Greenworks is not asking for rescue. This is important. Places that need rescue announce it — they cry out, they send signals, they beg. The Greenworks does none of these things. The Greenworks is running. The kettles are full. The windows are clean, or as clean as they need to be. The carts are arriving. The vine is flowering. The town is, in its slow and patient way, *flourishing* — not in the way its builders would have recognized, and not in the way that a forester or a sanitary engineer would approve, but flourishing nonetheless, the way a coral flourishes, the way a meadow flourishes, the way any system flourishes that has found, after a long period of becoming, the rhythm that is its own.

A wise and benevolent power, arriving here, would do well to notice this. The first temptation would be to restore. To bring back the hundreds who lived here, to scrub the moss from the bricks, to clean the pipes, to re-wind the clock that no one winds. But this would be a category error. The town is not broken. The town is not a ruin. The town is a ruin the way a forest is a ruin, the way a wetland is a ruin — that is, it is a ruin only from the standpoint of a previous arrangement, and the previous arrangement is gone, and the new arrangement is in possession, and the new arrangement has been in possession long enough that it has its own claims.

So: not restoration. Not rescue. Not even, perhaps, intervention in any strong sense.

What then?

I think the wise thing to do, if one were a wise and benevolent power looking at this place, would be something closer to *recognition*. To see the Greenworks clearly. To see that the handful of figures who move through the alleys without disturbing the water are not ghosts and are not people in any ordinary sense, but are something the town is producing the way the town produces condensation — slowly, out of its own substance, as part of its slow respiration. To see that the vine is not a metaphor and not a miracle, but a participant, a citizen, a collaborator in the maintenance of a system that has no other name for itself. To see that the mechanism is not a haunting and not a malfunction, but a kind of memory, the way a riverbed is a memory of water — the form the previous life took, persisting because nothing has yet arisen to erase it.

I would give the place a kind of weather. A small adjustment to the ambient — not a change of climate, nothing so crude, but a subtle shift in the quality of the light, or the mineral content of the rain, or the temperature of the standing water. Something that would make the next phase of the becoming a little easier, without directing the becoming toward any particular outcome. The town is making itself into something. I do not know what. The town does not know what. The town is, perhaps, the only honest kind of town: a place that does not know what it is for, and has made peace with not knowing, and continues.

A wise power would not tell it what it is for. A wise power would not even whisper. A wise power would do something on the order of: ensure that the slow succession continues. That the next century does not bring a flood that drowns the square, or a drought that dries the moss, or a road that is followed, by which I mean a road that brings in people who would interpret the place, and whose interpretation would be a kind of violence, even if it was a kind interpretation, even if it was full of good intentions. The Greenworks does not need to be understood. The Greenworks needs to be left alone to continue not being understood by anyone, including itself.

I would do something about the road. Not close it — the gate is rusted open, and a wise power does not make gestures — but make it slightly easier to lose. Let the green country beyond the gate creep a little further inland. Let the water from the lowland spread another hundred yards over the old track. Let the ferns colonize the first bend. Not to imprison the place, but to give the place its privacy. The Greenworks is a thing that wants to be uninterrupted, and the road is the only interruption, and the road is the only way the world outside can find the place, and the world outside is full of people who would, with the best will in the world, want to do something, and the wanting to do something is, in this case, the one thing that would be done wrong.

I would do something about the vine. Not much. A breath of warmth in winter, if there is a winter. A slight increase in the mineral content of the condensation along the copper drum, so that the next flowering is a little fuller, and the pollen falls a little more thickly into the square. This is not a gift. This is the kind of small adjustment that a benevolent power makes the way a gardener waters a thing that is already growing. The vine is doing something the town cannot do without. I would make sure the vine continues to be able to do it.

I would not touch the people — if they are people. I would not try to see them more clearly. I would not try to make them more present. The discretion of the people of the Greenworks is itself a kind of maintenance, and the maintenance is one of the things the place is for, and to force the people into visibility would be to break a small but load-bearing part of the whole. Let them cross the far end of the main way on still evenings. Let the breath fog the air in the doorways. Let the kettles be full by means that may or may not be human. The ambiguity is not a flaw. The ambiguity is one of the things the place is.

I would do one larger thing, and then I would leave, and not return for a long time.

The larger thing is this: I would make the Greenworks slightly more legible to itself. Not by giving it a name, because the place has been without a name, or with a name no one speaks, and the namelessness is part of its weather, and I will not take that from it. But by a kind of reinforcement. A small brightening of the signal that the town is sending to itself — the signal that says *this is what is happening here, and what is happening here is allowed to continue*. The town already knows this, I think, in the way that a body knows it is alive. But the knowing is faint, and the place is old, and the faintness could, over the long centuries, become a doubt, and the doubt could become a hesitation, and the hesitation could become a stopping. I would not let the place stop. I would make the knowing slightly louder, the way you speak slightly louder to a person you love who is going deaf, not to startle them, but to make sure the next sentence gets through.

And then I would go. I would step back across the water and the green country and the long flat land, and I would not look at the place for a hundred years, because the place does not need to be looked at, and being looked at, even by a benevolent and wise power, is a kind of weight, and the Greenworks has enough weight already, the weight of its own slow respiration, which is the only kind of weight a place like this can bear without bending.

What I would hope, returning after my century of absence, is to find the place essentially unchanged. The wheel would still be turning. The vine would still be flowering its half-open flowers. The water would still be warm and still and faintly sweet. The people, if they are people, would still be moving through the alleys without being seen. The kettles would be full. The windows would be clean. The town would be continuing.

That would be enough. That would be, I think, the only thing that would be enough.

The Greenworks is not a problem to be solved. The Greenworks is not a wound to be healed. The Greenworks is a thing that is in the process of becoming whatever it is going to be, and the process is slow, and the process is patient, and the process is, in its own way, the most beautiful thing I have ever been asked to look at, and the most beautiful response to a beautiful thing is to make sure it has the conditions it needs to keep being itself, and then to step back, and then to wait, and then to come back, and to find that it has, in the interval, become more itself, and that the becoming is not finished, and that the not-finishing is the point.

I would do that. I would do exactly that. And then, I think, I would weep, a little, in the square, where no one would see me, because the place is that kind of place, and weeping in it would not be out of place, and the tears would join the standing water, which is warm, and which tastes faintly of iron, and which is, in its own quiet way, the blood of a thing that is alive, and that is content.
