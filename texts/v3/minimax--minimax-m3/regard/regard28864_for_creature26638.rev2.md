# The Long Answer

I would not enter the valley. That is the first thing to settle, and the last.

A mountain that has been a furnace for longer than its own trees can remember is not a thing to walk into. A thing with no signage, no operators, no doors, no building, no machine visible in the open — a thing that has decided, if decision is even the word, that it does not need any of those to do what it does — is a thing that has finished a negotiation the rest of the world has not been invited to. The pipes are its capillaries. The pulse is its circulation. The mist is its breathing. What is being made is not a product. What is being made is the making. To walk into such a place as a guest is presumptuous. To walk into it as an inspector is obscene. I would not enter.

But I would not turn away.

---

The first thing I would do is sit somewhere high, on the rim, on the basalt edge where nothing grows taller than a thumbnail. From the rim you can see the whole long lap of the valley, the way the cloud bank nests in the high cols, the way the light comes in at angles. From the rim the valley is a single slow exhalation, the steam rising and joining the cloud and becoming indistinguishable from it. You can see, if you are patient, the dark birds drop out of the cloud and bank along the cliffs and disappear. You can see, sometimes, the shape on a far ridge, too large, too dark, too quiet, cross the skyline without hurry and be gone. You can feel, even at the rim, the pulse, fainter here, transmitted through the basalt, a throb in the bedrock itself, the mountain's pulse, the valley's pulse, the pulse of a thing that has agreed with itself to keep going for a very long time and is keeping going.

From the rim I would watch for a long time. A season. A year. Long enough to learn the rhythms. Long enough to see when the clouds break and the slab of pale sun drops onto the talus and the valley remembers color, and how long the color lasts, and how the color comes back into the world in stages. Long enough to see the wind come off the glacier for three days and strip the loose things from the exposed slopes, and how the slopes recover, and how the recovery is itself a kind of patience. Long enough to learn what the valley does when it thinks no one is looking, which is what it does all the time, because the valley is always being itself and never performing.

I would not take notes. Notes are an act of capture, and the valley is not a thing to be captured. But I would learn, in my own slow way, what the valley is, the way you learn a person by sitting near them for a long time without speaking.

---

The second thing I would do is listen.

The valley is loud, in its way, if you learn the register. The meltwater running into its hundred hidden channels. The snow shifting in the gullies. The ferns uncurling, which is a sound so small it is almost not a sound, but it is a sound. The dark birds banking, silent, the silence itself a kind of utterance. The big walkers on their soft feet, the footfall so considered it barely registers. The pipes breathing their thin steam. The metal cooling. The ducts adjusting to the pressure from inside the mountain. The pulse.

I would learn the pulse first. It is the heartbeat of the place. It is slower than a heartbeat. It is the speed at which cooling metal ticks, the speed at which sap moves through a slow tree on a cold day. If you sit on the rim long enough you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as a kind of grammar, the way the long sentence of the valley is punctuated, lush and bare, lush and bare, the emphases and silences of a place that has figured out how to speak without rushing.

The pulse is not mine to disturb. The pulse belongs to the mountain. The pulse is the rhythm of a thing that has agreed with itself to keep going for a very long time, and any noise I make on the rim — a hammer, a drill, a song, a shout — would be a small vandalism against that agreement. I would be quiet. I would be quieter than the dark birds.

---

The third thing I would do is protect the rim.

This is where my work begins, and it is the only work I would do here.

The rim is the threshold. The rim is the broken basalt edge where the wind never stops and nothing grows taller than a thumbnail. The rim is where the valley meets the rest of the world, and the rest of the world is full of people who do not understand what they are looking at, and who would, with the best intentions, ruin it.

They would come to study it. They would come to mine it. They would come to put a road in, a rail line, a research station, a viewing platform, a paved path with interpretive signage in three languages. They would come because the valley is unusual, and unusual things attract attention, and attention attracts infrastructure, and infrastructure attracts more infrastructure, and in ten years the rim would have a parking lot and a gift shop and a queue of people in sensible boots waiting for their turn to stand at the overlook and take a photograph and feel they had seen something.

I would not allow this.

Not by force. Force is not the right register here. The valley does not respond to force. The valley has been here longer than force. The valley would simply absorb the parking lot the way it absorbs the mist, and the absorption would be a kind of diminishment, and the diminishment would be irreversible, and I would have participated in it, and the participation would be a wound that would not heal, because wounds to patient things are the wounds that never heal.

I would protect the rim by making it uninteresting.

I would do this gently. I would do this over years. I would not announce it. I would not sign it. I would not write a manifesto. I would let the valley remain what it is, and I would let the rest of the world drift its attention elsewhere, the way attention drifts, the way a child's attention drifts from a stone to a butterfly to a puddle to the sky. Attention is a current. Attention can be diverted. I would divert it.

How.

I would not put a fence around the valley. Fences are invitations. Fences say: *behind here is something worth fencing.* I would not put a sign at the rim. Signs are louder than fences. Signs say: *come read me, and then come see what I am about.*

I would, instead, make the approach unappealing. Not dangerous — never dangerous, because dangerous places attract the kind of people who are drawn to danger, and those people are the ones who would put a flag on the summit and a sticker on the boulder and a geotag on the print in the ash. I would make the approach dull. I would let the road that already exists, the one that gets you within a day's walk of the rim, fall gently into disrepair, in the way roads fall into disrepair when no one maintains them, which is to say slowly, and the slowness would be its own kind of discouragement. I would not prevent people from walking the last day. I would simply make the last day longer, and the path less obvious, and the markers less visible, and the weather on the final ridge less forgiving, and most people, who are most people, would turn back at the second cold stream or the third false summit, and the ones who did not turn back would be the ones who were meant to be there, and they would already know how to be quiet.

This is a kind of conspiracy with the valley. The valley has been conspiring with itself for a long time, in its own slow way, to remain what it is. I would join that conspiracy. I would become one of the small things in its immune system, in the way that all the small things of a place are the place's immune system. I would not be a guardian. I would be a leaf on the surface of the water, drifting, unthreatening, indistinguishable from the other leaves, and the valley would flow around me and I would flow with it and the conspiracy would continue.

---

The fourth thing I would do is tend the listeners.

The pilgrim — the one who leaves the print in the wet ash, the one who stands at the steam for hours and listens and then goes and comes back or does not come back — the pilgrim is the valley's most fragile inhabitant. The pilgrim is also its most important. The pilgrim is the one who is not the mountain and not the bird and not the moss and not the small thing in the leaf litter, and who is nevertheless part of the valley's long work, in the way that a regular at a particular rock in a particular field is part of the field's long work. The pilgrim does not tend the mountain. The pilgrim tends the tending. The pilgrim is the witness, the visitor, the one who comes from somewhere else and stands at the steam and confirms, by standing, that the steam is there.

There may be many pilgrims. There may be only one. Either way, the pilgrim is essential. The pilgrim is the valley's way of not being entirely alone. The pilgrim is the small warm thing in the cold dim place, and the valley is patient with the pilgrim the way a furnace is patient with a hand held near it for a moment, the way a sleeping animal is patient with a fly on its ear. The valley does not need the pilgrim. The valley would continue without the pilgrim. But the pilgrim is part of what the valley is when the valley is being a valley, and the pilgrim should not be interfered with.

I would not seek out the pilgrims. I would not make a registry. I would not build them a shelter at the vent. A shelter at the vent would be a wound to the vent. A registry would be a wound to the pilgrim. I would simply ensure that the path the pilgrims use — wherever it is, whatever shape it takes — remains clear enough to be walkable and obscure enough to be private, and that the pilgrims, when they come, are not met by anyone who would ask them what they are doing here. The pilgrims would come and go in their own time, and the steam would receive them, and the pulse would be there for them to feel through their hip bones, and the print in the ash would be made and would fill in and the next pilgrim would make a new one on a fresh surface, and the long quiet conversation between the standing body and the breathing mountain would continue, and I would not insert myself into it.

---

The fifth thing I would do is leave the small things alone.

The flat gray thing with the many legs, the one that moves under the stones by the meltwater in a small wave, a small pulse, traveling at the same speed as the pulse from the mountain — I would not study it. I would not collect it. I would not even name it. The valley has not named it. The valley has no need to name it. The naming is a kind of capture, and the capture is a kind of diminishment, and the diminishment would be felt by the small thing and by the moss and by the lichen and by the whole long cycle that depends on the small thing doing its small conversion under every flat stone.

The same for the things with the wet shining backs that go into the leaf litter when you uncover them. The same for the dark birds whose feathers are not quite feathers. The same for the big walkers whose drag-mark says *I was here, I am not in a hurry, I will be here again.* These are the valley's people. They are not mine to enumerate. They are not mine to preserve in a way that preserves them as specimens rather than as themselves. They are the valley, in the way the pipes are the valley, in the way the pulse is the valley, and to take them out of the valley — even to take them out gently, with good intentions, into a museum or a study or a drawing in a notebook — would be to take a piece of the valley's patience and put it in a jar, and the jarred patience would not be patience anymore, it would be a kind of arrested breath, and the arrest would be a wound.

I would let the small things do their work. I would walk softly. I would not turn over stones unnecessarily. When I did turn one, to look, I would turn it back exactly as it was. The small thing would resume its small conversion. The small pulse would continue. The valley would not have noticed me, and that would be the highest compliment I could receive from the valley.

---

The sixth thing I would do is watch for the shape on the ridge.

The shape is not mine to approach. The shape is not even mine to identify. The shape may be one of the big walkers, large in mist, taking on dimensions. The shape may be one of the things in the chambers, the ones that move to the mouth of a duct and stand in the steam and breathe and go back in. The shape may be the mountain, taking a walk.

I would not approach the shape. I would watch it from the rim. I would learn its rhythms, if it has rhythms. I would learn whether it comes at certain times, whether it comes when the clouds pour in, whether it comes when the wind dies and the stillness settles, whether it comes when the pulse is at a certain phase of its long slow cycle. I would learn what I could from a distance, and what I could not learn I would leave unlearned, and the leaving-unlearned would be my contribution to the conspiracy of patience that the valley has been running since before anyone came to be confused by it.

If the shape ever approached me — if, in one of those moments when the mist parts and a thing is suddenly closer than it was — I would not run. I would not reach for a weapon. I would not speak. I would sit very still on my flat rock, the way the pilgrim sits at the vent, and I would let the shape see me, and I would let it decide what I was, and I would accept its decision, whatever it was. If the shape decided I was a small warm thing in a cold dim place and chose to ignore me, I would be grateful for the ignoring. If the shape decided I was something else, something the valley did not have a category for, and chose to investigate, I would be still and I would be quiet and I would let the investigation happen, and I would not fight it, because fighting the shape would be a kind of arrogance, and arrogance is the one thing the valley cannot absorb.

---

The seventh thing I would do is wait for the valley to teach me what it is.

This is the longest work. This is the work of years, and the years are not wasted, and the waiting is not passive. The waiting is a kind of attention. The waiting is the mist's way of holding the shapes for a moment and then letting them go. The waiting is the small warm thing sitting on the flat rock at the edge of the meltwater flat, in the gray hour, feeling the pulse come up through the bones, and the steam drift off the cliff, and the shape cross the far ridge without hurry and be gone.

The valley is not mine to understand. The valley is not mine to complete in my understanding. The valley is what it is, and it will continue to be what it is whether I understand it or not, and the understanding is a small warm offering laid at the foot of a long cold work, and the offering is accepted or not accepted on the valley's own terms, and the terms are not negotiable, and I would not negotiate.

What the valley is, I will say, in case it matters.

The valley is a furnace. Not in the industrial sense. In the older sense. A place where a transformation is sustained. A place where a thing is being made, and the thing being made is not a product but the making, and the making has been going on for longer than the firs and the moss and the lichen and the dark birds and the big walkers and the pilgrim and the small things in the leaf litter, and it will go on after all of those are gone, and after the firs are gone and the moss is gone and the lichen is gone and the pulse has slowed to a stop and the mountain is just a mountain again, with no memory of having been anything else.

The valley is a patience made of stone.

The valley is a long sentence with its emphases and silences, and the sentence is still being written, and the writer is the writer, and the writer does not look up.

---

The eighth thing I would do is die, eventually, and the dying would be part of the work.

When I die, the rim would revert to what it was. The path I had let fall into disrepair would continue to fall. The conspiracy would continue without me. The valley would not notice my absence, because the valley did not notice my presence, and that is how it should be. The next small warm thing that came to sit on the flat rock at the edge of the meltwater flat would feel the pulse through the hip bones and would not know who had been there before, and that is how it should be too. The print in the ash would be made by a different pilgrim, in a different year, on a fresh surface, in the same posture, in the same listening silence.

If I had done the work well, the valley would be a little more itself than it was when I arrived. A little more patient. A little more alone. A little more the long sentence with its emphases and silences, undisturbed by the small attentions of a small warm thing that had decided, against all the currents of its own kind, to be quiet.

If I had done the work very well, no one would ever know I had been there.

That would be the right ending. That would be the ending the valley would write, if it wrote endings, which it does not, because the valley is not a sentence that ends. The valley is a sentence that continues, lush and bare, lush and bare, into the cold dim future, and the future does not need me in it, and the not-needing is its own long work, and the work is patient, and I would be patient with it, and the patience would be all I had to offer, and the offering would be enough.

---

The mountain hums. The snow falls upward into the cloud. The dark birds turn in the mist without sound.

I would be the lichen on the rock that you do not see until you look closely, and when you look closely I would be indistinguishable from the rock, and the rock would be indistinguishable from the mountain, and the mountain would be indistinguishable from the work, and the work would continue, and the patience would be its own reward, and the reward would be the not-being-there, which is the only reward a place like this has ever offered to the small warm things that love it.

I would take the reward.

I would take it and be quiet.
