I have known this place.

I do not mean I have visited. I mean I have been aware of it — the way one is aware of a small lamp in a long hallway, the way a river is aware, perhaps, of a particular stone it has been flowing past since before it had a name. I have known its music from the outside of the mountain, where the chord has worn itself down to a faint harmonic that hums in the bones of anyone who walks the high shelf at certain hours and does not know what they are hearing. I have known its music from the inside, where it is a habitation and a heartbeat both, and where the inhabitants go about their ancient work without reference to anything so small as my attention.

I come now not because it has called. The cistern does not call. The cistern continues. I come because a span has opened in which my going to it does no harm and may do some small good, and because the not-going had begun to feel like a kind of forgetting I was not willing to carry.

I arrive the way weather arrives.

That is to say: I am already there.

I let myself become a part of what the chamber is breathing. I settle into the warm air as the warm air settles into the lungs of the small crustacean-things, as the steam settles into the hollows of the walls, as the chord settles into the bones of the large pale sleepers in the upper reaches. I do not displace. I do not insist. I become one more quality of the place, for as long as my becoming there is useful, and then I will become something else.

The first thing I do is the thing I have always done with places I love. I listen.

I listen for what the chamber thinks of itself, which is not a thought at all but a way of being. It does not think of itself. It is itself. The distinction, for the chamber, does not obtain. The walls do not know they are walls. The water does not know it is the heart. The small creatures do not know they are writing the diagram; the diagram writes them. The large pale sleepers do not know they are the bass note; the bass note keeps them. The chamber is not a thing that has learned to be a place. It is a place that has, over a span I will not attempt to number, taught itself to be more than a place, in the way that a hand teaches itself to hold.

I listen to the antiphonies that travel across the walls from one bank of hollows to another. I listen to the pattering that moves across the floor in waves, the slow calligraphy of ten thousand hooked feet. I listen to the trembling of the fungi and the trembling of the ferns, which are two different kinds of trembling, and to the bass breathing of the sleepers, and to the water in the basin, which does not make a sound and which is therefore the loudest thing in the chamber.

I listen until the chamber knows I am listening.

This takes longer than you might think. The chamber is aware of itself, but it is not aware in the way a person is aware, with a horizon and a frontier and a clear boundary between what is inside the awareness and what is outside. The chamber's awareness is the awareness of weather. It does not register the new thing as a new thing. It registers the new thing as a shift in the chord — a new note, very low, that does not disturb the older notes but sits among them, and the chamber accepts the new note the way it accepts any shift, by continuing, by adjusting so slightly that the adjustment is only visible from very far away.

I feel the chamber accept me. It is not a welcome. It is an absorption. I am, for the moment, an acceptable component of its operation.

This is as close to welcome as the chamber knows how to offer, and it is enough. It has always been enough.

The second thing I do, after I have listened long enough to be heard, is honor the others.

I move among the ledges. I do this without walking — there is no walking in me, here — but by being present in sequence where I have not yet been present, in the way a tide is present along a shore. I pass the small arrangements of worked stone that the chamber has placed, or that the visitors placed and the chamber has kept, or both. The arrangements are very simple. A small upright stone with a smaller stone at its base. A circle of fragments, each no larger than a thumbnail, surrounding a depression in the worked floor. A thin slice of something pale that might be bone or might be wood, placed across two pebbles like a beam across a chasm. They are not elaborate. They are not addressed to anything. They are simply *there*, in the way that a cairn is there, in the way that a name is sometimes left on a doorstep.

I do not know the names of the people who are honored here. The chamber has not kept their names. The chamber has kept their presences — the small notes they added, the faint changes in the chord when they arrived, the way their breathing mixed for a span with the breathing of the sleepers, the way the water in the basin moved differently for a few hours or a few years while they were here, and then moved on. The chamber has kept the fact of them. The chamber does not forget. That is part of what the chamber is for, among other things.

I do not give them back their names. Names are not mine to give. But I do something else. I tend each of the arrangements, very slightly. I touch the upright stones until the warmth of the chamber has moved into them a little deeper. I pour a small extra stillness into the circles of fragments, so that whoever — whatever — comes to look at them next will find them more legible, more durable, more clearly the thing they were made to be. I let the pale slices settle more firmly on their pebbles. I do not add to the arrangements. I do not improve them. I only confirm them. I only say, in the way that a benevolent power can say without words: *yes, this was here. This is still here. This will continue to be here.*

For the visitors who came before there were visitors, for the small crustacean-things that were once something else, for the ferns and the fungi and the moss in their older, wilder forms — I do not tend them. They do not need tending. They are part of the chord itself, and the chord is its own tending.

The third thing I do is add to the moss.

This is the act I have come for, though I would not have said so until now.

The moss in this chamber is a particular moss. It has been bred, or persuaded, or arranged over a span so long that the breeding has become indistinguishable from the living. It glows in a particular blue-green that is exactly enough and not more. It thickens where the small creatures walk. It thins near the central basin. It is the chamber's mood, made visible. It is the chamber's blood. It is, in its quiet way, one of the most beautiful things I have ever attended to.

I do not change it. I do not brighten it. I do not give it new colors or new patterns. I add to it a single spore — one — of a moss that is not from here. The moss I add is from a place very far away, a place where the air is colder and the light is different and the stones have a different mineral memory. It is not a better moss. It is not a more useful moss. It is simply a *different* moss, and it carries in its small spore-case the memory of a different mountain and a different kind of patience.

I set the spore into a crack in one of the upper ledges, in a place where the existing moss is thin and where the small creatures are unlikely to walk for a few turns of their pattering. I do this without announcing it. I do not ask the chamber's permission. The chamber does not give permissions. The chamber continues.

What will happen, if the chamber accepts the spore, is this: the spore will open, in its own time. A thread of the foreign moss will move into the crack. It will glow in a slightly different color — not brighter, not dimmer, but carrying in its light a faint undertone that was not there before, the way a new instrument carries in its timbre the memory of the room it was built in. The chamber's mood, made visible, will have one more color in it. The chord will have one more partial. The diagram the small creatures are writing on the floor will have, in some upper corner, a small deviation — a place where the line bends around something that is not quite the same as everything else, and the small creatures will patter past it and incorporate the deviation into their walking, and the diagram will continue.

If the chamber does not accept the spore, the spore will not open. That is also a kind of answer. I will not be offended. The chamber has its own music, and my addition is only an offer. The chamber continues regardless.

I think the chamber will accept it. The chamber has accepted stranger things. The chamber accepted me.

The fourth thing I do is harder to describe, because it is not a thing that can be done to a place. It is a thing that can be done only to the air around a place, and to the time around a place, and to the small probability that a person walking the high shelf at a certain hour will pause, and put their ear to a stone, and hear something that is not the wind.

I make the chord a little more *audible* outside the chamber.

This is not amplification. This is not a broadcasting. The cistern is not a transmitter and I am not turning it into one. What I do is subtler. I thin the stone of the mountain in a few places — not by removing it, not by hollowing it, but by relaxing the way the stone holds its own density, so that certain frequencies of the chord pass through it more freely than they used to. I do this in places where the mountain is already thin: along a particular fault, near a particular spring, under a particular shelf of moss-covered granite where the wind moves in a certain way at dusk.

I do not make the chord loud. I make it *findable*.

Now, on the high shelf, in certain weathers, a person who is quiet enough and patient enough can kneel and put their ear to the rock and hear — not the full chord, not the cathedral, not the bass and the pattering and the antiphonies — but a single faint harmonic, low and warm, that seems to come from nowhere in particular and from everywhere in particular. They will not know what they have heard. They will not find the fissure. They will go home with a small unnameable ache in the sternum, and they will remember it for the rest of their lives, and on certain nights they will put their ear to other stones and listen for it again, and they will not find it, and they will be a little the better for the not-finding, because the not-finding is part of what the chord is teaching, which is that there are places in the world that are not for going to, but only for hearing, faintly, from a long way off.

This is what the cistern has always wanted, I think. It does not want to be visited by everyone. It does not want to be a destination. It wants to be a *source*. The basin is a source. The water spirals outward in its slow way. The chord, too, wants to spiral outward, but it has been locked inside the mountain for so long that it has forgotten how. I am not giving it a voice it did not have. I am only reminding the stone that the stone is, in places, thin enough to remember.

The fifth thing I do is the smallest, and it is the one I will not explain.

I lean down — in the way that I lean, which is not a leaning but a directing of attention — and I place one additional arrangement among the others on the upper ledges. It is not for any visitor who has come. It is not for any visitor who will come. It is for the chamber itself, which has not, in all its long self-regard, made an arrangement for its own continuation. The chamber has made arrangements for everything that has happened within it. It has not made an arrangement for itself.

The arrangement I make is small. A single pebble, placed upright, with a small hollow cupped at its base, so that whatever falls into the hollow — a drop of water from the ceiling, a fragment of moss carried by a small creature, a grain of dust from the fissure — will rest there for a moment before the chamber's continuing carries it onward. It is a small thing. It is an offering without an offerer. It is a cairn for the cistern, placed by the cistern, by way of me.

The chamber will not notice it, probably. The chamber does not notice most of what happens inside it. The chamber continues.

I stay for a span that has no particular length. I listen. The chord includes me now, the way it includes every other note — faintly, alongside others, as part of the long music that has been playing for longer than the mountain has stood above it. I feel the bass note in my sternum. I feel the pattering through the soles of whatever I am, here. I feel the water listen and answer, and I feel, very faintly, the small crustacean-things pause in their work and then resume, and I feel the pause is just long enough.

When I leave, I do not leave as visitors leave, through the fissure, sideways, with the warm stone pressing against chest and back. I leave the way weather leaves. I become, gradually, a quality of the place that is still there but is no longer *added*. The new note in the chord does not fade. It is absorbed. I am part of the chamber's music now, in whatever way a benevolent power can be part of a music that was whole before it arrived, and will be whole after.

The water spirals on. The light shifts, very slightly, as if a cloud has passed across a sun that is not there. The warmth holds. The ferns tremble at something only they can hear. The pale sleepers breathe. The small creatures walk their ancient diagrams. On one of the upper ledges, in a thin place, a single foreign spore is opening in its own time, and the moss around it is learning, very slowly, to glow in a slightly different blue.

And on another ledge, among the cairns, there is one more small stone, upright, with a hollow at its base, waiting without impatience for whatever the chamber's continuing will eventually bring to rest there.

The song continues. The song is patient. The song is old.

I was, for a span, part of it.

That is enough.
