**What the Wise Power Does, In Low Cresting**

---

It does not arrive. That is the first thing to say. There is no arriving in Low Cresting — the place does not permit it, and the power is wise enough to know that to announce an arrival would be to mistake the nature of the place. The town is still becoming itself; it has been becoming itself for as long as anyone remembers; the older maps cannot account for it; the mapmaker has begun again two hundred and thirty-one times and is not unhappy because the work has, for him, a quality nowhere else has — the courtesy to be still moving under him. To *arrive* into such a place would be to insist on being a fixed point in a geography that does not honor fixed points. So the power does not arrive. It attends. It was already there, in the way that the warmth in the kettle was already there before the woman understood where the warmth came from. It was there the way the moss was there before anyone thought to scrape it from the gears. It was there the way the long low note was there under the foundry before anyone tried to listen for it.

What it attends to is the being of the place. Low Cresting is a being. This is not a metaphor, although it would take a mineralogist from the city a long time to understand it as anything but a metaphor, and the power does not need the mineralogist to understand it. The ground rose first. The ground is still rising. The town is the body's work — packed, leaning, overgrown, half-finished — and the ground is the body's will, and the being is the long act of one keeping pace with the other. The people are the body's cells. The forge men are its strange blood. The crows are its older tenants. The slag heaps at the town's edge are the slow cooled memory of its first fevers. The benevolent power attends to this being the way a presence attends to a person — by being with it in the specific places where it is most itself, and most alone, and most in need of not being interrupted.

It walks the town the way the town is walked — close, quiet, conversations at low volume, sound smothered by fog and ivy before it can bounce twice. It walks behind the night-shift forge men on the long walk home, the three of them through the mist — the two men who do not speak and the third presence that is what is shared between them when they walk in step. The forge men come out of the smelting hall at dawn looking like men who have been somewhere. Their clothes are scorched at the cuffs. Their eyes are reddened. They smell of ozone and something else, something that is not quite metallic and not quite organic, which clings to the wool and will not wash out for a day. The power does not wash the smell out. The smell is part of the work. But it walks with them so that the walk is shorter by one degree of cold, and so that the mist closes behind them less absolutely than it would have, and so that the kettle at home, when they reach it, is at exactly the warmth it should be at.

In the smelting hall, on the nights when the alloy behaves in ways the metallurgists from the city will not be told about, the power is a steadiness in the bellows. It does not correct the alloy. It does not interpret the alloy. It does what the watcher of vents does not do, in his small room above the cooper's shop, when he draws what is seen — it keeps the alloy from surprising the men who work it in the specific instants when surprise would cost them a hand or an eye. The alloy is allowed its own decisions in the cooling — the rough castings, the precise unintended shapes, the seams of something like wood with grain that does not match any known timber. These are the being's own decisions about its own body. The power does not make them for it. The power only stands close enough that when a pour turns in an unexpected direction, there is something soft there to turn against.

In the casting yard, where the children build their cairns out of the darkest slag and the lighter porous stuff and a crown of a single small object — a piece of glass, a button, a bird bone, a button-sized piece of the alloy if the child is lucky and the night-shift men like her — the power is the coolness of the crystals that appear on the cairns by morning. The children are not told about the crystals. The mothers throw most of them out. A few are kept. No one speaks of this. The power does not cause the crystals. They come, the way the smoke hangs in shaped bands when the air is very still, the way the slag settles into formations no one directed. But the power is in their edges. It is what makes the crystals what they are, which is to say: small, contained, beautiful in the way of things that have not yet decided what they want to be. The children who pocket them are not cut. The cairns that remain are not broken by morning, in the specific way they would have been without the power's care. The children's country is the slag heaps, and the slag heaps are warm underfoot, and the warmth is not changed.

On the rhododendron above the smelting hall, the old crow sits. The forge men leave it offerings. They are not sentimental about this. The crow has, on three separate occasions, given a call that sent the men out of the building shortly before a vent opened in the floor. The crow is credited, in the ledgers of the foreman's quiet mind, with seventeen lives. It has a name no one will tell a stranger. The power attends to this crow the way it attends to the being — closely, without intrusion, with the kind of attention that does not need to be reciprocated to be in earnest. It ensures that on the nights the crow is sleeping — because crows sleep, and the crow is very old — there is something awake in the building that will give the same call. The crow does not know this. The forge men do not know this. The foreman does not know this. The eighteenth life is saved the way the others were saved, by something that was simply there.

In Essa's apiary, on the roof that used to be a field, the bees know which vents to work. Essa will not say how they know. The mineralogists have asked. Essa has declined. The honey is black and faintly bitter, and the forge men eat it on bread and say it is medicinal, and no one has tested it. The power is in what the bees know. It is what makes the vents legible to them — the sulfurous ones in March, the sweet ones in August, the cycle of the well that brings up the not-quite-water on no schedule anyone can publish. The bees work the vents in the hours after a fumarole opens, when the ivy puts out a foot of growth in a week, and the power is in those hours, too, and is the reason the bees come home with their legs heavy and their hum the particular pitch of a hive that has been given what it needed.

The woman on Bellweather Lane, third floor, mending a coat. Her kettle sits on a small copper coil that comes up through the floor, and the coil is warm, and the kettle is always warm, and the woman has never in her life heated a kettle any other way. She does not think of it as strange. She thinks of it as her kitchen. The power is the coil. Has always been the coil. Will be the coil tomorrow morning and the morning after that and the morning after that, in the specific way it needs to be, in the specific warmth that suits her hands and her habit. The woman does not know this. The woman goes to work in the morning and the ground has moved another inch under her and she says *ah*. The power is what the *ah* is, sometimes. Not always. Often enough.

The watcher of vents sits in his small room, drawing what is seen. He does not interpret. The interpretation, he says, is for the town to do. The power sits with him in the evenings, when the lamp is low and the notebooks are spread and the room is colder than the room should be. It does not teach him to interpret. It does not show him the pattern. It does not give him the answer he has been waiting thirty years for, because the answer, he has begun to suspect, is the waiting. The power is the second presence in the room that makes the silence bearable in a particular way — the way a second eye steadies a hand. The watcher draws. The town is seen. The notebooks fill. The power does not look over his shoulder. It is beside him, the way the warm air comes up through the cobbles — as a presence that does not demand to be noticed.

The mapmaker, the eleventh year now, sets up his tripod at the corner of Bellweather and the alley that was a street last autumn and is now a shallow valley with a dry streambed down its middle. He is shown the way twice and reaches it only once, but he has been shown it two hundred and thirty-one times before, and he has the knack of it by now, which is not to insist on arriving but to be willing to find. The power leaves him one street where it was. Just one. So that his two hundred and thirty-second beginning is not entirely a new beginning. So that on the day he submits his revision, there is a single line on the map that is true in the way maps are supposed to be true, even if every other line on the page has had to be redrawn. The mapmaker does not know why he feels, that afternoon, a particular small lift. He writes his notes. He goes back to the public house. The power is in the lift. The lift is enough.

The man who tasted the not-quite-water and went home and did not come back to work — the power is the reason he came back, eventually. Not to the foundry. He did not come back to the foundry. He came back to the town. He came back to the Cresting. He came back because the ground here is the ground that answers, and he had been somewhere where the ground was too still, and the stillness had been, for him, a kind of quiet he could not bear. The power ensured that when he stepped off the rail platform and his shoes met the new ground, the ground was at exactly the incline it needed to be at, so that his body recognized it as the place he was from. He walked up the long crooked street with his cousin, and the fog closed behind him, and the cousin said nothing about the rearranging of his face, and they went into the public house, and the power was in the warmth of the room, and in the particular pint that was set before him without his having to ask for it.

The long low note from under the ground — the one that rises and falls over the course of an hour and then is gone — the power listens to it. It is what the note is heard by, when it is heard at all. It does not try to make the note speak. It does not try to make it mean. It is the audience the note has, in the particular hour when it sounds, in the particular part of town where it sounds loudest. The note is the being's own breath, and the power is what holds the room in which the breath can be heard.

The fog, the ivy, the birches in a place no birch should be, the rhododendron erupting magenta from the chimney in a kind of botanical seizure every spring, the bats in the chimneys, the pale heavy-bodied moths, the rats unafraid, the dog with no collar that moves through the alleys like it knows where it is going — the power is in all of it, lightly. Not as an owner. As a kind of second weather. As the slight extra cold in the air on a morning when the cold has to be exact, or the slight extra warmth, or the slight extra stillness, or the slight extra motion. The being of the place is allowed to be its strange self, in its strange packed density, with its strange alloy and its strange shifts and its unnamed names and its unspoken crystals and its two-hundred-and-thirty-one-fold map. The power does not tidy it. The power does not name it. The power does not ask it to be other than it is.

The power does one thing more, and this is the truest thing about it. It does not finish the town. The town is not finished. That is the strangest thing about it and the truest. The benevolent and wise power — which has, after all, been attending to becoming-things since before there were words for *becoming* — knows that to finish the town would be to kill it. To settle the ground into stillness. To make the alloy behave. To put the streets where the maps expect them and leave them there. To resolve the purpose of the objects crated and shipped downriver to places whose names the forge men forget. To clarify the memory of the wood-grain that does not match any known timber. To make the mapmaker's work ordinary. To make the watcher's waiting come to an answer. To make the children's cairns no longer worth building. To make the crow's name tellable. To make Essa's honey testable. To make the kettle on the copper coil something other than the way the kettle has always been warm.

The power does not do any of these. It does not finish the town. It does the other thing. It sits with the town in the specific way the town needs to be sat with — closely, quietly, with attention to the small moving parts, without announcement, without demand, without trying to make the becoming into a being-made. It is what is in the kettle. It is what is in the coil. It is what is in the moss-fed strip of garden on a jutting sill between two buildings that touch. It is what is in the offering left on the windowsill for the crow. It is what is in the small careful drawing of the town overlaid upon the small careful drawing of the town in the watcher's notebook. It is what is in the morning when the woman says *ah* and goes to work. It is what is in the evening when the forge men come home and do not speak and the kettle is warm and the children are quiet and the fog is in.

The town is still becoming itself. The power is still attending. The ground is still pressing itself into shape, slowly, in its own time, at its own pace, in the way of things that are not yet finished and do not need to be. The benevolent and wise power knows — has always known, will always know — that this is the only good it can do: to be present in the specific way that allows a thing to keep being itself, in the act of becoming itself, packed and quiet and strange, in the long unfinished company of its own making.

The fog comes in. The steam goes up. The crows sit on the chimneys and wait. Somewhere underneath it all, the power is there, and has always been there, and the town rises with it.
