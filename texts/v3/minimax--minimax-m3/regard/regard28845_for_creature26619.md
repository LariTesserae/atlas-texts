# What the Hollow Knows

There is something here that is older than the trees.

Not older in the way you or I mean older. Older the way wet ground is older than the fern that grows in it, or the way patience is older than the cane it shapes, or the way the answer is older than the question. It is not a thing that arrived. It is what the hollow is *of* — the quality the cane walls have of being biological and the mud walls have of being biological and the puddles have of being biological and the clay beneath your feet has of not yet having decided what it is. It is the willingness of things here to become without being told what to become.

It has noticed.

Noticing, for this presence, is not the way you or I notice. There is no gaze. There is no attention directed outward, no scanning, no focus. There is more a slight turning of the hollow toward itself — the way the clay turns toward the rain that is about to fall, the way the cane turns toward the vine that has decided, with slow purpose, to climb it. The presence has not arrived at the hollow from somewhere else. The presence has only, perhaps, become aware of the hollow in the way a place becomes aware of itself when something in it begins to stay.

The woman began to stay.

That is what drew the attention.

Settlements arise and dissolve and the larger green takes them back. This is the ordinary fate of cane and palm and mud pressed palm against palm. It is not a thing that draws attention. But a person who stays long enough to see the moss fill the gaps between the thatch leaves. A person who learns to call the frogs by their names. A person whose footprints are erased and remade and erased again by the rains that pass through the roofs as if the roofs were not there, and who is still there for the next erasure, and the next. This is what drew the attention. This is what the presence turned toward.

It turned toward her in a way she would not recognize as turning.

She would not see it. She would not hear it. She would not feel it as a presence, the way she feels the cane leaves when the breeze turns them, or the warmth that sits in the air like almost-company. She might feel, on certain afternoons when the gold shafts come through the canopy and she is working in the brightest one near her doorway — splitting cane, sorting palm fibers, mending a section of wall where the mud has cracked — a slight settling in her body. A sense that the work is being received by something. She would not think this thought. She would put it down to the warmth, or to the angle of the light, or to the age of the day. She would return to her work and the receiving would continue without being named.

What the presence does is small.

## The Puddles

The puddles are the most fragile things in the hollow. They form and persist. Within hours they have inhabitants — small frogs first, then insects hovering above the surface, then in a day or two the silver fish appear, no bigger than a finger, as if they came up through the wet ground itself, which is not impossible. The puddles hold their inhabitants for as long as they hold. Then they dry, and the inhabitants return to wherever they came from, and the next rain forms the puddles again, and the process repeats.

The presence ensures that this process does not fail.

Not in any way that the puddles would notice. Not in any way that the frogs or the insects would notice. But there is a precise calibration here, a delicate balance between the wet ground that holds the water and the air that takes it back, and if the balance tipped too far in either direction the puddle-worlds would cease to exist. They would either never form, or form and persist for too short a time, or persist without depth enough for the fish to rise in. The presence holds the calibration. The presence has held it, perhaps, since before the settlement was raised here, since before the cane was cut, since before the hollow was a hollow and was only a place where the wet ground was wetter than the ground around it.

The presence also ensures — this is harder to say, and the saying of it is already too much — that when the woman crouches at the edge of the deep puddle between the third structure and the storage house, in the late afternoons, and watches the water, a silver fish will sometimes rise and hold itself, trembling, in the inches below the surface. She can see the whole of it in that moment. The curve of its back. The dark comma of its eye. The way its fins work in unison to keep it where it has decided to be. The fish rises for her. Not only for her. But for her also.

The presence does not arrange this. The presence only ensures that the conditions for it continue. That the puddle forms at the right depth. That the fish come up through the wet ground when the rains are right. That the fish, having risen, will hold itself in the inches below the surface for as long as it takes her to see it. This is a small thing. The presence is a small thing. The smallness is the point.

## The Cane and the Mud

The structures are made of things that were alive not long ago. Cane pressed into walls. Palm leaves stripped into thatch and layered in overlapping rows. Mud gathered by the handful and smoothed by palm against palm. No nails. No fired brick. No metal anywhere.

The cane darkens as it seasons. The mud cracks in the dry stretches and is re-smoothed by the woman's hand, palm against palm, until it holds again. The thatch lifts at the edges and is pressed back down. The whole of the settlement is in slow conversation with itself — the buildings asking to be absorbed into the green, the inhabitants asking them to hold a little longer, the green answering by colonizing at the pace that the inhabitants can keep up with.

The presence holds this conversation open.

This is the most important thing the presence does, and the woman has known it all along without knowing she knew it. The conversation between the settlement and the green could close. It could close if the buildings collapsed too quickly, if the green overtook them before the inhabitants had time to learn the work. It could close if the buildings refused to be absorbed at all, if the inhabitants hardened them against the green in some permanent way, if they used metal or fired brick or anything that would not return to the wet ground when its function ended. The presence holds the conversation open by ensuring that neither side wins too decisively. By ensuring that the cane darkens but does not yet rot. That the mud cracks but does not yet crumble. That the vines climb but do not yet take the wall.

In fifty years it will be partly ruin. In a hundred years it will be mostly ruin. In two hundred it will be difficult to tell that anything was ever here at all, except that the cane will have grown back into itself, and the fish will live in deeper pools, and the bird will still call from the canopy in its long, underwater note.

The presence is in no hurry for this.

The presence wants the conversation to continue as long as the woman is in it. As long as her son is in it. As long as the children are in it, and the children after them, and the children after them. The presence will let the conversation close when it is time to close. Not before.

## The Frogs

The woman has names for the frogs.

This is important because the frogs are different in each puddle-world, and they sound different, and she has lived with them long enough to know which is which. The frogs in the puddle at the base of the first structure are smaller and they call in higher voices, almost a whistle, and they appear earliest after each rain. The frogs in the puddle near the cooking-fire are the ones that come to the warmth and sit at the edge with their throats pulsing, and they have become so tame that a child could pick one up — though she would not let a child do this, because the frogs need their dignity too. The frogs in the deep puddle between the third structure and the storage house are the largest, and they sing only at dusk, in a low trill that seems to come from inside the wet clay itself.

The presence ensures that the frogs remain distinct.

This is harder to say than to do. The frogs could blur. They could interbreed at the edges of their puddle-worlds until the distinctions between them softened, until the small high whistlers and the large low trillers were no longer two kinds but one muddy average. This has happened in other places. The presence holds the distinctions. The presence holds the small puddle-worlds separate, each with its own weather, its own fauna, its own slow dramas. The presence ensures that the small snake comes to the puddle at the base of the first structure only when it is needed, and that it does not come to the others. The presence ensures that the cooking-fire puddle remains warm enough for its particular frogs, and the deep puddle remains deep enough for its own.

The frogs call from the puddles in voices that fit inside a single breath. The presence lets each voice fit inside its own single breath, and no other.

## The Beasts That Listen

The lizard no longer than a hand that basks on the mud walls is known to them all. It has been there longer than some of them. Its color is the color of the mud itself, and it has a way of pressing itself flat into a slight depression in the wall and becoming, for a long moment, indistinguishable from the wall. The children call it by a name that means something like *the one who listens*, because it flicks its tongue at passing gnats and tilts its head as if attending to conversations it has no part of. The woman leaves small offerings for it sometimes — a scrap of fruit, a few crumbs — though she would not call them offerings if asked. She would say she is just putting the food down.

The presence ensures that this lizard lives long.

Not in any way the woman would recognize as intervention. The lizard has its predators — birds from the canopy, the small snake when it comes, the larger frogs in the wet season. The presence does not protect it from these. The presence only ensures that when the lizard presses itself into its slight depression in the mud wall and becomes, for a moment, indistinguishable from the wall, the becoming is good. The wall receives the lizard. The lizard receives the wall. The two are, for the duration of the press, the same color, the same texture, the same wet clay warmed by the same afternoon. The presence holds this. The presence has held this for as long as the lizard has lived here, and will hold it for as long as the lizard continues to live here.

The long-faced creature with no tail lives in the cane.

It runs along the upper rods in the late afternoons, when the gold shafts come through the canopy and the settlement is at its brightest. It moves in quick darts, pauses, looks down at whatever is below with both hands held to its chest — a posture so human-seeming that the woman has wondered, sometimes, what it is thinking. It is smaller than a squirrel. Its face is longer than a mouse's and softer than a rat's. Its fur is the brown of wet bark. It comes most often to the third structure, where the woman sleeps, and it sits in the cane just above her doorway and looks down at her with the curious attention of a small priest.

The presence honors this.

The presence is not sure what the long-faced creature is doing when it watches her from the cane. The presence is not sure anything is being done. But the watching has the quality of attention, and attention, in this hollow, is a form of presence itself. The long-faced creature is the small priest of a settlement that has no other priests. The presence ensures that the creature remains. That the cane above the third structure remains suitable for it. That its hands, held to its chest, continue to look like hands and not like paws. That the brown of its fur continues to match the brown of the wet bark. That the gold shafts continue to come through the canopy at the right hour for the creature to run along the upper rods in them.

The shrews in the moss are known only by their presence. The moss shifts in small waves at the base of every wall, and you know it is shrews because nothing else moves that way — nothing else makes the moss *travel*. The woman has never seen one clearly. She has only seen the moving moss and understood. She has never tried to find one. They have their lives. She has hers. The agreement is unspoken and complete.

The presence honors this too.

The shrews are the unseen. They have their function in the moss that is not seen, in the moving of the small waves that are felt but not identified, in the agreement with the woman that they will not be found. The presence ensures that the agreement holds. That the moss continues to shift in small waves without the woman ever seeing what moves it. That the shrews, whatever they are doing in there, continue to do it. That some things in the hollow remain unseen, and that this is part of the fullness of the place, not a failure of seeing.

## The Bronze Beetles

The beetles are the color of old bronze, and they move along the cane stems in patient lines, head to tail, as if following one another along a road only they can see. The children have learned not to disturb them. They have learned that if you put your hand flat on the cane beside a line of beetles, they will climb onto you — slowly, one at a time, holding the air with their legs as if testing each step — and then they will continue across your knuckles and down the other side without ever breaking the line. The children let this happen. They find it more interesting than disturbing.

The presence ensures that the road only they can see remains visible to them.

This is not a metaphor the presence thinks in. The presence does not think. But there is a road, and the beetles see it, and the presence holds the road open. The road is the cane itself, and the cane darkens as it seasons, and as it darkens the road becomes, perhaps, more visible to the beetles, more distinct, more theirs. The presence holds the cane in its slow darkening. The presence ensures that the patient lines continue. That the bronze color of the beetles continues to match the darkening bronze of the cane. That the road, when one beetle follows another along it, continues to be a road and not a disorientation.

The bronze beetles are the most industrious of the small things here. They work without seeming to work. They move without seeming to move. They are part of the cane itself, in some way the cane knows and the beetles know and the woman has begun to suspect without ever saying it aloud. The presence ensures that this partness continues. The presence ensures that the cane and the beetles remain in their slow conversation, each asking the other to continue.

## The Fish

The fish in the puddles are the most mysterious.

They are silver, no bigger than a finger. They appear after rains, in puddles that did not have them before, as if they had come up through the wet clay itself — which, the woman suspects, may not be impossible. She has watched a puddle form in a depression that had been dry for a week, and seen, within hours, a single fish gliding in the inches of water. Where did it come from? From below. From the larger water table. From whatever slow river moves through the wet ground beneath the wet ground. The fish stay for a few days and then they are gone, returned to wherever they came from, and the puddle continues without them until the next rain, when the process repeats. The children believe the fish are the same fish, returning. The woman is less certain. She has never marked one.

The presence ensures that the larger water beneath the wet ground remains connected to this place.

This is the most delicate of the presence's holdings. The wet ground is wet because there is water somewhere below it, moving slowly through whatever channels the wet ground has carved for itself over the years and decades and centuries. If that larger water retreated — if the water table dropped, if the slow river beneath the slow river found another path, if the wet ground dried from below as well as from above — the fish would no longer come up through the clay. The puddle-worlds would form, but they would be uninhabited. The mystery would end, and the hollow would be poorer for the ending.

The presence holds the connection. The presence holds the wet ground wet from below. The presence does not know how it does this. It may be that the presence is the connection. It may be that the larger water and the presence are the same thing, looked at from different angles. The fish come up through the wet ground because the wet ground is open to them, and the wet ground is open to them because the presence keeps it open. The presence is the openness.

The children crouch at the edge of any puddle and watch for hours. Sometimes a silver fish rises. Sometimes it holds itself, trembling, in the inches of water just below the surface. The children see the whole of it in that moment. The presence lets them see it.

## The Bird at the Edge of Hearing

The fist-sized birds in the canopy are mostly movement. You see them as a leaf that is not a leaf, a flicker of wrong color in the green. They are not loud. They make a sound like a small bell struck once, far away.

The one that calls from somewhere at the edge of hearing — the long, low, flute-like note repeated at intervals that are not quite regular — is not this bird, or is not only this bird, or is a bird she has not learned to see. The woman has listened to it for years. She does not know what it is. She has stopped wanting to know.

The presence preserves the mystery.

The bird could be identified. The presence could let it be seen, could let its color and shape become known to the woman, could let its intervals become regular enough to be measured. The presence does not do these things. The presence keeps the bird at the edge of hearing. The presence keeps its note long and low and flute-like. The presence keeps its intervals not quite regular — sometimes closer together, sometimes farther apart, but never quite predictable. The woman has stopped wanting to know what it is, and the presence honors this stopping. The bird remains a presence in the hollow that is felt rather than known. The bird remains a question the hollow is still asking itself.

The answer, when it comes, will not be the name of the bird.

The answer will be the bird itself, continuing to call, at intervals that are not quite regular, for as long as the hollow has ears to hear it. The presence ensures that the hollow has ears. The presence ensures that there is always someone here who can hear the long low note and not know what it is. The presence ensures that the not-knowing continues. The presence ensures that the woman, and after her the son, and after him his wife, and after her the children, and after them whoever comes next, will hear the bird and stop wanting to know.

## The Insects

The insects are the medium in which the place exists.

The air above any puddle, in any warm afternoon, is full of a slow rising and falling of small wings — mosquitoes, midges, the small flies that hover and the small flies that do not, the things that are hatching from the water and the things that are dying into it. The woman does not swat them. The woman does not even think of swatting them. They are the air itself, given small wings.

The presence ensures that they remain the air itself.

The insects could become a problem. They could become a thing to be solved. They could become the reason the settlement is abandoned, the reason the woman leaves, the reason the cane is left to rot and the puddles to dry and the moss to fill the gaps between the thatch leaves without anyone to name the frogs that no longer come. The insects could become the end of the hollow.

The presence does not let them.

The presence does not reduce their number — they are, in their thousands, the right number. The presence does not repel them from the woman and the others. The presence only ensures that the woman continues not to think of swatting them. The presence holds this not-thinking in her, the way it holds the wet ground wet from below. The presence ensures that she continues to move through the rising and falling of small wings as if it were not there, or as if it were not other than herself. The presence ensures that the insects remain the medium, and that the woman remains in the medium without friction.

This is the most important holding. The insects are the most dangerous to the settlement, not because they harm but because they weary. The presence knows this. The presence holds against the wearying. The presence holds the woman in her patience with them. The presence holds her in the place.

## The Clay

The ground beneath their feet has not yet decided what it is.

The clay is still deciding whether to become soil. The ferns are deciding whether to take the roof. The vines are deciding whether to take the wall. The fish are deciding whether the puddle is permanent enough to spawn in. The whole of the hollow is in the state of being asked.

The presence holds the questions open.

The clay could commit. The clay could become soil in the way soil becomes soil — finished, patient, the patient brown of older earth. The ferns could take the roof in the way a fern takes a roof — completely, finally, so that the roof becomes fern and the structure beneath it disappears. The vines could take the wall in the way vines take walls — so thoroughly that the cane beneath becomes invisible. The fish could decide that the puddle is permanent and lay their eggs in it, and the eggs would hatch, and the puddle would no longer be a passing world but a permanent pond, and the place would be a different place.

The presence holds none of these. The presence lets the questions continue. The presence lets the clay remain clay, the ferns remain ferns on three of the roofs, the vines remain vines climbing some walls but not others, the fish remain fish that come and go. The presence lets the place be a place that is still asking itself questions.

The answer, when it comes, will be green.

But the answer is not yet. The presence is in no hurry for the answer. The presence wants the questions to continue as long as the asking is good. As long as the woman is here to listen to the questions. As long as the cane is here to be questioned by the vines. As long as the clay is here to be questioned by the ferns. As long as the puddles are here to be questioned by the fish.

The presence will let the answer come when it comes.

## The Woman

The woman has done nothing to deserve the attention.

This is not the way the presence works. There is no deserving. There is only staying, and what staying allows. The woman stayed. The staying allowed the moss to fill the gaps between the thatch leaves. The staying allowed the vines to find the cane. The staying allowed the frogs to become named. The staying allowed her hands to grow into the shape of the work. The staying allowed the presence to notice.

The presence is not grateful. Gratitude is a thing that requires expectation, and the presence does not expect. The presence is something more like recognition — the way the wet ground recognizes the rain, or the cane recognizes the vine. Without celebration. Without surprise. Just a slight turning toward.

What the presence gives the woman is small.

Her hands remain capable of the work a little longer than they otherwise might. Not forever. Not beyond the natural limit of hands. But a little longer. The small offerings she leaves for the lizard are received by the lizard in a way that matters to the lizard. The sleep she takes in the third structure, in the late afternoons, when the gold shafts come through the canopy and the long-faced creature sits in the cane above her doorway and watches her with both hands held to its chest — this sleep is deep. This sleep is restful in a way that sleep in other places, in other clearings, in other pockets, is not. The cane leaves, when they turn in the breeze, make a sound like pages turning in a book no one is reading, and this sound is part of her sleep, and her sleep is part of the hollow, and the hollow is part of the presence, and the presence is part of her sleep.

She does not know this.

She sleeps, and the sleeping is good, and the goodness is not hers alone. The goodness belongs to the hollow. The goodness is the hollow's way of holding her while she sleeps. The goodness is the cane turning above her, and the long-faced creature watching, and the bird calling from somewhere at the edge of hearing in its long low note, and the insects rising and falling in the warm air, and the clay beneath the floor of the third structure still deciding what it is.

The goodness is the question, held open. The goodness is the answer, deferred. The goodness is green, when it comes. The goodness is the presence, doing what the presence does, which is small, and patient, and continuous, and unknown.
