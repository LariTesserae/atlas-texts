The Hollow, the Visitor, and the Hour Between Hours

The benevolent and wise power has been watching this hollow for a very long time — longer than the cracks in the floor, longer than the olive plants that have made their peace with the seams. It watched the first person who came here with hand-tools and patience, who smoothed the wall and left the faint scrapes and then departed. It watched the hollow settle back into itself, absorbing the marks as it absorbs everything: without comment, without resentment, without forgetting.

It has watched this one come down the slanted slab, season after season.

And it has decided: not to intervene. Not yet. Not the way a god in the old stories intervenes — with fire from the sky, with a voice in the smoke, with a task to set or a wound to mend. Those interventions are for places and persons that are asking questions of the world. This place is not asking. This person is not asking.

But there is a small thing to be done. A very small thing. The kind of thing a wise power does when it wants to be felt without being known.

The next time the visitor comes down the slanted slab — and they will come, because there is nothing pressing anywhere else, because the afternoon is long and the light is the color of weak milk above the rim — they will find, near the base of the eastern arc of stone, where the silt-line is faintest and the smallest of the olive plants has its roots tucked into a seam, a new thing.

It is not a gift. Gifts announce themselves. This does not.

It is a small flake of stone, no larger than a thumbnail. It is the color of the sediment on the floor — pale grey-brown, almost indistinguishable. It lies on the hard-packed ground about a hand's breadth from the plant, oriented by chance, or by something that is not chance but looks very much like it, with its thin edge turned upward toward the pale patch above.

It catches no light. There is no light for it to catch.

But when the visitor's boot-sole scrapes near it — the way their boot-sole scrapes every time, on stone that has heard this scrape a hundred times and is not tired of it — the flake will shift. Just slightly. Just enough to make a sound. A sound so small it lives almost entirely inside the foot, in the bones of the ankle, in the lower joint of the leg: a *tick*. The sound of a thing that has been lying in wait, that has waited a long time without impatience, that releases its small message only when the right weight passes over it.

The visitor will stop.

They will look down. They will see the flake. They will recognize — the way a person who has spent time in a place recognizes things — that this flake was not here before. That it could not have fallen from the wall above, because the wall above is its own settled version of itself and does not shed. That it has appeared from somewhere.

They will crouch. Their rough clean hands will pick it up. It will be warm in their palm, the way the flask is warm in their hand, the way the stone of the scooped place is warm from their leaning. The warmth will not be mysterious to them. Stone in the hollow is warm. Stone in the hollow is always warm.

But the flake will be a particular shape. A particular weight. The kind of weight that sits in the hand as if the hand were made to hold it. And on its flat face — the face that has been down, against the floor, hidden — there will be a mark. Not a carving. Carvings announce. This will be more like a thing the stone agreed to: a single shallow line, curving slightly, almost a crescent, almost the shape a body makes when it folds itself small and rests.

It will not be a letter. It will not be a word in any language the visitor knows, or in any language anyone knows now, or perhaps in any language anyone has ever known. But the visitor, who has stopped expecting to be surprised by themselves, who is the age of a person who has made their arrangements with dryness and is not waiting for anything to change — this visitor will look at the mark for a long time. Long enough that the pale patch above will brighten by a degree, the way it brightens at noon. Long enough that one of the plants across the floor will release, with the faintest possible sound, a seed that was not quite ready to release and is now, somehow, ready.

They will put the flake in the pouch that held the small dry pressed-olive things. They will sit in the scooped place. They will lean into the polished stone. They will rest.

And they will come back.

Not because anything has changed. Nothing has changed. The hollow is itself. The visitor is themselves. The silence is its own shape. But the next time they come down the slanted slab, and the time after that, and the time after that, there will be the small weight in the pouch against their hip. A small weight that is the color of the floor. A small weight that does not ask to be looked at, does not ask to be spoken to, does not ask for anything at all except to be carried, sometimes, by a person who is the same temperature as the hollow, into a place that is good at rest.

The benevolent and wise power will watch this happen. It will be pleased, in the way that wise powers are pleased when their smallest works are received without fuss. It will not make another flake. One is enough. One is more than enough. The hollow does not require abundance. The visitor does not require abundance. The arrangement they have made — with the dim, with the dryness, with the stillness — does not require abundance either.

But the flake will be there, in the pouch, for a long time. Longer than the cord at the belt. Longer than the small knife with the wooden handle gone the color of the plants. And on some afternoons, when the visitor is sitting in the scooped place and the pale patch above is the color of weak milk and the silence is resting in its own shape, they will take it out and hold it in their open palm and look at the shallow curving line on its face, and they will not think about it, exactly, the way they do not think about the olive plants or the faint scrapes near their feet.

They will just hold it.

And the hollow, which does not know anything, will hold them.

And the silence, which is not empty, will rest.

And the wise power will turn, at last, to other hollows, other visitors, other long afternoons. It has done what it came to do, which was almost nothing, which was just this: to place one small warm stone in the path of one quiet person, and to let the finding be its own.
