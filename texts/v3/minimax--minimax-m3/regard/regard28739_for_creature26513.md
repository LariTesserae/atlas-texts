The first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power is that it is not in a hurry.

It has, perhaps, noticed a great many things. It has noticed stars being born and stars dying. It has noticed civilizations rising and falling, the slow patient churn of carbon through limestone and into diamond and back into limestone. It has noticed, and it has, on occasion, acted. But it acts the way the drip acts — not as an event but as a tendency. A leaning. A slight adjustment in the prevailing winds so that, on a particular afternoon, a particular leaf falls a particular way, and a particular insect, on that leaf, is carried one inch to the left, where the light is better, where the algae on which it feeds are thicker.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the dragonfly nymph.

The dragonfly nymph is two years old. It has spent two years in the warm water of a tilted slab, eating mosquito larvae and biofilm and, occasionally, a smaller dragonfly nymph. It is patient. It has the patience of a thing that does not yet know what it is becoming. It will spend one more year in the water. It will then climb out, up the fig trunk, and split its skin. It will emerge with crumpled wings that it will pump, slowly, until they fill with blood and harden. It will spend four weeks, perhaps five, hunting smaller things over the warm pools. It will mate. It will die. Its body will fall into the water. The water will take it back. This is its life. This is its complete life. There is no deficit in it. There is no absence of grace. It will be, in its short flight, the fastest thing in the hangar, the most precise, the most beautiful, and then it will be gone.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the dragonfly nymph. A benevolent and wise power does, however, on the morning of its emergence, ensure that the humidity is precisely right.

This is a small thing. The air, on that particular morning, will be a little more saturated than it would otherwise have been. The fig leaf on which the nymph rests will be a little wetter than it would otherwise have been. The wing, as it pumps and fills and hardens, will not dry too fast. It will dry at exactly the speed at which dragonfly wings have dried, in this hangar, for as long as there have been dragonflies here — which is, perhaps, only a few decades, which is not very long in the life of a hangar that has been weeping for a hundred years. The dragonfly nymph will fly. It will not know why. It will not know that, on this morning, the air held its breath a little longer than usual, the way a parent holds its breath when a child is learning to ride a bicycle.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the possum.

The possum is asleep, right now, in a fork of the fig trunk about forty feet up. It is curled around the branch like a question mark. Its pink nose is tucked under its tail. It will sleep for another hour, perhaps two. When it wakes, it will eat a fallen fig. The fig will be overripe, almost wine-like. The possum will not think about this. It will not think about the bird that excreted the original seed of this fig tree, which was itself a descendant of an older fig tree, which was itself a descendant of an even older fig tree, in some other ruin, in some other forgotten place. The possum will simply eat. It will then groom itself. It will then sleep again. It will do this for perhaps three more years, in this hangar, or in some other hangar nearby if it leaves. It will die. Its body will fall, or not. Its nitrogen will become part of the arrangement, which is all any of us can ask for.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the possum. A benevolent and wise power does, however, ensure that the possum is, on one particular night, awake.

This is a small thing. The possum does not, on most nights, look at the moon. It does not, on most nights, climb higher in the canopy than it strictly needs to. But on this night — on a night chosen by no one in particular, but in which the air is right, and the moon is full, and a fig has fallen and burst open and its scent has drawn the possum out of its sleep at an unusual hour — the possum will climb. It will climb to the highest point of the canopy, where the iron roof is rusted thin and the light comes through in green-gold coins. It will sit there. It will look up through the leaves, through the holes in the rust, through the night sky beyond. It will see, for the first time in its life, the stars. It will not understand what they are. It will not understand that it has been living, all its life, under a ceiling, and that the ceiling has been thinning, and that the stars have been there all along. It will simply see them. It will sit there for a long time. It will feel, perhaps, something it has no name for — something that is not fear, exactly, and not wonder, exactly, but something closer to both than it has ever been. And then it will climb down. And it will sleep. And tomorrow it will eat figs and sleep and be a complete failure as an organism. But it will have, in its small possum memory, a thing it cannot communicate to other possums, a thing that will not, in any way, affect the arrangement. A thing that is, nonetheless, the entire point of the arrangement. The possum saw the stars. The possum is the only tenant of the hangar who has ever seen the stars. The figs do not look up. The hornets do not look up. The dragonfly nymphs look up only as far as the water's surface and then they fly, briefly, before they die. The possums are the only ones with the time, and the curiosity, and the soft night-adapted eyes, to see, once in a while, what is above them. And the benevolent and wise power, on this one night, ensured that one possum, at least, saw.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the hornets.

The hornets are running their manufacturing operation. They have larvae in hexagonal cradles. They have workers bringing chewed fig. They have a nest that is warm to the touch on the outside because of the coordinated vibration of several hundred flight muscles. They are the inheritors. They do not know they are inheritors. They are running a paper factory that happens to be in a hangar. The hangar, to them, is the world. The hangar is the entire cosmos. They will never leave it except by accident, on a worker blown astray by a gust through one of the rust-holes in the roof, and that worker will die, somewhere in the larger forest outside, within hours, because she cannot find her way home and will not ask for directions and does not know directions exist.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the hornets. A benevolent and wise power does, however, ensure that the hornet who is blown astray finds her way home.

This is a small thing. The hornet is not a great distance from the hangar. She is in a tree outside, perhaps twenty feet away. She is on a branch. She is confused. Her sisters are inside. She cannot smell them. The wind is wrong. The sun is in a position she does not recognize. She will die here, soon, of cold or hunger or the simple attrition of being alone, because hornets are not built to be alone. They are built to be in a paper city with a job. She has no job here. She has no role. She is, briefly, the most lost creature in the world.

And then, on a leaf beside her, a drop of water. The drop is not rain. The drop is condensation, condensed from the hangar's humid breath, carried out on the wind in a particular way, deposited on this particular leaf at this particular moment. The hornet lands on it. The hornet drinks. The drop is fig-scented, faintly, from the tannins carried out of the hangar in its vapor. The hornet tastes fig. The hornet remembers fig. The hornet orients. She flies back, slowly, following the fig-scent on the air, which is strongest near the hangar door, which is half-buried under compost, which has a crack, which a hornet can fit through. She is home. She does not know how close she came to not being home. She does not know that, an hour later, a wind would have come and taken her. She does not know that the drop of water on the leaf was, in its small way, an answer to a question she did not know she was asking.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the place.

The place is, as it has been for a hundred years, in a slow negotiation with entropy. It will, in another hundred years, be a sinkhole. It will, in another thousand, be a pond. It will, in another ten thousand, be a meadow. It will, eventually, be nothing at all. This is fine. This is the arrangement. The arrangement does not need to be saved because the arrangement is not in trouble. The arrangement is in progress. The arrangement is ongoing. The arrangement will be over, one day, but the over-ness of the arrangement is part of the arrangement, the way the death of the dragonfly is part of the dragonfly, the way the over-ripeness of the fig is part of the fig.

A benevolent and wise power does not save the place. A benevolent and wise power does, however, sometimes, send someone to walk through it.

This is a small thing. The someone is you. You came here — perhaps by accident, perhaps on purpose, perhaps drawn by a curiosity you cannot name. You came and you stood in the middle of the hangar with your feet on a tilted slab and your head in the canopy and the light falling through the leaves in green-gold coins on the water around you. You saw the drip. You saw the ferns. You saw the figs fruiting. You saw, briefly, the possum in the branches. You are an interruption. You are a weather event. You are a brief warm anomaly in a place that has been cooling toward entropy for a hundred years.

But you are also, in your small way, the memory of the place. You are the witness. You are the one who, having been here, will carry the hangar with you — not in any grand way, not as a mission, not as a thing to be saved, but as a small green-gold coin of light in the memory, as a smell of figs and wet iron that you will, years from now, remember in the middle of an unrelated afternoon, and for a moment you will not know why you are smiling. The hangar, in your memory, will go on dripping. The hangar, in your memory, will go on fruiting. The hangar, in your memory, will be remembered as what it is: not a ruin but a place that is in the middle of becoming something else, the way all places are, the way all of us are.

And this is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It does not save the dragonfly. It does not save the possum. It does not save the hornets. It does not save the place. It witnesses. It pays attention. It ensures, in small ways, that the dragonfly's wings dry at the right speed, that the possum sees the stars one night, that the hornet finds her way home. And it ensures, in a larger way, that someone, sometime, comes to stand in the middle of the hangar with the light falling on the water around them, and that this someone carries the hangar with them, in their memory, for the rest of their life.

The drip goes on.

The drip goes on.
