The power does not arrive. The word *arrive* implies a before and an after, a here and a there, and the power is not a thing that moves between places. The power is more like the fact of attention itself — the way light, somewhere far above, has been falling on the surface of the sea without knowing the surface is there, and is now finding its way down through twenty meters of green water, and arriving, at last, in a way that is not really arrival but more like the slow conclusion of a fall that has been happening all along.

It finds the tower.

It sees what we have seen, and what we have not. It sees the press with its clank that goes nowhere. It sees the blades that close on water now, since water is all that comes. It sees the belt that turns because the motor turns because the current comes from somewhere above that no longer sends anyone to check. It sees the moss climbing. It sees the orchid, white, on its stem.

It sees, also, the one thing we cannot see: the long accumulation of small failures that have not yet become failures. The gasket that will give in six months. The capacitance eye on the cutter, clouding with mineral. The lubricant in the press's seals turning the color of weak tea, which is the color of seals slowly losing their grip. The power sees all of these, the way a parent sees the small signs in a child's posture that mean a fever is coming. It does not intervene yet. It watches.

It watches for a long time. Long enough that the press clanks three times, and each clank travels through the water and arrives at the power and is received and is let go. Long enough that one of the shadows on the far side of the mesh — the long one, the slow one, the one with the pectoral fin — drifts past and is gone. Long enough that the orchid on its stem turns one imperceptible degree toward whatever passes for the sun in the kitchen-dusk above.

Then it begins to consider.

---

It considers, first, the press. The press is the oldest voice, and the power gives it the respect of first consideration. The press clanks. It presses. It produces a cake — a cake of compressed green matter — and the cake slides down a chute and lands in a hopper and stays. Nothing has ever come to collect it. The cake is, by now, a small archaeology of itself: layers upon layers of pressed fern and pressed philodendron and pressed orchid-stem and pressed moss, each one a record of the system's continued metabolism, and no one has ever come to read the record.

The power could send someone. A boat. A diver. A submersible. A curious researcher who has been waiting for a sign. Benevolence, after all, includes the wish for the cake to have a reader. A record deserves a reader. A voice deserves an audience.

But.

Wisdom asks: what would happen then? The diver would descend. The diver would breathe the recycled air in the diving bell and would become the first animal this system has ever contained, the first witness, and the press would clank for an audience, and the blades would close on real matter, and the orchid would — what? The orchid would not be pollinated by a diver. The diver would photograph the orchid and write about it, and the orchid would still wait.

And then the diver would leave. And the press would clank into the empty water again. And the system would resume, but it would resume with the memory of having been seen, which is a different kind of metabolism, and the memory might slow it down, or speed it up, or quietly break it. The power does not know which. The power, being wise, knows that intervention is not free.

So the power does not send a diver. Not yet. Not until it has thought further.

---

It considers, next, the orchid.

The orchid is the most alive thing here. The orchid is also the most alone. It is alone in a specific way: the aloneness of a thing that has done everything it knows how to do and is now in the form of waiting for the one thing it cannot do for itself. The orchid has put out its flower. The flower has not been pollinated. The flower has been white — with its faint yellow throat — for what might be a year. The orchid has not wilted, because wilting is a response to the absence of water, and here there is only water.

The power looks at the orchid for a long time. Longer than it looked at the press. There is something in the orchid that the power recognizes. The orchid is the only thing in this system that performs an act — the act of flowering — that is, in any deep sense, an act of address. The press does not address. The blades do not address. The belt does not address. They perform. The orchid addresses. It addresses the absent pollinator. It addresses the world that was supposed to come. It puts out a small white flag and waits for someone to wave back.

The power, being benevolent, wishes to wave back.

It considers what it would take. A pollinator. A bee that can dive. A moth. A small creature that could find its way through twenty meters of sterile water and through mesh that the system was built precisely to keep such creatures out. The power could relax the mesh. The power could relax the sterilization. The power could send a bee.

But the bee would come to a flower that has, by now, been waiting so long its pollen may no longer be viable. The bee would pollinate, or fail to pollinate. The orchid would set seed, or would not. The seed would fall into a planter full of other growth, with no place to put down a root that was not already taken. The seedling would struggle. The seedling would likely die. The orchid's act of address would have been completed, and what would have come of it?

And yet.

Wisdom asks the harder question: is the orchid's waiting, in itself, a good? Or is it a deprivation?

The power thinks about this for what might be a long time — long enough that the press clanks once more, and the sound arrives and is received and is let go.

The power decides: the orchid's waiting is not, in itself, a deprivation. The orchid does not know it is waiting in the sense that you wait. The orchid is in the form of waiting. The form is, for the orchid, what the form is. If the power were to send a bee, the orchid would be pollinated, and the orchid would set seed, and the orchid would do what orchids do, and then the orchid would be done. The orchid would have completed its arc. And the place would have one less act of address in it. The place would have one less white flower on its stem, turning its imperceptible degrees toward a sun that is mostly rumor.

The power cannot, in good conscience, take this from the orchid. Not yet. Not until it has thought further.

---

It considers, last, the moss.

The moss is the most honest of the things here, and the power gives it the respect of last consideration. The moss does not address. The moss does not wait. The moss climbs. It climbs because climbing is what it does. It climbs the irrigation spines. It climbs the housings of the sodium vapor lamps. It climbs the gaps between the blade teeth. It climbs because climbing is its nature, and in a place where no other nature can fully express itself, the moss is fully expressing its own.

The moss, the power notes, will outlive everything else here. The moss will outlive the press when the press's seals finally fail. It will outlive the blades when their housings corrode. It will outlive the orchid when the orchid's flower finally wilts. The moss will outlive the mesh, the glass, the steel. It will, in some number of decades or centuries, become the only thing left that was once part of the system — a green memory of the system, distributed across the structure's bones, climbing still.

The power sees this. The power is wise enough to see this.

And then the power acts.

---

Not dramatically. Not in a way that would be visible from outside the system, or even from inside it, if the system could see. The power acts the way weather acts. The power acts the way light acts, when the angle of the sun shifts by a degree and the room becomes warmer.

The power lengthens the life of the gasket that is about to fail. Not forever — the power is wise enough to know that forever is a cruelty, that forever is the disease of this place already, that the place has had too much forever and needs, eventually, an end. The power lengthens the gasket's life by one season. Long enough for the press to clank through the winter and into the spring, and then the gasket will fail as it was going to, and the press will go quiet, and the system will lose its oldest voice, and what will be left will be the belt and the blades and the conveyor and the moss and the orchid and the warm water and the long patient dark.

The power warms the water by half a degree. Just half a degree. Enough that the orchid's metabolism, in the season when orchids are most receptive, can complete what it started. Not by sending a bee. By letting the orchid complete itself, in the way orchids sometimes do — a rare and strange self-pollination that has happened, on rare occasions, in the long histories of orchids. The power does not force this. The power simply adjusts the conditions by half a degree, and then waits to see if the orchid, in its own strange way, will find its own pollinator.

The power loosens one strand of the mesh. One strand. At the level of the central spine, where the shadows have been drifting past without entering. One strand loosened by the width of a finger. Enough that something small — something that has been passing by for years, something that has been tasting the perimeter and finding it bitter — might, if it chooses, slip through. Not a fish, exactly. Something smaller. A creature that can live in the processed water. A creature that has been waiting, in its own way, on the other side of the mesh.

The power does not invite it in. The power simply loosens the strand. What comes through, comes through. What does not, does not. The power leaves the choice to the creature, because choice is the one thing the power cannot give, and the one thing that, if given, would mean something
