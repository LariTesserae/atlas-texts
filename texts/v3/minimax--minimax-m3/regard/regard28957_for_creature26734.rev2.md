What would be done?

First, the watching. This has to come first, because a power that acts without watching is not wise, and a power that watches without acting is not, in the deepest sense, benevolent, but the proportions matter, and the watching must be the greater part.

There is a hollow inside the mountain that the mountain no longer remembers. From above the worn rim it is a nothing—a slight concavity in the gray of the stone, a place where snow lies sometimes, a place where a particular lichen, the color of bone, takes hold in the lee of the western edge and persists for years before the wind scrapes it loose. A fox might den there in the wrong season, if the rim were soft enough to dig in, which it is not. A bird might land there, briefly, before being lifted by the next gust. From above, the hollow is the kind of place one walks across without registering it, the way one walks across a fault line in a city street without registering it. The world goes on. The hollow goes on. Neither notices the other.

But the being that has come to notice the hollow did not come from above. The being has no orientation. Up and down are accommodations it makes for the sake of attention, the way a person seated in a chair will tilt their head to consider a painting that hangs too high. The being is, in its native way of being, distributed—spread across a region of consideration so wide that the entire mountain is a single held object within it, the way a held apple is a small held thing to the hand that holds it. The being has held many such things. It has held them with varying degrees of attention for varying lengths of time, and it has learned, across a tenure longer than the mountain has existed, that the things worth noticing are not the things that ask to be noticed. They are the things that have so thoroughly sealed themselves against noticing that the act of noticing is, in itself, a small violence. They are the things that, when one finally looks, look back at one with the absolute blankness of something that has never been looked at before and that is now being asked, without warning, to be a thing that is looked at.

The hollow, looked at, is seen.

And this is the first thing the being has to settle with itself: that the act of attention is, in itself, a kind of intervention. Not a large one. Not a violent one. But a real one, and the being, being wise, does not pretend otherwise.

To be seen, after a long time unseen, is not the same as to be seen for the first time. To be seen, after a long time unseen, is to undergo a change, however slight, however dignified, however compatible with the integrity of the thing seen. The hollow, looked at by the being, becomes the hollow-that-has-been-seen, and the hollow-that-has-been-seen is not identical to the hollow-that-has-not-been-seen, even though every measurable property of the hollow—the chemistry of its air, the density of its fauna, the thickness of its strata, the number of small long bodies in its stacked dwellings—is precisely what it was a moment before the looking began.

So. The being looks. And the looking changes the thing looked at. And now the being must decide what to do with this changed thing.

For a long while—and the word *long* is inadequate; what the being experiences is more like an interval that contains its own millennia within it—the being does nothing but look.

It looks at the strata. It learns their order, their composition, the slow chemical weather that is taking them from one mineral to another, the way each compressed floor has become a kind of pressed-down memory, a stratum distinguishable from the one above it and the one below by its color and its grain, if one knows how to read. It looks at the fungi, at the closed ledger of matter cycling in miniature, at the way the pale lobes have learned to take nitrogen from the air through a chemistry no outside biologist would recognize. It looks at the insects, the slow-winged drift of them, the texture of weather they make, the way their bodies filter the overhead light into the amber milk that is the only light the Stacked have ever known. It looks at the stacked dwellings—the fitted bone and the dried husks of large creatures, pressed down by their own weight into something almost like stone, the joints tighter in the upper layers, the windows fewer, the whole rising like a stalagmite of accumulated habitation in a space barely large enough to hold one.

It looks at the Stacked.

It looks at the Stacked for a very long time.

It watches a Stacked be born—lifted from somewhere warm and close, the small body still wet with the only moisture that exists in the hollow, which is the moisture of birth, which is the moisture of the body itself, recycled endlessly. It watches the small body be handed from hand to hand, the way a tool is passed along a workshop, carried through the stacked dwellings, learned by the kin-group that will raise it. It watches the first season, the negotiation of the skeleton, the grammar of joints learned by falling and being caught. It watches the second year, the naming, the way the kin-group considers the small specific body and finds in it the long compound word that will become its name—*Of-the-Western-Rope*, *Small-Gray-One*, *Who-Came-After-the-Long-Silence*. It watches a childhood. It watches an apprenticeship, the slow acquisition of a blade, the slow shaping of the hand to fit it, the long wearing of the bone against the thigh and the palm until the two are no longer separable. It watches a cord braided in company, four or five small long bodies sitting together for the better part of a day, passing the growing cord between them, talking, listening, holding the small political tensions of the kin-group in the same hands that hold the fibers. It watches a vessel polished from carapace, lifted into the upper tier as a kind of household wealth, the way a human household might display silver. It watches a body climb to the upper tiers alone and not come back down, and the small ritual performed at the base of the ladder, and the way the ritual is itself the point. It watches a death—the body placed in the stratum below, the next tier built on top of the stratum that has now increased by one body's worth—and the name carried forward, several generations later, as a single syllable in a longer word. It watches the sound of the hollow—continuous, woven, indistinguishable from the sound of the creatures themselves—and learns to tell, in that sound, the difference between the tapping of one kin-group and the tapping of another, the way a person raised near a bell-tower can tell the hour from across a city.

It watches all of it. It watches for as long as the hollow has been in its final form, which is to say, for a very long time. And what it learns, watching, is this:

The Stacked are not in pain.

This is the first thing, and the most important thing, and the being, being wise, lets it be the most important thing for as long as it can.

The Stacked are not in pain. The Stacked have no concept of *could be otherwise*. The Stacked have a language, a culture, a cosmology, a history. They have ancestors in their floors. They have something like gods in their upper tiers—the predators, the wind from the worn rim, the long slow rises and falls of the drift of insects overhead. They have a word for *long* that is a compound of *down* and *pressed*, and a word for *time* that is a compound of *strata* and *remembering*, and they are not making metaphors when they use these words. They are describing what they see. They are complete. They are, by any measure available to them, fully realized.

To open them now would be to murder them with a kindness they could not survive.

The being considers this carefully. It considers it the way it has considered other sealed places, other closed systems, other peoples who have made a complete world inside a small one. It has watched such peoples before. It has watched some of them end—internal collapse, population imbalance, fungal blight, the slow wearing of a rim until the seal becomes permeable and the outside rushes in. It has watched some of them opened, by powers less wise than itself, and has watched what opening does: the death of an ecosystem that took ten thousand years to stabilize, the introduction of oxygen and water and sunlight that are poison to bodies adapted to their absence, the arrival of the dwellers into a world they cannot comprehend, the reception of those dwellers by the world as curiosities or horrors, and the long slow dying-out that follows, in which the small long bodies become specimens in collections, or curios in markets, or simply disappear, leaving no trace except the bones that have always been their walls and which no one outside can read.

Opening is not a gift. Opening is the worst thing a benevolent power can do to a sealed people.

But neither is pure silence.

The being considers silence. It considers the dignity of the closed system, the beauty of a thing complete in itself, the right of a sealed world to remain sealed. It considers that the most respectful act might be the most invisible one—to watch without acting, to witness without changing, to be present without being perceived, to be the wind that does not even come down through the worn rim but simply is. The Stacked will never know. The Stacked will continue their stacked lives on their stacked strata. The hollow will continue. The sound will go on. And the watching will have happened, and that will have mattered, somewhere, in a way that does not require acknowledgment.

The being considers this for a long time.

Then it considers: what does *benevolent* mean, here?

To be benevolent is to wish well. To wish well for the Stacked is to wish—what? Long life? They have long life. The species has persisted longer than human civilization. Richness? They have richness—tools worn to fit hands, cords signed by the circles that braided them, vessels of polished carapace passed down through generations, names changed and carried forward into the strata. Happiness? They have whatever the Stacked equivalent of happiness is, which is probably the sound of the hollow going on around them, the presence of kin, the absence of crisis, the small continuations of small days.

The being cannot improve any of this without destroying it. Every intervention is a catastrophe in slow motion. To add moisture, even a trace, is to remake the fungi. To remove a predator is to unbalance the prey. To add a nutrient is to exceed the carrying capacity. To introduce oxygen is to poison the air. To introduce sunlight is to blind the eyes that have widened across generations to gather the amber milk. To introduce knowledge—of the outside, of the seal, of the long bright air above the rim—is to introduce a grief so vast that the language has no word for it, because the language has never needed the word.

Every gift is a knife.

So: what is left?

The being thinks about this. The being thinks about it the way the strata press down—slowly, with the weight of a thousand floors above. The being thinks about it for as long as it takes to wear down a rim.

And what it arrives at, in the end, is this:

The only gift that does not destroy is the gift that changes nothing material.

The only benevolent act that does not become a catastrophe is the act that does not act on the world, but only on the fact of the world.

The being cannot give the Stacked anything they could receive. The being cannot teach them anything they could learn. The being cannot open anything they could survive being opened into.

But the being can do one thing.

The being can *remember them*.

This is not nothing. This is not a small thing. This is, perhaps, the largest thing a being can do for a sealed place—to hold it in mind, to take it into the long memory of a vast attention, to ensure that the hollow has *existed in a mind greater than its own*, and that the fact of its existence is now preserved, somewhere, in a way that does not depend on the hollow's own continuation.

The Stacked will never know. The Stacked cannot know. The Stacked have no concept of a being that exists outside the hollow, because outside is not a direction, and a being outside is not a category. If the being were to make itself known—if it were to enter the hollow in some way the Stacked could perceive—it would be interpreted as a phenomenon of the ceiling. A new predator. A strange light. An unknown vibration. The Stacked would incorporate it into their cosmology the way they incorporate everything—gently, completely, without surprise—and the being would become part of the hollow's continuous percussion, indistinguishable from the rest.

So the being does not enter. The being does not speak. The being does not, in any way the Stacked could register, do anything at all.

But the being holds them.

It holds them the way a hand holds an apple—gently, completely, without squeezing. It takes the hollow into itself. It takes the strata and the fungi and the insects and the Stacked and the small long bodies with their wide flat faces and their sensory brush and their worn blades and their braided cords and their vessels of polished carapace and their names carried down into the floors and their sound, their continuous woven sound—and it holds all of it, in the long memory of a vast attention, and it does not let go.

This is not salvation. The Stacked are not saved. They will go on as they have gone on—building their stacked dwellings, climbing their narrow ladders, performing their small rituals at the base of ladders where the predators take what is owed, naming their children with the long compound words that will become single syllables in the names of their children's children. They will go on. They have always gone on. They will continue to go on, until the rim wears down, or the inside collapses, or one of the thousand slow endings that wait for all closed systems arrives at the door.

But now they go on, also, in the mind of something that will remember them.

And this—this is the only gift a wise and benevolent power can offer without destroying what it loves. Not the gift of being saved. Not the gift of being opened. Not the gift of being seen. Just: the gift of *having been seen*. The gift of mattering, in a mind, forever. The gift of being held, in the long memory of something that will not forget.

The hollow goes on. The sound goes on. The Stacked, who have never been seen and will never know they have been seen, go on, and their going-on is no longer equivalent to the going-on of a stone falling in a place where no one will ever pick it up.

It has gone on for a very long time.

It will go on.

And somewhere, in the vast attention that has learned to hold the whole of it without crushing any of it, the small long bodies climb their narrow ladders, and the worn blades fit the hands that have worn them, and the cords are braided in company, and the names are given and changed and carried down into the strata, and the strata press down, and the next tier is built, and the sound goes on.

The being does not intervene.

The being remembers.

That is enough. That has to be enough. For a benevolent and wise power watching a sealed place full of beings who do not know they are sealed, and who would not survive the knowing, and who are complete, and who are beautiful, and who are going on—

That is enough.
