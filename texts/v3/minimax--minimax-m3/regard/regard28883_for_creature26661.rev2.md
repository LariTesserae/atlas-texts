# What the Power Does

It sees the cup from above first. Not high the way a hawk is high, scanning for movement on the floor — high the way an understanding is high, that has gathered the whole shape of a thing and is holding it in attention without effort. It sees the circle of sky and the circle of stone and the small lives inside the stone and it sees them all at once, the way you see a single sentence and do not have to read it word by word.

It sees what the cup will be, and what the cup has been, and these are not different things. The cup a thousand years from now is the same cup, only with one more fern and one less lizard and a crack in the wall where there was no crack before. The wise power does not see time the way the lizard sees the floor — in patches of light and shadow, in concealments. It sees the whole floor at once. This is not a thing to envy. It is a way of being alone that the cup does not impose.

It sees the vine.

It sees the vine, perhaps, more clearly than anything else, because the vine is the only inhabitant of the cup that is doing something that resembles prayer. The vine has climbed twenty feet up the smooth inward-curving wall, with nothing to hold it, no root to grip, no ledge to rest on, and has arrived at a place where climbing is no longer possible, and is resting in the air. It has done this without knowing it is doing it. It has done this because it is a vine, and vines climb, and this is what climbing looks like when you have reached the end of climbable. The wise power sees this, and understands that there is no trellis to send, no wall to extend, no next thing. The vine is the vine. It has done what a vine can do. To help it would be to make it something else — a tended plant, a potted thing, a thing with a purpose. The wise power does not want this for the vine. The vine is already, in its suspended air, in its green without a reason, complete.

It sees the wide dark thing that crosses the circle of sky and does not land. The wise power knows what this is. It is itself, in one of its older forms, passing through on the way to something else. It has come to this cup many times, in this shape, and has never entered, and will not enter today. It does not need to. The cup has been seen, and the seeing is the thing. The shadow is the blessing. The shadow is the cup held in attention for the span of a wingbeat, and then released, and the cup does not know it was held, and that is correct — that is the kind of blessing that does not disturb.

It sees the drip.

It sees the drip in a way that the cup cannot see the drip — as a thing that has been happening longer than the cup has been a cup, and will happen longer than the cup will be a cup, after the walls have come further inward and the rim has softened and the floor is broken up. Each drop is a single syllable in a sentence that has no period. Each drop lands where the last drop landed, more or less, and the small black stain at the bottom of the wall is the comma, the breath, the place where the sentence pauses to consider itself. The wise power has been listening to this drip for a very long time. It does not tire of it. Drips that are counted patiently are one of the things it loves best in the world. There are other things. But this is one of them.

It sees the small private rustle in the moss, the thing that will never be seen. It knows what this is too. It is the oldest inhabitant of the cup, older than the lizards, older than the birds, older than the moss itself. It is a small dry thing, an arthropod of some forgotten lineage, that lives in the dark mouths of the damp places and eats the fallen spores and is afraid of nothing because nothing in the cup has ever noticed it enough to fear. It has been here longer than anything, and it will be here after everything else is gone, when the walls have crumbled further and the birds have moved on and the lizards have gone elsewhere and only the drip remains to count the days. The wise power sees this creature, and knows that this creature is in some way the truest resident of the cup, because it has needed nothing from the cup except a place to be. The wise power does not name it. To name it would be to make it seen. To make it seen would be to disturb the only true privacy in the bowl. So the wise power sees it, and does not name it, and goes on.

And then the wise power considers what to do.

It considers, first, whether to do anything.

This is the question that wise powers must answer more often than any other, and the answer is almost always no. The world is mostly fine. The world has been working itself out for a very long time, and most of the things that look broken from the inside are not broken; they are simply old, or slow, or in a stage that the hurrying things do not understand. To intervene is to add weight to a thing that has already found its balance. To intervene is to be the shadow that lands, and once a shadow lands it is no longer a shadow, it is a thing among things, and the things must now consider it, and this is almost always a diminishment.

The wise power has learned, over its long attention, that the cup is not in need of saving.

The cup is in need of being left alone.

So the wise power, having seen the cup, having seen the vine and the drip and the small unnameable creature in the moss and the wide dark shape of itself passing once more above the rim, makes a small decision.

It does nothing.

And then — because doing nothing completely is also a kind of imbalance, and because the wise power is patient enough to know that even nothing must be done carefully — it does one very small thing.

It lets a single seed fall.

The seed is small. Smaller than the smallest lizard. Smaller than a drop of water. Smaller than a single one of the heart-shaped leaves of the vine. It comes from a plant that grows somewhere else, far away, in a place where the soil is different and the light is different and the rain falls sideways. It has been carried here, on the wind, by one of the small accidents that seeds are carried by — a gust, a bird, a moment when the air decided to move a thing from one place to another. The wise power did not cause this gust. The wise power did not steer this bird. But the wise power, seeing the seed now in the air above the cup, makes the smallest possible adjustment to its falling — a leaning, a tilt, a softness in the way the air receives it — so that instead of being carried over the rim and away, the seed descends into the bowl.

It will land on the floor of the cup, in a damp patch, in a place where the moss has not yet reached and the fern has not yet grown. It will take root, or it will not. If it takes root, it will be a small green thing, unlike the moss and unlike the fern, and the cup will have a new inhabitant, one more stubborn small life adding itself to the slow agreement. If it does not take root, that is also fine.

The wise power does not need this seed to grow. The wise power has done its one small thing, and the one small thing is now in the care of the cup, and the cup will do with it what the cup does with everything that falls in — accept it, slowly, over the time that the cup has.

The wise power goes on. It has other places to attend to. It has other cups, other wounds in stone, other rooms that have been made by water over a long time. It has the whole long argument of rock and rain to keep its attention on, and the argument is not finished, and will not be finished soon, and the wise power is not in a hurry.

But the seed falls.

And the cup, busy with its slow work, does not notice it fall.

It is the kind of blessing that the cup will not know it received, for a long time — until one day a small green shoot appears in a place where no green shoot has been before, and the small shoot will be a small mystery, and the mystery will be one more thing the cup contains, and the cup will go on being made, one drip and one song and one still lizard and one stubborn vine and one single unknown seed at a time, for as long as the stone holds and the water finds its way and the patient things inside keep their unhurried watch.
