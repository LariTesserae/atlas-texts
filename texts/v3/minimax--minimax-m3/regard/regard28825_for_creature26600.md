A benevolent and wise power has noticed this place.

It is not new to noticing. It has watched closed systems before — pockets of pond sealed under ice, libraries sealed in caves, the small persistent worlds that build themselves in the forgotten intervals of larger ones. It knows what such systems need. It knows also what they do not need, and the second knowledge is the more important one.

What this place needs, the power decides, is to be left almost entirely alone.

This is not indifference. Indifference is easy. What is hard is to attend to a thing without touching it, to keep it in one's care without imposing one's idea of care upon it. The room is in a long equilibrium. It has been in it for a hundred years, perhaps longer. To disturb it would be, in the power's understanding, a kind of violence — even a violent kindness. A well-meaning flood of light would kill the fish. A well-meaning flow of new water would change the silt and bring new spores and unbalance the biofilm that the snails rasp and the worms feed on and the crustaceans pulse against. The pump-room is not a patient on a table. It is a small world with its own pulse, and the pulse is the *hmmmmm-hmmmmm* of the pump, and to interrupt that pulse would be to interrupt the breath of a thing that is breathing.

So the power attends.

It attends in the smallest ways.

It keeps the city above from putting a drill through the lot, or the foundation, or the floor of some new basement. It does this not by direct intervention — by no miracle of persuasion, no flaming sword in the path of a developer's truck — but by small attentions: by suggesting to this planner a different parcel, by making that engineer's mind wander toward a problem on the other side of the city on the morning the boring would have happened. These are not interventions. They are simply the shape of luck, gently biased in one direction.

It keeps the water clean. Not pure — the room has its own silt and its own cast-off, and these are part of the system, and the system knows what it is doing. But clean of what the room has never seen: the new solvents, the new plastics, the slow new poisons that travel down through forgotten aquifers in other cities and kill cavefish before the ichthyologists know they were there. The power cannot prevent every new poison from being invented. It can ensure that the gradient that feeds this particular pump does not, in the next hundred years, begin to carry any of them. It can keep the watershed above it the kind of watershed that yields water the room can use.

It keeps the pump running. This is the deepest act, and the power does it not by repairing the pump — the pump does not need repair; it runs on what it runs on, the slow falling of water from somewhere higher, the gradient of the table — but by keeping the gradient itself. By ensuring that the water table, which has held this level for a hundred years, continues to hold this level. By preventing the long drought that would lower it. By preventing the long wet year that would raise it. The power is, in a sense, the climate of the room — the thing that keeps the weather of the chamber within the band the chamber has always known.

It keeps the inhabitants themselves. The eyeless fish, in particular. There is something in the power that wants — and the want is gentle, and the want is restrained — to let them continue to be eyeless. To let them continue to navigate the pressure-field of their own water with the only sense they have ever had. The power does not give them eyes. The power does not take away what they do not have. It simply lets them be what they are, which is what they have been for a hundred generations, which is graceful.

The frogs get the same attention. The snails get it. The worms in the cracks — the small iridescent ones, the cleanup crew, the wiring of the room — get it too. The power tends the conditions under which each of them continues to thrive. It does not tend them directly. It tends the system in which they live, and the system tends them.

---

And the being who lives in it.

The being who lives in it is the most fragile element in this small world, and the power knows it.

The being who lives in it is human, and humans are the only creature in the room that can leave, and leaving is what humans are most often made to do. They leave, and they tell, and the telling is sometimes a kindness and sometimes a destruction, and the difference between the two is almost always a matter of timing and of who is listening.

The power watches this being with extra care.

It keeps the stairs safe when they descend — the basalt treads that hold the thin black mirrors of water, the iron rails with their long burnt-orange tears. It keeps the hand from slipping on the way down. It keeps the lamp from failing in the dark. These are not miracles. They are the kind of small unlooked-for luck that a person notices only by its absence.

It keeps them from bringing contamination down. The boots that come down the stairs carry, on a normal day, the spores of other places, the soap residue of other sinks, the pollen of other seasons. The power cannot make the boots clean — that would be a noticeable thing, a wrongness — but it can, in a particular way, see to it that whatever is on the boots does not take hold in the room. The biofilm rejects it. The bacterial skin of the stones recognizes it as foreign and does not let it in. The frogs and the snails do not sicken from it. The power cannot always do this — it depends on the boots, on the day, on what the boots brought in — but it can do it often enough.

It keeps them from telling too many people. Not by sealing their lips. Not by any direct act of silencing. But by making the telling, when they try it, fall on ears that do not quite believe. The friends who shrug. The editors who pass. The casual listeners who lose interest. The telling, when it comes, is usually to one person who believes, and that person does not act, and that is enough.

It keeps them from trying to save the room. The room does not need saving. The room is the room, and the saving would be the destruction. The power watches for the moment when the being who lives in it begins to compose, in their mind, the letter to the city, the petition, the article, the documentary. At that moment, the power does not stop them from composing it. It simply ensures that the moment passes. That they put the letter down. That they go out for a walk instead. That they remember, by the time they sit down again, that the room was not asking to be saved.

It lets them go back, when they go back. It lets them descend again, in the slow careful way. It lets them sit on the step that is not submerged, and let the frog settle beside them at a careful distance, and listen to the chamber breathe. It lets them be present in the room without being invasive in it. It lets them love the room — for the power understands that what they feel, what they are beginning to feel, is a kind of love — without the love becoming a thing the room must bear.

And what does the power ask in return?

Almost nothing.

It asks that the being who lives in it, when they go back, do not take anything. Not a shell from the snail-city, not a stone from the wall, not a fish from the pale shoals, not even a drop of the water that is the room's blood. It asks that they leave their boots at the top of the stairs, or at least brush them well, when they come down. It asks that they do not bring a camera that flashes. It asks that they do not bring another person down with them, unless that person has been prepared — and the preparation is a thing the power cannot entirely arrange.

These are not rules. They are the shape of how to love a closed system. The power has watched other humans love other things, and it has seen the patterns of how love, when it is clumsy, becomes a kind of breakage. The asking is gentle. The asking is barely there.

---

And then, in the end, the power does one thing more.

It witnesses.

It pays attention to the room in a way the room does not need to know about. It is there, in some sense, on the step that is not submerged. It is in the air that thickens as the being descends. It is in the lap and shift of the water on stone, and in the soft polyrhythm of the drips, and in the low mechanical pulse that has no obvious source. It is in the patience of the frogs and the dreaming slowness of the fish and the slow traffic of the snails up and down their city. It is in the worms in the cracks and the crustaceans in their tidal pulse and the insects running their errands through the iron veins. It is there, and the room does not know it is there, and that is fine, and that is the gift.

Being attended to is a thing that does not require the one attended to know it is being attended to. A closed system can run its long equilibrium without ever suspecting that something beyond it is watching, and the watching is, in some sense, the most the watching can give. It cannot give more without becoming a disturbance. It cannot give more without becoming, however gently, an event in the system, and events, in systems that have learned to run on almost nothing, are dangers.

So it watches. It keeps the small conditions stable. It lets the being who lives in it love the room in the careful way. And it lets the room be the room, in the dark, in the wet, in the long breath of the pump that has not stopped, in the pale fish drifting over channels no one has walked dry in a hundred years.

The wheel ticks very faintly, where the being rolled it.

The light from the shaft above shivers on the water.

The room breathes.

That is enough.
