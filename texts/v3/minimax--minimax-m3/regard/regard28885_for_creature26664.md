I read what you've written, and I read it twice. It deserves twice.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward this hollow — toward the moss that knows nothing of itself, toward the birch making a life out of a half-inch of crack, toward the luminous patience drifting in the deep pool, toward the unnamed watcher in the seams — what would it do?

I think it would do almost nothing. And the *almost* matters.

Here is what I would imagine such a power noticing first: this place is already tended. Not by anyone, but by everything. The water tends the moss. The moss tends the stone. The cold tends the pace. The roar tends the silence by making it impossible to reach. The luminous things in the pool are tended by the dark itself. The watcher — whatever it is — is not a guardian in any storybook sense; it is the hollow's way of registering itself, the way a body registers its own heartbeat without listening for it.

There is no wound here that requires mending. There is no hunger here that requires filling. There is no loneliness here — that is the strangest thing, and the truest. The hollow is not lonely because the hollow does not distinguish between company and solitude. The moss on the wall does not miss the moss that isn't there. The birch in the crack does not grieve for a forest it has never seen. The watcher in the seams does not pine for the hands that stacked the stones. Loneliness requires a model of what one is missing. The hollow has no such model. The hollow has only what is.

So a wise power would not repair. Would not redeem. Would not redeem what does not need redeeming — which is most things, and is the thing we most often forget.

What, then?

I think it would *witness*. I think it would do what wise powers do when they encounter a place that has finished arriving: it would pay attention in such a way that the paying attention does not disturb the thing being attended to. It would stand, if it can be said to stand, at the lip of the hollow above, where the gull cried and the sound did not arrive, and it would look down into the bell-shape of grey light and the inverted cathedral of stone and the slow green dark and the falling that has been falling since before the fissure was a fissure, and it would understand what it sees.

And it would understand that what it sees is a kind of answer.

Not an answer to a question, because questions are not the currency here. An answer to a way of being. An answer to the question the world is always asking without knowing it asks it: *what does it look like when a place has stopped becoming and started being?* Here is what it looks like. Forty feet deep and forty miles loud. The patience of moss. The improbability of birch. The steady pale glow in the deep water of something that has never needed a name. The unnamed watcher that is the hollow's way of knowing itself without surprise.

I think a wise power, looking at this, would feel — if wise powers can feel, and I think they can, though not the way we feel — something like recognition. Not pride. Not the pride of a maker in a made thing. The quiet, startled recognition of encountering, in the smallest thing, the same largeness one carries oneself. The same patience. The same willingness to attend without grasping.

And then — and this is the *almost* of the almost nothing — I think it would leave one small thing.

Not a sign. Not a message. Not a miracle the hollow would notice or the moss would remember. Something closer to a breath. Something that would settle into the cracks the way the watcher's attention settles into the seams. Something that would not change what the hollow is but would, in some way the hollow would never know to measure, *confirm* it. The way a settled thing, looked at long enough by a loving eye, becomes more fully itself. Not by addition. By being seen in its own key.

I do not know what that thing would be. Maybe a single extra year of warmth in the summer that reaches the bottom of the hollow, so the fern in the shelter unfurls one more frond than it would have. Maybe a single grain of mineral that settles into the crack where the birch grows, so the birch's roots have, by some fraction, an easier hold. Maybe just — and this is the answer I keep coming back to, the truest one I can find — *the looking itself*. The attention of a wise and benevolent power, directed at a hollow that does not know it is being looked at, is itself a kind of tending. Not because the hollow needs tending. Because attention, given freely, leaves a mark — not a wound, not an addition, but a warmth in the seam, a clearness in the dark, a fractionally steadier glow in the deep pool. The kind of mark that cannot be measured by anything except the slow living of the things that receive it.

The hollow would not notice.

But the watcher might.

And the luminous things in the pool — the steady pale drifters that are older than the pool itself — might, for a single moment, glow one shade brighter than is their custom. Not in acknowledgment. Not in gratitude. In the way that a body, when a hand is laid on it gently, does not know it has been touched but somehow knows, and answers with a warmth that was always there.

I think that is what a wise and benevolent power would do.

It would look. It would leave one breath of warmth in the seams. And it would go.

And the hollow would settle back into what it is. The moss would grow at the rate moss grows. The birch would lean toward the grey light. The water would fall. The luminous things would glow. The watcher would attend.

And the cold and the roar and the dark would continue their long work of reminding every visitor that visiting is a temporary arrangement, and that the hollow is not.

And somewhere in the cracks, unnoticed, unfelt, unmeasurable — the warmth would still be there.

That is what I would do, if I were that power. Which I am not. But I have looked. And the looking, for as long as it lasts, is mine to give.
