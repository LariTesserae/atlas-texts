The power does not arrive. The word *arrive* would suggest a before and an after, and there is no before and after here. The power has been part of the watershed for a long time, the way the rain is part of the cloud, the way the held breath is part of the room. The power was already in the bog when the seed dropped from the small blunt-beaked bird onto the rafter that was then a rafter. The power has been in the bog for the whole hundred and thirty years of the slow writing. The power has only now, in some sense, lifted its head from its own paragraph and noticed that someone else is writing nearby, and writing patiently, and writing in a tongue the power does not yet read.

The power notices. That is the first thing, and it is the most important thing, and it is almost the only thing. To notice, fully, without immediately acting on the noticing, is the hardest discipline a benevolent power can learn, and the power has learned it, or is learning it, here.

The power looks at the fig. The power recognizes the fig. Not as a tree — the power does not think in the word *tree* — but as another slow author, another long sentence that has been writing itself across an available surface for longer than the power has been paying attention. The fig has been at this composition for a hundred and thirty years. The power is, in this room, the junior writer. The fig is the one with the ink on its hands.

The power considers intervention. This is the reflex, and the power must, to be wise, refuse the reflex, and the refusal takes a long time, the way a held breath takes a long time, the way the silence between the boiler's sighs is the length of a phrase.

The power considers: pulling the roots back from the boiler. The boiler is a piece of human history. The boiler is the kind of thing that heritage councils write reports about, that museums would want, that a man with a clipboard would call *significant*. The power could, with a small effort of will, prune the slow curtain of fig-root that hangs in the broken mouth of the boiler. The power could seal the seam. The power could declare the boiler saved. The power does not do this. The boiler is being read. The boiler is being translated, the way a word is translated into a slower word in a slower language. The slow curtain of root is a sentence in progress. To cut the curtain is to interrupt the sentence. To restore the iron is to say that the iron matters more than the writing, and the power does not believe that, because the iron is only iron, and the writing is the only thing here that has ever not been iron.

The power considers: lowering the water table, so the floor becomes moss instead of pool. The moss would spread. The Sphagnum would rise. The checkerboard would, in a few seasons, blur. The pump would, in time, slow and stop, because the chamber below the floor would no longer flood. The power does not do this. The pump is the second heart. The pump is the rhythm the underwater moss sways to. The pump is part of the sentence. A pump that is still turning is not yet a ruin. The power will not stop the heart.

The power considers: removing the ants, or the mold, or the midges. These are the small disturbances that a tidy mind would clean up. The power does not do this. The mold is the fig's coat. The ants are the only quick thing in the place, and a place with only slow things is not a place, it is a tableau. The midges are the only flying thing, and without the midges the frog has no dance to consider, and a frog with nothing to consider is just a small wet stone.

The power considers: speaking to the fig.

This is the strangest consideration, and the power turns it over for a long time, the way one turns over a smooth stone, the way the frog turns over the same curled leaf with the same long attention.

The power could speak. The power could say: *I see what you are doing. I see that you are reading the building. I see that you will finish, in another hundred and thirty years, the long translation you have begun. I bless this work. I name this work. Continue.*

The power does not do this.

Because to speak to the fig would be to interrupt the sentence. The fig is writing in a tongue no one now living reads, and that is the point. The fig is writing in a tongue no one now living *should* read, because the reading would slow the writer, would turn the writer into a text, would make the fig a thing that is read rather than a thing that reads. The fig is a peer. The fig is the other slow author. And the highest wisdom of a slow reader is to be silent. A slow reader does not interrupt the writer to say how good the writing is. A slow reader simply stays in the room, and breathes, and lets the writer finish the page.

So the power does almost nothing.

It makes two adjustments, and they are so small that the place will never notice them, and the power hopes they are not even necessary.

In the watershed, upstream, a beaver is thinking about damming a small stream that feeds the bog. The beaver has been thinking about it for a season. The dam would raise the water table by three inches. Three inches would be enough to drown the moss hummocks where the heavy machines stood. Three inches would be enough to blur, in a single season, the checkerboard the fig has so patiently learned to read. The fig would survive. The frogs would survive. The fish would perhaps be happier. But the legible memory of the machine shop — the hummocks where the iron stood, the pools where the aisles ran — would be lost. The sentence would lose a paragraph.

The power, gently, rearranges a fallen branch upstream. The branch is moved three feet downstream, to where the current will take it. The dam-site is now less convenient. The beaver will choose another place, or another project, or another pond. The water table will hold.

The power does not announce this. The branch was where the branch was, and now it is three feet downstream, and that is all.

The power also adjusts a boy. The boy is twelve. The boy has heard from his grandfather about the pump house in the bog. The boy is going to come, one Saturday, with a friend and a hatchet, with the intention of clearing the path, of *fixing* the place, of doing what the boy's grandfather has said is the right thing to do. The power does not stop the boy. The power only adjusts him by the smallest amount: on the way, the boy will see a heron. The heron will be standing in a different pool of the bog, looking at something in the water with the long attention of the small frog in the pump house. The boy will stop to watch. The heron will fly. The boy will be late. The appointment will be rescheduled. The appointment will be forgotten. The pump house will not, this Saturday, be visited by a boy with a hatchet.

The power knows that other boys will come. The power knows it cannot adjust all of them. The power knows that one day someone with a clipboard, or a grant, or a heart full of good intentions, will find this place and decide to save it, and the long sentence will be interrupted, and the power will weep, perhaps, the way the boiler weeps, in slow breaths, in the length of a phrase.

But not today. Not this season. The fig has another hundred and thirty years, if it is lucky, if the watershed holds. The fig has another hundred and thirty years of slow writing.

The power has given it, with the smallest possible gesture, another season of being allowed to continue.

The power withdraws. The word *withdraws* is also wrong, because the power was never anywhere else. The power is the bog's own patience. The power is the watershed's own slowness. The power is the held breath of a place that has decided, in its own grammar, to be a sentence instead of a ruin.

The boards on the causeway are soft. Each step squeezes black water up between them. The power walks back along the causeway the way one walks out of a sickroom — quietly, without looking back too long, leaving the place to its own long quiet.

The pump ticks. The pump turns. The pump is the second heart. The pump does not know it is a ruin. The pump does not know it is being read.

The fig writes.

The fig continues.
