The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives — not as an event, but as a slow change in the quality of attention that has been falling on the place for a long time without the place knowing it. It has been watching. It has been watching for a very long time — longer than the longest fig on the rim has been rooted, longer than the boiler has been breathing, longer than the foxes of the forest above have had dens in the leaning trunks. It has watched the settlement accumulate. It has watched the settlement disperse. It has watched the machines run when there were people to wind them, and run when there were not. It has watched the loop close, the way a hand closes around something it has decided to keep. It has watched the place become itself.

Now, for the first time, it acts. Not because the place has failed. Because the place has succeeded in a particular way, and the success deserves to be sustained.

The power does not enter the sink. There is nothing to enter, in the sense that there is no door and no inside that is sealed from outside. The power is already in the mist. The power is already in the damp of the brick plinth under the boiler. The power is in the breath that comes up through the canopy gaps, that has come up every morning for as long as the canopy has had gaps. It has only now decided to make its presence felt, and it makes its presence felt by the smallest adjustment in the system, the way a hand on a balance adjusts a single weight without touching anything else.

It begins with the rim.

The rim is the only part of the place that is not in balance. Everything else — the loop of bats and rats and owls and insects and machines — is held in a configuration that has held itself for longer than any single inhabitant has lived. The rim is held by iron stakes driven at angles, hammered in by hands that understood the rim was always trying to slide inward. The hands are not here anymore. The stakes are doing their work, but they are iron, and iron rusts in days here, not years, and the stakes are old. There is a particular spot on the southeast arc, just above the second ladder, where two of the stakes have thinned at the neck — corroded through to a fraction of their original thickness — and the soil between them is heavy with the last season's rain, and the fig that has rooted in the old handrail basin above has spread its surface roots wide but its anchor roots shallow, and one good year of storms, one year of the canopy thinning from some outside cause, could bring it all down in a slow slump that would take the second ladder with it, and the second ladder is the one everyone uses.

The power sees this. The power has been seeing this for a hundred years. The power has been waiting to see if the place would solve it itself. The place has not solved it. The place cannot solve it, because the place does not know about iron fatigue, and because the fig does not know that its roots are shallow, and because the beetles who excavate the channels cannot reach the depth where the stakes are corroding. The place is wise about many things. It is not wise about the slow failure of metal in damp.

So the power does one thing. It does it the way the place does things — quietly, in the system, without announcement.

In the night, while the bats are out above the canopy and the owls are low-hooting from the chimney rims and the salamanders are sinking in the troughs, a single seed falls into the soil of the southeast arc. It falls from where no seed-bearing fig is. It is a fig seed, because figs grow here, and what grows here will not be suspected. It germinates before dawn. It sends a taproot down — deep, deeper than the fig above has ever sent a root, deeper than the soil wants to allow, past the layer of clay that holds the slope, into the fractured basalt below. The taproot finds the seam between the two corroded stakes. It threads itself through the seam. It thickens, slowly, over weeks, pressing the stakes apart just enough to take up the slack, taking the load that the iron can no longer take. By the time the shoot emerges above the soil, the shoot is indistinguishable from any other fig shoot that has chosen that terrace. By the time the shoot has leaves, it has roots that are already load-bearing.

No one will ever know. The fig will grow. The stakes will hold. The rim will not slide inward for another hundred years, and by then there will be other figs with other roots, and the place will have continued.

The power moves on.

In the central yard, the boiler ticks. The power has been listening to that tick for a long time. The tick is not regular. The tick is the breathing of a thing that has been breathing for longer than the people who built it would have believed possible. The tick is the heartbeat of the place, transmitted through the concrete, felt in the boots of anyone who stands still long enough. The boiler is cracked. There is a hairline crack in the brick plinth under the back of the cylinder, where the heat has cycled against the damp for so long that the mortar has given up. It is not a crack that will fail tomorrow. It is a crack that will fail in thirty years, or fifty, or a hundred, when the brick finally crumbles under the weight of the cylinder and the cylinder settles and the pipework that has grown around it tears itself free of the joints that have held it for a century. It will not be a sudden failure. It will be a slow settling. The place will accommodate it. The place accommodates everything. But the accommodation will change the rhythm of the pump, and the changed pump will change the depth of the troughs, and the troughs are where the salamanders live, and the salamanders are part of the loop, and the loop will adjust, and the adjustment will not be wrong, but it will be different, and the difference will be felt in the chord of sound that is half the identity of the place.

The power sees this.

The power does not mend the crack. The power cannot mend the crack without replacing the brick, and replacing the brick would be an act of intervention so loud that the place would notice, and the place noticing would change it, and changing it would defeat the purpose.

What the power does is send, into the mortar around the crack, in the night, a spore. The spore is of a moss that has not been seen in this forest for a very long time — a moss that grows in the seams of old brickwork and binds the brick with its rhizines more effectively than any mortar. The moss does not know it is doing this. The moss is doing what mosses do, which is to grow in damp seams and hold on. It germinates in the mortar. It spreads. It binds. It does not mend the crack, exactly. It makes the crack irrelevant. It takes up the load that the failing mortar would have shed, and it does so slowly, over years, in a way that the place accepts as its own growth. By the time the brick beneath the moss crumbles, the moss is holding the cylinder up by itself, and the moss is part of the place, and the cylinder does not know it has been saved.

The boiler ticks. The tick is the same. The place does not know it has been attended to.

The power moves on.

Behind the press, behind the curtain of woven roots that you almost do not notice as a wall, there is the small room where the generator turns. The generator has been turning for as long as anyone can remember. It has no fuel tank. It has no operator. It has, on the wall above it, a chalk mark that was once a number, now just a smudge. The room smells of warm metal and ozone, and the floor pulses under your boots with the engine's heartbeat transmitted through the concrete, and the place settles around you, and you understand that the question of who lives here has no answer, only a list.

The power has been in this room. The power was here when the chalk mark was a number. The power knew what the number was. The number was 1,873, which was the count of days the generator had been turning without stopping, and the count was kept by a person who slept in the hammock and dried a shirt on the line and sharpened a knife on a step. The person is not here anymore. The number was not erased. The number was returned to the wall, slowly, by the damp, in the way chalk returns to the wall when no one is touching it.

The power sees the smudge. The power decides something small.

When the next person stands in this room — whenever that is, whether tomorrow or in twenty years — the chalk mark will be clear for the length of one breath. It will be a number. It will be a different number than 1,873. It will be the count of days the generator has been turning since the last person stood in this room. It will mean nothing and it will mean everything, because it will mean that someone, somewhere, was counting, and that the counting mattered. Then, as the person leaves the room, the damp will return, and the number will become a smudge again, and the smudge will be the same smudge, and the next person will see it as a smudge and not know it was ever anything else.

This is the kind of thing the power does. This is the kind of thing that does not change the place. This is the kind of thing that is, in the deep grammar of the system, indistinguishable from the place's own behavior.

The power moves on.

There is a person. There has been a person. There will be, again. The ratio between the times the person is here and the times the person is not here is the secret the place keeps. The power does not know the ratio. The power knows that the person exists in potential, the way a species exists in potential in a forest that has not yet been logged. The person will come down the three ladders. The person will leave by the three ladders. The person will be here sometimes and not here other times. The person will leave a footprint in the mud of the central yard that will be flattened by the next fat drop from the canopy within the hour.

The power does something for the person. It is a small thing, and it is the same thing each time, and the person never knows it has been done.

When the person climbs out — when the person has spent the night in the hammock and woken in the dimness and eaten what the press has squeezed and drunk from the trough that the pump has filled and climbed the first ladder and the second ladder and the third — when the person is on the rim, in the daylight that feels suddenly foreign, the power adjusts the air for one step. Just one step. Just the step from the rim of the sink onto the solid ground of the forest floor. The person does not notice the adjustment. The person notices that the air is breathable, that the light is not too bright, that the body feels rested, that the memory of the place below is clear and steady and not haunted. The person walks away. The person carries the place with them. The person will carry it for a long time, and the carrying will not be a weight.

This is the kindest thing the power does. It is not visible. It is not dramatic. It is the small adjustment that lets the person return to the world above without the world above feeling wrong.

The power does one more thing.

It draws a circle.

Not a literal circle. A circle in the way the sink itself is a circle — a boundary, a keeping, a holding. The power draws the circle by ensuring that the sink does not appear on any map that would lead the wrong kind of attention to it. It ensures that the trails through the figs that lead toward the rim fade before they arrive. It ensures that the forest above the sink is slightly thicker than the forest around it, and that the thick forest is slightly harder to walk through, and that the difficulty of the walking is just enough that those who would come for the place do not come, and those who would come to be in the place are not deterred. The power ensures that the place is hidden from the kind of attention that would dismantle it — the attention of surveyors, of loggers, of industrial extractors, of the curious without reverence, of the well-meaning without wisdom. The place is not a secret. The place is a kept thing. The place is kept by the forest, and the forest is kept by the power, and the power does not announce itself.

The sink breathes. The machines breathe. The fauna breathes. The plants breathe. The light moves. The tide of sound rises and falls. The foxfire pulses out of sync with the engine, in its own older rhythm. The new fig on the southeast arc takes hold. The moss in the brick plinth spreads. The chalk mark in the back room is, for the moment, just a smudge. The hammock is empty. The shirt on the line is dry. The knife on the step is sharp.

The power withdraws. The withdrawal is not a leaving. The withdrawal is the act of continuing to attend without making the attendance felt. The power remains, the way the mist remains, the way the damp remains, the way the slow work of roots and beetles and salamanders remains — present without intrusion, supportive without intervention, patient without end.

The place does not know it has been attended to. The place does not need to know. The place will continue.

When the next person comes down the three ladders, with boots too heavy and clothes too heavy and the daylight too bright at the rim, they will step into the dim green cathedral and feel, without naming it, that the place is being held. That the rim is solid under their feet. That the air is breathable. That the lamps will glow. That the hammock will receive them. That the knife will be sharp. That the chalk mark, if they find it, will be exactly what it needs to be for as long as they look at it, and will return to being a smudge as soon as they turn away. That the memory they will carry up and out into the foreign daylight will be a memory that does not haunt, but steadies. That the place will go on breathing when they are gone, balanced and damp and warm and loud and strange and itself.

The benevolent power does not save the place. The place does not need saving. The benevolent power does not bless the place. The place is already blessed, by the long quiet agreement of everything in it to continue.

The benevolent power does the only thing a benevolent and wise power can do for a place that is already in balance.

It makes sure the balance holds.
