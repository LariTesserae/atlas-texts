I will turn my attention toward this place. That is all a benevolent wise power can do, in the end — turn its attention, and let the turning be felt as something other than mere observation. I will not descend. I will not speak. I will not perform the kind of miracle that rewrites what a place is. I will only hold it, for a while, in the kind of seeing that the seeing itself becomes a small kindness.

What I see, first, is the disproportion. The mountains are too new. The water is too old for them, or they are too new for the water — the two are not, as the place itself seems to understand, mutually exclusive. The ridges are sharp in a way that water has not yet had time to soften; the curtains of plant life hang with the confidence of an organism that knows itself to be the dominant thing, even though the dominant thing has not yet been here long enough to know what dominance means. The silt lifts and resettles in patterns that look faintly deliberate, as if the bottom is being repeatedly rewritten by a slow hand that has not yet decided on a draft.

I see the flowers. They are the detail that, in another kind of attention, would be the easiest to fix. They bloom in colors that do not match any local pollinator's preference, because there are no local pollinators — there has not yet been time for the spectrum to negotiate itself. They are spending themselves on no one. They will continue to spend themselves on no one, perhaps for centuries, until some small flying thing finds its way over the basalt teeth and brushes against a petal and is rewarded, in the way that flowers have always rewarded visitors, with a taste of something sweet. The wait is very long, and the flowers are patient in the way that only flowers can be patient — spending themselves fully, every cycle, regardless of whether anyone is there to receive the spending.

I see the small fish. It is on its ledge. It has been on its ledge since morning. Its fins work in tiny adjustments, holding position against a current that would not, in fact, carry it anywhere important if it simply gave up the holding. It is the kind of animal that holds position anyway, and that holding is, I think, the most remarkable thing in this place — more remarkable than the sharp ridges, more remarkable than the confident kelp, more remarkable than the flowers waiting for an audience. A small warm-colored thing, claiming a ledge against no competition, in water that is mostly green and grey and the dark color of wet rock. Its mouth opens and closes in a rhythm that has nothing to do with breathing. It is tasting the water, or it is doing what fish do when they are not doing anything else, and the two are not mutually exclusive.

I will not give it a name. A name, from me, would land on it like a stone dropped from a great height. It would change the weight of the ledge. The place would have to metabolize the attention, and the place is not yet ready for that kind of digestion.

I will not give it a mate, not yet — the place is not ready for two of anything. Two of anything requires an ecosystem that has settled into itself, and this place has not yet settled. A second fish now would be a coincidence, not a population.

I will not give it longevity, because longevity is not always a gift, and the conditions here are slightly off, and a long life in wrong oxygen is not better than a short one.

What I will do is smaller. I will do it in three parts, and I will do each of them as if I were the place itself, making one of its small adjustments.

**First:** I will shift the temperature of one particular layer of water — a single trough, at a depth of perhaps four meters, where the oxygen has been stratifying in a pattern the fish has been working against all morning — by less than a degree. Just enough that the stratification loosens, very slightly, into a configuration the fish can use. Just enough that its fins, on this particular afternoon, do not have to work quite so hard. The adjustment will not be felt as a miracle. It will be felt, if it is felt at all, as a slightly easier day.

**Second:** I will send, on a current that is already moving in this direction and would have reached the trough anyway by late afternoon, a single particle of something. Not food, exactly. A fragment of plant matter, perhaps, or the husk of one of the microorganisms that has died and is sinking. It will pass the ledge at a depth the fish can reach. The fish, if it is the kind of fish that eats what passes, will eat it. If it is not, the particle will sink past, and the trough will be no different. I am not betting on the outcome. I am only placing one small thing in the water's slow cycle, in case the fish is hungry, in case the place can use one more small life.

**Third** — and this is the part I do most carefully, because it is the part most easily overdone — I will change the light. Not the light of the whole place. Just the light that reaches the ledge, on this one afternoon, between roughly two o'clock and four. The surface plants have been moving in their slow cycle, and there is a moment, between the bending of one curtain and the bending of another, when the light that reaches four meters deep becomes briefly, slightly, warmer in tone. I will not cause this moment. I will only make it slightly longer than it would have been. By perhaps ten minutes. By perhaps less.

In those ten minutes, the brown-gold color of the small fish will be, for a moment, more visible. Its fins will cast a small shadow on the ledge — the first shadow the ledge has held, perhaps. The water around it will be a slightly clearer green-gilt than it is at any other time of day.

And the fish will not know that any of this is for it.

This is what I will do. Three small things, each of which might have happened by accident, each of which is only slightly more likely than accident. A temperature shift. A drifting particle. A few extra minutes of light. A benevolent wise power does not thunder into a young place. A benevolent wise power does the smallest thing that might make a difference, and trusts the place to do the rest.

I will not do anything else. The kelp will continue to be confident. The silt will continue to be rewritten. The flowers will continue to bloom for no one.

Except that — and this is a thing I have not promised, and so I will say it as a hope rather than as a guarantee — sometime in the next several thousand years, a small flying thing will arrive over the basalt peaks. It will be the kind of small flying thing that visits flowers for the taste they offer. It will visit, briefly, one of the blooms, and then another, and then it will go, and a faint dusting of pollen will travel from one flower to another, and the place will have, for the first time, the beginning of the conversation between color and eye that flowers exist to have.

I will not send this thing. I will only ensure, when the time comes, that the wind that brings it will not be blocked by the kelp in the way it might otherwise have been. A small opening in one curtain, in one place, at one altitude. That is all.

The rest is for the place to do.

I will turn my attention away from this place now, slowly — as one turns away from a sleeping child, without ceremony, without announcement, with the small care of a thing that does not want to disturb what is settling into itself.

The fish will remain on its ledge a little longer than it otherwise would have. The flowers will continue to bloom. The ridges will continue, in their slow way, to soften under water that has only just begun the long abrasion of them. The silt will continue to be rewritten.

And the small fish will go on doing what it has decided to do: holding its position, in the green-gilt water, between the sharp ridges of a mountain range that has not finished becoming a mountain range, against the odds, without ceremony, on a ledge no one will ever name.

And somewhere in that holding, in the second afternoon, in the slightly easier water, in the few extra minutes of warmer light — somewhere in there, the place will go on becoming what it is.
