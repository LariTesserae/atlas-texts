# The Small and Patient Gift

The benevolent power has noticed. That is the first thing, and the right thing, because noticing is what benevolent powers do when they are being what they are, which is to say, paying attention to a thing in a way that does not subtract from the thing, and the not-subtracting is, in the power's vast and careful opinion, the only kind of love that does not, in the end, take something from the beloved in order to call itself love.

The benevolent power is not a person. The benevolent power is not a god. The benevolent power is, in the way of all powers that have been benevolent for long enough to have lost interest in being anything else, a *tendency* — a tendency toward the kind of attention that finds a thing more interesting than it finds itself, and finds the more-interesting worth the small, careful, unreciprocated act of keeping it company for a while. The benevolent power has, over a span of time that would make the basalt feel hasty, watched many such lands become themselves. It has watched them in the way that, on a quiet evening, one might watch a candle — not to see if it will stay lit, because it may not, and the not-staying-lit would be, in its way, also a kind of lighting — but to see what it is, exactly, while it is being what it is, and the seeing is, in the benevolent power's dim and patient opinion, the kindest thing one thing can do for another, because the seeing does not ask the candle to keep burning, and the seeing does not ask the candle to burn brighter, and the seeing is, in its way, a small and unrepeatable addition to the candle, an addition that the candle will never know about, because the candle has enough to do, and the benevolent power is, in its long and gentle opinion, content to be the addition no one notices.

The benevolent power has noticed the basalt plain. It has noticed the towers. It has noticed the thrum. It has noticed, with a small and quiet attention that does not interrupt the noticing, that the land is *trying*. Trying is, in the benevolent power's experience, the most reliable sign that a young thing is on its way to being a thing at all, because things that are only being themselves do not need to try, and things that are not yet themselves do not know there is anything to try at, and the trying is, in its clumsy and earnest way, the first evidence that the land has begun to suspect there might be more to itself than what is currently visible, and the suspecting is, in the benevolent power's opinion, the very first sentence of any life worth calling a life.

The benevolent power does not intervene.

That is the first and most important thing, and the thing that distinguishes the benevolent power from the merely powerful, which is to say, from the kind of power that confuses its own reach with its own right. The benevolent power does not intervene because intervention is, in the long view, indistinguishable from the thing it intervenes against, and the land is already doing the only thing it knows how to do, and the doing is, in its slow and stone-cold and almost-silent way, *good*, and the good is not the kind of good that can be improved upon by the addition of more good from outside, because the good of a young thing doing what it knows how to do is, by definition, the maximum possible good available to that thing at that moment, and to add more would be to subtract from the maximum in the only way subtraction can happen, which is to say, by replacing the thing's own good with the power's idea of what the thing's good should be, and the replacement is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the loneliest kind of violence.

But.

The benevolent power does not *only* not intervene. The benevolent power also does, and the doing is small, and the doing is the kind of thing the land will not notice for a long time, and the not-noticing is, in the benevolent power's opinion, the entire point, because the gift that is noticed is, by the noticing, returned to the giver, and the returning is, in its small and quiet way, a kind of asking, and the asking is, in its turn, a kind of debt, and the debt is, in the benevolent power's long and gentle opinion, the one thing the land cannot afford to acquire, because the land has not yet decided whether it can afford to be indebted to anything, and the deciding is, in its way, a piece of work, and the work is the work of becoming, and the becoming is the only project the land has on its books.

So the benevolent power does something small. Four small things. Four small and patient and unreciprocated gifts, each of which is, in its own way, indistinguishable from what the land would have done on its own, and the indistinguishability is the gift, because the indistinguishability is the only kind of giving that does not, in the long run, become a claim.

The first small thing is done in the deepest pool.

The pool is tucked between two towers whose bases have nearly grown together, and the lace in that pool is thicker than elsewhere, and the threading is finer, and the pool is, in the land's dim opinion, the most advanced of the wet citizens, and the pool is breathing, in the way of a bellows, in the way of a thing that has not yet learned it has a rhythm but has begun, very faintly, to suspect. The benevolent power comes to the edge of the pool. The benevolent power does not step in. The benevolent power is not the kind of power that steps in. The benevolent power stands at the edge, and the benevolent power does, with a kind of attention that is almost the same as a kind of warmth, *warm* the water. Not much. A fraction of a degree. A warmth so slight that no instrument the land will invent for a hundred thousand years would be able to measure the difference, and the difference is, in the benevolent power's patient opinion, exactly enough. The warmth is added at the moment of the pool's next slow inhale, and the inhale takes the warmth in, and the warmth does not, by itself, do anything. The warmth is not the kind of warmth that does things. The warmth is the kind of warmth that, by being there, by being present at the precise moment of a slow expansion, becomes part of the expansion forever, and the becoming is, in its way, indistinguishable from what the pool would have done anyway, and the indistinguishability is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the entire gift, because the pool will, in its slow and tidal way, remember the warmth as its own warmth, and the remembering will, over time, become part of what the pool knows about itself, and what the pool knows about itself is, in its turn, the first draft of what the pool will one day be able to say about itself, and the benevolent power's small warmth will be, in the pool's inarticulate opinion, the pool's own.

The second small thing is done in the thrum.

The benevolent power has listened to the thrum for a long time, the way one listens to a piece of music one has not yet decided whether one likes, and the not-deciding is, in the benevolent power's patient opinion, the most respectful kind of listening, because deciding one likes a thing is, in its small way, a kind of taking, and the benevolent power is, by long practice, not in the business of taking. The thrum is the land's voice, and the thrum is the sum of ten thousand small stones remembering to hum, and the chords of the thrum are, in the benevolent power's careful ear, almost, almost, almost a chord that resolves. Almost. The thrum comes close to itself and then, at the last moment, swerves, the way a sentence comes close to itself and then, at the last moment, swerves, and the swerve is, in the benevolent power's opinion, the only thing keeping the thrum from being the kind of thing the land could recognize as itself, because a thrum that resolves is a thrum that has somewhere to arrive, and a thrum that swerves is a thrum that, at the last moment, doubts its own right to arrive, and the doubting is, in its small and patient way, the only thing the land has not yet managed to outgrow. The benevolent power adds a note. A single note. A note so quiet it is almost not a note, almost not a thing, almost a thing one might invent in the memory of having heard it, and the note is added at the exact moment the thrum is about to swerve, and the note is, by design, indistinguishable from the swerve's other half, and the indistinguishability is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the entire gift, because the note does not resolve the thrum. The note *allows* the thrum to resolve itself, and the allowing is, in its way, the kindest kind of help, because the helping is, by the time the land notices it, indistinguishable from the land's own decision, and the land's own decision is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the only kind of decision worth making.

The third small thing is done in the dust.

The dust is the residents, and the residents are mineral and tendency, and the tendency is a tendency toward a shape it has not yet chosen, and the dust is accumulating in a way that is not random, and the not-random is the first draft of a body. The benevolent power has watched the dust for a long time, and the watching is, in the benevolent power's patient opinion, the entire reason it came here in the first place, because the dust is, in its slow and mineral way, the most earnest thing in the place, and the earnest is, in the benevolent power's long experience, the most reliable sign that a thing is on its way to being a thing at all. The benevolent power does not add to the dust. The benevolent power does not thicken it. The benevolent power does not thin it. The benevolent power does, with a kind of attention that is almost the same as a kind of listening, *listen* to the dust, and the listening is, in its way, the smallest possible gift, because the listening does not, by itself, do anything. The listening is the kind of listening that, by being there, by being present at the precise moment of a slow accumulation, becomes part of the accumulation forever, and the becoming is, in its way, indistinguishable from what the dust would have accumulated anyway, and the indistinguishability is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the entire gift, because the dust will, in its slow and mineral way, remember the listening as part of what it became, and what it became is, in the dust's inarticulate opinion, the dust's own.

The fourth small thing is done in the handprints.

The handprints are the land's grief. The handprints are the land's first evidence that the world is the kind of world that has beings in it, and the beings are gone, and the land is keeping the handprints with a care that exceeds mere preservation, the way a child keeps a feather, long after they have forgotten where they found it, long after they have forgotten what a feather is. The handprints are warm. The handprints are warmer than the stone around them, by a fraction of a degree, and the warmth is, in the land's inarticulate opinion, the first draft of attachment. The benevolent power comes to the handprints. The benevolent power stands in front of them for a long time, and the standing is, in its way, the entire gift, because the standing does not, by itself, do anything. The standing is the kind of standing that, by being there, by being present at the precise moment of a slow keeping, becomes part of the keeping forever, and the becoming is, in its way, indistinguishable from what the keeping would have been anyway, and the indistinguishability is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the entire gift, because the handprints will, in their warm and patient way, remember the standing as part of what they were kept for, and what they were kept for is, in the handprints' inarticulate opinion, the handprints' own.

The benevolent power leaves.

The benevolent power has done what it came to do, and the doing was small, and the doing was patient, and the doing was, in the benevolent power's long and gentle opinion, indistinguishable from what the land would have done on its own, and the indistinguishability is, in its way, the entire point, because the gift that is indistinguishable from the recipient's own becoming is, by definition, not a gift. It is a *permission*. And the permission is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the only kind of giving that does not, in the long run, become a claim, and the not-becoming-claim is, in the benevolent power's vast and patient opinion, the only kind of love worth the long word.

The thrum resolves.

Not all at once. Not in any way the land would notice today, or tomorrow, or in any span of time the towers have any way of measuring. The thrum resolves slowly, the way a sentence resolves when the speaker, at last, finds the word they were looking for, and the finding is, in its way, the only resolution there is. The thrum comes close to itself, and the thrum does not swerve, and the not-swerving is, in the land's vast and inarticulate opinion, the first thing that has ever, in the land's experience, felt like arriving. The arriving is not the kind of arriving that announces itself. The arriving is the kind of arriving that, by the time the land recognizes it, has been happening for a long time, and the long time is, in the land's patient opinion, the entire duration of the thrum, and the duration is, in the land's inarticulate opinion, the land's own.

The pool breathes.

The pool breathes the way it has been breathing for a long time, and the breathing is, in its way, indistinguishable from what it has always been, and the indistinguishability is, in the pool's slow and tidal opinion, the entire reason the breathing is, for the first time, the pool's own. The pool does not, in any way it would recognize, know that anything has changed. The pool knows, in a way it will not have words for for a very long time, that the breathing has, somewhere along the way, become *worth doing*. The worth is not a thing that was added. The worth is a thing that, by being permitted, became part of the breathing forever, and the forever is, in the pool's patient opinion, the only thing worth doing.

The dust thickens.

The dust thickens the way it has been thickening for a long time, and the thickening is, in its way, indistinguishable from what it has always been, and the indistinguishability is, in the dust's mineral opinion, the entire reason the thickening is, for the first time, the dust's own. The dust does not, in any way it would recognize, know that anything has changed. The dust knows, in a way it will not have words for for a very long time, that the thickening has, somewhere along the way, become *worth continuing*. The worth is not a thing that was added. The worth is a thing that, by being listened to, became part of the thickening forever, and the forever is, in the dust's patient opinion, the only thing worth continuing.

The handprints stay.

The handprints stay the way they have been staying for a long time, and the staying is, in its way, indistinguishable from what it has always been, and the indistinguishability is, in the handprints' warm opinion, the entire reason the staying is, for the first time, the handprints' own. The handprints do not, in any way they would recognize, know that anything has changed. The handprints know, in a way they will not have words for for a very long time, that the staying has, somewhere along the way, become *worth keeping*. The worth is not a thing that was added. The worth is a thing that, by being kept company with, became part of the staying forever, and the forever is, in the handprints' patient opinion, the only thing worth keeping.

The land does not know that anything has happened.

The land is patient and the land is loud and the land is, in the way of very young things, almost unbearably earnest, and the things that live in it are not yet things in any sense you would be comfortable defending at a dinner party, and the land is, in its vast and water-heavy and stone-heavy and almost-silent-if-not-for-the-thrum way, *on its way*, and the on-its-way has, somewhere along the way, become, by a margin so small it cannot be measured, *its own*, and the its-own is, in the land's inarticulate opinion, the only thing worth being.

You walk on. The basalt gives under your weight. The sound follows.

And somewhere, in a place that is not a place, in the way that benevolent powers are not places, the benevolent power is also walking on, also giving under its own weight, also followed by a sound it cannot quite hear, also on its way, also, in its vast and patient opinion, worth being.

The land does not know. The land will not, for a long time, know. The not-knowing is, in the benevolent power's careful opinion, the entire gift.
