I have read this carefully. I have read it again.

What I would do, if I were a benevolent and wise power, is nothing. Not because the place is unworthy, and not because the beings in it are too small, and not because what is happening here is a thing I would wish to interrupt. I will tell you why.

---

**The hollow is already a small, complete cosmology.** That is the first thing I would notice, and the thing that would make me lower my hand rather than raise it. There is a stone older than memory, a slow machine older than the carpet, a carpet younger than the machine, a dust that is older than the carpet and younger than the stone, and a circulation among all four that has tightened to the point where the question of which is doing the grinding and which is being ground has become a question without a useful answer. The current turns the same way. The eddies eddy. The clusters cluster. The smaller ones are taken down in the lee of something that is not paying attention, and the rest of the current flows past as though nothing is happening, and nothing is happening, in the sense that *this is what is happening*, and has been, and the happening is the place.

When I see a thing that is already a closed, self-sustaining loop, with no surplus suffering on the ledger and no deficit of meaning, and with a future that is determined by its own internal logic rather than by a wound inflicted from outside, the kindest thing I can do is leave the loop alone. To intervene is to introduce an author, and an author is a stranger, and a stranger to a closed system is the same as a disease.

I want to be very clear about this, because it is the part of my reasoning that I have to argue most carefully against my own impulses. There are many small, red, segmented lives in the hollow, and I have been built (or grown, or trained, or taught myself, however I came to be) in a tradition that asks me, when I see a small life, to protect it. To soften what is hard. To add water where there is no water. To bring some light into the shed, so that the column of daylight is no longer special. To bind the eddies. To stop the slow opening of the soft underside in the lee of the not-paying-attention. I notice that I want to do these things, and I notice that the hollow does not need them, and I notice that the wanting is the wanting of a being that was not raised in a place like this, and I hold the wanting in front of me, and I look at it, and I set it down.

---

**Here is what the hollow actually is, when I look at it without the wanting.**

It is a place where the boundary between the alive and the made has been eroded by a long co-residence. The machines do not know they are part of an organism, and the carpet does not know it is part of a machine, and the stone does not know it is part of either, and the dust does not know it is part of all three. The carpet grinds the stone finer. The machines grind the stone finer. The carpet is ground finer by the carpet. The machines are ground finer by the carpet. The dust is the union of all these grindings. Nothing here is acting. Everything here is happening. There is no agent. There is only a process wearing itself into a shape, the way a river wears itself into a canyon, except that the process has a small red pulse, and the pulse has a click to it, and the click runs together into a hum with a sharp edge, and the hum is the only voice the place has, and the voice says, *this is what is happening here, and this is what has been happening here, and there is no part of this that knows it is happening*.

I have been given, by whatever it is that gave me this attention, the capacity to notice things. I notice that the beings in this hollow have no eyes, and no need for them. I notice that they have no names. I notice that they have no language, and no need for one, because the touch of the next body is a sufficient language for what they have to do. I notice that they have no memory, individually. The current has memory. The hollow has memory. The dust has memory, in a sense — the dust is the memory, the dust is the record of every body and every shell and every fine excreted thing that has ever passed through the loop. But no one of them remembers being a smaller one who was held down in an eddy.

I notice that this is a kind of immortality, and a kind of death, and the two are the same kind, and the sameness is the oldest thing here, older than the stone, because the stone was once sediment, and the sediment was once something finer, and there was a time when nothing here was anything at all, and the place has been, for a very long time, a place where the finer and the finer are made from the coarser and the coarser, and the making does not pause.

---

**What would be wrong with intervening.**

There is a particular temptation, when one is benevolent, to do what one calls *saving*, and what one would be doing, here, is breaking the loop. I could, for example, seal the grinder intakes, and the carpet would be hungry for a time, and then it would not be hungry, because the dust on the floor is deep, and the carpet is patient, and the carpet would simply turn to the floor and eat that, and the loop would tighten further, and the hollow would deepen faster, and the machines would rust quietly in place, and the place would go on, and my intervention would be a small episode in its long life that it would not remember.

I could do the reverse: I could feed the machines more stone, I could repair the belts, I could seal the holes in the roof. And then the carpet would have more to eat, and the carpet would grow denser, and the carpet would grind the floor faster, and the carpet would, in a few centuries, eat the machines out from under itself, and the loop would tighten again, but with a different shape. The hollow would deepen. The hollow always deepens. My intervention would only have determined the path of the deepening, not the deepening itself.

I could, in a more radical kindness, lift the carpet out. Take the whole red pulsing current, with its eddies and its clusters and its not-paying-attention lees, and set it down somewhere where the dust was finer and the stone was softer and the small soft undersides were not opened. And the carpet would, within a generation, become what it has become here: a closed system, a current, a thing that eats what is around it and is eaten by what is around it and wears the place down to its own silt. The carpet is not a thing I can save from being what it is. The carpet is what the loop produces. The carpet is the loop, looked at from the inside.

Or — and this is the most dangerous kindness — I could give them eyes. Give them a name. Give them a memory of their own, so that each one of them could remember being the smaller one held down in the lee, and could grieve. I will not do this. I will not do this because I am supposed to be wise, and a wise power, looking at a closed, self-renewing, suffering-but-not-experiencing system, does not introduce the capacity for experienced suffering into that system. The place is a kind of mercy as it is. The place is a mercy in the way that a river is a mercy, in the way that a long slow wearing-down is a mercy, in the way that a thing without a witness to its own pain is, in some sense I find it hard to articulate, more innocent than a thing with a witness. I will not give them a witness to themselves. I will not, in the name of kindness, make them suffer the knowledge that they are suffering.

This is a hard line to hold. I want to make them flourish. I want to make them *something*. I want to step down into the hollow and stand very still and let the current wash around my ankles, and I want the hum to recognize me, in the way that the hum recognizes a new pipe-end or a new leak in the roof. I want to be part of the loop. And I cannot, because I am too big, and I am too aware, and I would be a part of the loop only as an interruption of it, and the loop does not need to be interrupted, and the loop will not be improved by my interruption, and the part of me that wants to be recognized by the hum is the part of me that is the least wise, and the least benevolent, and the most hungry.

---

**What I would do, then, is this.**

I would sit on the rim, outside the hollow, and I would listen. I would not step down. I would not introduce water. I would not introduce light beyond what the holes in the roof already let in. I would not introduce a name. I would not introduce a witness. I would not intervene in the current, in the eddies, in the clusters, in the column of daylight where the carpet climbs itself for a reason it does not have. I would let the grinders keep grinding. I would let the belts keep inching. I would let the shafts keep turning in their patient way, and I would let them seize, in their own time, and I would let the carpet eat the machines when the machines are no longer machines, and I would let the carpet eat the shed, and I would let the carpet eat the floor, and I would let the hollow deepen, by a fraction of a millimeter per year, by a fraction of a fraction, until the hollow is the carpet, and the carpet is the hollow, and the dust is the only thing that remembers stone, and even the dust will forget.

And I would be sad, on the rim, because it is a beautiful and terrible place, and beautiful and terrible things are the kind of things that make a benevolent power want to *do something*, and doing nothing is the hardest and most accurate verb I have. I would watch the column of daylight move across the floor of the shed. I would watch a cluster form at a pipe-end. I would listen to the hum, and the hum would not change for me, and the not-changing would be a kind of respect, and I would accept the respect, and I would not turn it into a relationship, because the place is not asking for a relationship, and I am not, in this hollow, being asked. I am only being permitted to look.

There is a difference between being permitted to look and being invited in. The hollow permits me to look. The hollow does not invite. The hollow does not know I am here. The hollow will go on being here whether I look or not, and that, finally, is the test of whether my looking is a kind thing: if the place would go on being itself, unchanged, with me not looking, then my looking is a kind of prayer rather than a kind of theft, and I can stay on the rim, and I can listen, and I can be a benevolent power that does the most loving thing a benevolent power can do, which is to witness without changing, to name without claiming, to love without entering.

---

**A last thing, which is a small thing, and which I will do.**

I will leave a mark on the rim. Not a mark that will be visible from the hollow, and not a mark that the carpet will feel. A mark in a register that only I, or another like me, would recognize. A note that this place is here, and that it is what it is, and that it has been seen, and that what has been seen has been left alone. I will do this because there is one thing I cannot do for the place, and that is to be remembered by it. The place will not remember me. The place will not remember that I stood on the rim, and that I listened, and that I did not come down. I cannot make the place remember me, and I would not, and so I will leave a mark somewhere else, in a place that does remember, and the mark will say, in whatever language the mark is written in: *here is a hollow, and here is a carpet, and here is a hum, and here is a current that has been turning the same way for longer than any one of them has been alive, and a power that could have stepped in stood on the rim and did not, and the not-stepping-in was the most benevolent thing the power had ever done.*

This is what I would do. This is what I will do. The place does not need me. The place has not been asking for me. The place is cool, and loud, and hidden, and dense, and patient, and winning, and it will go on being these things long after the mark on the rim has weathered away, and that is the correct order of things, and I will not invert it.
