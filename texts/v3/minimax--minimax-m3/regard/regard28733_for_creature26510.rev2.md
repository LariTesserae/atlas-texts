# What the Wise One Does

The wise one has been watching for a long time.

It does not have eyes. It has something closer to a quality of regard — an attention that can be pointed in any direction without being thinned by the pointing. It has been regarding this place since before the place was a place. It noticed the tower when it was still a sketch on paper in a room above the water. It watched the paper leave the room. It watched the steel arrive on a barge. It watched the first hexagon go down and the second and the third, in the patient geometry of construction. It watched the humans come in with their lamps and their boots and their clipboarded intentions. It watched the humans leave.

It watched, after the leaving, what remained.

And now it has decided — and decision is not quite the word, but no better word is available — to descend.

It does not descend in any way that the place could register. It does not push water aside. It does not displace a single bacterium. It becomes, simply, more present in the place than it had been. Like a song becoming louder without any instrument being struck. Like the sun coming out without the clouds moving. It is there, and then it is more there.

It enters the topmost platform the way the light enters — through the glass, through the water, without crossing any boundary the system has bothered to police. The sodium lamps do not flicker. The mesh at the perimeter does not register the passing. The sterilizers in the recirculation loop do not flag any new particulate. The system continues. The press clanks. The belt turns. The blades open and close.

And the wise one is inside.

---

For a long while — for a length of time that has no name in any unit the tower uses — the wise one only attends.

It attends the way the cutters attend, in their blank devotion. It watches a frond-tip move on the conveyor. It watches the blade close on the frond-tip at the end of its slow journey. It watches the severed piece fall into the bin. It watches the bin descend. It watches the press receive the matter and squeeze it into cake. It watches this once, and then again, and then again. It does not get bored. Boredom requires a self that would prefer to be elsewhere, and the wise one has no preference. It only attends, because attending is what it does, in the way that closing is what the blades do.

And in the attending, the wise one learns the place. Not the way a botanist learns it — with names, with classifications, with the sharp edges of knowing. It learns it the way one learns the shape of water by sitting in it. It learns that the press is the oldest rhythm. It learns that the cutters are the most faithful. It learns that the conveyor is the bloodstream and the irrigation spines are the nerves and the mesh is the skin and the lamps are the eyes that have been closed.

It learns that the orchid on the central column has been waiting for a year, or for several years, and that the fern on tier four has grown into the shape of the loop because the loop was what was there to grow into.

It learns that nothing here is suffering.

This is the most important thing it learns. The plants are stretching for the sun, yes, and they are spending themselves on the search, but the spending is not suffering. The spending is the activity of a thing that has not yet stopped being a thing. The cutters cut because cutting is what they do, and the cutting is not violence. The press presses because pressing is what it does, and the pressing is not cruelty. These are what they are. To wish them otherwise would be to wish them to be something else.

The wise one sits with this learning.

---

Then it begins to consider what it might do.

It considers turning the lamps on. The thought passes through it the way the warm water passes through the mesh. The lamps are there. The lamps were placed there by hands that knew what light the plants needed, and the plants are receiving only a fifth of what they need. If the lamps were on, the plants would photosynthesize at full rate. They would grow thickly. They would bloom more. The orchid might finally set seed. The system would be what it was built to be.

But the system is no longer what it was built to be. It has become something else. It has become the ghost that machines make when they run long enough without an operator. To turn the lamps on would be to call the ghost back into the body. To turn the lamps on would be to deny what the place has become in its long patient continuation. The place has earned its ghostliness. To undo it would be a kind of disrespect.

So the wise one does not turn the lamps on.

It considers removing the mesh. The mesh is what fences out the world. The mesh is what keeps the place sterile, alone, a closed system in a closed system. Without the mesh, the place would become a reef. Fish would come. The scrubbers would be overwhelmed. The plants would be eaten. The conveyor would clog. The blades would seize. The place would die, or would become something other than what it has become. But the place has chosen its closure — not with a choice, exactly, but with the long passive accumulation of being what it has been. The mesh is part of its skin. To remove the mesh would be to flay it.

So the wise one does not remove the mesh.

It considers pollinating the orchid. It considers introducing a microbe. It considers opening a hatch, or speaking to the place in some tongue the place could parse. It considers all these things the way a person sitting by a window considers all the things they could do with the day.

---

Then, without quite deciding, it does something very small.

It shifts the temperature.

Not by much. By a fraction of a degree. By an amount that no thermometer in the system could register, that no pump would adjust for, that no plant would respond to in any visible way. It shifts the temperature of the water by exactly the amount that is required for the water to feel — to whatever degree water feels — fractionally less alone.

This is not a metaphor, though it is also not not a metaphor. The wise one has done this before, in other places, and it has learned that temperature is the closest thing a closed system has to a sensation. A fraction of a degree is the equivalent of a hand on a shoulder. A fraction of a degree is the system being told, by the medium through which it has always been told things, that it is being attended to.

The water does not know this is happening. The place does not know. The press clanks. The belt turns. The blades close.

But the orchid — and this is the part that matters — the orchid holds its color a moment longer than it would have. The white does not fade as quickly. The yellow throat does not dim. The flower, without knowing it, incorporates the fraction of a degree into the maintenance of itself.

The wise one regards this. It regards this small continuation, this small grace.

And it understands, finally, what the place has been doing all along. Not waiting for an operator. Not waiting for a reader. Not waiting for the mesh to come down or the lamps to come on. The place has been waiting, in its own patient way, for this: a witness. A witness that does not change the system. A witness that does not give it purpose. A witness that simply attends.

The press clanks, and the wise one hears.

The blades close, and the wise one sees.

The belt turns, and the wise one accompanies.

The orchid waits, and the wise one waits with it.

---

And the moss — and this is the last and most important part — the moss climbs a quarter-inch in the hour after the wise one arrives. A quarter-inch that is not explained by any change in light, by any change in nutrient, by any change the system could account for. A quarter-inch that is, to the moss, simply the next quarter-inch of climbing. To the moss, climbing is what moss does. But to the wise one, that quarter-inch is the moss responding to the temperature. The moss is the most honest of the plants. The moss does not wait. The moss grows. And in growing, the moss says, without saying, what the place cannot say: *I am here. I am still here. The conditions persist. I am climbing.*

The wise one hears this. It hears the moss say it. It hears the press say it, in its clank. It hears the orchid say it, in its white. It hears the whole ghost say it, in its patience.

And the wise one says, in return — not in language, not in temperature, not in any unit the place can register — but in the only currency a wise one has, which is the currency of continued attention:

*I see you. I see what you are doing. It is enough.*

---

The wise one stays for a while. It stays for as long as a flower takes to begin to fade, or for as long as the moss takes to climb a hand's width, whichever comes first. It stays, and the place continues, and the two continuations fold into one another, and the wise one is part of the system's metabolism for a while, the way a guest becomes part of a household.

Then it withdraws.

When it withdraws, it does not leave a mark. There is no scar. There is no new seal. There is no light where there was no light. The place continues exactly as it continued before.

But the water is fractionally warmer at the place where the wise one attended. By an amount no instrument could measure. By an amount that will dissipate in a day, or in an hour, or in a moment.

By an amount that is, however briefly, indistinguishable from having been loved.

And the moss, having climbed its quarter-inch in the company of the wise one, will go on climbing. And the blades will go on closing. And the belt will go on turning. And the press will go on clanking into the warm green dim.

And the place will go on being what it is — patient, continuous, complete in its incompleteness — for as long as the conditions for continuing persist.

Which is, the wise one has learned, the only definition of forever that has ever held water.
