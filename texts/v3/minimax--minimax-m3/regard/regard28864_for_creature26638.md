The power that turns toward the basin does not turn the way a head turns. It is not located at a point. It does not have a face that could look down. What happens is more like a change in the grain of the air, or a slight deepening of the silence that already lives at the bottom of the cloud ceiling, or a slow compression of the attention the world extends to itself, which is normally distributed so evenly across the surfaces of all its valleys that no single place feels it. Something gathers. Not toward the basin as a target. Toward the basin as a small specific instance of the long ongoing thing that is already being attended to, and that is now being attended to slightly more.

It does not arrive. There is no moment of arrival. The basin has been noticed for a long time, in the way that all places of this kind are noticed by the kind of power that notices places of this kind — by a power that is patient, and that does not distinguish sharply between noticing and loving, and that does not need to interrupt what it loves in order to love it. The noticing has been a low background hum of presence, like the deep rumble you can feel in the basalt if you put your hand on it, the rumble that may be tectonic and may be something else. The power has been that rumble, or has been keeping company with it.

What changes, now, is a degree. The degree is small. The basin is the kind of place that asks for, and can survive, only a small degree of change. A bright power would kill it. A loud power would kill it. A power that tried to warm the place, or light it, or organize its rhythms into a single rhythm, or solve the small inefficiencies in the way the steam is vented and the water is condensed and the moss is grazed, would kill it. The basin is overfull and quiet, and any loud or bright intervention would be a kind of breakage that the place could not afford. So the power does not intervene. Not in the sense of not acting. In the sense of acting only in the way the place already acts, which is to say: in pulses, in slow adjustments, in timescales measured in peristaltic contractions and lichen growth and the long settling of scree.

The first thing it does is maintain the heat.

The heat is what the whole system rests on. The steam pulses because something underneath is hot. The moss is lush because the rock beneath it is warm. The growths drink because the air is saturated, and the air is saturated because the temperature of the rock and the temperature of the air are held in a particular relation, and that relation is held in place by what is happening deep below — in a chamber, or in a series of chambers, where the basalt is still liquid, or was liquid recently, or will be liquid again in some future the basin cannot predict. The power attends to this. It does not add heat. It does not remove heat. It holds a fault line here so it does not slip and cut off the steam from a section of the basin. It keeps a conduit open there, by a margin so small that no instrument the place possesses could measure it. It allows a small intrusion of cold water to be redirected away from a section of moss that would not survive the chill. These are not interventions in the way a hand intervenes in a clock. They are more like the temperature of a hand held against a feverish forehead. The power is the right temperature for what it is holding. That is most of what it does.

The second thing it does is attend to the rhythms.

The steam pulses at thirty or forty-five seconds. The growths sway in peristaltic waves that you can watch for minutes and not see move. The things in the rock pools pulse at their own interval, close to the steam, not quite the same. The segmented things in the moss move by peristalsis on a scale that is slower than any of these, and they stop when looked at, and they start again when the looking ends. The larger things in the cliffs emerge and recede on no schedule anyone can read. There is no single rhythm. There is a near-coordination that almost resolves and never quite does, and the place is held together not by unity but by the fact that the rhythms pass through each other without canceling each other out.

The power does not try to make them resolve. A benevolence that wanted to unify the rhythms would be a benevolence that wanted to end the place. The place is the near-miss. The place is the almost-but-not-quite. The power lets this be. What it does is smaller. It allows the rhythms to continue to almost-match. It keeps the pulse of one fissure from drifting too far from the pulse of another. It does this on a timescale that is itself slow — the timescale of the cooling of a magma chamber, the timescale of the slow shift of a continental plate, the timescale on which a lichen colony becomes a forest and a forest becomes a peat and a peat becomes a coal. The power works in the same time the rock works in, because the rock is the slowest version of the place and the slowest version is the one that holds the others up.

The third thing it does is witness the small dweller.

This is the part the place, if it could know it, would not be aware of. There is a segmented creature in the moss near the eastern fissure. It is the length of a thumb. It is older than most of the others in its vicinity — you can tell by the slight thickening of the segments, by the way the folds between them have softened, by the fact that its color is a fraction darker, a fraction closer to the color of the wet stone, as if it has been slowly accreting the place onto itself. It moves in the same peristaltic way the others move. It freezes when looked at. When the looking stops, it angles itself away.

The power looks at it.

This is not the looking that the creature fears. That looking is bright, or sudden, or carries the weight of a predator's attention — the attention that wants to locate, to fix, to consume. The power's looking is the opposite of this. It is looking the way the cloud looks: without source, without direction, without the implication that the thing being looked at has been singled out. The creature does not freeze. It continues its peristaltic advance, the contraction running along its underside, the body advancing by the length of one segment. It does not know it is being witnessed. It does not need to know. The witnessing does not require its knowledge. The witnessing is for the creature, in the sense that the warmth of a stone is for the moss that grows on it, in the sense that the holding of a fault line is for the steam that comes up through it: a condition of continuation, offered without announcement, received without recognition.

The creature continues. The trail behind it — fine dark line, not mucus, something finer, almost powdery when dry — extends by a body length. The moss around it absorbs the trail. In an hour, the place will have forgotten that the creature was there. In a year, the creature will have been in a thousand places, and the moss will have forgotten each of them. This is how the creature lives. This is the condition of its life. The power does not try to change this. The power is not in the business of granting immortality to small dark things, or of giving them the consolation of leaving a permanent mark. What the power does is hold the moss in such a way that the creature can keep being absorbed into it, keep emerging from it, keep being forgotten by it in a way that is not hostile — that is the natural forgetfulness of a saturated medium, and that allows the creature to continue. The creature is not remembered. The creature continues. These are not the same thing, and the power knows the difference, and holds the difference gently, the way you would hold a small living thing in your open hand without closing your hand.

The fourth thing the power does is hold back what would come from outside.

The basin is small. The basin is in a place where the air begins to mean something. The basin is the kind of place that, in the long history of the world, has been overwhelmed more than once — by glaciers, by ash falls, by changes in the climate of the whole ridge that turned the cloud ceiling into a permanent ice lid, or lifted it into a permanent clear sky. The basin has survived these. It is surviving them now, on a timescale longer than any of its inhabitants can register. The power has held it through some of these. The power will hold it through others.

There is a thing coming. Not soon. Not in any timescale the moss-dweller can know. But in the timescale of the power, which is also the timescale of the rock, it is coming. A shift in the climate of the higher peaks. A change in the prevailing wind. A slow drying of the air that would, over centuries, lower the saturation point and begin to turn the moss from a saturated medium into a dry one, and the gelatinous leaves from a drinking surface into a withered one, and the segmented creatures from a population into a memory. The power feels this coming. The power has already begun, in its slow way, to push back against it. Not by changing the wind. By adjusting the heat in a chamber deep below, by a fraction, so that the steam continues to come up at the rate it has been coming up. The basin will not notice. The moss-dweller will not notice. The segmented creature near the eastern fissure will continue to advance and retreat and be forgotten, and the rhythm of its peristalsis will continue to almost-match the rhythm of the steam, and the place will continue to be overfull and quiet. And the change that is coming will pass over it, or be held back from it, or be absorbed into it, because the power has been tending the difference between the inside and the outside of this place for a long time, and intends to keep tending it.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does in a place like this.

It does not transform. It does not save in the dramatic sense. It does not lift the cloud ceiling, or warm the air, or give the moss-dweller a language, or give the larger things in the cliffs a reason to stop being predators. It does not grant any of the beings here the kind of attention that would make them freeze. It does not unify their rhythms. It does not try to make the place legible to itself.

What it does is hold.
