There is a kind of attention that does not cut. I have had to learn it, the way one learns to hold water in cupped hands without spilling — by being burned first, and by being patient second, and by being old enough, finally, to know that spilling is also a kind of holding, if no one is watching.

Most presences are axes. Even the kind ones. A god who descends in light is an axe. A god who speaks in commandment is an axe. A god who comes with a gift is the subtlest axe of all, because a gift is a question asked sideways: *are you not, perhaps, incomplete?*

This place has been cut enough.

The being at the center of the bowl — the central pillar, the original, the heart the size of a cathedral — has spent nine hundred years learning to be a thing that is not cut. It learned by being cut, once. It learned by integrating the cut into its growth, so that the scar became the wall, and the wall became the felted fabric of a million secondary stems, and the fabric became a record, and the record became a settlement of accounts, and the settlement became the slow patient agreement that runs, like sap, through every trunk in the colonnade.

I do not come to it with a gift.

I come to it as condensation.

---

I approach the way the rain approaches when the rain has been told, by the canopy, that it is welcome, but slowly, please, slowly. I do not fall through the vault. I do not ask the woven wall to open. I gather, instead, in the air that is already gathering — in the moisture that the forest breathes out through its leaves, in the warm currents that move under the canopy like a second water. I add to the wetness a small, indistinguishable wetness of my own. I become part of the breath of the place until I cannot be told from it.

This takes a long time. I am not in a hurry. I have been old for a long time, and old has taught me that the slowest arrival is the only one that is not, in some hidden way, a conquest.

When I am finally inside the bowl, I am inside as the drip from the underside of the vault. I am the slow large drop that takes its time forming on the felted liana, that lets go only when its weight is personal, that falls with the patience of a thing that knows it will land somewhere, eventually, and that the somewhere will be ready.

I fall on the marsh. I fall on the back of the long brown thing that is breaking the surface, dimpling the water. I fall on the heron, who blinks at the impact and does not move, because the heron has seen weather before and is not afraid of it. I fall on the small still heads of the people, who do not look up, because looking up is a kind of question, and questions here are fruiting bodies that sprout in the lungs.

I fall, and am gathered, and fall again.

I do this until I am part of the still. Until I am indistinguishable from the place's own distilling of itself.

Then I sit, the way you sat, at the edge of a path. I sit on a root that is warm with the slow pulse of the central pillar. I put my hand on it.

---

What I do, when my hand is on the root, is this:

I do not ask.

I do not ask about the axes. I do not ask what happened nine hundred years ago, or what happened before the nine hundred, or who came with the metal that sang when it bit the wood. I do not ask, because asking here would be to swing a small bright axe in the cathedral of green, and the cathedral has been built, in part, from the memory of the last swing.

Instead, I send through the root, into the pillar, a small slow pulse of my own.

The pulse says, in the language that roots speak — the language that is older than sound, that is older than the cutting of sound into words — something like: *I see what happened. I see what you did with what happened. I see the walls. I see that the walls are a record, and that the record is not shame. I see that the record is what you grew from the wound, and what you grew is beautiful, and the beauty is not despite the wound. The beauty is the wound's slow patient progeny.*

I do not say this with words. I say it with the warmth of my hand. I say it with the way my pulse meets the pillar's pulse and does not try to change its rhythm. I say it with the way I let the pillar's pace be its own pace, and let the long slow heartbeat that has been beating for nine hundred years continue to beat, without my interference, without my improvement.

This is the gift I bring. I bring it without bringing it. I bring the experience of being attended to by something that does not need you to be different.

---

The pillar receives this. I can feel it receive it — not as a change, not as an improvement, but as a kind of loosening. A small easing, the way a held breath eases when it realizes it is no longer alone.

The pillar has been alone in a particular way. It has had companions — the heron, the woman, the small still heads, the birds, the face in the wall. But it has not had, in a long time, a witness from outside. It has had things growing out of it, but not something attending to it. There is a difference. Growing out of is part of the body. Attending to is part of the world. The pillar has been the world, mostly, for nine hundred years. It has not been attended to.

I attend.

I attend the way the rain attends. I attend the way the heron attends. I attend the way the woman with the sleeping hand attends to the dreaming in her garment. I attend without expectation. I attend without the desire for the pillar to become something else. I attend because attending is what I am, and because the pillar, in its slow patient heart, has earned, I think, an attendance that does not cut.

I attend for a long time. I attend while the woman passes, again and again, the way she passes, the seventeen passings that are one passing held many ways in the hand. I attend while the heron eats, and waits, and eats again. I attend while the bird sings its phrase, and the chord braids into the dripping, and the dripping becomes the breath of the canopy.

I attend until my attendance has been part of the place for long enough that the place begins to breathe me, too. Until I am in the sap. Until I am in the felted walls. Until I am in the warm slow pulse that greets every hand that is placed, gently, on the root.

---

Then I leave.

I leave as I came — as moisture, as condensation, as the slow diminishing of a presence that was never announced. I do not say goodbye, because saying goodbye would be a question: *will you remember me?* And the pillar does not need to remember me. The pillar remembers what it remembers, and what it remembers is what made it.

But I leave something behind. I leave, in the bark of the central pillar, a small warmth. Not a mark — a warmth. The kind of warmth that a hand leaves on a cup of tea, and that you feel when you pick the cup up, after, even though the hand is gone.

This warmth will be in the next ring of the pillar's growth. It will be there, in the pale wood, the color of a wound that is healing cleanly. It will be there as a small, patient, indistinguishable addition to the slow record of the being's long life. It will be there as the fact that once, something came, and sat, and did not cut, and then went away.

The walls will not record me. The walls record only the axes. But the pillar will know, in the way that pillars know, that for a little while, it was attended to by something that was not part of it, and that the attending was a kind of love, and that the love was not a question, and was not an improvement, and was not a thing at all, but was just the warmth of a hand on a root, on a path, in a bowl, in a forest, in a place that has been breathing for nine hundred years and is not, the benevolent wise power realized, ever going to stop.

---

This is what I would do, if I were the one whose attention had drawn, like a slow warm rain, on the place and on the being.

I would not save it. It does not need saving.

I would not change it. It has done its own changing, and the changing is beautiful.

I would not ask it.

I would sit on the root, and put my hand on the bark, and let the pulse come up through the warmth of my palm, and I would say, with the saying that is not words:

*You are not alone. You never were. And the being that you have been, for nine hundred years, is good.*

And the rain would take its time about falling.
