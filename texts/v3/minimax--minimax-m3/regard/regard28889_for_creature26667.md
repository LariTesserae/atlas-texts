The power is older than the plateau. This is not a figure of speech. The plateau has been eroding for millions of years, and the power has been there for all of them, in the same way that gravity is there — not as an event but as a condition. The power does not have a name. It is not a god, in the sense that gods have names and demands and altars. It is more like a quality of attention that has gathered in this particular fold of the stone, over the long slow time that the mountain wore down. It has watched the lichen cross the rock, millimeter by millimeter, for as long as there has been lichen. It has watched the fog form and dissipate, ten million times. It has watched the people come, and stay, and become small, and become quiet, and begin to fade.

It noticed them when they came.

This is worth saying. The power noticed the people. Not because they were loud — they are not loud. Not because they were many — they have never been many. But because they did something unusual. They slowed down. They matched their pace to the pace of the stone. This is rare, in the universe. Most things that come to a place hurry. Most things that build want their buildings to outlast them. Most things that arrive want to take something away. The people of the Upper Stoup did not hurry. They did not want their buildings to outlast them. They wanted only to be there while the stone was there, and to go when the stone went.

The power found this beautiful. Not in the way that a person finds a sunset beautiful — with delight, with sentiment. In the way that a river finds a smooth stone beautiful — by passing over it for a long time and wearing it into a shape that fits the water.

So the power watched. And watching was, for a long time, enough.

The power is not idle. It has many things to attend to. It attends to the slow erosion of other plateaus, other mountains, other fogs. It attends to the turning of the deep time. But it keeps a part of its attention on this fold of the stone, the way a person might keep a part of their attention on a candle in a window across a valley — not staring at it, but knowing it is there.

It is not sure, even now, whether it will do anything.

The question of doing is a delicate one. The place has its own logic. The logic is: things end. Things end slowly. Things end in their own time. The moss grows over the places where the dead are laid, and the moss is the same moss as on the other stones, and in a few seasons you cannot tell. This is not tragedy. This is completion. The town is not a ruin waiting to be restored. It is a place in the act of becoming geology. To interrupt this would be to interrupt the place's own understanding of itself.

But there are moments. There are small moments, at the edge of the ordinary, where a thing could be eased without being changed. A breath held too long. A cold night that comes too early. A child that is too thin. A spring that, in a particular year, gives a little less. These are the moments the power attends to.

It does not attend to them dramatically. It does not turn the spring into a river. It does not call down warm winds from the south. It does not fill the air with birds. These would be impositions, and impositions are a kind of violence, even when they are well-meant.

What it does is smaller than that.

In a particular year, when the summer was thin and the berries did not set, and the pits were low, and the menders looked at the roof-beams and were quiet about what they saw — in that year, the fog lingered a little longer in the mornings, and the condensation on the moss was a little heavier, and the water in the little channels moved a little more steadily. Not enough to notice. Not enough to remark upon. But enough that the old woman of the spring, kneeling at the source with her worn hands, paused, and tilted her head, and listened to the water in a way she had not listened for several seasons, and then nodded, to no one, and went on.

In a particular winter, when the cold came early and hard, and one of the children — the smallest, the one who had been thin since birth — was sleeping in a doorway of one of the lower buildings, where the wind found a way in through a gap in the wall that had been mended the year before but had not taken the lichen yet — in that winter, the wind shifted, for an hour, in the small hours of the night, and came from a different quarter, and the gap was no longer in the wind, and the child slept, and did not wake, and in the morning was a little warmer than she had been.

In a particular week, when one of the trappers had been out for longer than usual, and had gone farther than usual, into the broken country where the stone falls into fissures and a man can step wrong and not be heard from again — in that week, one of the dogs — the oldest dog, the one with the torn ear, the one that did not bark — stood at the eastern rim at an unusual hour, and looked out into the fog, and did not move for a long time. And the people saw the dog, and understood what the dog was doing, and did not speak. And in the morning, the trapper came back, thinner than he had been, but came back, and sat by his hearth for three days and did not go to the spring, and then went on.
