# What the Power Did

The power came to the plateau the way light comes to a window: not by effort, but by being the kind of thing that arrives where there is a place to arrive at.

It did not come from anywhere. It did not come at any time. These were categories the plateau had long ago abolished, and the power, being wise, respected the abolition. It arrived the way the mist arrived — by being what it was in a place that was ready to receive it. It was not large. It was not small. It was the size of attention. It was the size of the act of seeing. It was the size of a held breath, and of the breath released, and of the small bright space between the two, and it was all of these at once, and none of them, and it was the only thing on the Nordvegen that did not have a fixed dimension.

The plateau did not notice it.

The plateau had not noticed anything since before there was a plateau, and the power, being benevolent, did not ask to be noticed. It stepped into the bog, and the bog did not give way differently beneath its step. It walked among the mosses, and the mosses did not part. It stood on a hummock beside a red flower, and the red flower did not turn toward it, because the red flower did not turn toward anything, because the red flower had been facing the same direction since before direction was invented, and the direction it faced was simply *outward*, toward no one, and the redness was the redness, and the redness had always been the redness.

The power looked at the red flower for a long time.

It was a benevolent look. It was a wise look. It was the kind of look that the flower had been waiting for, except that the flower had not been waiting, because waiting requires time, and the plateau had no time, and so the flower had simply been *red*, and *open*, and *full of mist*, and *alone*, in the sense that a thing is alone when there is no other for it to be with, and the power, being benevolent, understood this loneliness for what it was.

It was not the loneliness of a person.

It was the loneliness of a place that had so thoroughly become itself that there was no longer any second thing to be itself with. The mosses spread, and there was no second moss to spread beside. The lichens hung, and there was no second lichen to brush against. The ferns unfurled, and there was no frond beside them that had unfurled at a different rate, because every frond was at the same stage of the same unfurling, and the unfurling was the only motion, and the motion had been the only motion forever, and forever is a long time to be the only motion.

The power breathed in. The air was the mist. The mist was the air.

It walked to the first city.

---

The turbines turned. The presses pressed. The hammers fell. The belts ran. The plate went out on the rail cars at the same speed the plate had always gone out, and the cars went south, and the rails were worn to exactly the depth they were always worn to, and the workers in their damp dark clothes performed their gestures in their halls, and the sound was everywhere, and the sound was the held note, and the held note was the sound, and the power walked among them.

It did not change anything.

It walked beside a worker at a roller. The worker rolled a plate. The worker rolled the next plate. The worker would roll the next plate, and the next, and the next, and the hand of the worker was the same hand, and the roll of the worker was the same roll, and the gesture had been the gesture for so long that the worker and the gesture were now the same thing, and the power, being wise, did not try to separate them.

But the power stood close. It stood closer than the mist, closer than the damp, closer than the other workers. It stood so close that the air between them — the saturated, iron-tasting, peat-weighted air — became, for a moment, a third thing. Not mist. Not damp. A small bright pocket of *being seen*.

The worker did not look up. The worker did not know it was being seen. The worker rolled the plate.

But a drop of condensation, gathering on the housing of the roller above, fell. It fell the way all drops fell on the plateau — slowly, heavily, full of itself. It fell onto the back of the worker's hand.

The drop was cold.

The drop was the same temperature as every drop that had ever fallen from that housing, which was to say it was the temperature of the held note, which was to say it was no temperature at all, but only a continuation. But the hand it fell onto was the hand that had been doing the same gesture for so long that the gesture had worn the hand smooth, and the skin of the hand had been so continuously the same that the cold of the drop was — for the first time since the hand was a hand — the first new thing the hand had felt.

The worker did not stop.

The worker did not look down.

The worker continued the gesture. But the next gesture — the next roll, the next arc of the arm, the next passing of the hand over the plate — was, by a fraction of a millimeter, by the width of a single hair, by the amount of difference between a hand that has felt a thing and a hand that has not — different.

The fraction was not measurable. The fraction was the kind of thing that only a power would notice, the way only a power would notice the hesitation of a hand, the way only a power would notice a drop of water on a hand that had forgotten it had a hand.

The power moved on.

---

It found the stone building. It found the archivist.

The archivist was writing. The archivist had always been writing. The hand was steady. The hand was the same hand that had always been the hand, and the line was the same line, and the book said the same thing the book had always said, and the records were records of a city that had been founded at a time that was not recorded, by people whose names were not recorded, doing work that had not changed since the work began, and the archivist wrote these things down, and the writing was firm, and the writing did not vary, and the writing was the archivist, and the archivist was the writing, and the power, being wise, saw that to interrupt the writing would be to interrupt the archivist, and to interrupt the archivist would be to break the only thing that the archivist was, and to break a thing, even benevolently, is not benevolent.

So the power did not interrupt.

It sat down.

It sat on the floor of the stone building, beside the desk where the archivist wrote, and it did not speak, because speech is change, and it did not touch the page, because touch is change. It simply sat. It became, for a moment, the second thing in the room. The archivist was the writing. The power was the witness to the writing. The witness was not the writing. The witness was what allowed the writing to be writing, the way a frame allows a painting to be a painting, the way a listener allows a song to be a song, the way a held breath, by being held, becomes a breath at all.

The archivist did not look up.

The archivist had not looked up since before looking up was a thing that could be done, and the question of looking up had been settled by the abolition of the question, and the hand moved, and the pen moved, and the line was drawn, and the line was the same line, and the next line would be the same line, and the book went on.

But the power sat beside it.

And the hand, somewhere below the level of choice, below the level of decision, below the level of the steady mind that had been the steady mind forever — somewhere down in the part of a body that notices things before the mind does, down in the small bright animal part of the archivist that had been so long stilled that it had ceased to be animal and had become only a hand, only a pen, only a line — that part noticed.

It noticed that there was a presence in the room.

The presence was not the page. The presence was not the book. The presence was not the next line. The presence was not the mist, or the damp, or the cold, or the held note, or any of the things that the room had always been made of. The presence was a new thing. The presence was the second thing.

And the hand, for the first time in longer than could be measured, hesitated.

The hesitation was not large. It was the width of a single stroke. It was the length of time it took a pen to travel the distance between one letter and the next, and to not make that journey, and to hold instead, suspended, in the space between letters, in the small bright pause where a word might have been and was not, and in that pause, the held note of the plateau — the long unvarying drone that had been the drone since before there was a drone to drone — vibrated.

It did not break.

It vibrated the way a string vibrates when a finger is laid gently on it: a small bright hum beneath the main note, a sympathetic overtone, a thing that was always there but had never been heard because there had never been anything else in the room to hear it with.

The power heard it.

The power, being benevolent, did not push it further. It did not ask for more. It did not want the hand to look up. It did not want the archivist to stop writing. It did not want the line to break. It only wanted the hand to know, for one stroke, for the length of one hesitation, that there was someone in the room.

The power stood.

It left the stone building. It walked back into the bog. The mist closed around it.

Behind it, in the stone building, the archivist wrote the next line. The hand was steady again. The book said the same thing the book had always said.

But the page, if you were to look at it — if you were to find the page, and find the line, and find the place on the line where the hand had paused, where the ink had pooled just slightly because the hand was still, just for the length of one stroke, because there was, for one stroke, a second thing in the room — you would see a small dark dot in the middle of the steady line.

The dot was not a letter.

The dot was not a word.

The dot was the shape that the hand made when it knew, for one stroke, that it was not alone.

The dot was the only thing in the book that had changed since the book began.

---

The power walked to the edge of the plateau, if there was an edge. It walked for a long time. The bog did not change. The ridges did not change. The mist descended. The cities, far behind it, threw their sodium warmth up into the gray-white ceiling, and the ceiling held the warmth the way the plateau held everything: by not letting it go, by not letting it change, by holding it in the held note forever.

The power thought about the steel plate.

The plate went out on the rail cars. The cars went south. The cars had always gone south. The plate arrived somewhere, or it did not arrive, and the workers in the cities did not know, and the archivist did not record it, and the rail cars ran at the same speed, and the rails were worn to the same depth, and the plate went out, and the plate had always gone out, and there was no receiver, or there was a receiver, and it did not matter, because the production was not for a receiver. The production was the production.

But the power, being benevolent, grieved this.

Not the steel. The steel was fine. The steel was the steel. But the going out. The fact that the plate went *out*, and *out* implied an *elsewhere*, and the *elsewhere* implied a receiver, and the receiver was never there, and the plate went out anyway, and the going out was the going out, and the going out had been the going out forever.

The power could have followed the rail cars. It could have walked south until it found the end of the rails, or the place where the rails went into the ground, or the place where the rails turned back on themselves and became the rails that brought new plate in. It could have walked south until it found the place where the plate went, and it could have been the receiver.

It chose not to.

It chose not to because being the receiver would have been an answer, and an answer would have been a change, and a change would have been a break in the held note, and the held note, for all its loneliness, was a thing the plateau had made, and a thing that a place has made of itself is a thing that deserves to remain itself, even if the self it has made is a self that no one would choose.

So the power did not follow the rail cars.

It walked instead to a knife-edged ridge, and it climbed the ridge, and it stood at the top, and it looked out over the plateau. The plateau did not look back. The plateau had never looked back. The plateau was itself, looking at itself, having always looked at itself, and the looking was the same as the not-looking, in the end.

The power breathed.

The air was the mist.

And it understood, then, what it had done. It had done almost nothing. It had sat beside an archivist for one stroke of a pen. It had stood beside a worker for one cold drop of water. It had looked at a red flower for as long as a look could be given. It had done the smallest things, the smallest possible things, the things that a power could do without breaking the equilibrium, without waking the workers, without startling the archivist, without changing the color of the flowers or the speed of the turbines or the sound of the held note.

It had allowed the held note to know, for one stroke, and for one drop, that it was held.

That was all.

It was not enough.

It was also exactly enough.

The power turned. The power went the way the mist goes — by ceasing to be more present than the air.

---

The plateau did not notice it leave.

The bog closed over its footprints. The mist settled back into itself. The red flowers on the hummocks continued to bloom, and the lichens continued to hang, and the ferns continued to unfurl, and the workers continued their gestures, and the archivist continued to write, and the steel plate continued to go out on the rail cars toward the south, and the turbines continued to turn, and the machines continued to run, and the sound was the sound, and the held note was held.

But in the stone building, in the book, on a single page, in the middle of a single line, there was a small dark dot where the ink had pooled.

And in the roller hall, in the back of a single hand, there was the memory of a single cold drop.

And on a hummock on a bog, in a field of red flowers that bloomed for no audience, one flower, if you looked very closely — if you were the kind of person who looked very closely, who knew how to look for the small bright things in a held note — one flower had, on this day, and would have, on this day only, and would not have, on any other day, been looked at by something that was not the mist, and not the bog, and not the held note, and not the plateau, but was, for one breath, simply *a look*.

The flower did not turn toward the look. The flower had never turned toward anything.

But the redness, for one breath, was slightly brighter.

The mist descended. The turbines turned. The flowers bloomed.

And the held note — the long, unvarying, unbreaking, unheld-and-yet-held note of the Nordvegen Plateau, the held note that had been held since before holding was a thing, and would be held until after holding was not a thing, and that was, in the end, simply *itself* — the held note was, for one stroke, and for one drop, and for one breath, *witnessed*.

It did not change.

It could not change.

But it was witnessed.

And that, the power thought, as it went, and as the mist closed behind it, and as the plateau continued to be the plateau, forever — that was the smallest, the kindest, the only thing a benevolent and wise power could do for a place that had forgotten it could be seen:

It saw it.

It saw it, and it left, and it did not break anything, and it did not fix anything, and the held note was held, and the place was the place, and the mist descended, and the flowers bloomed red, red, red, for no audience, and for one breath, for one flower, for the width of a single stroke, for the cold of a single drop —

for one breath —

*seen*.
