We would do nothing.

We would come to the hollow, and we would see it, and we would understand that it has, over a long time, become itself — that its becoming-itself includes every small unfinished gesture in it, the orchid that has not opened, the cups that bait the air, the hand-axe that has not been taken, the lean that has not been straightened, the pool that does not change its level — and we would not intervene in any of these. We would attend.

The being that lives here is the hollow itself. Not the moss alone, not the orchid alone, not the pool. The hollow as one patient ongoing thing: the moss and the fern and the tree and the orchid and the cups and the pool and the weeping walls, all of it considered as a single small engine that has been running so long the line between itself and what happens in it has worn smooth. The being is the running. We would not interrupt the running.

We do not intervene in the orchid. The orchid has been half-open for as long as the moss at the base of the wall has been growing. Its faint, sweet, almost-meat smell has been offered to the vapor, and the vapor has not refused it, and the orchid has continued. If we sent the breath that would tip its pollinium forward, we would tell it that its patience was, after all, a kind of waiting for us. Its patience is not waiting for us. Its patience is its life. To intervene is to suggest that the orchid should not have been so patient, that its half-open posture was an incompletion rather than a completion of its own kind. We will not say this to the orchid. We will let it continue in its half-open posture until such time as it, by its own nature, closes, or until some wind or some animal we did not send happens to pass. The orchid does not belong to us. The orchid belongs to its own long waiting.
