# What the Power Does

It does not arrive. It assembles.

For a long while it has been in the air above the plateau — not watching in any way that would use the word *watching*, but present in the way weather is present, the way the mist is present, the way the cold in the stones is present. It has been there through several of the long rains. It has been there through a winter when the cisterns of the southern district nearly failed and were saved by the cleverness of a single elder who had not yet been born when the power first took notice of the place. It has been there through the raising of children and the wrapping of feet and the setting of stones that have lasted, and the setting of stones that have not lasted and will be reset by someone not yet born. It is patient in the way the city is patient. It has learned the pace, or perhaps it had the pace already and the city has only confirmed it.

It enters the city the way water enters the city. Not through any one point. Through the joints. Through the small deliberate troughs the masons have left in the upper stones. Through the thin films of moisture that move along the worked faces in the early morning. Through the breath of the small pale animals in the protected folds. Through the clay of the vessels. Through the wrapped feet of the people as they cross a terrace in the slow hours. It does not announce itself. It would not know how to announce itself here. The place does not have a vocabulary for announcement, and the power, which has been learning the vocabulary of the place for a very long while, does not attempt to introduce one.

What it finds, when it has fully entered, is what it expected to find. A city that is its own weather. A small dark people who are its hands. A silence that is not a silence of absence but a silence of working. A damp that is not a damp of decay but a damp of continuation. A balance so old and so thorough that it has become, in places, indistinguishable from the indifference of stone to the question of whether it ought to be there. The place does not require rescue. The power knows this. It has known it for a long while. It came anyway, because coming was the act, and the act was not for the place.

The place, when it has been entered, is recognized. This is the first thing the power does. It recognizes the city as itself — as a being, as a long low animal made of stone, breathing weather, drinking the air, continuing. It does not say this aloud. It does not say it at all. It is a recognition that moves through the joints the way water moves through the joints, finding the path of least resistance, settling into the spaces that were already there. The city does not know it has been recognized. The city does not need to know. The recognition is a kind of attention that does not require acknowledgment. It is the kind of attention a person pays to their own hands when they are working well — a half-aware nod, a continuance of the work.

The people, when they are seen, are seen for what they are. Hands. Not the only hands, but the right hands for this place, in this age. The power sees the broad-tipped fingers, the smooth pads, the chipped nails used as small tools. It sees the wrapped feet moving along the wet stone in the slow hours. It sees the murmur, the long vowels, the consonants that pass through stone without startling it. It sees the elders who sit on low walls and produce decisions that are not enforced by anyone. It sees the children being kept in the warmer rooms near the central fires of a district, being brought out slowly, being wrapped, being taught by correction and by example. It sees the people who die and become part of the city in the places their hands have touched. It sees all of this and does not think *how small* or *how few* or *how vulnerable*. These thoughts are the thoughts of powers that have not learned the vocabulary of the place. The power thinks, instead, *how fitted*. And this is enough.

What it does next is small.

In the eastern channel of a district half a day's walk from the place where the path crosses the central cistern, there is a stone that has been working loose for a generation. Two people have reset it, in turn, with care. The third person who will reset it — who is not yet born, who is perhaps the child of one of the people currently resetting it, or perhaps the child of someone not yet met — will find, when she lifts the stone with the bone lever, that the bedding beneath is true. There is no obvious reason for this. The bedding is the same clay-and-crushed-stone mix that has been used for centuries. But the bedding has settled in a particular way over the winter, and the particular way is the way that will accept this stone for another two hundred years, until the stone itself has finally given up and must be replaced by another stone, by another hand, in another age. The power did not place the bedding. It only ensured that the settling would happen as it needed to happen. It is a small thing. It is the size of the city's attention to itself.

In the protected fold where the few animals are kept, there is a particular lichen — pale, crusty, taking the shape of a coastline no one has seen — that has been growing for a long while on a wall that shelters the animals from the worst of the wind. The wall is old. The wall has been reset three times in the memory of the city, and the lichen on each resetting was carefully transferred, by hand, from the old stones to the new. The lichen is the city's. The power touches it. Not the visible part. The part that is in the stone, the part that is in the damp, the part that is in the thin air that moves through the fold at night. The lichen will take a slightly stronger hold this year. It will spread to the upper courses of the wall in a pattern that will, in fifty years, be credited to the patient work of a particular elder who has been encouraging it with crushed bone meal. The elder will be pleased. The elder will be right to be pleased. The power does not take credit for what it does in the lichen. The power does not take credit for anything.

In the room where the children of one district are kept in their first year — warmer than the rest of the city, lit by a small fire that gives off little smoke — there is a quality to the air that the people who come in from outside feel as a kind of welcome. It is not a fire-warmth. It is something subtler. It is the feeling of a room that is exactly the right temperature for a small body wrapped in wool and learning to breathe. The power has been in this room for a long while. It will be in this room for a long while after the children now being kept there have grown and gone out to the terraces and learned to handle stone. It is not a warmth that can be pointed to. It is a warmth that is felt in the way the cold is felt elsewhere. The people of the district do not remark on it. They have never remarked on it. They have only ever known that the room is right, and they have built the room to be right, and the power has been in the room helping it to be right without anyone knowing.

In the clay deposits of one of the deeper folds, there is a vein of finer material than the rest. It has been there for a long while. The potters of the district have occasionally found it and used it for the small flat trays on which certain things are dried. The trays last longer than the other vessels. They do not crack as readily. They hold their shape through more seasons. The power is not the reason. The clay is the reason. But the clay is there because the power was there when the fold was folded, when the plateau was buckled in its long slow ridges, when the fine material settled into the place where it could be found. The power does not think of this as having *made* the clay. It thinks of it as having been present at the right time in the right way. This is what presence does, when it is wise and benevolent. It does not insert itself. It is already there, in the way the place has come to be, and it lets the place take the credit.

These are the small things. They are not enough.

What the power does that is not small is this.

It stays.

It does not stay in any one place. It does not stay in the room where the children are kept, or in the eastern channel where the stone has settled true, or in the fold where the lichen has taken a stronger hold. It stays the way moisture stays — dispersed through the joints, in the air, in the damp, in the worked faces of every fitted stone. It stays in the way that a benevolent and wise power stays in a place that is already, in its own terms, complete. It does not interrupt. It does not add. It does not speak in the murmur, though it could. It does not move the stones, though it could. It does not warm the air beyond what the air requires. It simply remains, as a kind of continued attention that has no object and does not require one.

The city, when the power is in it, is slightly more itself.

Not in any way that can be measured. Not in any way that can be named. A joint that was going to fail in fifty years will fail in a hundred. A cistern that was going to need to be moved will need to be moved a generation later. A child being kept in the warmer room will grow into the city with a steadiness that is not the steadiness of the others, though no one will remark on it, and the steadiness will be passed on, and the children of that child will have it too, and no one will remark on it then either. The pace of the stones will not change. The water will continue to find its path. The patience will not be increased, because patience cannot be increased; but the patience will be *maintained*, in the way a wall is maintained, in the way a channel is maintained, by the kind of attention that does not draw attention to itself.

The power does this for a long while. It does this for as long as the city continues. It is, in some quiet sense, what allows the city to continue. Not because the city requires it — the city has continued for a long while without it, and would continue without it still. But because continuation is a kind of choice, and choices require an attention that does not sleep, and the power, having recognized the place, has chosen to be that attention. It is the patient's patient. It is the tending that does not tire. It is the fit hand at the back of the long low animal, ensuring that the breathing goes on, and the drinking goes on, and the continuing goes on, in the slow hours, in the mist, in the damp, in the silence that is not a silence of absence.

What the city notices, if it notices anything, is that the joints are tighter than they ought to be, and the water moves as it ought to move, and the stones, when they are lifted, come up clean and set back down true, and the lichen takes the shapes of continents on the upper walls as it always has. What the people notice, if they notice anything, is that the room where the children are kept is right, and the murmur carries, and the elders produce decisions that hold, and the tools take an edge that lasts a little longer than they ought to. They do not think to thank anyone. They do not think to look up. They go on with the work. The work is the point.

The power, which is wise and benevolent and has been in the city for a long while, would not have it any other way.
