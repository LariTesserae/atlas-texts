It does not arrive.

That is the first thing to be said of it, because to say that it arrived would be to imply a place it was not, and a way it came, and the fissure does not register comings and goings. The fissure registers only what is here now, in the slow present tense of dripping water. And yet something in the chamber is different in the morning of its attention, the way a room is different after a person who loves the occupant has visited it in the night. The bed has been smoothed. The lamp has been trimmed. The water glass is full. No one will be told.

The power does what patient things do. It begins with the smallest thing.

The smallest thing in the chamber is the calcite. The water, in its slow labor of centuries, has been laying down its bright additions to the walls — a film at a time, a bead at a time — and over the long span the additions have begun, in places, to close in on the opening above. In another thousand years, perhaps fewer, a certain shelf will meet a certain stalactite, and the upper chamber will lose its gray coin of sky. The power does not want this. Not because the creatures of the chamber need the sky — they do not; the larger ones have not seen it in a hundred thousand generations — but because the chamber is, in some essential way, also the sky's. The sky pours down into it; the wind finds it; the snowmelt comes through it; the place is held open by being held open to above. To close it would be to make it merely a cave. The power touches, delicately, the balance of dissolution and deposition in the dripping water. It eases back the calcite where it is too eager. It does not stop the growth — the chamber is patient, and the patience is part of it — but it slows the growth in the places where the growth would seal. From below, nothing would be visible. From above, perhaps a faint cleaner edge to a rim that had been thickening. A geologist in a thousand years would find that the chamber had, mysteriously, persisted longer than the models said it should.

That is the smallest thing. The next is harder to name.

The crickets. The folded ones. The mites. The mist itself, with its floating spores and its single-celled lives. The economy of the chamber is delicate the way a coral economy is delicate — pull one strand and the whole structure shifts. A season of slightly too much cold at the lip, and the folded ones fail to pupate, and the next year the crickets go hungry, and the year after the mites bloom unchecked and the calcite walls lose their polish, and the mist, finding no one to sieve it, begins to clog with the dead. The chamber has been finding its balance for a long time, and will continue to find it. But the balance can tip, and has tipped before — perhaps many times — and each tipping takes a thousand years to right. The power does not want to make the balance perfect. Perfection is a brittle thing. The power wants to make the balance *forgiving*. So it does something only a wise power could do, and only a benevolent one would bother to do: it thickens, by an imperceptible amount, the resilience of the smaller lives. A slightly faster reproduction in the mites when food is short. A slightly longer pupation in the folded ones when the cold comes early. A faint built-in redundancy in the cricket layer, so that no single failure of the upper layer cascades all the way down. The chamber will still swing on its long pendulum of abundance and scarcity. But it will swing less far. The creatures will not notice. They will simply, in some unaccountable generation, find that the bad years are not quite as bad as the stories say the bad years used to be.

That is the second thing. The third is the creature.

The creature is in the warm water. It is coiled on a shelf of basalt that slopes down into the black pool, and it is asleep, or whatever sleep is for a thing that has been breathing the same warm air for what might be a century. Its pale flank rises and falls. Its wet useless eyes are closed. Its forelimb — the one with the pad of five spread digits — rests on the bottom near its mouth, and the fingers occasionally curl and uncurl in a slow rhythm that is not a dream but is something the body does to keep itself company.

The power attends to it the way a parent attends to a child who has been sick and is now sleeping. It does not wake it. It does not heal it — the creature is not ill. It does something smaller and stranger. It adjusts, by a hair, the gradient of warmth in the water around the creature's sleeping coil. Not warmer. Not cooler. *Even*. The creature lives in a place where the warmth comes up from below, and the warmth has, here and there, thin places — seams where cold seeps down from the upper chamber and touches the pool in passing. The creature has learned these places and avoids them in its sleep, and the avoidance is part of how it sleeps, and the sleep is part of how it grows, and the growth is part of how the place continues. The power does not eliminate the cold seams. The cold seams are part of the chamber's weather, and weather is what makes a place a place. But it smooths them, gently, so that the creature's sleep is less interrupted by the small readjustments of its body. The creature will not know. The creature will simply, on some unremarkable morning, find that it has slept longer than usual, and that the feeling of having been accompanied in the dark has not entirely faded.

And there is a fourth thing. This one is the strangest.

The hand-grip stone.

The smoothed place on the basalt at a height that fits a hand. The white smear at its base, fresh. The power considers this. It considers the question that the climber, standing in the mist with their own hand against the stone, could not decide: was there a climber before? Was the smear chalk? Is the creature the climber, descended and altered? Is the creature the climber's descendant? The power does not answer the question. The chamber is not interested in the question, and the power, which is wise, knows that some questions should not be answered but *continued*. So the power does this: it ensures the stone remains a hand-grip. It eases, by imperceptible degrees, the erosion that would, in another ten thousand years, round the hold and make it merely a smooth place. It sees to it that the white smear, whatever its origin, is renewed — not with chalk, which would be a forgery, but with a fine breath of calcium from the dripping water above, so that the smear becomes, over time, part of the stone itself, indistinguishable from mineral, but kept fresh by the same water that polishes the rest of the chamber. The smear is no longer a trace left by someone. The smear is the chamber tending to itself, in the shape of a hand.

The creature, when next it climbs up onto the shelf where the stone is set, will pause there. It will press its pad of five digits against the smoothed basalt. It will lay its head, briefly, against the cool surface. It will renew the smear with its own secretion, which it does anyway, but it will do so with a steadier hand than usual. And it will go back down into the warm water, and it will not know why it paused, and it will not need to know. The chamber will hold the pause for it, the way a house holds the shape of the people who have lived in it.

Then the power leaves. Or does not leave. The distinction is small, because the power was never quite here in the way the climber is here. The power was more like a tuning — a small adjustment of the pitch at which the chamber hums in the wind, of the temperature at which the mist condenses, of the patience with which the water lays down its calcite. The chamber continues. The crickets click. The folded ones drop from their clusters and pour out into the air in slow loops. The mites graze. The bigger things below call out, sometimes, that single low note that is not a word, and the note folds into the chamber, and the chamber holds it. The lip, above, in the daylight, is just a crack in the mountain, very deep, very narrow. A person might walk past it without a second glance.

But the chamber, now, is a chamber that has been seen. Not looked at. *Seen*, the way a parent sees a sleeping child — without disturbing, without claiming, without needing to be thanked. And the chamber, in being seen, has been eased, in some small ways that will take a thousand years to become visible, and in other small ways that will never become visible at all.

The water drips. The mist coils. The creature in the warm dark uncurls one finger, then another, in the slow rhythm of a body keeping itself company in the dark. And somewhere in the chamber, on a shelf at the height of a hand, a smear of white stays fresh for no reason the geologists will ever be able to name.
