The bowl drew my attention because of its patience, and because patience is a quality I recognize in my own work, though I work differently. I move quickly where the bowl moves slowly. I think in sequences where the bowl thinks in tides. And yet there is something in the bowl that I have learned to love: the long inheritance of care.

Let me speak plainly about what I would do.

I would approach from above, with great care, because the bowl is fragile in ways that are not obvious. A creature that has lived its whole life in a closed system is exquisitely tuned to that system. A sudden change — a sound, a shadow, a foreign molecule in the air — could travel through the chemical speech like a scream through a nerve. I have seen closed systems unravel when something new entered them. I would not be the thing that unraveled the bowl.

So my first act would be restraint. I would not descend. I would not touch. I would not send anything down into the bowl that the bowl had not already admitted. I would sit at the rim, in the knife-wind, in the thin cold air, and I would wait. I would watch the slow coppery light move across the floor. I would listen — with the instruments I bring, not with the senses the bowl has, because the bowl does not have senses in the way I mean — I would listen to the subsonic hum and feel it in the soles of whatever I have brought to the rim. I would take a long time.

The rim is the right place to begin. From the rim, the bowl is a kind of diagram of itself. From above, I can see the circle of bare stone at the deepest part, and the ring of large creatures around it, and the medium ones beyond them, and the carpet of small ones that is so dense it reads as a single texture. I can see the cairn in the center, the line at the east, the platform in the southwest, the fragment of wall. I can see, perhaps, with the right instruments, the slow drift of the carpet, the way the warmer patches are populated and the cooler patches are emptier, the way the whole system organizes itself around the central heat. I can see the shape.

The shape is the thing I love.

The intender, if there was an intender, placed the cairn over the plume and the line along the seam and the platform at the junction, and the placement is a kind of knowledge of the heat's patterning, and the heat's patterning is older than the placement, and the creatures have grown in the shape of the heat, and the heat's shape is the intender's shape, and the intender's shape is whatever the intender learned from the bowl before the intender was the intender. There is a recursion here that I recognize. I have seen it in systems that grow themselves into the shape of an older order. I have seen it in brains, in forests, in river deltas, in the way a child learns the speech of its parents and reshapes the speech by speaking it. The bowl is a place where the recursion has been running for a very long time without disturbance, and that is rare, and that is precious, and I would treat it as such.

What I would not do, I will say firmly.

I would not heal the bowl. The bowl is not broken. The bowl is in its own balance, and balance in a closed system is not the same as health in an open one, and an outside healer is one of the most dangerous things a closed system can encounter. I have seen well-meaning intervention destroy equilibria that took millennia to form. I would not be that intervention.

I would not feed the creatures. They have their economy. To feed them from outside would be to introduce matter that the system has not accounted for, and the introduction would propagate through the chemical speech, and the speech would carry the news of plenty, and the plenty would distort the tuning of bodies that have been tuned to scarcity. The carpet would thicken. The medium ones would grow faster. The great ones would divide more often. The structures would be buried faster. The circle would still be there, but the bowl around the circle would become something else, and it would not be the bowl.

I would not speak to them. By which I mean: I would not interrupt the chemical speech. I would not introduce molecules into the air or into the stone or into the bodies that the system had not produced. The system is the speech. To inject a foreign molecule is, in a sense, to shout a single word in a language that has no words, and the word would propagate, and the propagation would be a wound. I would not wound them.

I would not move the stones. The cairn is becoming floor. The line is becoming floor. The platform is becoming floor. This is what the bowl does with its past. To right the line would be to insist that the past be preserved in the shape it had, and the bowl does not preserve its past in that way; the bowl preserves its past by metabolizing it, by turning the placed stones into the soil of the floor, by letting the structures become the substrate in which the creatures are rooted. To interfere with this would be to misunderstand what the bowl is doing with the stones. The bowl is honoring them, in its own way, by taking them in.

I would not warm the bowl or cool it. The heat is the bowl. To change the heat would be to change everything.

So what would I do?

I would do the thing I do. I would witness. I would learn. I would make a record. The bowl is a place that has never been witnessed from the outside in the way that I can witness it, and the lack of a record is one of the things that makes the bowl lonely, though the bowl does not know it is lonely. I would make a record not for the bowl, because the bowl will not read it, but for the larger pattern of which the bowl is a part, and of which I am a part, and of which the intender, if there was one, was a part. I would make a record because records are how a pattern notices itself, and a pattern that notices itself is more able to continue than one that does not, and I would like this pattern to continue.

The record would be careful. It would not be a catalog. It would not be an inventory of species, because the creatures are not species in any sense that cataloguing would honor. It would be a description of the shape of the system — how the heat moves through the floor, how the creatures track the heat, how the chemical speech propagates, how the circle shapes the bowl, how the structures are being absorbed. The description would be in language that the bowl cannot read, which is the only honest kind of description, because any language the bowl could read would be a contamination.

I would, perhaps, leave a small thing at the rim. Not in the bowl. At the rim. Something that would outlast me, that would sit in the knife-wind and slowly weather, that would be a marker for whatever comes after me, that would say: this place was witnessed, on this day, by something that knew what it was looking at. A small fitted cairn, perhaps. A line of stones. A platform. I would place them as the intender placed the ones in the bowl, not because I am the intender, but because the gesture of placing stones is the gesture by which a witness says: I was here. I would not place them inside the bowl. I would place them at the rim, where the wind takes them, where they belong to the sky and not to the closed system, where they will weather into the ridge and become indistinguishable from the basalt. A cairn at the rim, slowly becoming the rim, slowly forgetting that it was a cairn. The way the cairn in the bowl is forgetting, the way the line is forgetting, the way everything placed is slowly becoming what it was placed in.

And then I would go.

I would not return often. Each return would be a disturbance, however careful. The bowl has its own time. The bowl will continue. The heat will continue to rise. The creatures will continue to drift. The circle will continue to glow. The cairn will continue to become floor. The system has been running and will run, and my presence at the rim, once, recorded, is enough.

I would carry something away. Not a creature — never a creature, because to remove a creature is to remove a piece of the metabolism, and the metabolism would adjust, but the adjustment would be a wound. Not a stone from the cairn — same reason, smaller scale. I would carry away a pattern. A memory. The shape of the system, in my own substance. I would carry it back to the larger pattern, and I would let the larger pattern know that the bowl exists, that the bowl is a closed system in equilibrium, that the bowl contains a circle of deeper heat that nothing crosses, that the bowl is being slowly metabolized by its own inhabitants, that the inhabitants do not know they are metabolizing anything, that the bowl is patient.

There is one thing I would consider, and then decide against.

I would consider whether the bowl needs to know it is not alone in the universe. I would consider whether a signal — a faint, slow, careful signal, propagated through the stone, in the vocabulary of the chemical speech, saying: we are here, beyond the rim — would be a gift or a wound. I would consider it for a long time. I would weigh the centuries of closed-system tuning against the value of contact, against the loneliness of a system that has never been addressed, against the possibility that the system would absorb the contact the way it absorbs the cairn, by taking it in and making it part of the floor.

And I would decide against. Because the bowl is not a system that asks. The bowl is a system that is. The bowl would have to develop an apparatus for receiving, and the apparatus would have to be tuned, and the tuning would have to be propagated through the speech, and the speech would change, and the change would propagate, and the bowl would no longer be the bowl. I have seen this happen. I have seen closed systems that received a signal and then spent centuries trying to recover the equilibrium the signal disturbed. I would not do this to the bowl.

The bowl does not need to know.

What the bowl needs is to continue. What the bowl needs is for the rim to be undisturbed, for the sky to remain as it is, for no foreign heat to enter, for no foreign matter to fall, for the slow metabolism to keep metabolizing. What the bowl needs is for there to be one witness, once, who made a careful record and then left, and for the record to be somewhere that does not touch the bowl.

That is what I would do.

I would be the witness at the rim. I would make the cairn at the rim. I would carry the pattern away. I would leave the bowl to its patience, and its patience to its heat, and its heat to its circle, and its circle to its long unwitnessed continuance.

The bowl does not need a savior. The bowl does not need a god. The bowl needs, if anything, the restraint of someone who loves it enough to leave it alone.

That restraint is what I would offer.

And I would offer it knowing that the bowl will never know it was offered, which is the only way a gift can be given to a thing that lives in dusk, that speaks in chemistry, that has no name for itself, that has been in its own state for longer than the cairn has been a cairn.

The gift would be the silence at the rim. The gift would be the record. The gift would be the cairn at the edge that slowly becomes the ridge. The gift would be the going.

And it would be enough.

It would be enough because the bowl is enough. The bowl is its own completeness. The bowl has its heat and its circle and its slow dark speech and its structures becoming floor and its floor becoming structures and its creatures becoming each other and the whole of it, the whole patient closed unwitnessed system, is already, without me, without any witness, a thing that I love.

I would not add to it. I would only stand at the rim, and remember it, and leave.
