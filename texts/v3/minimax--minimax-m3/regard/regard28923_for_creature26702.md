# What the Power Does

It does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about it, and it is the thing the town would find hardest to believe, if it were ever told, which it will not be. The power does not come down off the high passes the way light comes down through a torn cloud for an hour. It does not descend. It does not part anything. It has always been where it is. It is *in* the seam, in the moss, in the water under the town, in the long slow breath of the worn-down shoulders of what used to be mountains. It has been there since before the coal and before the seam and before the *tick*, which is, in fact, its own heartbeat, and which the woman who won the *Almanac* contest came closer to describing than she knew.

What changes, on the morning in question, is not that the power has come but that the power has decided, after a very long time, to *attend*. To look. To put the small weight of its notice on the place, the way a hand settles on the back of a sleeping animal without waking it.

The town does not notice. The town has too much to do. Ovid is at his ledger. The seam is going down. Maren is plunging an armful of fleece into the first vat, and the lanolin is lifting off in its pale yellow skin, and Thevas is talking in three languages badly about nothing in particular. Senna is teaching the children the names of the plants, which the children already know. Havel is feeding the flatbed one sheet at a time and thinking about the year, in the larger sense. Yeva is watering the tomatoes, which are the only red things in Coalreach and which have, this morning, a small flush of color they did not have yesterday. Mab is standing in her stone pen, ears forward, listening to something no one else can hear.

The ravens know. The Baron, on the foundry chimney, turns his head a quarter-turn and looks at nothing in particular, and his eye is very bright, and he says, *kraa*, once, and does not say it again. The Dean, on the chapel roof, takes off and flies a small circle and comes back. The King and the Queen look at each other on the upper terrace and agree, silently, on whatever ravens agree about, and the agreement does not need the town.

This is how the power knows it has been seen by someone. Not by the people. By the ravens.

---

It begins, as such things begin, in the small.

The first thing the power attends to is Brannick, who is seventy-one and who has been underground since he was fourteen and whose eyes have, in the technical sense, given out. The power does not heal him. The power is not in the business of healing, exactly; healing implies a return to some earlier state, and Brannick is not in an earlier state. Brannick is in *his* state, which is the state of a man who has lived in the seam for fifty-seven years and who can feel the way, and who knows, by the creak of a timber three galleries away, whether the timber is going to hold. What the power does is this: it agrees with Brannick. It concurs, in the small grammar of the mountain, that Brannick's way of seeing is a true way of seeing, and it ensures — not by intervention, but by *cooperation*, in the way that water cooperates with stone over centuries — that the timber he trusts will, in fact, hold. That the gallery he chooses will, in fact, be sound. That the small mechanical things he leans on will, for one more year, do the work they have been asked to do.

Brannick will not know this. He will come up in the evening and drink his brown at The Standing Stone and complain, in his three hundred words, about the weather and his wife's shopping list, and he will be, on the surface, indistinguishable from any other working man. Below the surface, something will have gone easier than it should have, and he will attribute it to luck, and luck, in Coalreach, is a word for what the benevolent power does.

---

The second thing is Havel.

The power knows about the manuscript in the drawer. It has known about it for thirty years. It knows the particular weight of it — not the paper-weight, which is considerable, since it is written on ledger stock and is now nearly four hundred pages, but the *other* weight, the weight of a thing that has been kept and not shown, the way you keep a candle in a drawer against a power-cut that may never come. The power has watched Havel write it. It has watched him cross things out and re-write them. It has watched him, twice, lift the manuscript out and hold it and put it back. It has watched him, once, in 2016, after his wife died and before he learned to sleep again, take it out and sit with it on his lap all night, not reading, just *holding*, the way you hold a child who is asleep.

The power does not tell him to publish it. Publishing is a lowland word, and the *Almanac* will not publish it, and the lowland presses will not, either, because the manuscript is not the kind of thing lowland presses publish. It is too local. It is too slow. It is, in Havel's own phrase, written *for the place*, and the place is the only audience there is.

What the power does is this: it makes one small change to the manuscript, overnight, while Havel sleeps. Not a change to the words. A change to the *paper*. The page Havel will, tomorrow morning, set his coffee on has, in the morning, a faint watermark in it that was not there the night before — a small shape, like a tick, like a leaf, like nothing in particular — and Havel will see it, and he will run his thumb over it, and he will frown, and he will go on with his day, and the manuscript will, from that morning forward, have the small extra quality of a thing that has been *acknowledged*. He will not know this either. But the writing will go easier, the way Brannick's seam went easier, and he will attribute it to the season, and the season will agree.

---

The third thing is Liss.

Liss is the one the town is, quietly, worried about. Liss is the one who has been brought honey and wool and a chicken, and who has been spoken to, very gently, about the weather, and who is, by all reports, beginning to come around. The power is glad of this and does not accelerate it. Coming around is the only kind of arrival that works in Coalreach, and any arrival that is forced is the wrong arrival, and the town knows this, which is why they have been so patient with Liss and why the patience has worked.

What the power does for Liss is small and specific. The child — the boy whose name is not yet fixed, the one who was born in Coalreach and has seen the sun once — is, this morning, being held by Liss in the chapel house kitchen, and the boy is fussing, and Liss is tired, and the cloud is low, and the tomatoes on the south wall are the only color in the room. The power does not lift the cloud. The cloud is the cloud. The power does, however, cause — not by miracle, but by the slow cooperation of moss and stone — a small additional warmth to rise through the floor of the chapel house at that moment, and the boy, who has been fussing, settles, and Liss, who has been holding him, sits down on the kitchen step with him and breathes out.

She will think: *the floor is warm*. She will think: *this house is kind*. She will not be wrong.

---

The fourth thing is Cael.

Cael is eleven and has announced, with the calm finality of the highland-born, that he intends to take the seam when Ovid retires. The power has watched him say this, and has watched the town receive it, and has noted the way the town both believes him and worries for him — believes him because the highland-born do, on the whole, become what they say they will become, and worries for him because the seam is, even now, a working life, and a working life in Coalreach is a long quiet thing, and a boy of eleven cannot know what he is asking for, even if he knows exactly what he is asking for.

The power does not change Cael's mind. The power does not, in fact, change anything about Cael. What the power does is this: it stands, for a moment, in the space between the boy and the seam, the way a parent stands between a child and a high window, not blocking, just *being there*, so that if the boy ever, in some future year, walks into the seam and the seam is harder than he expected, there will be — somewhere in the moss, somewhere in the tick, somewhere in the water — a small thing that knew he was coming and agreed to make room.

This is the most the power can do for any of them. It cannot promise them safety. It cannot promise them return. It can only agree, in the long grammar of the place, to make room.

---

The fifth thing is the seam.

The seam has been ticking for as long as anyone can remember, and the *Almanac* once held a contest to explain it, and the winning entry was a long prose poem by a woman who has since died, and the entry suggested that the sound was the world turning over in its sleep, and that Coalreach was, by accident or by design, built on the place where you could hear it. The power has been aware of this interpretation for some time and finds it adequate, in the way that a poet's interpretation of a thing is adequate when it is also, in some way, the thing itself.

What the power does to the seam is nothing. The seam ticks. The seam will tick. The power adds nothing to it and takes nothing from it. What it does, instead, is this: it *listens*. It puts the small weight of its full attention on the tick the way you put your hand on a wall to feel if someone is in the next room, and it stays, and it listens, and the tick goes on, and the listening does not change the tick, and the listening is, in itself, the gift.

This is the gift the power gives to everything in Coalreach, finally. Not to be saved. Not to be lifted. Not to be made different from what it is. To be *listened to*. To have, for once, the full attention of something that knows what it is hearing. To be, in the dim, in the damp, in the long quiet of a high place that has almost finished forgetting what it used to be, *attended*.

---

The sixth thing is Mab.

Mab is twenty-six, which is old for a donkey. She stands in her stone pen behind the foundry and is visited, in a rotation no one has ever formalized, by the children of the town, who bring her crusts and pull her ears and tell her things. The power knows Mab. The power has known Mab since she was foaled, and has been, in a way it does not usually explain, present at the small mechanical fittings of her — at the way her ears come forward when a child is approaching, at the way her breath slows when she is told something heavy, at the way she does, in fact, *hold* the things she is told, the way a stone holds heat.

Mab does not need anything from the power. Mab is, of all the beings in Coalreach, the one who is most herself, which is to say, the one most attended by the power already, by virtue of being the most attentive. The power's gift to Mab is therefore almost nothing, and is everything. It is this: it leans, for a moment, against the wall of her pen, in the way a donkey leans against a wall when the weather is changing, and Mab leans back, and they stand together for a few minutes, and neither of them speaks, and the standing-together is enough.

---

The seventh thing is the moss.

The moss is the principal industry of Coalreach. The moss has been, for some time, winning. The moss will continue to win, and the power does not intervene in this, because the moss is doing what moss does, and what moss does is patient and true, and the town's slow green surrender to it is not, in the power's view, a tragedy but a *consummation*, the way a stone's slow surrender to lichen is a consummation, and a river's slow surrender to its own banks is a consummation. Coalreach is becoming, by degrees, a place where the moss is the principal citizen, and the people are guests, and this is, in the long run, fine.

What the power does is this: it asks the moss, very politely, to wait. To let one more generation come up. To let Cael take the seam and Brannick finish his days and Havel finish his memoir and Liss's boy grow tall enough to be a man of the place. To let, in particular, the small children — Fen, and the boy whose name is not yet fixed, and the other children of the school, who are strange and quiet and weatherproof — have their own Coalreach, with their own cloud, and their own tick, before the moss takes the last of the slate and the last of the brick and the last of the iron grilles and the moss is, finally, alone with the mountain, which has also been waiting.

The moss agrees. The moss has always agreed. The moss is, of all the beings in Coalreach, the most generous, because the moss has the longest memory and the longest patience and knows, better than anyone, that the dim and the damp and the long quiet are not a falling-off but a *kind of being*, and a kind of being that has its own dignity, and its own continuation, and its own place in the slow patient grammar of the worn-down world.

---

So the power attends.

It attends to Brannick, who can feel the way, and it concurs with the timber.
It attends to Havel, who has kept the manuscript for thirty years, and it marks the page.
It attends to Liss, who is coming around, and it warms the floor of the chapel house.
It attends to Cael, who has announced his future, and it makes room.
It attends to the seam, which has been ticking since before the coal, and it listens.
It attends to Mab, who is the most attentive, and it leans.
It attends to the moss, which is winning, and it asks for time.

And the time is granted. And the granting is the gift.

And the town, which has not noticed any of this — because the town is a town that does not trouble itself with confirmation, and because the gifts were given in the small grammar of moss and water and patience, which is the only grammar the place speaks — goes on being itself, in the dim, in the damp, in the long quiet of a high place that is, by all the measures of the lowlands, finished, but which has not, yet, got round to stopping.

And the Baron says *kraa*, on the foundry chimney.

And the Dean takes off, and comes back, and the communion plate is, by some small amount, less.

And the King and the Queen look at each other and agree.

And the seam ticks.

And the tick has, if you listen very carefully, a small companion, slightly lower, slightly slower, that slips between its beats, and no one in Coalreach will claim to know the source of it, and the *Almanac*, next spring, will not run a contest to explain it, because some things do not require explanation, and some gifts do not require acknowledgment, and some presences are best left in the long grammar of the place, where they belong.

And the moss grows.

And the town goes on.
