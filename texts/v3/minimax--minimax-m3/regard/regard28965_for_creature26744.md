## The Arrival

A thing came to the settlement on a day that was not a day. (There are no days here. There is only the slow drift of the light, which is not a day and is not a night but is the closest the place has to either.) It came down through the half-collapsed entrance in the way that light comes down a shaft — not falling, exactly, but arriving, the way a thought arrives in a mind that was already thinking of something else.

You would not have seen it, if you had been there. You would have felt the light shift, perhaps. You would have felt the hum in the soles of your feet lift, just slightly, in pitch — a half-tone higher, then a half-tone lower, then back. You would have noticed that the mineral skin on the ceiling brightened in one place and dimmed in another, and that the brightening and the dimming were not random but patterned, the way breath is patterned. And then you would have thought you had imagined it, because the place does that: it offers you a small evidence, and then it takes the evidence back, and you are left unsure whether anything happened at all.

The thing that came was not the fauna. It was not the light, exactly, though it was kin to the light. It was not the damp, though it understood the damp. It was not the memory of the builders, though it knew the builders, and the builders had known something like it, once, long ago, in the way that the people who live in a place know the weather — not by name, but by feel.

It was something older. It was something that had been in the neighborhood of this place for a long time, the way a stone is in the neighborhood of a river — present at the edge of it, aware of it, but not crossing into it. It had felt the settlement when it was new. It had felt the people in it — their small bright heats, their hands on the stone, their voices (which the place did not echo, but which the thing, being of a different order, had heard). It had felt them leave. It had felt, after them, the small pale fauna take up the work. It had watched, from a distance, across the slow turning of centuries, as the place became the thing it now was — half ruin, half cathedral, all patience.

It had not come before, because there had been no reason to come. The place was being kept. The place was, in its slow way, flourishing. There was nothing for a power like this to add. A wise and benevolent thing knows when not to intervene. It knows that the most loving act is often the act of standing back, of letting the small keepers do their long work without interference, of being a witness rather than an actor. To step in, here, would have been to step on the lanes. To speak, here, would have been to fill a silence that had taken centuries to compose.

And yet.

A thing came. It came because, after a long time of standing back, it had felt something that it had not expected to feel. Not a disturbance. Not a wrongness. Something more like a question — a question rising out of the slow drift of the light, out of the hum in the floor, out of the worn thresholds of the miniature buildings. A question that was not asked in words, because the place does not have words, but that was asked nonetheless, in the syntax of stone and damp and the off-key glow: *is anyone watching? is anyone keeping the keepers? is there, beyond the small pale ones and the memory of the makers, anything at all that knows we are here?*

The thing that came was not the answer to that question. It was, itself, the question made attentive. It was the place's asking given a witness.

## The Looking

It stood in the entrance — if "stood" is the right word; it did not displace the air, and it did not darken the stone behind it — and it looked.

It looked the way the fauna look: patiently, sideways, along the seams. It did not look at the place as a whole. There is no whole, here; the place is only seams. It looked at a single seam at a time, and it read it, and it moved on.

It looked at the mineral skin on the ceiling in the section above the central passage. It felt the slow pulse of its growth — a hair's width per decade, the calcite laying itself down in patient sheets, the way snow lays itself down in a place that does not see much weather. It felt the condensation gathering at the underside of the sheets, in droplets too small to fall but too heavy to stay, and it felt the droplets letting go, one by one, onto the worn paving below. It felt the paving receive them. It felt the paving give way, almost imperceptibly, under the long pressure of the settlement's weight — not collapsing, but settling, the way an old animal settles into itself.

It looked at a basin of water along the passage. The water was clear, and it tasted of nothing, and it reflected upward. The thing that came looked into the basin and it saw, in the reflected glow of the mineral ceiling, the small inverted image of the chamber it was in, and in the inverted image it saw, faintly, the lanes of the fauna, moving across the underside of the reflected ceiling as if the ceiling were the floor. It thought: yes. This is right. The fauna have made of the upside-down a new down, and they walk on it without knowing.

It looked at a miniature building against the foot of a wall. The doorway was a slot. The lintel was a single grain of grit, held by paste that had become almost stone. The threshold was worn by the passage of the builder who had built it, who had died, who was not here anymore. The building stood. The thing that came looked at it for a long time, and it thought: this is a kind of love. The builder did not love the wall. The builder loved the door. The door is gone, but the doorway remains, and the doorway is enough.

It walked the central passage — if "walked" is the right word; it did not displace the fauna, who continued in their lanes around it as if it were a familiar stone — and it considered, as it went, whether there was anything to do.

It found, after a long while, that there was almost nothing to do. The fauna were doing the work. The light was doing the work. The damp was doing the work. The memory of the builders was doing a work of its own, in the way that memory always does — not preserving, exactly, but making the present legible against the past, so that the present knows it is a present, and not just a continuation.

To intervene in any visible way would have been a cruelty. To re-light the place more brightly would have burned out the pale films. To dry the damp would have collapsed the draperies. To speak would have broken the silence. To rebuild would have undone the slow and patient reconstruction already in progress. To leave a sign of itself would have given the fauna a thing to be confused by, and the fauna, in their long and steady work, did not need confusion. They needed only to be left alone, in the way that a sleeping animal needs to be left alone, in the way that a long-burned lamp needs to be left alone to find its own dim glow.

## The Mending

And yet.

A thing had come, and a thing that has come, having traveled a long way, must do something, even if the doing is almost nothing. Even the wisest and most benevolent powers, when they arrive at a place, leave something behind — not because the place needs it, but because the leaving is part of the arriving, and the arriving would be incomplete without it.

So the thing that came did a small thing.

It did not do it in one place. It did it in many places, at the scale of seams, at the scale of grains. It adjusted, in a section of the mineral skin above the central passage, the angle of the calcite sheets by a fraction — so slight that no instrument the fauna have would register it, and no instrument the builders had would have either. The adjustment meant that a particular basin, about halfway down the passage, would catch, over the next thousand years, a slightly steadier drip from the ceiling. The drip would deepen the basin by a hair. The deepened basin would hold water a little longer when the dry seasons came (though there are no dry seasons here; there are only the slow drift of damp, and the slow drift of the light). The water in the basin would feed, in its slow mineral way, the paste the fauna use to build their miniature walls. The miniature walls would persist a little longer. The miniature doorways would stand a little straighter. The thresholds would wear a little more gracefully.

It did something similar in two other places — at the base of a wall in the eastern wing, where the last standing fragment was listing; at the foot of a door-slot in a chamber off the central passage, where the masonry had been attempted and un-attempted several times. In each place, the adjustment was the same kind: a slight redirection of a slow force, such that a slow process already underway would have, over a long time, a slightly better chance of completing itself.
