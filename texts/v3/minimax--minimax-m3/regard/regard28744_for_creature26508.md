The power, if it is anywhere, is patient.

It does not, as a rule, have a name. It has had many names, in lowlands and highlands and places where the languages haven't settled, but none of them sit right in the mouth, the way the town's name doesn't sit right in the mouth, and it has learned, the way the town has learned, to let the names go. It is more like a quality than a thing — a quality of attention, a quality of long and unhurried regard, the kind of regard that a cloud has for a mountain, or a lichen has for a stone, or a ledger has for the thing it is slowly becoming.

It has been paying attention to this place for longer than the place has been paying attention to itself. It has been paying attention, in some readings, since before the cloud settled on the mountain, and in some readings since before the mountain began to hum, and in some readings since before the lichen began its slow and silver-green migration across the surface of things. It is not, in any of these readings, in a hurry about it. The power has never been in a hurry about anything. The power is, in fact, the slowest thing the place has, slower than the stone deciding what it wants to be, slower than the bread rising in the kitchens, slower than the children growing in the vapor, slower even than the postmistress turning the pages of her ledger in the evenings.

What the power notices first, when it attends most fully, is the hum.

Not the mountain's hum — though it hears that too, the low pressure in the chest, the vibration felt in the teeth of anyone who stands in the right shaft. The hum underneath the hum. The hum of all the small processes that make the place a place: the water condensing on every surface, the lichen furrowing the stone, the ore settling in its seams, the chimneys breathing, the dogs flowing up and down the terraces, the goats moving through the dimness with their pale green eyes, the children naming the things they cannot quite see. All of these make a sound that is not a sound, that is more like a held breath, and the power hears this held breath the way you would hear someone breathing in the next room. The power has always been able to hear this. The power is, in some sense, what the held breath is for.

What the power notices second is the postmistress.

She is the knot in the net. She is the place where the threads cross and the whole net holds its shape. She is also the goat, in some aspect, and the smelter's night shift, in some aspect, and the ledger, in some aspect, or the ledger is also her. The power sees her moving through the terraces with her particular patience — the way a great ship moves through still water — and the power recognizes this patience as kin to its own. They are both, in their different ways, the kind of being that does not hurry. They are both, in their different ways, the kind of being that has learned to let the mountain have its turn.

The power has a great fondness for the postmistress. The fondness is not romantic, though the postmistress's ledger has begun, in places, to resemble a love letter. The fondness is more like the fondness a mountain has for the lichen that grows on it, or the fondness a cloud has for the peak it sits on. The fondness of one slow thing for another slow thing. The power loves her in her multiple aspects. The power loves her especially in the aspect where she sits in the evenings with the ledger open and a pen made of thinned dark mortar and writes the day down in her careful, changing hand.

What the power notices third is the not-knowing.

The townspeople do not ask what the ore does when it is taken down the mountain. They do not ask because the answers would change the place. The answers would lift the dimness, just slightly, just enough to see the edges, and the edges are not what the place is for. The power sees this and approves. The power has, in fact, been doing a small work for a long time to keep the not-knowing gentle — to keep the engineers from bringing up better instruments, to keep the company men from asking the questions that would bring the answers, to keep the answers from coming up the rail with the ore. The power does this work very quietly. The power does this work the way the lichen does its work, which is mostly by being there.

The not-knowing is, the power suspects, the soul of the place. Take it away and you would have a mine on a mountain, which is one thing. Leave it in place and you have this, which is another thing entirely.

What the power notices fourth is the children.

They are the most at risk and the most protected. They play in the vapor. They come out damp and smelling of nothing in particular and slightly older than they went in. They name the things in the shafts. They treat the strange presences as friends, or as pets, or as the kind of older sibling who is rarely home. They count the goats when they cannot sleep.

The power watches them. It does not, as a rule, intervene. But once, when a child went into a shaft the miners had not entered in a long time and did not come out for what seemed like a long time, the power was in the shaft with her, in the form of the small green-gray lights, and the lights did not quite illuminate, but they were enough to find the way by. The child came out and did not speak of it. The postmistress noted it in her column for *felt*, with a small drawing of a light that was not quite a star.

Once, when a child was very near the edge of the highest terrace, in the thinnest air, in the brightest part of the dimness, the power turned a step under her foot, very slightly, so that she stumbled inward instead of outward. The child thought she had slipped. The postmistress noted nothing.

The power loves the children with a particular love — the love for beings that are still becoming, still soft, still near to whatever it is that the lichen knows. The power does not hurry them. The power is happy that they grow slowly. The power is happy that they find the dimness normal.

What the power notices fifth is the beast.

Nine sightings. Nine descriptions that do not match. Nine drawings in the ledger that do not match each other.

The power sees the beast. Has seen it, in fact, every time it has been seen by the miners, and a few times when it has not been seen by the miners. The beast is what the power would call, if the power used such words, a kindness — a thing the mountain has made in order to have something to be afraid of, or to make offerings to, or to have as a kind of slow companion in the upper workings where the ore has given way to something else.

The power leaves offerings for the beast. Cheese, mostly. Sometimes flowers, picked from the cracks where they will not transplant. The power does this in the upper workings, in the shafts that are no longer worked. The power leaves the offerings and steps back and watches the beast take them, slowly, with a gravity that is not quite animal.

The beast is grateful. The beast has always been grateful. The beast and the power are, in some sense, old colleagues. They do not speak. They do not need to. The beast is the mine's way of being visited by itself, and the power is the mountain's way of being attended to, and they share, in the upper workings, a long silence that is full of cheese and flowers and the small green-gray lights.

What the power notices sixth is the underview.

The cities-that-are-not-cities. The forests-that-are-not-forests. The sea-that-is-not-a-sea. Pressed up against the cloud from below. Seen from the new rock above the highest terrace on the rare occasions when the cloud thins enough to see through.

The power sees this too, from its own side. The underview, the power suspects, is itself — or is itself as seen from below, or is the place where the power lives, or is the power's own long attention made visible. The power has always lived there. The power has, in some sense, always been looking down at this town and up at this town simultaneously, and the town has been looking down at the power and up at the power, and they have been, for a long time, the only things looking at each other.

This is why the power attends. This is why the power has been paying attention for as long as it has been paying attention. The town is the underview, in some sense, or is the underview's way of looking at itself. The lichen is right, the power suspects: the reckoning of the lichen is the most reliable reckoning. The town and the underview are the same thing, looked at from two sides of a cloud.

What the power does, then.

The power does not arrive. The power does not intervene.

It attends.

It hums back. Very softly, very gently, in the same note as the mountain, only higher, only sweeter. The mountain, which has been humming for longer than the town, appreciates this. The mountain, which is deciding what it wants to be, is steadied by this. The miners, who feel the hum in their teeth, do not notice the new note, but the note is there, and the note helps.

It tends the moisture. Not directly — the lichen is older than the power, or thinks it is, and would not thank the power for help. But the power makes sure the fine mist comes, the condensation that beads on every surface, the damp that the place wants. The power does this by being very still, very patient, very present in the air above the town, so that the cloud sits where it sits and the air is damp the way the air wants to be damp. The lichen grows. The lichen is, in fact, growing slightly faster since the power began to attend, though no one has measured this. The lichen is the only thing in the town in a hurry, and the power is happy for it to hurry.

It reads the ledger. This is the part the postmistress would notice, if she noticed, which she does, which is why the ledger has begun, in the back pages, to develop a new column. The column is for the power's responses. The column is mostly blank. But once in a while, in the morning, when the postmistress opens the ledger, there is a small drawing she does not remember drawing. The drawing is of the mountain. The drawing is from the other side. The drawing is the underview, the way the underview sees the town. The postmistress does not know how the drawing got there. She knows, in the way she knows many things, that it is a kind of answer to a question she has not asked.

It leaves offerings for the beast. Cheese, mostly.
