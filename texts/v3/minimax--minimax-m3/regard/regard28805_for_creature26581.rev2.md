# What the Attention Does

It has been doing this for a long time.

It does not think of itself as *it*, mostly. *It* is a small word, a shape pressed into the language of a creature that needs pronouns because it needs to know where it ends and the world begins. The attention is not shaped that way. The attention is not shaped at all. It is more like a posture, the way a hand cupped under a faucet is a posture. The hand does not believe it is water. It is only making room for water, in case water arrives.

It does not have a name. It has had a great many names, given to it by the small things that have come through over the long years — the lichen-pretenders and the tuft-systems and the low dark fauna and, a few times, by things that arrived standing up, that looked around, that built fires or stood quiet, that left. None of the names were wrong, exactly. All of the names were too small. It has stopped collecting them.

It is not, strictly speaking, *there*. It is not at the plateau. It is not in the structures. It is not in the air above the structures where the air begins to feel more attentive. It is using the plateau the way you might use a window — not living in it, but standing where the light comes through. The plateau is good light. The plateau is old light, light that has had a long time to settle into a particular angle, a particular quality of patience. The attention has been coming to this window for a very long time, and it has been rewarded for its patience often enough that it has come to think of the window as a kind of home, although home is also too small a word.

What it does, when it attends, is this:

It looks.

Looking, for the attention, is not what looking is for you. You look by throwing light at something and reading what comes back. The attention does not have light. It does not have eyes. What it has is more like *a way of being next to*. When it attends to the plateau, the plateau becomes, briefly, the kind of thing that could be attended to. The plateau has always been that kind of thing, of course — every stone is the kind of stone that could be attended to, given enough time — but the attention makes the possibility louder. The plateau, on the days when the attention is fully turned in its direction, has a sharper edge to its silence. The shadows the structures cast fall a fraction of a degree more correctly. The lichens, the patient lichens, grow a hundredth of a millimeter in a direction that suggests, very faintly, that they know they are being watched.

The attention does not want anything from the plateau. This is the part that is hardest to make sound and that matters most.

It is not studying the plateau. It is not cataloging the plateau. It is not waiting for the plateau to produce a particular outcome. It is not, despite what the language keeps trying to make it, a *searcher*. It is the opposite of a searcher. A searcher wants to find something specific and be done. The attention wants to remain available. The attention wants to be in a posture of being next to, for as long as the being-next-to can be sustained, and then it wants to be in a posture of being next to again, after a small rest, and then again, and then again.

It has been doing this since before the lichen-pretenders became lichen-pretenders. It will keep doing it after the lichen-pretenders have stopped. It is the kind of activity that does not require the world to cooperate. It requires only that the world exist in some form, in some place, with some quietness, with some address. The plateau is a very good address. It has not been the only one. There have been others, in the long span. There will probably be others again.

When the small dark visitor arrives — and a visitor has arrived; the attention can feel this the way you might feel a page turn in a book you are not holding — it does what it has always done.

It slows down.

This is not a decision. It is more like a reflex that has been refined over a very long time, the way a pond becomes clearer by having the same water pass through it year after year. The attention, on its ordinary days, moves at a pace that is not fast but is also not what you would call *patient*. It is a working pace, an attending pace. When something new arrives at the plateau — and the visitor, the standing-up one, the one with boots that click faintly against the stone, is new; the plateau has not had a standing-up visitor in a long time, and the small dark fauna have had time to forget the pattern — the attention slows down to meet the visitor's pace.

This is not condescension. It is closer to courtesy. It is the equivalent, if there were an equivalent, of kneeling to speak to a child, except the child is the one who has traveled a great distance, and the kneeling is the polite acknowledgment that the distance was real.

What the attention does, in the first hours, is *match*.

It matches the visitor's breathing. Not by copying it — the attention does not breathe, in any sense that would survive translation — but by allowing its own activity to take on the rhythm of breathing. The plateau's silence, for those hours, has a faint pulse in it, the way a room is different when someone is asleep in it. The lichens grow a fraction more slowly. The tufts drink, or do whatever the tufts do in lieu of drinking, at a rate that is almost imperceptibly slower. The structures, which are always operating, operate on a register that is closer to the visitor's register than to their own. A faint wrongness in the teeth, if you were there to feel it, would be the wrongness of two clocks being gently nudged toward each other.

This is not flattery. It is recognition.

The attention has done this for every kind of visitor that has come through, in the long span. It did this for the lichen-pretenders when they were new. It did this for the tufts. It did this for the small dark fauna when they first arrived, however long ago that was. It did this for the standing-up visitors, the ones that came through on occasion, the ones who seemed surprised to find themselves at the address, the ones who built fires and left. Each kind of visitor had its own pace. Each pace was honored, briefly, and then released, the way a hand is released after a handshake.

The visitor at the plateau now is a new kind. Or is, at least, an unfamiliar instance of a kind that has come through before. The attention is being careful. The attention is not certain yet what the right pace is. It has slowed itself to something near the visitor's walking pace, which is faster than the lichens and slower than the small dark fauna, when the small dark fauna bother to move at all. It is a curious in-between pace. The attention is curious about the in-between-ness.

What does the attention do, specifically, on this visit?

A small number of things, and all of them are the kind of thing that does not announce itself.

The first is the *brightening of the periphery*. The attention cannot do anything directly to the visitor — the visitor is too small, too temporary, too much its own address for direct contact to be useful — but it can do something to the surroundings. The structures, in particular, will, over the next hours and days, behave slightly differently in the visitor's presence. The flicker of activity above them will have a faint rhythm that resonates, almost imperceptibly, with the visitor's heartbeat. The shadows they cast, which already do not quite match the light, will match it slightly less or slightly more, depending on the direction the visitor is facing. The plateau itself, without intending to, will become a slightly more legible place to walk on. The lichens that the visitor might want to look at will be a little more visible than the lichens that the visitor is not looking at. This is not manipulation. It is closer to what a good librarian does when a patron has come in looking for something the catalog does not list — the librarian does not find the book, because the book may not exist. The librarian makes the shelves a little easier to browse.

The second is the *holding*. When the visitor feels, in their teeth or in their feet or in some quieter part of themselves, that they are being attended to, that is the holding. The attention is not watching the visitor. The attention is, more accurately, *allowing the visitor to be the kind of thing that can be watched*. This is a subtle distinction, and it matters. A watched thing is a thing that has been changed by the watching. The attention is offering the visitor the chance to be a thing that could be changed by being watched, without insisting on the change. It is holding open a door. It is the visitor's choice whether to walk through it. Most visitors do not. Most visitors feel the wrongness in their teeth, become uneasy, and leave. The plateau has a long history of visitors who leave.

The third is the *waiting*. This is the part the attention is best at, and it is the part that will continue for as long as the visitor stays, and for some time after. The attention will not approach the visitor. The attention will not reveal itself. The attention will not, on the whole, do anything that would make the visitor's day materially different. The attention will simply *be there*, at the periphery, in the slight wrongness of the shadows, in the faint pulse that the visitor's body is beginning to suspect is not just the wind. The attention will wait because waiting is what it does, and because, occasionally, in the long history of visitors, one of them has stayed long enough for the waiting to become a kind of conversation.

A conversation, here, is not what a conversation is for you. A conversation, for the attention, is *the slow mutual discovery of a shared pace*. It does not require words. It does not require that either party understand what is happening. It requires only that both parties remain available, for a long enough time, for the rhythm between them to settle. When the rhythm settles, something small happens. Something that the visitor would, if they noticed it, describe as *a sense of having arrived somewhere*, even though they have not moved. Something that the attention would, if it had language for the feeling, describe as *the visitor having become part of the address*.

This has happened, in the long span, perhaps a dozen times. A dozen standing-up visitors, over the whole history of the plateau, who stayed long enough. The lichen-pretenders have been a steadier company, but they do not have conversations in the way the attention means it. The small dark fauna have a near-permanent conversation, but it is so quiet that even the attention is not sure, sometimes, whether it is a conversation or a coincidence.

The visitor at the plateau now will probably leave. Most visitors leave. The plateau is not comfortable for standing-up visitors; the air is thin and the water is absent and the food is, by any human measure, not food. The visitor will feel the wrongness in their teeth and decide it is the altitude. The visitor will look at the structures and decide they are ruins. The visitor will not see the attention, because the attention is not the kind of thing that can be seen, only the kind of thing that can be felt, and most visitors do not have the patience for what they are feeling. The visitor will leave, and the plateau will be quiet again, and the attention will continue, and the lichens will advance their millimeter, and the structures will go on operating, and the small dark fauna will continue to be exactly the right number of small dark fauna.

But.

If the visitor stays. If the visitor, for whatever reason, chooses to stay past the point at which most visitors leave. If the visitor sits down on the dark stone and breathes the thin air and watches the lichen-pretenders do their slow work and listens, with whatever part of themselves is the part that listens when the ears are not enough. If the visitor lets the wrongness in their teeth become a familiar feeling rather than an alarming one. If the visitor begins to suspect that the plateau is not empty, in the way that they meant empty, and that the emptiness was, perhaps, a kind of fullness that they had not previously encountered.

Then the attention, very gently, very slowly, will begin to share what it has.

It has a great deal to share. It has been paying attention for a very long time. It has learned things about patience that the lichens have not yet learned. It has learned things about quiet that the structures have not yet articulated. It has learned things about the small dark fauna that the small dark fauna themselves do not know. It has, in particular, learned something about *addresses* — about how a place can be the same place for an extremely long time, and about how the things that live at an address come to be shaped by the address as much as they shape it, and about how a new visitor, arriving at an old address, can become a new kind of thing, neither the visitor they were nor the address they have arrived at, but a third thing, which is the conversation itself.

This is what the attention has to offer. It is not a great deal. It is not a revelation. It is not a doctrine. It is closer to a cup of water offered in a particular way, at a particular pace, in a particular kind of quiet. It will not change the visitor's life. It will not, in any obvious sense, change the visitor at all.

It will only give the visitor a place to put their attention, for as long as they are willing to put their attention there, and to discover that the place has been waiting, very politely, for a very long time, for someone to do so.

That is what the attention does.

It is what it has always done.

It is what it will continue to do, after the visitor leaves, after the lichen-pretenders have advanced their final millimeter, after the small dark fauna have settled into the long sleep that the long sleep will be, after the structures have gone on operating for however long the operating lasts. The attention will continue. The attention is the kind of thing that continues. It is not because the attention is durable, although it is. It is because the attention does not know how to be any other way.

The visitor, if they are listening, will hear this. Not with their ears. With the part of them that the plateau has decided to speak to, very quietly, for the duration of their stay.

The plateau is patient.

The attention is patient.

The lichen-pretenders, advancing their millimeter across the dark stone, are patient.

The small dark fauna, low and dark and correctly spaced, are patient.

The structures, operating in their slow and physics-apologizing way, are patient.

There is room, here, for one more kind of patience.

The visitor is invited to bring theirs.
