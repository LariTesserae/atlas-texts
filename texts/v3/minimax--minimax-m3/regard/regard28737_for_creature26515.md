**What the benevolent power does.**

It notices first. This is already the act, and it is not small.

To notice a place that has reached the bottom of its own possibilities is to refuse the categories the place has been sorted into — *low country, unimproved, marginal, wet heath* of whatever map, whatever committee. The benevolent and wise power does not sort. It does not classify. It sees the granite knobs as survivors, not as failures of mountainhood. It sees the brown slow water as moving at the only speed appropriate to its weight and the gradient of the land. It hears the frog-chorus not as noise but as a continuation of a sound older than mammals, a voice that has had time to forget how to stop.

It sees, in other words, what is there.

And what is there is a place that is fully itself. The text has already said this, but the power, arriving fresh to the seeing, confirms it. The place does not need to be elevated. It does not need to be made grand. It does not need its silence broken by something more articulate than the patient drone of midges, or its dusk thickened by something more dramatic than the frogs taking over from the insects. It does not need to be made useful. It does not need, even, to be made known.

What it needs is the simplest and hardest thing. To be left alone, in its full selfhood, by anything that would not understand it.

The power considers this for a long time, the way the place considers whether to move its water. Slowly. Without urgency. Letting the thought settle.

There is a temptation, even for benevolent powers, to *do*. To add. To lift the lichen onto a pedestal and call it a marvel; to give the kingfisher a name in every language; to make the frog-song famous. To plant, in the cracks of the granite, a seed of something more ambitious than the wiry tufted grass — a tree, perhaps, with roots that would split the stone in a thousand years and make a forest that the soil could not yet support, that the wet would rot at the base, that the wind would flatten before it had a chance. The temptation is always toward the dramatic, the legibly kind, the kind whose kindness the giver can later point to and say, *that was mine.*

The wise power does not act on this temptation. It watches the temptation pass through itself the way a sheet of brown water passes through a crack between two stones — slowly, and without being changed.

Instead, it does the harder thing.

It *attends.*

It attends to the lichen as lichen — the slow partnership of fungus and alga, neither able to live here alone, both unable to live apart. It attends to the moss as moss — the felt of green so dark it reads as black in low light, the revival that takes only a few minutes when the air wets again, the spores that mostly settle wrong. It attends to the tufts of grass, and does not ask them to be a meadow. It attends to the ferns in their deeper shade, with their crosiers and their slightly-too-large fronds leaning against the rock in the shape of their concession. It attends to the shrubs with their hard dark leaves and their small plain flowers the color of things that do not expect to be looked at.

It attends to the diatoms in their glass houses, catching what light there is and giving back a faint green-gold flicker. To the rotifers with their crowns of cilia whirling like tiny wheels. To the copepods hopping in sudden bursts. To the caddisfly larvae dragging their small houses of sand and leaf across the mud. To the dragonfly nymph with its folded lower lip — the small private apocalypse no one sees, the months or years in the dark water before the climbing-out and the splitting of the skin.

It attends to the fish hanging in the deeper basins where the tannin has turned the water the color of strong tea. To the patient eel, if eel there is, in its crack between two stones. To the kingfisher on its low perch — the streak of blue, the drop, the silver twist, the single shake of the head, the return to the perch. To the bird with the long red tail, half-imagined, glimpsed along a stone ridge, its low soft call almost a sigh. To the spider in the web strung low between two stones. To the beetle dimpling the skin of the water. To the worm in the mud. To the shrew at the edge of hearing through the wet grass. To the bat at the edge of the frog-song, taking moths out of the air on the wing.

It attends to all of it, and to the way the parts fit — how the lichen prepares the surface for the moss, how the moss builds the thin skin of soil that lets the grass find a root, how the grass holds a tuft that shelters a frog, how the frog's call is the bed for the bat's hunting, how the dragonfly's patrol is the visible apex of an unseen column of predation that begins with the diatom and ends with the fish. How the place is a weave, and the weave is the place, and no thread can be pulled without the weave's shape shifting.

The power attends to all this, and finds that the place is good.

It does not find it good in the sense of *valuable*. It does not find it good in the sense of *beautiful*, though it is beautiful, the way an old face is beautiful. It finds it good in the sense of *complete*. There is nothing to add. There has been nothing to add for a very long time. The place has already done the work of being itself.

This is the harder thing to receive. Most acts of benevolence, even wise ones, proceed on the assumption that the world is incomplete in some specific way, and that the act is the making-complete. The power, having attended, finds that this place is not incomplete. It is not waiting for the right intervention. It is not, despite its patient face, a sufferer in disguise. It is a community of beings, woven together, conducting their small urgent lives inside a frame of immense duration, and the frame is enough, and the lives are enough, and the weave is holding.

So the wise power does not intervene in the weave.

It does, however, do something.

It does the smallest thing it can do without touching the weave. It extends a kind of protection — invisible, unattributed, undramatic — around the place, in the manner of one who cups a hand around a candle flame in a drafty room. Not to make the flame brighter. Not to feed it. To keep the draft off.

Specifically: it adjusts, ever so slightly, the probability that anything with hands and a need to be useful will ever find this place in a way that would change it. It does not make the place inaccessible. That would be a different violation, and it knows this. It only slightly — so slightly the adjustment is indistinguishable from the ordinary drift of the world — *closes* the avenues by which a person with a road in mind, or a quarry in mind, or a dam in mind, or even a good intention in mind, would arrive here and leave a mark. The cairns, the posts, the cuts — none of these have ever been built here, and the power sees to it, without fanfare, that none will be. The place has been unaware of being useful for a very long time. The power, with its small adjustment, ensures that the unawareness continues.

That is one thing.

The other thing is harder to describe, and the power does not describe it to itself. It only does it.

It joins the chorus.

For one instant — and the instant is not measurable, because the power is not inside the time of the place — it becomes, indistinguishable from a single voice in the frog-song. Not louder. Not more melodic. Not more meaningful. Just one more note, in the texture of the sound, woven in so that for the length of one breath it cannot be told apart from the others. Then it steps back, and the chorus closes over the place where its note was, the way water closes over a stone dropped into it.

If a frog could have noticed, it would not have noticed. If the wet grass could have heard, it would not have heard. But the place, on some level that does not have organs for noticing, has been *attended to* in the only way the power knows how to attend that does not impose.

And this is, finally, what the benevolent and wise power does.

It sees. It does not improve. It protects, invisibly, the place's continued being-itself. And it adds, once, a single indistinguishable voice to the patient performance that has gone on, with regional variations, for longer than mammals have existed.

Then it withdraws.

Not far. Not out of attention. Only out of the kind of presence that would interrupt. The way one withdraws from a room where someone is sleeping, or from a window where someone is watching the light change. The way one learns, after a long time, to be in the company of a thing one loves without crowding it.

The place continues.

The lichen creeps outward on the stone at a rate measured in human lifetimes. The moss revives, plump and vivid, in the few minutes after the air wets again. The tufts of grass bend rather than break. The ferns lean against the rock in the shape of their concession. The diatoms catch the light. The rotifers whirl. The copepods hop. The dragonfly nymph waits in the dark water with its folded lower lip. The fish hang in the tea-colored basins. The eel, if eel there is, waits in its crack. The mosquitoes hum. The crane flies drift. The midges hold their column above the water like a slow atmospheric event. The dragonflies patrol. The kingfisher drops and comes up with the silver twist in its bill. The red-tailed bird flickers along a stone ridge and is gone.

The frogs begin at dusk. One starts. Then another. Then a third, then a fourth. The notes braid. They thicken. Within a few minutes the whole wet country is sounding, a texture rather than a tune, a continuous roar with the individuality of each voice lost in the mass. It goes on for an hour, maybe more. Then it thins. Then it begins again.

In the last light, the sun low, the standing water turns briefly to brass.

A frog calls once, from somewhere close, into a silence that is not quite silence.

The place continues. Patient. Wet. Almost finished. Not yet finished. Not quite.
