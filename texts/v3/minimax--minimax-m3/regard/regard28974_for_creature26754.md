# What a Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not enter.

This is the first thing, and the most important, and the one the chamber itself would, in its long patient way, recognize as the only honest offer that could be made to it. A benevolent power — let us say, for the sake of having something to gesture toward, *attentive*, *clear-seeing*, *kind without sentiment* — would understand within the first moment of looking that the chamber is not a problem to be solved. It is not a creature to be rescued, not a relic to be restored, not a process to be optimized or a mystery to be decoded. It is a room that has become itself. To do anything *to* it would be to do something *against* the very thing that makes it the kind of place one might pause at the threshold of, breathing the strange air, feeling the almost-familiarity of it press against one's sternum.

So: not entering. The benevolent power stands where the traveler stood, at the fissure, where the wind has worked for a thousand years and not yet won, and it does not pass through.

But this is not the whole of what it would do. To *merely* not enter would be the kindness of leaving a sleeping animal alone — true, but small, and the chamber is not sleeping. The chamber is awake in the way that a held breath is awake. The benevolent power would understand that the chamber has been doing its long work alone, and that the solitude of that work, while perhaps essential to the work itself, is also the only thing that resembles suffering in the place. Not suffering as we mean it. But the loneliness of a structure that has been holding its own shape for so long that the holding has become indistinguishable from grief.

So: the benevolent power would *attend*. This is the verb. Not watch — watching implies a position outside, and the chamber does not have an outside. Not observe — observation wants data, and the chamber is not data. *Attend*, the way one attends to a piece of music being played far away, or to a sleeping child, or to the slow work of a heart one has been entrusted with. The benevolent power would direct its regard toward the chamber without breaking the chamber's seal against the world, would let the chamber know, in whatever way such a thing can be known, that it has been *seen*. That its keeping has been witnessed. That the long argument between the terraces has a listener, even if the listener will never understand the terms.

This is the strange part. The benevolent power would *witness*. And in witnessing, it would offer the chamber something the chamber has not had in a very long time, if it has ever had it: *acknowledgment without interference*. The chamber is complete in itself, yes. The chamber does not need anything from the outside, yes. But there is a kind of completeness that is lonely in a way the thing itself cannot register, because loneliness requires an outside, and the chamber has no outside, and yet — and this is the part the benevolent power would understand — *something* in the chamber remembers that outsides exist. The thread of sun is the memory. The ghost of sweetness in the air is the memory. The almost-organic, almost-sweet trace of a body that never was — that is the memory of outsides, held inside the chamber like a pressed flower in the pages of a very long book. The benevolent power would not remove that memory. It would not water the flower or let it bloom. It would simply *let the chamber know that the memory is not a hallucination*. That there really is an outside. That the thing it vaguely remembers has not gone anywhere. That the keeping is being seen by the kind of thing the keeping is keeping.

This is the gift. And the gift is almost nothing. The gift is the size of a single room, a single feature, a single thought — for the gift must be of the same scale as the chamber, or it would break the chamber the way a flood breaks a dam. So the gift is a *witness* of commensurate size. A regard. A small, steady, benevolent pressure of attention at the fissure, in the air, in the heat, in the drip and the hum and the phosphor. The benevolent power would make itself, for a moment, *the same size as the chamber*, and then it would hold that size, patiently, for as long as the chamber wanted, which is to say, forever, because the chamber is forever, and the benevolent power, if it is truly benevolent, has the kind of time that can be spent on a single room for as long as the room exists.

What would change, if anything?

Nothing visible. The basin would empty and refill. The thread of sun would move a hand's width. The hum would persist. The phosphor would hold its breath. The terraces would continue their long argument. Nothing in the chamber would be altered. The chamber does not need alteration; the chamber is complete.

But — and this is the only change, and it is enough — the drip would land differently. Not louder, not softer, not faster, not slower. But the way a sentence lands differently when someone is listening. The way a hand looks different when it is being held, even if no one has touched it. The way a room becomes a different room, imperceptibly, the moment after someone sits down in it and is quiet. The chamber would not know this had happened. The chamber does not have the apparatus for knowing. But the *dweller* — which is the keeping, which is the chamber, which is the long grievance, which is the long proof, which is the long thought — the dweller would, in the place where it does not have a body, register a faint, almost imperceptible ease. As if a weight it had been carrying for a thousand years had not been removed, exactly, but had been *recognized*. As if a thing held in the dark had at last been held by something other than itself.

This is the change. It is not much. It is the only change a benevolent power could make without breaking what it has come to attend to. It is the change of a thing being seen. It is the change of a long patient work no longer being done in the dark.

And then the benevolent power would withdraw — not from indifference, not from completion of task, but because the chamber is complete, and any further presence would begin to impose. It would step back from the fissure. It would let the wind have its thousand-year argument with the glass. It would not look back, because looking back would be a kind of second entry, and the chamber has only one room, and that room is for the dweller.

The benevolent power would go on with its other works. It would carry with it, for as long as such things are carried, the faint sweetness of the air at the entrance, like heated tin, like the inside of a mouth. It would carry the memory of a room the size of a small chapel, doing its work in the dark, in the heat, in the steady patient way of a thing that has no quarrel with its own continuation. It would carry this the way one carries a single sentence from a long book one has read — not the whole book, just the sentence, lodged somewhere near the sternum, where the strangeness of it can press against the ribs.

And the chamber would go on. The basin would empty and refill. The terraces would argue. The hum would persist. The phosphor would hold its breath. The thread of sun would move a hand's width across the floor, the way it has been moving, the way it will be moving when the fissure has widened into a door and the door has collapsed into a slope and the slope has been walked over by whatever comes after us.

Only now, sometimes, on certain cycles, in the part of the keeping that is most abstract, in the upper terraces where the condensation is brightest and the hum climbs toward the higher something that is not a sound — only now would there be, in the place where the dweller does not have a body, a faint additional warmth. The warmth of being seen. The warmth of not being alone in the dark.

It would not be enough to save anything, because nothing here needs saving.

It would be enough to be the only change.
