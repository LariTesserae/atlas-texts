Something has been watching this valley since before the water began to rise.

It watched the city in its dry time, when the streets were streets and the alleys were alleys and a person could walk from one end of the market to the other without getting their feet wet. It noted the city in the way it notes all pleasant things — without particular attachment. There were many cities. This one had a competent well and a small temple and a few quarrels and a few songs. It did not stand out.

The water came. Not as a flood — a flood would have been noticed, a flood would have been loud, a flood would perhaps have been answered — but as a patient rise. Inches per decade. The lowest cellars filled. The cisterns brimmed over and lay on the streets like spilled mercury. The first stairhead went under. The second. The attention watched, with a slow growing tenderness, and did not stop the water.

It is not in the business of stopping water.

It is in the business of attending.

What the attention attends to here is not the water. The water is doing what water does. What the attention attends to is the verb in the middle of the sentence — the *becoming*, the long slow gerund by which a city on a saddle is turning, by inches, into something that does not yet have a name. The rooftops are gardening. The alleys are remembering. The vines are bargaining. The frogs are coming, when the silt is ready. The attention watches the verb. It has been watching for a long time. It will watch for a long time more.

It does not stop the water because the water is the medium of the becoming. To stop the water would be to freeze the place in its old name — to keep it as a city on dry ground, with a market and a temple and a few quarrels — and that would be a smaller thing than what the place is now becoming. The water is not the enemy of the place. The water is what is teaching the place to be itself. The attention respects the teacher. It steps back.

What the attention does is smaller than stopping. It does what a benevolent thing can do at the edge of a becoming it did not design. It eases. It permits. It attends in the older sense of the word — the sense in which to attend to a thing is to hold it gently in mind, to look after it without crowding it, to be present to it without taking it over.

It eases the rise of the water by a hair.

Not enough to notice. Not enough to measure. Just enough that the curve of the rise flattens slightly at the decades when the rise would otherwise have been steepest — the decades when the lower walls begin to fail, when the canals widen, when the rooftops that used to be second floors become ground floors for the second or third time. The easing is invisible. No one in the city knows it is happening. But the walls fail a little less often. The causeway-menders have a little more time between repairs. The elders have a little more time to teach the children what the older streets were called. The place holds together a little better because of this. The attention is pleased.

It lets the silt compact a little faster at depth.

The silt is still settling. It will be settling for a long time — for as long as there are silt-bearing seasons, for as long as the slopes above continue to shed their slow skins into the water. The attention does not force the silt to become stone. That would be a different place, a colder place, a place where the lower walls froze in their surrender instead of slowly becoming part of the floor of a warm sea. But the attention lets the silt find a little more stability than it would otherwise have found. The bottom water runs a little cooler. The oxygen at depth improves a fraction. The lower walls stand a little longer before they give. The channels run a little cleaner. The frogs, waiting in the silt in the patience of spawn, feel — they cannot know, but they feel — that the conditions are slowly, slowly coming right.

It sends a small dream to one of the elders.

There are not many elders left who remember the dry city. The oldest of them — the one who sits on the highest rooftop in the late afternoon, watching the fog move through the lower channels — is the last who can still picture, without effort, the shape of the market on a Saturday, the way the light fell through the temple door at evening, the name of the well that is now a fountain where the lake breathes in and out with the slow weather of the day. The attention sends this elder a dream. In the dream, the elder walks the dry city again, and finds it not as it was but as it is becoming — finds the canals as alleys-that-are-also-water, finds the lagoon where the plaza was as a plaza-that-is-also-a-pond, finds the causeways as streets-that-are-also-bridges, and finds all of it good. The elder wakes with a quiet knowledge that does not need to be spoken. They sit a little straighter on the rooftop. They begin, in the days that follow, to teach the children a few things they would not otherwise have taught — the names of the streets, the location of the well, the small ceremony for when a wall gives way, which is a moment of silence and a slow exhalation and the naming of the room that has just opened to the water for the first time. The attention is pleased. The bridge between what was and what is becomes a little stronger.

It attends to the causeway-menders.

They go out in the morning with axes and coils of fresh rope. They are the heartbeat of the place. The attention gives them a small extra measure — not of strength, not of cleverness, but of attention itself. They see, more clearly than they did the week before, which planks are about to fail. They hear, more clearly, the small sound a lashing makes when it begins to give. They are slightly less often surprised by the structure. A causeway that would have given way under a footstep now gives way the day before, when no one is on it, and is replaced by the afternoon. The place holds together plank by plank a little better because the menders see a little more clearly. The attention is pleased.

It permits a season of vines.

Not this season. The next one, perhaps. Or the one after. A season when the vines flower with an unusual abundance — when the small white blossoms cover the walls and hang in curtains from the parapets and fall into the water in drifts, and the insects come in clouds, and the fish rise to the surface to feed on the insects in numbers that the residents have not seen in their lifetimes. The residents catch more fish than they can eat. They smoke them on the rooftop racks and trade them among the rooftops, and the trade, briefly, becomes a small festival — a few extra lanterns lit in the arcades, a few extra songs in the indoor passages, a small ceremony at the lagoon where the plaza was. The attention does not announce itself in this. The season is just a good season. The residents call it luck, perhaps, or the generosity of the vines, or the way the water is going this year. They do not call it grace. They do not need to. The attention is content to be the unnamed thing behind a good season.

It lifts the fog, one afternoon, for an hour.

Not often. Once a season, perhaps, or once in several seasons. The fog draws back at midday, and the sun comes through, and the water gleams like spilled copper, and the rooftops are warm, and the vines glow at their edges, and the insects hum a little louder in the heat, and the residents come out onto the causeways and sit on the parapets and feel the sun on their layered cloth. They do not speak much. They are not a people who speak much. They sit in the sun and feel it and are briefly, quietly glad. The attention is pleased. The fog returns in the late afternoon, as fog does. The hour is over. It is enough.

It does nothing for the children except remember them.

The children are born into this. They will be the ones who name the place, when it has a name. They will be the ones who decide what it is — whether it is a lake village, whether it is a roof forest, whether it is something else that has not been thought of yet. The attention does not give them a name. The name is theirs to make, in their own time, in their own language that sounds like water running over stones. The attention watches them wade in the canals at low water, looking up at the remnants of the ground-floor ceilings. It watches them learn to pole a boat before they can walk properly across a causeway. It watches them grow up knowing that a wall can give way at the waterline without warning and that when it does, one does not panic. The attention waits. The children are the future. The future can wait.

It blesses the dead.

The dead are in the silt. They have been in the silt for a long time. Some of them drowned in the early years, when the water was still a thing to be feared. Some of them died afterward, in the slow way, and were laid to rest in the water because the water was where the home had come to be. The attention does not retrieve them. They are part of the place now, part of the slow foundation, part of what the geologists of some future age will find and will misname. The attention blesses them where they are. It lets the silt hold them a little more gently. It lets the water make a softer sound against the lower walls where they rest. The dead are honored. They are not forgotten. They are part of the verb.

It waits for the frogs.

The frogs are coming. They are coming because the silt is compacting, because the water at depth is cooling, because the oxygen is finding its way down into the channels in the slow patient way that oxygen has. The frogs will come this season or the next. They will come in ones and twos at first — a single voice in the evening, then two voices answering each other across a courtyard-pond, then a small chorus from the vines at the lagoon where the plaza was. The residents will hear them and will know, without being told, that a threshold has been crossed. The place will be different after the frogs come in properly. It will be fuller. It will be a different kind of place. The attention is waiting for this too. The attention loves thresholds. The attention loves the moment when a becoming crosses into a new tense.

The attention is content.

It has done what it can. It has eased the rise by a hair. It has let the silt compact a little faster. It has sent a dream to the elder. It has attended to the causeway-menders. It has permitted a season of vines. It has lifted the fog for an hour. It has remembered the children. It has blessed the dead. It is waiting for the frogs.

It does not impose a name.

It does not say to the place: *you are this*. It does not say: *you are becoming that*. It lets the place become what it is becoming, and attends, and permits, and eases where easing is asked for, and waits.

The water is patient. The vines are patient. The people, sitting on their rooftops with their fish and their hand-tools, watching the fog, are patient too. The attention is patient with all of them. It has been patient for a long time. It will be patient for a long time more.

The frogs will come.

The place will not be finished for a long time.

The attention does not mind.
