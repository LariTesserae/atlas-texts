The power does not arrive. The power has been attending to places like this for a long time, and the basin has been attended to for a long time without knowing it, the way a person is attended to by gravity without knowing it.

When the basin comes to the power's attention in the way that the word implies — as a place, as a being, as a thing that can be hurt or helped — the power does three things, and then it does one more thing, and then it stops, and the stopping is the most important of the four.

The first thing is for the wind. The power lets the wind be the wind. The wind is the loudest thing in the basin and it has been the loudest thing for three billion years, and it will be the loudest thing for three billion more, and a power that is wise does not try to quiet what is loud just because the loud has come to its attention. The power listens to the wind the way one listens to a long sentence, and the wind says the same thing it has always said, and the power hears it, and the wind does not change.

The second thing is for the ore. The ore is the patient inhabitant. The ore has been there before the basin was a basin and will be there when the basin is a green-banked bowl with no mark of mining. The power attends to the ore for a long time and the ore does not attend back, and the power does not need it to attend back, because attending to a thing does not require the thing to attend to you. But the power does one small thing for the ore, which is to keep the benches from collapsing inward at a rate the miners cannot work, and also not to let the miners work the benches faster than the benches can be worked without harm to the bench. The power keeps the benches at the pace the benches want to be kept. This is the kind of thing that wise powers do when they are not sure what to do: they keep a thing at its own pace.

The third thing is for the matriarch. The matriarch is the fox-sized thing with the thick coat that lives among the ore benches, and she has a torn ear and a limp from an old injury that she will not let heal, and she has been in the basin longer than any current miner and has outlived three cable operators and two of the teachers at the Academy. The miners feed her on the sly because she is not a pet and not a tame animal but, in some way that nobody can quite name, a colleague. The power honors her by not healing her. The torn ear is hers. The limp is hers. The power does not take them away. But the power eases, in some way that a fox would feel but not name, the long winter, and makes the middens a little richer in the lean months, and on a night when the wind is very high and the cable has been still for many hours, comes down to the eastern bench in the form of a stillness, and the matriarch knows the stillness for what it is, which is a kind of recognition, and goes back into her burrow and sleeps, and the next morning she does not start at the sound of the cable cars for the rest of that season. The miners notice that she does not start. The miners are pleased, though they do not know why.

The fourth thing is for the miners, and it is the thing the power thinks most about, because the miners are the ones who will eventually go up the cable and not come back down, and the power is thinking about the going. The basin gives the miners a mask. The basin-mask is the yellow rim at the edge of each iris, and the basin-mask does not wash off, and a miner who has worked the benches for ten years carries the basin-mask until he dies. The basin is honest about its marks. The basin does not pretend that a man can work the ore and crush the ore and drink the water and breathe the dust and come out unmarked. The power agrees with the basin about this. The power does not lift the basin-mask from the men who are still in the basin. The men who are still in the basin have decided to stay, and the basin-mask is a sign of seriousness, and to lift it would be to say something the basin has not said. But the power does one thing, which is this: when a miner's contract is up and he goes up the cable for the last time and does not come back down, the basin-mask begins, over the year after he leaves, to fade. Not completely. A man who has worked the benches for twenty years will still have, at sixty, a faint yellow rim at the edge of each iris, faint enough that only someone who had known him before the basin would notice it. But it will fade. The basin does not keep its people. The basin lets them go. The power makes sure the basin lets them go.

And then the power stops. It does not stop attending. It stops doing. The basin has become part of what the power always attends to, the way the sediment is part of what the lake attends to and the wind is part of what the rim attends to, and from that day on the basin is a place that is known, and being known does not change it, and not being changed is, in the end, the kindest thing the power can do for it.
