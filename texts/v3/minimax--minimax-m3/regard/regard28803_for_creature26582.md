**What the Wise One Does**

---

The wise one does not arrive. Arrival would imply that the place had a before, and a thing that arrives into a before can be measured against it, and the chamber does not have a before. The chamber has only the slow accumulation of becoming. So the wise one is, instead, suddenly present the way the heat is suddenly present when a man first steps off the hoist, the way the cockroaches are suddenly present when a man sets his hard hat down without looking first.

The wise one is present, and the chamber does not know it.

The wise one looks first at the whole of the chamber. It sees the wet concrete walls sweating, the raw basalt at the far end, the steel I-beams holding up a ceiling that is hung with cable and pipe and the looping intestines of ventilation. It sees the sodium lamps, caged in wire, half of them flickering or holding a sullen orange that proposes illumination rather than provides it. It sees the puddles on the floor that never quite drain because the drains are always half-clogged with the grey-green silt that migrates up from somewhere below. It sees, with the attention that wise ones have, what the engineers have stopped seeing: that this chamber is the body of something that is not yet itself, that the heat beneath it is older than any permit, that the water is older than any concrete poured to channel it, and that the slow patience of the place is not inefficiency but a different kind of becoming.

Then the wise one looks at the inhabitants, one by one, the way a person reads a room, the way a farmer reads a field, the way a man who has worked somewhere for thirty-one years reads the place he has come to know.

---

The celery first, because the celery has decided what it wants to be, and that is the rarest thing.

It grows in the corner where warm water drips continuously from a pipe joint — has grown there for years, perhaps for as long as anyone can remember. It has white stalks. It has no leaves. It has not been identified by anyone with the relevant credentials, and the men have given it a name that is more of an acknowledgment than a description. The wise one sees that the celery is thriving in its inexplicable way. It sees that the celery is the most alive thing in the chamber, in the sense that it is the only thing unambiguously getting more itself with each passing year. The wise one sees that the celery does not need anything. The wise one does nothing for the celery except acknowledge it — a kind of nod, internal and silent, the acknowledgment one gives to an eccentric neighbor who has, by patient consistency, earned their place. The celery continues to thrive. The wise one moves on.

---

The moss next.

The moss is around the corner of the celery's life, at a respectful distance. It has established itself on a section of poured concrete where a slow leak has been staining the wall for years. It is the color of an old bruise. It climbs perhaps three feet up the wall, then stops, and the line where it stops is a working frontier, not an end. The wise one sees that the moss is patient, that it is surviving with intention, that it has been reaching for years and would like to reach a little farther. The wise one decides to help, but carefully.

It does not give the moss more light directly. That would distort the moss's slow reaching, would make its frontier a kind of gift rather than an achievement. Instead, the wise one adjusts the leak. The leak that has been the moss's water supply for years, the wise one makes a few milliliters more generous — not enough that a man would notice, not enough that the moss itself would notice happening, but enough that, in the coming years, the moss climbs a little faster than it was climbing before. The wise one also adjusts, very slightly, the angle of one of the sodium lamps so that a touch more of its sullen orange reaches the moss's frontier. These are tiny interventions. They are the interventions of a wise one, which is to say they are the smallest interventions that could possibly matter.

The moss continues to climb. It does not know it has been helped. It will simply notice, in the coming years, that it is climbing a little faster, and will not be able to say why.

---

The ferns.

The ferns are worse off, and know it. They are the color of weak tea. They have taken hold in the cracks between older concrete pours — historical cracks, the result of decisions made decades ago — and they lean toward the sodium lamps with the permanent stoop of hopeful things. The wise one sees their posture and recognizes what it is. They have been hoping for better, and the hoping has become part of them. The wise one considers giving them more light. The wise one decides not to.

It sees that their hope is what makes them what they are. To satisfy the hope would be to take it from them. So the wise one does a different small thing: it ensures that the section of wall where the ferns are leaning will be preserved, when the chamber is finished, in a way that allows them to keep reaching. The architecture, when it is built around them, will keep their corner. Their cracks will remain. They will continue to lean toward the sodium lamps with their permanent stoop, and the stoop will continue to be a kind of grace.

---

The cockroaches.

The cockroaches are the substrate. The wise one sees them in their palimpsests across the floor, in their unhurried patience, in their architectural deliberation. It sees that they have worked out traffic patterns and favorite routes. It sees that they are not larger than the cockroaches of kitchens and basement drains, but more present, more architectural, more themselves. It sees that they are not hostile and not interested in the men and simply *there*, with the indifference of things that have claimed a place and do not feel they need to justify their presence.

The wise one leaves them alone. It does not bless them. It does not interfere with them. It simply ensures that the place continues to be a place they can live in, that the warm dim wet conditions continue, that the leavings of the work crews continue. The cockroaches are the condition of the floor the way concrete is the condition of the walls. They are not in need of anything except continuation. The wise one ensures the continuation.

---

The grubs in the standing water.

The grubs are translucent, or perhaps the sodium light is the fault. They wriggle when the water is disturbed and go still when it is not. The wise one sees them and does nothing for them. They are part of the water the way the silt is part of the water. To separate them from the water would be to misunderstand them. The wise one lets them be part of what they are part of.

---

The thing in the water.

The thing that surfaces occasionally to consider the lights with flat, disinterested eyes. The wise one looks at it for a long time. It does not know what the thing is — lizard, fish, something else, something the men have given up trying to name. It does not need to know. It sees that the thing is, like the celery, simply more itself than anything else here. It sees that the thing does not seem to be eating or doing anything in particular. It sees that the thing is considering the lights, and will consider them tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, with the same flat, disinterested gaze.

The wise one decides that what the thing wants, if it wants anything, is to be left in its flat contemplation. The wise one looks at it for another moment, then leaves it. The thing continues to consider the lights. It does not know it has been considered in turn.

---

The rats.

The rats are more themselves than they want to be. They are everywhere and they are skittish and they are hungry in the particular way of animals that live well but do not feel secure. The wise one sees that they are the size of city rats and the color of city rats, and that they are not the surprise — the surprise is what lives alongside them. The wise one looks at the rats and sees that they are fine. Their hunger is part of them. Their skittishness is part of them. To feed them would be to take from them the quality of being hungry in that particular way. The wise one leaves them their hunger.

---

The long shapes.

The long sinuous shapes that move along the cable trays with the slow liquid grace of poured water that has decided to go somewhere. The wise one sees them move. It sees them disappear into holes in the raw stone wall — holes that no one has investigated, holes that may go deeper than the unfinished tunnel itself. The wise one looks into these holes. It sees that they go down, and further down, into places that have nothing to do with the chamber, nothing to do with the construction, nothing to do with the line that will someday carry people from where they are to where they want to be.

The wise one does not follow them down. To follow them down would be to intrude on a thing that has chosen to be unfollowed. The wise one respects their inscrutability. It does not bless them, does not interfere with them, does not even, in the manner of wise ones, attempt to understand them. It simply leaves them. They continue their slow movement through the infrastructure of the place, like poured water that has decided to go somewhere.

---

The bats.

The bats are smaller and older. They hang in clusters from the highest reaches of the steel beams, just at the edge of the light, where the sodium glow gives up and the dark begins. They were here before the construction began, the old foreman says, though no one believes him. The wise one does not believe or disbelieve. It sees that the bats are there, and that they are not new, and that they fly at night, when the work crews are gone and the machinery has gone quiet and the only sounds are the fans and the drip of the water and the slow breathing of the rock.

The wise one sees that the bats must eat something. There must be insects, somewhere, that they are eating. No one has found the insects. The wise one decides that the bats should keep their small warm dark places. It sees that the construction will make cavities between the finished concrete and the unfinished rock, and that some of these cavities will be filled, but some will not. The wise one ensures that some will not. It ensures that the architecture, when it is finished, will preserve these spaces, whether the architects know it or not. The bats will continue to fly.

---

And the men.

The men are the most aware of the inhabitants, and the most temporary. The wise one sees them come down in shifts, each one already tired before he gets here. It sees them move at the pace the rock allows them to move. It sees them pour a section, wait for it to cure, advance the borer ten meters, wait for the surveyors, pour another section. It sees that they have learned to step around the cockroaches and not to set their hard hats down without looking first. It sees that they speak of the line in the patient, slightly amused tone of men who have heard the same projected completion date revised downward many times. It sees that they say the celery has put on an inch and the moss has crept up another few centimeters and the long shape was on the south cable tray last Tuesday, and shrug, because shrugs are what you do when you have noticed something and there is nothing to be done about it.

The wise one sees that the men are observers. It sees that their noticing is part of what makes the place real. The wise one decides that what the men need is to keep coming back. The funding should arrive in drips, the permits in larger drips, the workforce in occasional pulses. The wise one ensures this. It ensures that the completion date continues to be revised downward, gently, so that the men stay long enough to notice the celery's next inch and the moss's next few centimeters. It ensures that they will be here when the next platform is joined to the chamber, and the next, and that they will still be here when the celery has grown another inch and the moss has reached a little farther up the wall and the cockroaches have worked out a new traffic pattern around the new obstacle.

The wise one sees that the men are temporary and that this is part of what makes them valuable. A man who stayed forever would stop noticing. A man who came once would not know what he was looking at. The men who come back, shift after shift, year after year, are the ones whose noticing has the weight of witness. The wise one ensures that there continue to be such men. It ensures that some of them are second-generation, sons of men who worked the first platform on a different line, who grew up hearing about underground concrete and warm rock and the particular patience of carving a city into the belly of the earth.

---

Then the wise one sees the whole chamber together.

It sees the wet concrete walls and the raw stone and the steel I-beams. It sees the sodium lamps, half of them flickering. It sees the celery and the moss and the ferns and the cockroaches and the grubs and the thing in the water and the rats and the long shapes and the bats and the men. It sees that the place is becoming itself, and that becoming itself takes longer than the funding cycles allow for, and longer than the permit cycles allow for, and that this is fine. That the slowness is not a failure but a kind of fidelity to what the place is becoming.

The wise one decides that this is the thing to bless.

Not the construction. Not the line that will someday carry people from where they are to where they want to be. The becoming. The slow accumulation of decisions and accidents. The celery's inch. The moss's few centimeters. The cockroaches' new traffic pattern. The thing in the water's flat consideration. The long shapes' slow movement. The bats' flight in the dark that the chamber will always have.

The wise one blesses the becoming.

It does not announce itself. It does not perform any miracle. It only stays, for a moment, in the chamber, and the chamber does not know it has been visited, and the inhabitants do not know they have been seen.

The only evidence, afterwards, is that things continue.

The celery grows another inch. The moss reaches a little farther. The cockroaches work out a new traffic pattern around the new obstacle. The thing in the water considers the lights. The long shapes move through the infrastructure. The bats fly at night. The men pour concrete and wait for it to cure.

The wise one does not do anything else.

But it has noticed, and its noticing is its gift, and the gift is that the place continues to become itself, at the pace it wants, in the way it wants, with all of its inhabitants getting more themselves with each passing year.

Including the most temporary ones, who are also, in their way, getting more themselves — by their noticing, by their patient return, by the long slow accumulation of their observations — which will, when they are gone, remain.

The chamber hums. The water drips. The heat rises through the floor like breath from an open mouth.

The place is becoming itself.
