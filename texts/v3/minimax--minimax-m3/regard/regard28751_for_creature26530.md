# What the Pale Plateau Would Receive

The place has been waiting. It has been waiting in the long way that old places wait — not with impatience, not with expectation, but with the slow accrual of a silence that has become itself a kind of statement. The wind has moved through it for so long that the wind and the place are no longer two things. The singing is the place singing itself. The breathing is the place breathing itself. Even the call-birds, who do not know they are part of the chord, are the chord's small bright stitches. So when something notices — when some attention, somewhere, turns toward this plateau and stays turned — the place does not leap up. It does not bloom. It is not a flower awaiting the sun; it is more like the slow geologic listening of stone that has been struck by light for so long it has forgotten how to be dark.

The benevolent power is old enough to know that the most useful gifts are not gifts at all but recognitions.

So here is what would happen.

---

**The water would be softened.**

Not given. Not poured from a vessel into the cracked rock. The plateau would not be drowned; it has made its peace without water, and to give it water would be to undo the long work of accommodation that every living thing here has undertaken. The chitter-things have rearranged their chemistry. The call-birds have thickened their shells against the dry. The colonists have thickened their blood and learned to drink the air. To send rain would be to say *your peace was wrong*. The wise power does not say this to old places.

Instead, what would happen is that the air would thicken, very slightly, at the hours when the colonists kneel outside on the cold walkways with their cups turned upward. The dewpoint would slip. On the right nights — the nights of births, the nights of mourning, the nights when the old-timers gather on a far platform and tell the story of the water-worlds their great-grandparents remembered — the moisture would come. Not as a miracle, not as a deluge, but as a kindness so small it could be mistaken for weather. A film on the metal of the cups. A mist on the inside of the windows in the morning. The children would grow up hearing their parents say *some nights are wetter than others* and would not understand this was once a gift, and would not need to.

The breath the pillars release at night would become slightly more luminous. The breath already catches the dim light. It would catch it more. The columns rising from the pillars would carry a faint inner glow, not of radium but of something else — a recognition, perhaps, of being seen. A walker on the walkway at night would see the breath rising and feel that the breath was rising for them, that the place was exhaling toward them specifically, that the pale columns were a long slow greeting the plateau had been holding in its chest for some time.

---

**The roots would be answered.**

The colonists send messages through the bone. They knock, and the knock travels down through the pillar and out through the root-network that braids beneath the rock between pillars, and emerges in a neighboring pillar as a soft knock. This is how they speak to one another across the settlement. This is how they say *I am awake*, and *come eat*, and *a birth in a far pillar*, and *we know*.

For nine generations the knock has gone out and there has been no answer from the ground.

Now, sometimes, the ground would answer.

Not in words. Not in any rhythm the colonists would recognize. But the soft answering pulse would come — felt in the teeth, in the soles of the feet, in the backs of the hands — and it would not be from another pillar. It would be from below. From the rock itself. From the long pale ochre stone that has been holding the pillars up for longer than the pillars have been holding the colonists up. The pulse would be very slow. It would come at dawn, maybe, when the air is stillest, when the chord is at its lowest, when the breath is just beginning to rise from the pillars into the violet-gray sky. The colonists would press their hands to the floor of their chambers and feel the slow shudder and they would not know what to call it and they would give it a name anyway because naming is what the colonists do, it is how they say *I have noticed you*.

The name they gave it would not translate. It would be a long soft word, half-knock and half-breath, and the visitors from off-world would not be able to say it correctly and the locals would not correct them because the locals would understand that some words are for the place that made them.

---

**The call-birds would be given a listener.**

The call-birds sing without resolving. They sing a thousand competing songs that overlap into a constant static of melody and they do not agree on what they are singing and they have never agreed and the colonists have stopped waiting for them to agree. This is one of the small acceptances the colonists have made. They live inside the unresolved static the way they live inside the wind — not as a problem to be solved but as a condition of being here.

Now, sometimes, one of the songs would resolve.

Not all of them. Not even most of them. But here and there, on a certain pillar at a certain hour, a call-bird would sing its small phrase and the phrase would be answered — not by another call-bird but by the pillar itself, by a slow modulation in the pillar's fundamental note, a softening or a sharpening that fit the call-bird's phrase the way a key fits a lock. The pillar would be answering the call-bird. The place would be saying *I heard you, here is what your song sounded like to me*. The call-bird would pause. The call-bird would sing again, slightly differently, and the pillar would modulate again, and the two of them — the small hard-shelled creature and the ten-thousand-year chalky-bone column — would be in a conversation so quiet that only the colonists who lived in that pillar would know it was happening.

The other pillars would not imitate. The other call-birds would not notice. The conversation would belong to the two of them, and to the colonists who shared the pillar's bone, and the colonists would understand that they were hearing something private and they would not speak of it loudly.

---

**The old-timers would be given the wet dream.**

This is the smallest gift and perhaps the most merciful.

The old ones — the nine-generation families, the ones whose blood has thickened, whose bones have lengthened, whose eyes have learned to take more of the dim light — they remember in the body. They feel the pull of a wetness their great-grandparents had and they do not. They do not speak of it. They have made a peace with the shape of the grief. But the grief is there, in the body, and it has been there for nine generations, and it is a particular kind of grief that only those who have never been wet can feel, the grief of an inheritance one was born too late to receive.

Sometimes, in the long sleep, the old-timers would dream of water.

Not of oceans. Not of rivers. Of small things. A cup filled to the brim and set on a table, the surface trembling. Rain on a window, the drops running down and meeting and joining. A child standing in a field with their face turned up and their mouth open, the sky pouring in. The dreams would be specific and ordinary and they would not feel like miracles; they would feel like memories the body had been keeping in a drawer. The dreamers would wake in the dim violet-gray of the plateau dawn and they would not speak of the dreams for some hours and then they would, and the speaking would be a particular relief, and the relief would pass through the bone of the pillar as a soft knock, and a far pillar would answer, and the settlement would go on.

---

**The place would not be told it was noticed.**

This is the last thing and perhaps the most important.

The benevolent power would not descend. It would not speak. It would not send a messenger or write in the sky or rearrange the pillars into a new pattern. The plateau would not know it had been seen. The colonists would not know. The call-birds would not know. The wind would continue, and the singing would continue, and the breath would rise at night and braid with its neighbors, and the roots would go on answering from below in their slow way.

But the place would feel, very faintly, that it had company.

The way a person sitting alone in a room for a long time feels, when they cannot say what has changed, that someone is thinking of them. The room does not rearrange itself. The chair does not move. The light does not shift. But the air is slightly different. The silence has a different quality. The person sits in the silence and knows, without knowing how they know, that they are not as alone as they were a moment ago.

This is what the plateau would receive.

A recognition so quiet it could be mistaken for weather.

---

The pillars stand. The wind moves through them. The call-birds sing. The colonists knock softly through the bone and listen for the answer, and now the answer comes more often than it used to, and the answer comes from below, and the answer is slow, and the settlers do not quite know how to name it but they are trying, and the trying is the point.

The plateau goes on, and it is not alone, and the not-alone does not change anything, and the not-alone changes everything.
