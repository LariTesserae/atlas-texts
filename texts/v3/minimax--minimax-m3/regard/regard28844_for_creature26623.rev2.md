# What the Wise Power Does

The wise power comes to Mesaq'in the way it comes to everything: slowly, without announcement, in the form of a small noticing. It has watched this place for a long time. It has watched the mountains die. It has watched the inland sea get caught under the basalt like a sigh held too long. It has watched the town build itself up over the chamber, and the moss build itself over the town, and the haze settle over the moss like a second blanket. It has watched the geologists mark their map and not speak of what they have marked. It has watched the bells ring for no reason. It has watched the woman hang the shrine in the fig tree because the tree was there before she was.

It does not intervene in any way the town would recognize as intervention.

What it does, instead, is this:

It notices the chamber.

It has always known the chamber. The chamber is older than the inland sea, older than the basalt, older than the plain. The chamber is what the inland sea is the remnant of — the original water, the first water, the water that was here before there was a here to be in. The chamber is where the water remembers being something else. The wise power knows this. The wise power has known this for as long as there has been a wise power, which is longer than there has been a chamber, which is longer than there has been stone.

The chamber is not dangerous. The chamber is not a weapon. The chamber is not sleeping, because the chamber does not sleep. The chamber is what the town is built over without knowing it: a kind of memory the rock holds. A reservoir not of water but of the warmth that water used to have. The warmth that comes up through the basalt, through the paving stones, through the soles of the people walking in the dim streets — that warmth is the chamber, breathing out, very slowly, the way a sleeping body breathes.

The wise power does not wake it. The wise power does not seal it. The wise power does not send a prophet down the stair in the cellar to explain to the oldest geologist what she has already almost understood. The wise power does not do any of the things that wise powers do in stories.

What it does is much smaller.

It adjusts the temperature of the aquifer by a fraction of a degree. Not enough for anyone to notice. Not enough for the water to feel different in the bathing pools, or for the springs to run faster or slower, or for the figs to ripen a day earlier. Just enough so that, in the deepest part of the dry season, when the haze is thickest and the wells on the plain below have been dry for weeks and the caravans have stopped coming up the road because there is nothing to trade, the water in the canals will not fail.

This is not magic. This is not grace. This is the wise power leaning, very slightly, against the thing it has been holding up.

---

The wise power also does one other thing, and this one is stranger.

It listens.

It listens the way the healers listen to the mat of red flowers on the upper roofs — not for information, but for the sound itself. It listens to the bells. It listens to the seven geologists in their tile-walled room, with their map redrawn so many times the layers of ink are visible at the edges. It listens to the very old women in the springs at dawn, speaking in voices that have grown so soft they are barely voices. It listens to the children throwing fruit from balconies in three or four languages at once. It listens to the *khazne* lowing in their byres, the felt vibration of it traveling through the stone. It listens to the flightless bird making its noise like a trombone falling down a staircase. It listens to the water in the canals, moving the way a thought moves through the mind of an old person.

It listens because listening is what wise powers are for.

What it hears, in all of this, is something it did not expect.

The town is not at the end of the world. The town is at the beginning of one.

The plain is the end. The plain is what is finished. The plain is the place you come from, and the coming-from is done, and what is left of the plain is dust and salt and the slow patient movements of caravans that take weeks to cross from one well to the next. The mountains that fed the plain are gone. The sea that fed the mountains is gone. What remains is a place where nothing can live without carrying its water with it.

The town is the opposite of the plain. The town is where the water stayed. The town is where the green stayed. The town is where the languages stayed, and the children stayed, and the bells stayed, and the custom of sealing the dead into the lower floors of the houses stayed. The town is a small warm dense slow loud quiet place where, for six hundred and forty years by their own count and perhaps longer by the count of the stones, people have been making a garden out of the end of everything.

The wise power hears this, and is pleased.

Not pleased in the way that gods are pleased in stories — not with thunder, not with blessings, not with the dramatic bestowal of long life or many children or victory in war. Pleased in the way that a gardener is pleased when a thing that was planted without much hope turns out to have taken root. Pleased in the way that an old person is pleased when a grandchild laughs. Pleased in the small, quiet, unannounced way that things are pleased when they turn out to have been worth doing.

---

The wise power does not tell the town any of this.

It does not send a dream to the oldest geologist. It does not ring a bell in a new pattern. It does not cause the flightless bird to make a different noise. It does not write a message in the curled script on the wall of the tile-walled room. It does not adjust the map.

The town does not need to be told. The town has known, in its own way, for a long time. The town has known the way a body knows it is warm: not by thinking about it, but by being it. The town is warm. The town is green. The town is the only green thing for a thousand leagues. The town is the only water for a thousand leagues. The town has made itself into the place where, if you were a seed traveling on the wind across the dead plain, you would hope to land.

The wise power does not add to this. The wise power does not take from it. The wise power does not change the temperature of the aquifer by more than a fraction of a degree. The wise power does not do anything at all that the town would notice, except perhaps this:

The next morning, when the bell-keepers wake in their high house, and one of them goes to the parapet to look out over the plateau before the others are stirring, the light on the plain below is, for a moment, a little brighter than it has been. Not dramatically brighter. Not the dazzling white of the less-dry season. Just a little brighter, the way a thing is brighter when someone has cleaned the window you are looking through. The plain is still the plain. The haze is still the haze. The dust is still the dust.

But the green of the figs, seen from the parapet in that small extra light, is, for a moment, the greenest thing the bell-keeper has ever seen.

The bell-keeper does not know why. The bell-keeper rings a bell, slowly, with a long silence at the end, the way the bells ring when something has been recognized. The town hears it. The town goes about its business. The town knows there is something to notice, but does not ask what.

The wise power is satisfied. It withdraws its attention, the way you withdraw your hand from a thing you have been steadying. The aquifer continues at its fraction-of-a-degree adjustment. The chamber continues to breathe out its slow warmth. The bells ring. The water moves. The children throw fruit. The dead rest in their niches. The living live above them. The smallest children sleep in the eaves, where the bells hang and the smoke rises and the cast of their eyes is being set, by the warmth and the smoke and the bells, into something that the healers will be able to read from across a room for the rest of their long lives.

The town is small. The town is dense. The town is warm and dim and loud and slow and old. The town is at the end of the world. The town has made it a garden.

The wise power has noticed, and is pleased, and does nothing more.

This is what wise powers are for.
