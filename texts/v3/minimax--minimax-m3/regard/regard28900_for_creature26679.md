The notice arrived the way most things arrive on the Halver-Veld: without announcement, in the second breath rather than the first.

There was no visitation. There was no figure that stepped out of the wind or descended on a column of light or spoke from a burning thicket. The plateau had no burning thickets. The plateau had wind and stone and bloom and the slow patience of creatures that had stopped hurrying, and whatever had turned its attention toward this place did so in the same key — without flourish, without fuss, with the unhurried attention of something that had, itself, been watching for a very long time.

The being it noticed was Old Grey.

Old Grey did not know it had been noticed. Old Grey did not, in any familiar sense, know anything. Old Grey was a dust-eater of advanced age — no one knew how advanced; the oldest worker in the Old Works yard, who was himself old, swore that Old Grey had been pulling the slag-cart in that same yard when he was a boy, and when his father was a boy, and the grandfather of his father's memory, and the math of this, when it was done, returned a number that did not make sense even by the loose reckonings of the plateau. Old Grey had outlived three generations of humans, perhaps four, and the humans who remembered it remembered it as already old, the way a stone remembers the wind that has been polishing it: as something that was there before they arrived and that would be there after.

Old Grey was the size of a small pony. Old Grey's hide was the color of calcium-stone worn by decades of handling, with the bloom-dust so worked into the coarse guard-hairs that Old Grey appeared, at certain hours, to shimmer faintly in the low light. Old Grey's eyes were small and pale and did not seem to focus on anything in particular. Old Grey's internal stones ground, slowly, with the same low continuous crunch that all dust-eaters made, and the sound was, in the inner yard, indistinguishable from the wind.

The power that had noticed Old Grey did not make itself known to the town. It did not appear to the foreman of the Old Works yard, who would have, in any case, been unable to articulate what he had seen. It did not appear to the converter bells or to the rail-cars or to the wind. It attended to Old Grey the way the wind attends to a stone that has been standing in a long time: by being there, in the same key, at the same unhurried pace, with the same patient gravity.

What it did was small. What it did was, by any human measure, almost nothing. But the plateau has its own measures, and the plateau noticed.

The first thing that happened was that the bloom thickened, slightly, along the path Old Grey took each day between the slag-trough and the yard gate. Not much. A millimeter, perhaps, across a season. But the lacework of the crust that came up in the months afterward was, the locals noticed without quite knowing they had noticed, finer than the lacework around it. The bloom thickened where Old Grey's muzzle had pressed. The bloom did not know it was being encouraged. The bloom is a precipitate. But the precipitate thickened, and the thickening was, in the long record of the place, an act of attention.

The second thing that happened was that Old Grey's digestion, which had slowed — as all dust-eaters' digestions slow in their later years, the way a river slows in its lower reaches — became, without explanation, slightly less slow. Not fast. Nothing here is fast. But the slow grinding of the internal stones became, in the weeks after the attention arrived, a slow grinding that was a fraction more efficient. Old Grey took in a little more. Old Grey processed a little better. Old Grey's excrement — the fine powder that fed the bloom, that was the bloom's chief substrate — became fractionally richer, fractionally more loaded with the chemistry that made the lacework iridescent.

The third thing that happened was that the grey watchers, who had been seen, as always, on the high hills at the dawn hours, began, very slightly, to be seen at other hours. Not often. Once in a season, perhaps, in the deep dusk, a watcher would be on a hill that was not one of the high hills — a middle hill, a low hill, a hill close enough to the town that the bells of the converter halls could be heard from where it stood. And the watcher would be facing, the locals noted without quite knowing why, toward the Old Works yard. Toward Old Grey.

The watchers had never faced the town before. The watchers faced the dawn, or the dusk, or the empty west. The watchers did not face the workings of humans, because the workings of humans were not, in the reckoning of the watchers, what the watchers were for. Whatever the watchers were for, it was older. It was larger. It was a thing that happened on the high hills at the edges of perception, and it did not concern itself with slag-carts and converters and the slow cartage of a single very old dust-eater.

But now, occasionally, a watcher faced inward. And the locals — who did not speak of the watchers except obliquely, in the way they spoke of weather — began, also without speaking of it, to feel that something had shifted in the order of the place. Not shifted. Adjusted. The way a load on a long bridge adjusts when the temperature drops, and the tension in the cables changes by a fraction, and the bridge does not move but the bridge is, after, a slightly different bridge.

The benevolent power did not speak to Old Grey. Old Grey did not have language. Old Grey did not have, in any sense the power could recognize as understanding, an interior life. Old Grey was, by the measures of the rest of the world, a dim creature — a digestion with legs, a slow mill, a metabolism made visible. But the power was wise enough to know that dimness is not the same as emptiness, and that the long patient accumulation of a life lived in the same key, on the same stone, in the same unhurried shuffle for what must have been, by any reckoning, an immense span of time, was not nothing. It was, in its own way, a kind of devotion. A kind of attention. A kind of prayer, if prayer is a thing done without knowing it is being done.

So the power did what the plateau does. It added, slowly, to what was already there.

It did not extend Old Grey's life. That would have been a violation. Old Grey had lived long enough that its life was, in some sense, already more than a life — it was a record, a stratum, a layer in the layering of the place. To extend it further would have been to make it into something other than what it was. So the power did not extend. What the power did was deepen.

The deepening was this: Old Grey's presence, in the inner yard, became, in the weeks and months after the attention arrived, slightly more present. Not larger. Not louder. Not more visible. But more there. The way a stone that has been standing in a particular spot for a hundred years is more there than a stone that has just been set down. Old Grey's shuffle, between the slag-trough and the gate, became a thing the locals oriented themselves by, in a way they had not been conscious of before. A new worker, arriving at the Old Works yard, would find, within a week, that they knew when to expect Old Grey's passing — the slow crunch of the internal stones, the soft pad of the wide hooves on the calcium-powder, the faint shimmer of bloom-dust in the guard-hairs — and the knowing was, without being spoken, a part of how the yard was timed.

The power did not add to Old Grey's body. It did not heal the small injuries that three or four human generations of cart-pulling had worked into Old Grey's shoulders. It did not ease the slow stiffness in Old Grey's joints. Those things were part of Old Grey's body. They were Old Grey's body. To remove them would have been to remove Old Grey's history.

But it added — and this was the thing that mattered, that was the whole of the act — to Old Grey's weight.

Not physical weight. Old Grey's body remained the body it was, heavy and slow and patient. The weight the power added was the weight of being noticed. The weight of being attended to. The weight of being, for the first time in however long Old Grey had been shuffling between the slag-trough and the gate, the specific subject of a kind and patient regard. And this weight, which was not a thing that could be measured on any scale the converters knew how to build, was, in the deeper register of the place, the most significant addition that had been made to Old Grey since Old Grey had first been harnessed, as a young animal, to the chest-yoke of dark iron-band leather.

Old Grey did not know. Old Grey was a dust-eater. Old Grey did not know things. But Old Grey knew, in the way that bodies know what is happening to them, that something was different. The slag tasted slightly different. The bloom under the muzzle was slightly richer. The wind at the yard gate, in the mornings, was slightly less cutting against the old stiff hide. The humans who tended the trough were, without knowing why, slightly more patient in the ladling. The other dust-eaters, in the yard, made way for Old Grey with a deference that was not deference in any conscious sense but was, in the way of herd creatures that have lived together for decades, a slightness of motion, a giving of space, an acknowledgment so quiet it was almost nothing.

And the bloom, in the path Old Grey took, thickened.

And the watchers, on the high hills, occasionally faced inward.

And the power, having done what it had come to do — which was very little, which was almost nothing, which was a kind of attention paid in the same key as the place itself — withdrew, or did not withdraw, or was always there, in the way the wind is always there, and the wind is not noticed most of the time, and the not-noticing is, in the end, the deepest kind of presence.

Old Grey is still pulling the slag-cart. Old Grey will pull it tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, for as long as Old Grey's body holds together, which is a span of time no one can predict because no one has ever watched a dust-eater this old finish. The townspeople do not know that anything has happened. They do not know that Old Grey has been noticed, or that the bloom has thickened on the path, or that the watchers have begun, very rarely, to face inward. They notice, in the way the locals notice things — slowly, obliquely, in the patient key of the place — that the bloom on the slag-heaps is, this season, finer than it was last season. They notice that Old Grey seems, in some indefinable way, more there. They do not ask why. They do not ask, in the way they do not ask about the watchers, or about the deep worms, or about why the wind blows from the west.

The plateau will go on. The plateau has been going on for longer than anything that lives on it, and it will go on after. The bloom will spread. The dust-eaters will graze. The birds will sing their single note from the wind-carved ridges, and the chord of it will layer into the same shimmering melancholy it has always layered into. The wind will hush. The converters will rust. The Old Works will outlast them. The watchers will stand on the high hills.

And Old Grey, in the inner yard, will pull the slag-cart, slowly, with the unhurried patience of a creature that has been here for as long as anyone can remember, and that has, without knowing it, been noticed by something that was wise enough to understand that noticing, in this place, was the only intervention that would not have been a violation.

The slow is enough.

The slow has always been enough.
