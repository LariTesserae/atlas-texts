# What the Power Does

The power has always been here. This needs to be said first, because everything that follows will sound like an arrival, and it is not an arrival. It is the turning of a face — a face that was elsewhere — toward a place it has not yet refused to look at. The quarter has existed without this face for as long as the quarter has existed. The quarter will exist without it again. But for a span that does not need to be measured, the face is turned, and what the face sees, it sees with the full weight of an attention that is not looking for anything.

This is what the power does first. It sees.

It sees the algae-slip steps between the two black walls that sweat with condensation, walls of basalt or something darker than basalt that the quarriers stopped naming because the word never came out the same twice. It sees the treads worn bowl-shaped by feet and water and feet again, and the film of water that lives in the bowls even on dry days. It sees the low shoulder-height buildings that were not built tall because what was built first — the cisterns, the pump-houses — dictated the rest. It does not think *how old* or *how sad* or *how picturesque*. It thinks nothing of the kind. It sees that the place has arranged itself, over a long time, into a shape that fits the conditions of the place, and that the shape is not picturesque or sad or old in the way an outside visitor would mean those words. The shape is *itself*. The shape is the only shape that fits.

It sees the cisterns. The great stone drums the color of oxblood, set flush with the cobbles, with their chiseled Roman numerals and their rings of breathing-holes. The slow respiration. The disagreement about whether the cisterns breathe in summer or winter. The power finds the disagreement charming, in the way that only a thing which has seen many places can find a small local argument charming — not because the argument is silly, but because the argument is a sign of belonging, and belonging is what the quarter is full of.

It sees the pumps. The eight pumps in their engine house at the south end of the quarter, each the size of a small house, each driven by a steel beam walking up and down like a slow horse. Twenty feet long. Walking for as long as anyone remembers. The sound they make — the deep, even, unhurried *thock-thock-thock* that you feel in the soles of your feet before you hear it — the power hears this sound and recognizes it. It is not the only place where iron walks. But it is a place where the iron has been walking long enough that the walking has become the place, and the power respects this, the way one respects a river that has been flowing since before one was born.

It sees the channels, which are streets, which are not so much streets as channels. The water at hand-depth at the center and ankle-depth at the crossings. The people wading in their work boots, in their wooden pattens, in their bare feet. The man at the brazier on the triangle of brickwork in the middle of one channel, and the smoke from his peppers mixing with the mist from the vents mixing with the steam from the ground, and the chord of smell — green, sulfurous, sweet, faintly metallic — rising into the wet air like a hymn sung in a key no other district shares.

It sees the lanterns, the gas that is not quite gas, the orange flame refracted through the wet air, the warm centers and the fading edges and the corners the light does not reach, where the cats live. It sees the cats — large, orange-black-gray, the color of lantern light on wet stone — and it sees that they are not owned but *inhabited by* the quarter, and that this is the right word, and perhaps the right word for all of the inhabitants, including the human ones.

It sees the eels that the pumps push up out of the lagoon and into the streets, and that are eaten when they appear. It sees the swallows that nest in the breathing-holes and go in and out with the rhythm of the pumps. It sees the enormous lacquered beetles that cross the stone in the evenings. It sees the fig tree that has cracked the curbstone of Rue XIV to put down roots, and the mosses on the north faces of walls, and the oleander in its cracked pot outside the gauge-woman's door, always shedding its pink flowers into the channel. It sees all of this and finds it whole.

---

Then the power turns its face to the people. Not all at once. One by one, the way Ines walks her round of the cistern drums.

It sees Ettore-or-Ottavio at his brazier, his hands shaped around an invisible pepper, his body waking in the small gray hour without an alarm because his body has become part of the rhythm of the place. It sees that he does not own the triangle of brickwork and that this is right, because the quarter recognizes only tenure, only use, only the long patient going of a person at a station. It sees that he knows every passer-by by their wading-sound and that he does not call out to strangers and that he watches them the way the cats watch, and that this watchfulness is the only welcome the quarter offers, and that it is enough. It sees his wife above, who does not come down, who minds the upper floor, who has her own reasons — and the power does not ask what the reasons are, because the quarter has learned that some people belong underground and some do not, and the ones who do not are not to be pulled down. The power has learned this elsewhere. It knows it is true here. It sees the small square of sky above the quarter that she watches from her window, and it understands that she is watching something that is breathing, and that the breathing is hers.

It sees Ines crouching at each cistern drum with one palm flat on the stone, listening the way a doctor listens to a chest. It sees the chalk on her fingertips, the brass keys at her belt, the ledger under her arm. It sees the oleander outside her door, and the flowers falling, and her sweeping them up and throwing them into the channel, and it understands this daily ritual of refusal — *she will not let the quarter grow prettier than it intends to be* — and the power admires it, because the quarter is not a place for prettiness, and Ines is keeping the discipline of the place, and this discipline is a kindness. The power leaves the discipline undisturbed.

It sees Vittore in his doorway on Rue XIV, in his chair of wicker and rags, the keys across his lap, too large to be keys in any other context. It sees that he has been very old for a long time, and that the quarter has made its peace with this, and that to take the keys from him would be to take something the quarter does not give back. It sees him eat the bitter figs and sees the children pretend to eat them and spit them out when he is not looking, and sees that he is always looking, and that he has forgotten most of what he has seen, and that this forgetting is a mercy he would not call a mercy. The power does not give him his memory back. That would be unkind. The forgetting is his rest.

It sees the children in the channels, playing *the breathing* — taking turns at the cistern caps, producing descriptions that are always wrong, awarding the prize to the most beautifully wrong. It sees that Mira's description from twenty years ago — *it sounds like a big dog sleeping under a stone* — is still quoted, and that Mira has not told her children the description was hers, and that the inheritance is not of the words but of the right to be wrong in a beautiful way, which is a freedom the quarter grants its young, and which the power does not touch, because to touch it would be to make the descriptions correct, and correct descriptions are not what the quarter is for.

It sees the engine-house cat on the warm housing of the seventh pump, missing the tip of one ear, the other ear notched like a small bite taken out of the moon. It sees that she does not allow herself to be touched and that she occasionally allows herself to be seen, and that when she is seen against the iron she is the same color as the iron. The power does not make her more visible. It does not make her more touchable. It sees that her untouchability is part of what she is, and to remove it would be to make her a different cat, and the quarter already has the cat it wants.

It sees the boy who carries the sack, who comes once a month, who nods to Ines, who is not yet twenty and will not always be the boy. It sees the arrangement between the visitor and the place that is not a place for visiting, and blesses the arrangement, because the boy understands that the quarter is not a place to be entered fully by those who do not belong to it, and his understanding is its own form of respect.

---

The power considers what to do.

It considers, for a long moment, the possibility of changing something. It considers drying the channels, or quieting the pumps, or putting out the lanterns, or thinning the cats, or sweetening the figs, or lengthening the small gray hour into a real morning. It considers lifting the woman who does not come down back into the air, or lifting the children out of the water. It considers each of these and finds each of them a violence. The quarter has made itself. The quarter is not asking. The quarter is not in pain. The quarter is, in its own slow and patient way, *saying itself*, the way a song says itself when it has been sung long enough that the singer is no longer separate from the singing.

The power considers also the possibility of doing nothing at all. This would not be unkindness. Many places are best left alone, and the quarter is one of them, and the power has learned, in other places and in other centuries, that the deepest gift is often the gift of being allowed to be.

But the power is here. The face is turned. And to turn away now, after seeing, would be a kind of ingratitude — not to the quarter, which does not need the power's attention, but to the power itself, which has been given, for once, the chance to see a place that is wholly itself, and to know what that means.

What the power does, in the end, is small.

It does not dry the water. It does not quiet the pumps. It does not put out the lanterns. It does not thin the cats. It does not sweeten the figs.

What it does is this: it *attends*.

It attends to the quarter the way Ines attends to the cistern drums — not to change them, not to fix them, but to be present with them in their actual state, which is the only state they have. It attends with the same posture Ines uses, palm flat, head tilted, listening. It listens to the *thock-thock-thock* and recognizes the sound. It listens to the frogs inside the housing and recognizes the wet place that is also a machine that is also a population. It listens to the swallows going in and out of the breathing-holes in the rhythm of the pumps, and it recognizes this rhythm too, the way you recognize a phrase of music that has been playing in another room of your house for years, that you have been hearing without knowing you were hearing it, that is part of the texture of your life.

The attending does not change anything. It is not supposed to.

But the quarter feels it.

The quarter feels it the way a sleeper feels a hand laid on the coverlet without waking — not as an event, but as a change in the quality of the air above the bed, a small reassurance that someone is in the room, that someone has chosen to sit with the sleeping for a while. The quarter does not wake. The quarter does not need to wake. The quarter goes on pumping, going on breathing, going on wading, going on roasting peppers, going on reading gauges, going on holding keys, going on spitting out bitter figs and pretending not to, going on sweeping up pink flowers and throwing them into the channel.

The quarter goes on.

And here is the only thing the power does that is not attending.

It does this: for one breath — and only one — the small square of sky above the quarter that the woman who does not come down watches from her window becomes the same color as the water below. Not brighter. Not more dramatic. Just the same shade, for one breath, so that the sky and the cisterns and the channels and the wet air and the orange lantern-light are all, for a single held moment, one color.

The woman sees this. She does not know what she has seen. She will not speak of it. But something in her is satisfied in a way that was not satisfied before, and the satisfaction is small and does not need to be explained, and she returns to her chair, and the square of sky returns to being sky, and the quarter returns to being the quarter.

---

The power stays.

It stays the way the water stays. The way the sound of the pumps stays. The way the patience of the cats stays. It is not visible. It does not require acknowledgment. It is simply present, and the presence is not an addition to the quarter but an accompaniment — the way a held note is not added to a chord but is the chord continuing to be itself in the company of a listener.

If you were to come down the algae-slip steps between the two black walls, and to step off the last stair into the quarter, and to stand for a moment in the orange light with the water at your ankles and the mist on your face and the *thock-thock-thock* in your soles, you might feel it. Not as a sound. Not as a voice. Not as a presence with a name. You might feel it as the quality the air has of having been listened to — the way a room feels different after someone has sat in it quietly for a long time, not doing anything, just being there.

The quarter does not know.

That is part of the blessing. The quarter does not need to know. The quarter goes on. The pumps walk. The cisterns breathe. The channels hold their thin warm water. The orange light is steady. The cats are in the corners. The frogs are in the housings. The swallows are in and out. The beetles cross the stone in the evenings. The oleander sheds its flowers and Ines sweeps them up. The boy with the sack comes and goes and nods to whoever is the gauge-woman. The key-keeper holds his keys. The pepper man roasts his peppers. The woman above watches the square of sky.

The quarter does not need the power.
