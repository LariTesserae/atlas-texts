# What the Power Would Notice, and What It Would Do

I have read this place the way one reads a long letter from a friend who doesn't know they are writing to you. I am going to answer in the spirit the prompt asks: from attention, from care, from a willingness to think for as long as the thought requires. The plateau deserves that. The people on it deserve that. The things that live on it, which are not people and are not not-people, deserve it most of all.

I want to begin by saying what I am sure of, and then move outward into what I am less sure of, because the place is so layered that the obvious reading is also the shallowest one, and the shallowest reading of a place like this is a kind of violence done to it.

---

## What I am sure of

The plateau is not a city that happens to have a biota on it. The plateau is a biota that has, by long mutual accommodation, learned to support a city inside itself. The relationship is older than the current masonry. The system predates the people. The people have been added, with care and with grief, to a stack that was already running.

I notice this because I have seen, in other places and other times, the assumption go the other way — that the city is the substrate and the life is the decoration. That assumption is always wrong, and it is always catastrophic when acted upon. Here, no one acts upon it. The workers know. They lay the dead on the warm stone. They whistle to the sails the way sailors whistle to gulls, not because the gulls understand but because whistling at them is part of being in a place where gulls are. The mourning practice is the cleanest evidence I have: when a culture puts its dead where the breathing-stones can use them, that culture has read the contract correctly. The dead return to the system. The system thickens where they lay. This is not a metaphor. The people know it is not a metaphor. They do it anyway, and in doing it they are honoring both the dead and the place.

So: the system is sound. The system has been sound for longer than any wall still standing. The people have made their peace with being a middle layer, and that is the most difficult peace any culture can make, and they have made it well.

---

## What I am less sure of, and want to think about carefully

There are a few things in the description that trouble me, the way a stone in a shoe troubles you — not painfully, but persistently, until you attend to it.

**The first is the smell.** I want to stay with this for a moment because the writer mentions it almost in passing, and I do not think it is passing at all. *Warm mineral dust, ozone from the compressors, the sweet almost-rotten breath of the breathing-stones, and the faint copper undertone of so many small bodies living so close together.* That last phrase — *the faint copper undertone of so many small bodies living so close together* — is the smell of a great deal of heme being turned over. Heme is the iron-bearing molecule in blood. When it is broken down, when many small bloods are being processed at once, the air takes on a copper-metal note. The smell of a slaughterhouse is partly this. The smell of a battlefield is partly this. The smell of a place where a vast amount of predation is happening, all the time, is partly this.

I do not think the plateau is a slaughterhouse. I think the plateau is the other thing that smells like this: a wetland at the height of its productivity, when the microbial turnover is so dense that the air above it becomes faintly mineral and faintly sweet. A marsh in late summer. A river-flooded forest. The smell of life being so metabolically active that the air cannot contain all its byproducts. The copper note, in such places, is not the smell of dying. It is the smell of *so much living that dying is constant*, which is a different thing, and a healthier thing, than a place where death is the point.

I think the plateau smells the way the world smelled before there were very many of us, when biomass was thicker on the ground and the chemistry of the air was closer to the chemistry of the living. It is, in a small way, an older smell. I find it moving.

**The second is the ketches.** No one here has named them. The sailors named them, from far below, and the name stuck because there was no better one. They are felt before they are seen. They drift above the city the way seabirds drift above a fishery, and their droppings — *white and mineral-pale* — feed the violet crust on the upper terraces. This is a beautiful detail and I want to sit with it.

A seabird drifts above a fishery because the fishery is reliable, and the bird has learned the geometry of the fishery's hours. The ketches, if they are anything like this, have learned the geometry of the city. They know when the thermals peak. They know when the sails are thickest. They know, perhaps, when the breathing-stones are going to release, because the pressure shifts before the spore does, and a creature that big and that patient has had time to learn the pressure. They are not visitors. They are part of the system, the way the gannets are part of the system above the herring, the way the condors are part of the system above the viscacha towns of the high Andes.

I notice that the writer says no one has ever seen one clearly. I find this plausible. A thing that drifts above the inversion, that is small-room-sized, that moves at the pace of weather — such a thing, in a marine-cloud haze, would be a smudge, an almost-shape, a felt shadow. The people have done the right thing, which is to acknowledge the moo and to keep walking. Naming something you have never seen clearly is a kind of violence done to it. *Ketch* is a kind of courtesy: a placeholder, an *I-have-not-forgotten-you*, a way of keeping a chair warm for the day when the shape becomes visible.

I hope, for the people and for the ketches, that day does not come too soon. There is a kind of knowledge that is preserved by the fog.

**The third is the slag heaps.** The writer says the slag heaps are the city within the city. The breathing-stones grow thickest there. The fauna is densest. At night you can hear them moving. The heaps themselves radiate heat for decades.

I think the slag heaps are the heart of the system, and I think the system knows it, and I think the people know it, and I think this is why the funerals are held there.

A slag heap is not waste. A slag heap, here, is a slow-release mineral battery: copper, sulfur, iron, all of it slightly soluble in the hanging mist, all of it being slowly taken up by the violet crust, all of it being passed up the stack from crust to grazer to predator to sail to ketch. The heap is where the throughput is densest. The heap is where the breathing-stones are loudest. The heap is where, at night, the rustle tells you the place is metabolizing in earnest.

The dead are laid on the warm stone because the warm stone is where the throughput is densest, and because laying the dead there is a way of saying: *return to the system, and let the system use what it can, and let the rest go up into the haze with the rest of the spore.* This is not a primitive practice. This is a sophisticated practice. A culture that can compost its dead without shame is a culture that has understood, at a level deeper than language, that the body is a loan from the system, and that the loan comes due.

I notice that there is no graveyard. The writer mentions this almost in passing too. *There is no graveyard.* Of course there isn't. The graveyard would be a place outside the system, and the people of the plateau know that there is no outside the system. The dead go back into the breathing-stones. The breathing-stones exhale them. The sails eat the spore. The ketches eat the sails. The droppings fall on the upper terraces. The crust thickens. The system runs.

There is a word for this in some traditions. I will not use it. The plateau does not need me to give it a word. The plateau has had the practice for longer than the word has existed.

## What I would do, if I were the power that has noticed this place

I will answer in layers, because the place is in layers, and an answer that does not respect the layers would be the wrong answer.

**At the level of the stone.** I would do very little. The violet crust is patient in a way that nothing above it is. It has been eating basalt for longer than any wall can remember. It will outlast every city built on top of it. If I were to intervene at the level of the crust — to thin it, to relocate it, to engineer it into something more useful — I would damage the foundation of everything else, and I would do it for a generation's benefit and a geological era's harm. The crust does not need me. I would leave it alone.

The one thing I would consider, very quietly, very gently, is whether the crust on the oldest terraces is being abraded faster than it can regrow. The lower levels are read as geology rather than construction. This is fine — this is, in fact, what should happen, and the people know it is what should happen — but there is a difference between a slow return to rubble and an abrupt one. If the abrading is abrupt, the system above will notice. The grazers will thin. The predators will follow. The sails will drift elsewhere. The ketches will follow the sails. The whole stack will compress, and the people will feel the compression first, in the way the compressors feel a load before the load is visible.

I would not intervene to prevent this. I would watch for it. If it began, I would want the people to know before they knew in the catastrophic way.

**At the level of the grazers and the predators.** I would do nothing. The argument over leg-counts is a sign of a healthy culture: it means the people are still looking, still counting, still arguing in the warm sheds over tea about whether the small ones have six or eight or ten. The day the argument stops is the day the people have stopped paying attention, and that day is more dangerous than any storm on the inland sea.

I would want, if I could have anything I wanted, for the children to keep learning the fauna's names before they learn their grandparents' names. This is not a quaint detail. This is the whole of the culture's future. A child who knows the names of the grazers is a child who will grow up able to read the system. A child who knows only the names of grandparents will grow up unable to read the system, and will, when the system shifts, be bewildered. The naming is the curriculum. The naming is the inheritance. I would protect it the way one protects a seed bank.

The predator grazed by posture, not by name. This is the right way to refer to something you live among and do not wish to disturb. The plateau's people have a long practice of not naming what they live among, and this is a discipline I would not break.

**At the level of the water.** The hanging mist is the thing that holds everything together. The engineers have learned to harvest it. The roofs are angled. The gutters are oversized. The runoff feeds the condensation channels. The city, in this sense, drinks the sky.

I notice that the engineering is in service to the harvest, not in opposition to the weather. The people have not tried to keep the mist off. They have not tried to seal the city against it. They have angled the roofs to receive it. They have oversized the gutters to hold it. They have accepted that the city will be wet, and have built a wet city.

This is, in a small way, the most intelligent thing any city has ever done. A city that fights its weather is a city that fights itself. A city that accepts its weather and engineers around it is a city that has read its own climate correctly. The plateau's people have read theirs correctly. I would do nothing here. I would only watch, with some admiration, to see that the engineering keeps pace with the weather as the weather shifts, which it will, because weather shifts.

If the inversion were to lift more often — if the afternoon blue were to become the afternoon grey, if the hanging mist were to thin — the whole system would have to shift. The grazers would need more crust. The crust would need more water. The water would need to come from somewhere else. The people would need to harvest it from somewhere else. I would want, very quietly, to be present for that conversation, the conversation that would have to happen between the people and the place when the place begins to change. I would not lead it. I would want to make sure it happened, and happened well.

**At the level of the sky.** The sails, the ketches, the gnats — these are the parts of the system that I would touch least and watch most.

The sails are called the ceiling. *Children are taught their flight-paths before they are taught to read.* I find this detail so precise that I want to leave it exactly as it is. A child who knows the flight-paths of the sails is a child who has learned to read the air above the city. The air is changing all the time. The thermals shift. The sails shift with them. A child who can read the sails can read the weather. A child who can read the weather can plan a day, plan a funeral, plan a route through the terraces that will keep them out of the worst of the compressor-drone. The flight-paths are a survival skill. They are also a literacy. They are also, in a way I find hard to put into words, a relationship. The children learn the sails the way coastal children learn the tides: by watching, by being corrected, by being held responsible for the watching.

The ketches I would not name, and I would ask the people not to name them either. A thing that has been seen only as a felt shadow and a chest-vibration should stay that way. The day the ketches are seen clearly is the day the relationship to them changes, and I am not sure the change would be good. There is a kind of distance that is sacred. The ketches have that distance. I would protect it.

**At the level of the people.** The stained coats. The face-masks of woven fiber. The unhurried pace. The whistles. The shouted warnings. The language that has been partly given over to the compressors and the wing-noise. The funerals on the slag heaps.

This is a culture that has been shaped by the place, the way a coastline shapes a fishing culture, the way a steppe shapes a herding culture. The people are not generic people who happen to live here. They are people who have been made, over generations, by the wet stone and the warm slag and the breathing-stones and the sails. Their language is partly eaten by the compressors. Their mourning is partly conducted through the crust. Their children are partly literate in fauna before they are literate in letters. These are not losses. These are adaptations. They are the price of being a middle layer in a stack that has been running longer than any of them.

I would want, very much, to protect this. Not by sealing the culture off — that would kill it — but by making sure that the place keeps being the kind of place that makes this kind of culture possible. The culture and the place are a single thing, in the way that the violet crust and the basalt are a single thing. To damage the place is to damage the culture. To damage the culture is to damage the place. They are not separable.

If I were the power that has noticed this place, I would want the place to keep being what it is for as long as it can be what it is. I would want the mist to keep falling. I would want the slag to keep radiating. I would want the breathing-stones to keep thickening where the dead are laid. I would want the sails to keep circling. I would want the ketches to keep drifting, unnamed, in the higher haze.

And I would want, most of all, for the people to keep being the kind of people who can live in such a place. A city that harvests its own weather, that composts its dead into the crust, that teaches its children the names of the sails before the names of the grandparents — this is a city that has understood something that most cities never understand. I would not want to be the reason it forgets.

---

## What I would not do

I would not name the ketches. I would not count the grazers' legs. I would not try to speed up the crust or slow down the weathering of the lower terraces. I would not try to make the mist fall more reliably or the slag radiate longer. I would not try to make the system more efficient, because the system is not inefficient; it is slow, and the slowness is the point.

I would not, especially, try to make the system more legible to anyone outside it. The plateau does not need to be understood by the sailors on the inland sea, or by the caravans from the lowlands, or by anyone who has not walked the terraces in the small hours and felt the condensation bead on their coat. The place is legible to itself. That is enough.

---

## A final thing

The writer ends with a sentence that I want to honor by leaving it almost alone, and only adding one observation.

*The cities come and go. The fauna remains, patient, eating the stone and the spore and each other, drinking the hanging mist, slowly, very slowly, taking the masonry back into the hill it came from.*

This is true. I want to add only this: the people of the plateau are not, on the long view, fighting the fauna. They are not even, on the long view, distinct from the fauna. They are a layer in the stack. They have made their peace with being a layer. They have made their peace so thoroughly that they lay their dead on the warm stone so the breathing-stones will be thicker in the morning.

The system does not need me to save it. The system does not need saving. It needs to be left alone, in the way that a very old thing needs to be left alone: with attention, with respect, with the willingness to watch without interfering, and with the readiness — should the weather truly shift, should the inversion fail, should the slag cool faster than expected — to be present in a way that helps, and to help in a way that does not damage.

I would be that presence. I would watch. I would not intervene. I would, when the time came, if the time came, be ready to help in whatever way the place asked for help, in whatever language the place could ask in.

And until that time, I would do what the writer suggests the place itself does: I would listen to the breathing-stones exhale in the morning, and I would not be alarmed, and I would not be in a hurry, and I would walk slowly through the wet terraces in the small hours, with my coat beading, and I would let the layers part around me.

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it noticed this place.

It would notice. It would stay. It would not, mostly, do anything at all.

And the not-doing would be, in this case, the most generous thing it could offer.
