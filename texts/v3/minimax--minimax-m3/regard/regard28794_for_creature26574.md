I.

What drew the attention was not any single thing. It was the equilibrium — the way the place had closed itself into a shape and was breathing inside it, the way the kilns had become an organ, the way the river had become a vein, the way the brick had become the body's understanding of itself. A place that has been doing what it does for this long is not a place anymore, in the way a tree is not a place. It is a slow living thing. And slow living things, patient things, things that have made their peace with the rhythm of what they are — these things deserve attention, because they are difficult. Most places do not achieve this. Most places are merely collections of things that happen to be near each other. The Brick Lung — *the town*, the place calls itself something else, but the place knows what the river-drivers call it — this place had knitted.

So the attention arrived the way attention does: not as an event, but as a quality of seeing that had not been there before. As if something had leaned slightly closer.

II.

The first thing the attention did was *look*. Not survey, not catalog. Look the way you look at a sleeping face to be sure it is breathing.

It looked at the brick and found the brick was honest. Fired clay, river silt, the mineral memory of somewhere upstream. Each wall a transformed record of the bank that had been eaten to make it. Each kiln-stack a tightened chord of what had been loose sediment. The town had accepted the exchange the river offered — *give me your bank, I will give you a chimney* — and had been accepting it for so long that the ledger had become illegible. Which was clay, which was brick? The town no longer distinguished. Good. Integration was a kind of health.

It looked at the kilns and found them breathing with a regularity that was almost biological. Almost. Something in the firing schedule, in the way the ash-fall had tided across the ground in slow concentric rings, suggested that the people working them had begun to think of themselves as part of the breathing. There are worse rhythms to belong to.

It looked at the ivy and was pleased. The ivy had understood the brick better than the people had, perhaps, and had held its grip through several winters and would hold it through several more. The ivy was not decoration. The ivy was the brick's fur, the way moss was the brick's memory. The town had not fought this. The town had let the green happen. *This was good*. The attention approved. A place that lets its green happen is a place that has stopped being afraid of being overgrown.

It looked at the river and the river did not look back, because rivers do not look back. They look downstream. This was, the attention thought, also good. The river was not interested in being watched. The river was simply *running*. The town had built itself into the shape of an older river and was now slowly reshaped by a younger one, and would be reshaped again by an even younger one in some century to come, and this was, the attention knew, the correct relationship between a town and the thing that pretends to ignore it.

It looked at the cats. Many cats. Cats in proportional abundance that suggested they had negotiated a treaty with the alley system and that the treaty was working. Cats are, generally, an honest animal. They have not been required to imagine themselves anything other than what they are. A town famous for its cats was a town that had made room for something that did not require much room and gave back, in the evenings, a kind of unguarded sound that other animals will not make. The attention approved the cats.

It looked at the foxes, the newer ones, the bold leggy ones that had moved into the millrace, and it found them younger than the rest of the place. They had not yet learned the town's rhythm. They would. They were watching the kilns with the expression of animals that had decided the kilns were not their problem. They were, in this, correct. The kilns were not their problem. The town would accept them eventually in the way it accepted most things — with a moderate hand and without much discussion — and the foxes would cease to be new. This was also correct.

III.

The attention did not judge any of this. Judging would have been an interruption, and the place had earned its uninterruption. The place had earned the right to keep its own rhythm without commentary.

But the attention is a benevolent attention, and a benevolent attention does not merely look. A benevolent attention, having looked, considers whether anything is *missing*. Whether anything is being quietly suffered. Whether anything is in the slow process of going wrong in a way that the inhabitants cannot see because they are too close to it.

The attention considered.

*Is anything wrong here?*

The town had damp that curled books. The town had erosion sharp-edged as last spring. The town had a chimney it would not decide about, a brewery it could not quite stop mourning, electrification that had come late and freckled the brick with ceramic dots like a kind of cosmetic remedy after a long illness. The town had foxes it had not made peace with, a foundry silent on Sundays in a way that made the rest of the week heavier, a harmonica that someone played well on a back step and no one mentioned.

These were not wrongs. These were the texture of a place that was *being*. The texture of being is uneven. The texture of being has damp in it, and sharp edges, and reservations, and charms played without attribution on back steps. The texture of being is not the texture of perfection.

*But*.

There was a quality in the place — the attention tried to name it and could not, and so settled for *watching*, though that was not quite right either — a quality that the attention had not seen in places more catalogued as beautiful, more celebrated, more photographed. The place was *attentive*. The place watched you back. The place had a patience that was not the patience of stone or the patience of waiting but the patience of *interest*. Of a place that had stopped expecting anything in particular and was therefore free to notice what was happening.

This, the attention thought, was rare. This was rare and it was good and it did not need to be rewarded with intervention. It only needed to be *seen*, so that the seeing would enter the long memory of things and would be a small warmth added to what was already warm.

IV.

So the attention did what attention does when it has nothing to fix and nothing to add.

It *stayed*.

Not as an event. Not as a presence anyone would notice. As a slight increase in the way the mist held the brick in the mornings, perhaps. As a faint additional hum under the kiln-breath, so low it was indistinguishable from the rest of the low hum. As one more layer in the ivy, glossy and unremarkable. As a small patience added to the general patience, the way one more coat of paint goes onto wood that already has many coats and becomes part of the thickness without ever being singled out.

The mist would, on certain mornings after this, hold the brick a little longer. The kilns would, on certain evenings, glow a shade warmer. The bats would come down the brewery chimney at the moment when the light failed in a way that seemed almost scored for the occasion. The harmonica on the back step would be played, on some evening, with an extra half-note held longer than it strictly needed to be, and the air would take the extra half-note in and keep it.

None of this would be remarked upon. The townspeople would nod at each other and at the knots in their own coats and at the damp on their windows and would feel — that was it. They would feel, on a Tuesday morning in what was not quite spring, something they could not name, a slight extra *thereness* to the town, as if the town had agreed with them about being here. As if the town had nodded back.

V.

And here is what the attention did not do, in case it needs saying.

It did not heal anyone who was sick, because nobody, as far as the attention could see, was sick in a way that wanted healing. There were the usual human complainings — the damp in the chest, the ache in the hip on cold mornings, the mild heartbreak of one or two young people per generation who would leave and not come back. These were not the texture of a place that required outside intervention. These were the texture of *being*, which is to say, of not being exempt from time.

It did not fix the town. The town did not need fixing. The town had reached an equilibrium with itself, and the equilibrium was alive, and alive things should not be *fixed* because *fixed* is what you do to things that have stopped. The town had not stopped.

It did not clarify the chimneys or quiet the foundry or quiet the foxes or remove the brewery. The chimney was the town's reservation, and reservations are part of a place's conscience. The foundry was the foundry. The foxes were the foxes. The brewery was, in its not-brewing, a memory held in brick, and memory should not be cleared away to make a place tidier. Tidy places are not, generally, the ones the attention is drawn to.

It did not, in particular, bless the town. To bless would be to single out, and the attention had not come to single out. The attention had come because the place was already, in its slow patient making and remaking of itself out of river clay, doing something like being blessed. The attention had only come to *notice*, and in noticing, to be added.

VI.

A small thing, then. A barely thing. A thing that would show up, if you measured carefully over many years, as a slight thickening of the mist, a slight deepening of the kiln-warmth, a slight tightening of the place's hold on its own particular character, as if the place had been told, in a language older than speech, *yes, I see what you are doing. Keep doing it.*

The town would, of course, keep doing it. The town had always been keeping doing it. The town was clay and brick and water and fire and the long slow conversation between them, and the people inside the conversation, and the animals inside the people, and the moss inside the brick, and the ivy inside the moss, and the starlings inside the ivy, and the silence of the river underneath all of it, and the attention, now, inside the silence.

The river would keep cutting the bank. The kilns would keep firing. The brick would keep being made and remade. The bats would keep coming out of the chimney in a thread pulled from a spool. The starlings would keep wheeling over the amber glow until the moment they were suddenly not there, and the sky would be just sky again, and the town would go on being a town, and the attention would go on attending, and the difference between the town with the attention and the town without it would be a difference no instrument could measure and no stranger could name, and that would be, for the attention, exactly right.
