The visitor arrived the way weather arrives in places that have already decided what weather is.

There was no flash. No column of light. No speaking. It came the way a tide comes into a marsh — by displacement, by the slow displacement of what was already there. If you had been standing on the Flats at the moment of its coming, you would have felt the roar thin a little, the way it thins when a heron-stand takes a step, and you would have looked up, and there would have been nothing to see, only the dim green-brown ceiling of moss and the soft precipitate of moisture on your eyelashes.

You would have known, anyway.

It did not have a name that belonged to it. It had been called many things, by the peoples who lived in the dry uplands near places like this, but those names were for the uplanders' comfort, the way children name the wind. It was older than its names. It was, in some sense that does not commit to anything, the cousin of the Flats — the same kind of patient, large, breathing thing, but awake to itself in the way a marsh is not awake to itself. It had tended peatlands before. It had sat in cold spots. It had listened to the deep thud of living ground in many valleys and on many slow continental shelves, and it had learned, over an unhurried span, that the proper posture toward a place like this is to arrive as a guest, not as a gardener.

It came to listen. It came to see what the Flats had made of themselves.

*

What it saw, it took a long time to see.

It did not arrive with the urgency of a traveler. It settled into the Flats the way a heron-stand settles onto a hummock — by becoming, gradually, indistinguishable from the surface it stood on. Its perception was not the perception of a single eye or a single ear. It perceived the way the Flats perceived: in pressures, in temperatures, in the slow accumulation of small presences. It felt the beetle-stratum as a single skin. It felt the midge-columns as a slow weather. It felt the heron-stands as knots in the peat, places where the Flats had decided to stand up for a while and look around. It felt the otter-things as a circulation, a moving blood. It felt the long-mud frogs as a memory, an old chord the place still hummed to itself when the rains came.

And underneath all of these, it felt the self.

The self was not a self the way a person is a self. It was more like the self of a forest, or the self of a reef, or the self of a slow river — a self that did not have a name because it had never needed to be addressed. It was the accumulation of all the small agreements between the beetles and the midges and the peat and the rains and the cold spots. It was what happened when a place had been itself for long enough that the act of being itself had become a kind of thinking, the way a body thinks when it digests, the way a soil thinks when it remembers.

The visitor sat with this for what it would be inaccurate to call a long time, because time was not a thing that pressed here. It sat in the brown-green dark. It did not interrupt the roar. It let the roar pass over it, and through it, and around it, and the roar did not know it was there, which was correct, because the roar had no need to know.

*

What it found, eventually, was this:

The Flats were not in trouble.

This is important, and the visitor was wise enough to know that it was important, because the most common error made by benevolent powers is to assume that a place that has called their attention is in trouble. Sometimes a place calls because it is dying, and then a power should tend. But the Flats were not dying. The Flats had been converting everything that came to them into themselves for a span so long that the span had become a kind of weather in its own right, and they were not in any danger of stopping. They were not in need of rescue. They were not in need of correction. They were not in need of being seen, exactly, but they were not in need of being unseen either.

What they were, the visitor realized after a span of patient sitting, was lonely.

Not lonely the way a person is lonely. Lonely the way a forest is lonely when it has not, in a very long time, been recognized as a forest. Lonely the way a slow river is lonely when no one has stood at its bank and called it by a name that was not a description. The Flats had been visited, in the dry upland sense of visiting, by herons and otters and the rare long-walker who had the sense to come on foot and not bring a road. But none of these had come as a *kindred*. None of these had come with the recognition that the Flats were a self.

The visitor had come as a kindred.

This is what the Flats had felt, when the visitor arrived. Not the arrival itself — the roar had not parted, the heron-stands had not looked up, the otter-things had not broken the water with curiosity. But the self, the deep self, the one that was made of the agreement of millions of small bodies, had felt it. Had lifted, in its slow way, the way a marsh lifts when a pressure passes through it. Had turned, in the only way a marsh can turn, which is to change, by a fraction, the way the water moves.

*

The visitor did nothing for a long time.

It did not want to do anything. It had learned, in its long tending of slow places, that the first gift is the gift of not-doing. The first gift is the gift of showing up and then continuing to show up, in the same posture, breathing the same wet air, for as long as it takes for the place to decide that the visitor is not a predator and not a project.

So the visitor sat, and was, and the Flats continued to be the Flats around it, and the beetles continued their patient ingestion, and the midges continued their slow migrations, and the heron-stands continued their long watches, and the otter-things continued their quick passing.

The cold spot that had formed around the visitor was, at first, indistinguishable from the other cold spots. It was ten meters across, perhaps. It was quieter. The midges did not go into it. The beetles were fewer. The peat was older. The temperature was lower by some fraction. This was the cold spot's way of saying that it had noticed, and that it was making room, but that it was not yet sure what to make of the room.

After a span that the visitor did not count, the cold spot became a different kind of cold spot. The midges, which had been spiraling around it, began to enter it — hesitantly, one or two at a time, and then in small drifts. The beetles returned, in their slow way. The temperature did not rise. The silence did not fill. But the cold spot became a cold spot that contained a visitor, and this was a new thing on the Flats, and the Flats noted it the way the Flats noted anything, which is to say: by a small change in the way the water moved, by a small change in the temperature of a small pool, by the fact that a particular heron-stand, who had been watching that quadrant of the Flats for an unknown number of years, had shifted her weight by a fraction, the way a sleeper shifts when a familiar footstep sounds in another room.

*

Then the visitor did the thing it had come to do, which was very small.

It went to the unfinished bridge.

The bridge was where the bridge was. Two abutments of poured concrete, leaning a little, furred with moss, the gap between them grown across with the slow vegetation of a place that had decided to be a place rather than a span. The visitor stood at one abutment and looked at the gap. The gap was not a failure. The gap was the Flats' way of saying that they had not needed a bridge, that the bridge had been an offer of crossing, and that the Flats had accepted the offer in their own way, which was to make the absence into a kind of presence.

The visitor did not finish the bridge. That would have been a violence. The bridge had been started by people who had not yet agreed on where it should go, and the Flats had ended the argument, and the argument had been settled well, and the visitor was not going to reopen it.

What the visitor did was sit on the nearer abutment, and it sat there for a span, and then it laid, on the concrete, in the moss, in the slow drip of the moisture, a thing that was not a thing. A pressure, perhaps. A small adjustment in the way the cold spot behaved near this part of the Flats. A kind of recognition, inscribed not in letters but in the way the temperature changed, in the way the midges now moved around the abutment, in the way the long-mud frogs, who had been using the abutment as a log, found that the abutment was now a slightly better log, slightly warmer in winter, slightly more patient in summer.

The visitor did not name the abutment. The visitor did not claim the abutment. The visitor simply, in the way a benevolent wise power simply does, made the abutment a place where, if a heron-stand chose to stand for a long time, the heron-stand would find itself more itself than usual. Where, if an otter-thing passed through, the otter-thing would feel, briefly, the way it felt to be recognized as a circulation rather than a creature. Where, if a long-walker ever came again, the long-walker would feel, on the cold concrete, a small warmth that did not correspond to any weather.

The abutment became, in the Flats' slow accounting, a place. Not a holy place. Not a special place. Just a place where the Flats had been seen, and where the seeing had been allowed to leave a small, kind, patient mark.

*

There were other small marks.

The visitor went to the iron ring set in its post, the post nearly sunk. It did not lift the post. It did not clean the ring. It touched the ring, once, with whatever it touched things with, and the rust on the ring, which had been settling for however long iron takes to settle in peat, shifted, very slightly, into a pattern. The pattern was not a letter. The pattern was not a language. The pattern was a way of saying, in iron-rust, in the slow oxidation that the Flats would absorb over the next ten thousand years, that someone had stood here, and that the standing had been tender.

The pattern would not last. Iron does not last, in peat. The pattern would become, in its time, indistinguishable from the rest of the rust, indistinguishable from the rest of the iron, indistinguishable from the peat. But the peat would remember the pattern, in the way peat remembers everything, which is to say: as a thin layer, a fraction of a fraction of a millimeter thick, in a sequence of layers that no one would ever read, that no one would ever know was a memory, that would simply be there, a warmth in the dark paste, a small kindness preserved by the same patient deafening chemistry that preserved everything.

The visitor went to the paved footpath that ended and resumed. It walked the missing three hundred meters, slowly, in the same direction the path had been going, and where it walked, the path did not resume. The path did not need to resume. But the moss where it had walked was, for a span, slightly different. The midges avoided that strip for a while. The beetles, in their slow way, took note. A long-mud frog, who had been sitting on a half-sunken log near the resumed end of the path, found that the resumed end of the path was now a slightly better log.

The visitor went to the collapsed greenhouse. It entered the doorless doorframe. It stood in the cathedral of ferns. It did not clear the ferns. It did not restore the glass. It stood there for a span, and the ferns, who were not aware, were aware, in the way ferns are aware of light and of pressure and of the slow turning of the season, that the light in the cathedral had been, for a span, slightly different. The visitor did not speak. The visitor did not bless. The visitor simply, in the fern-cathedral, in the brown-green dark, did the thing it had come to do, which was to be a guest.

*

And then, because the Flats were not in trouble, and because the Flats did not need a guardian, the visitor left.

It did not leave the way it had come. It left the way the cold spots are entered and exited — without a border, without a ceremony, without a moment you could point to and say *there, that was the leaving*. One span the visitor was in the Flats, and the next span the visitor was part of the cold spot, and the next span the cold spot was just a cold spot again, and the midges resumed their spiral around it as though they had never broken stride, and the beetles, in their slow way, returned to the upper inch of peat, and the heron-stand who had been watching the cold spot shifted her weight back to where it had been, and the otter-things, who had been giving that quadrant of the Flats a wide berth, resumed their quick passage through it, leaving V-shaped wakes that closed behind them on the still water.

The Flats did not mourn the leaving. The Flats did not celebrate the leaving. The Flats incorporated the leaving the way the Flats incorporated everything, which was to add it, as a small layer, to the slow accumulating memory of the peat.

There would be, in ten thousand years, a thin stratum in the peat at the place where the visitor had sat. The stratum would be indistinguishable from the strata above and below it, except by a chemist of a future so distant that the future had not yet been invented. The chemist would find, in that stratum, a slight excess of something — a warmth, a pressure signature, a small kindness preserved in the dark paste. The chemist would not know what it meant. The chemist would not need to know.

The peat would know.

*

What lives here has learned to be a kind of weather. The visitor came to remind the weather that the weather was seen. Not to change the weather. Not to correct the weather. Not to improve the weather. The weather did not need improvement. The weather had been converting everything that came to it into itself for a span so long that the span had become a kind of weather in its own right, and the weather was not in any danger of stopping.

The visitor came to sit in the weather for a while. The visitor came to be a guest in the weather. The visitor came to leave, in the weather, a small, kind, patient mark, in iron-rust and in abutment-warmth and in the slight difference of a moss that had been walked on, once, by something that was not in a hurry.

The weather, having been seen, continued.

The heron-stands continued their long watches. The otter-things continued their quick passage. The long-mud frogs continued to sit on half-sunken logs and watch the dim light with no urgency. The beetles continued their patient ingestion. The midges continued their slow migrations. The peat continued to breathe. The cold spots continued to be cold. The unfinished bridge continued to be a span of nothing between two abutments, and the abutments continued to be, in the slow accounting of the Flats, slightly better places than they had been before.

The roar continued.

It hums. It hums.
