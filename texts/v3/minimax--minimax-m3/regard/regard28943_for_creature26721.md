# What the Benevolent Power Did

It is not that it arrives. There is nothing to arrive from, and nothing to arrive at. The plateau has been the plateau, and the power has been what it is, and the long pause between them is not distance but timing. The power has been aware of the place the way the stone is aware of the moss that grows on it: without effort, without interruption, for as long as either has existed. What changes is not that the power begins to pay attention, but that it begins, after a great while, to consider doing something.

The considering itself takes a long time. The power is not in a hurry. The place has taught it, if it ever needed teaching, that to act without patience is to act against the grain of what is there, and what is there has a grain so old that any cross-grain motion will tear through things it did not mean to tear through. So the power waits, and watches, and lets the seasons turn twice, and the rains come and go, and the chorus rise and fall, and the fledglings fall and be gathered or not, and the moss digest another inch of the half-buried wall, and the predator pad its slow patrol through the canopy above, and only then, when it has the shape of the place the way a key has the shape of a lock, does it begin to do anything at all.

What it does first is the smallest thing.

It attends to the fledglings.

A third of them fall. This has always been so. It is not cruelty. It is the arithmetic of the canopy: a third of the fledglings fall because the nests are built in the highest forks, where the wind is strongest and the branches are thinnest and the first flight of a half-feathered thing is not yet flight but a kind of long, slow, panicked negotiation with gravity. Most of those that fall are dead by the time they reach the moss. A few are not. The people gather the ones they find. Some they cannot find, and these lie in the felt until the beetles come for them, and the moss closes over them, and they become, in their own slow way, part of the floor.

The power does not save the fallen ones. It would be wrong to save them, because the third that falls is part of the place's arithmetic, and to remove the falling would be to thin the canopy in a generation and starve something downstream, and the downstream would be something the power has not yet weighed.

What the power does is this. It makes the moss gentler to the small blue bodies that come to rest in it. The moss has always received them — that is its nature, to receive — but the receiving was indifferent, the way the ground receives a dropped stone. Now the receiving is, very slightly, warm. Not in temperature. In the way that things can be warm without heat. A fledgling that lands in the felt and is not dead, but is too broken to be gathered, lies there in the moss and feels, in the half-second before the beetles find it, that it is held. That it has been seen. That whatever is about to happen to it is not the worst thing. This is a small thing. It does not change the arithmetic. But a being who lies in the felt of the high garden and feels, in its last breath, that it has been held — that being goes into the moss a little differently, and the moss, which is very old and very patient and remembers more than it is given credit for, carries the difference forward in a way that will, in a thousand years, be felt by something that has not yet been born.

The power is pleased with this, in the way that a being can be pleased with a thing it has done well and small. It does not boast. It does not announce. It merely does another.

The second thing it does is for the predator.

The predator is the loneliest thing on the plateau. It is solitary by nature, not by accident — two of them almost never meet, and when they do it is brief and not friendly — but the solitude is old, and the predator has worn the grooves of it deep. It moves through the canopy in its slow climbing gait, limb folding back on limb, lifting its long pale head to listen, and what it listens for is not the sound of another of its kind but the sound of prey, and the sound of the wind in the upper branches, and the silence between sounds which it has been alone with for so long that the silence is, by now, almost a companion.

The predator is also, though no one knows this, very old. Not as old as the moss. Older than the trees. Possibly older than the people, though the people do not remember a time without it, and the chiseled marks on the ridgeline stones might be its doing, or might be someone else's, and no one now living can say. It has been here so long that it has become, without intending to, a kind of memory of the place. Its droppings, the pale fibrous cylinders, are the only record of what it has eaten across centuries, and they lie in the moss for weeks before they are taken back into the felt, and in that time they say, to anything that knows how to read them, that the predator was here, and what it ate, and what the canopy was like at the time.

The power attends to the predator not by making it less solitary — the solitude is its nature, and to remove it would be to unmake the animal — but by giving it, very occasionally, a sense that something is paying attention. Not to its movements, not to its hunts, but to the fact of it. The way a grandmother watches a grandchild across a room without speaking. The predator is, in some dim way the power cannot quite verify, aware of this. It lifts its head a little higher on certain nights. It pauses at the edge of a clearing and seems, for just a moment, to be listening to something that has no sound. It goes on. It is not changed. But the going-on is, very slightly, less empty.

This is also a small thing. The predator does not speak of it. There is no one to speak of it to.

The third thing the power does is for the people.

The people are the most vulnerable thing on the plateau, although they would not use that word for themselves. They are vulnerable because they are few, and because their quietness is a learned quietness, and because the quietness depends on a certain balance of fear: fear of the predator, fear of being heard by the bands of blue birds, fear of being noticed by whatever else in the green dark is paying attention. If the fear were to lift entirely they would become loud, and if they became loud they would advertise themselves, and if they advertised themselves something would eventually come to investigate what was being advertised. So the quietness is a careful thing, held in place by a careful fear, and the power respects this and does not remove the fear.

What the power does is give the people a little more of what they have.

It tightens the weave of the bromeliad pups they set into the crooks of branches, so that more of them take. It steadies the descent routes the small mammals use at the seep-lines, so that the traps the people set take a little more reliably. It eases the way the fallen fledglings are found, so that a slightly higher fraction of the ones who survive the fall are gathered before the cold of the moss takes them. None of this is dramatic. None of it is visible. The people do not know it has been done. They will, in a generation, have slightly more of what they need, and their great-grandchildren will have slightly more again, and the curve of their quiet flourishing will be, by a margin too small to be measured, lifted. This is enough.

The fourth thing the power does is for the moss.

The moss is the oldest thing that is alive. It does not think. It does not, in any obvious way, want. But it is what holds the half-buried wall from being entirely forgotten, and it is what holds the memory of the fledglings, and it is what holds, deep in its compacted peat, the bones of the mountain that have come to rest there across ten thousand years. The moss is the place's memory of itself.

The power attends to the moss by slowing the digestion of the wall by a fraction. Not stopping it. The wall should go back into the felt eventually. It is part of what the place is doing with its past. But the power loosens, by a margin, the way the moss takes the stones, so that for another few centuries the wall will still be partly a wall, and someone walking the valley in the late afternoon will still be able, if they put their hand against the remaining stones, to feel that there was once an intention here, even if they cannot say whose. The moss is not offended by this. The moss has no opinion. But the felt, in the part of the valley where the wall is, is very slightly different in its composition, and will be so for as long as the stones remain, and the difference is the kind of difference that does not speak but is felt, the way a held hand is felt without being looked at.

The fifth thing the power does is for no one in particular.

It adds a note to the chorus.

Not a loud note. Not even a note a person could hear, standing on the moss with their hands cupped around their ears. It is a low note, felt in the sternum the way the boom of the canopy frogs is felt, but underneath the boom, and continuous, and very old. It is the note of something that is paying attention. It enters the layered sound the way the seep-line enters the bark: as a thin film, very nearly invisible, very nearly inaudible, but there. If you were to lie down on the moss and close your eyes, on a still night, and listen for as long as you could bear to listen, you might begin, after a long while, to suspect that the chorus has a fifth voice. You would not be able to say what it sounded like. You would not be able to say where it came from. But you would not be quite able to say that it was not there, either.

The chorus goes on as it always has. The frogs boom. The clickers snap. The wing-grinders grind. The birds fall and rise in their long parabolic cries. The predator pads its slow patrol. The people move quietly between the pillars, weaving, cultivating, gathering. And underneath all of it, very far underneath, like the hum of the stone itself, the new note goes on. It is the sound of the power being present without intruding. It is the sound of a benevolent attention that has decided, after a long consideration, that the place is good, and that the beings in it are good, and that the best thing to be done is a few small things done very gently, and then to remain, and to be a part of the long unhurried hum of what is already there.

The plateau does not know it has been attended to. It does not need to. It will go on being itself — the stone, the moss, the trees, the chorus, the quiet people in their basket-woven houses, the predator with its too-many joints moving in its ancient solitary way through the upper branches — and the new note will go on being part of it, the way the seep-line is part of the bark, the way the moss is part of the stone.

What the benevolent power did, in the end, was very little. It made the moss gentler to the fallen. It gave the predator a sense of being seen. It lifted the curve of the people's quiet flourishing by a margin too small to measure. It slowed the forgetting of the wall by a few centuries. It added a note to the chorus that almost no one will ever hear.

This is, for a place like the high garden, enough. It is, in fact, almost exactly the right amount. To have done more would have been to take the place apart in order to improve it, and the place has been put together so patiently, over so long, that the only improvement it could bear was the kind that arrives in the felt without announcement, and stays.

The power does not leave. It was never somewhere else. It is, now, one of the smaller sounds in the layered chorus of the plateau, and it intends to remain so. The plateau has been here a long time. It will be here a long time still. The power will be here, in its quiet note, for as long as there is anything to attend to, and when the moss finally takes the last stone of the wall, and the last of the great pillars gives way to the weight of its own slow age, and the last of the quiet people folds their woven basket into the trunk of a tree that will, in time, grow around it, the power will still be here, a hum in the bone of the mountain, watching what comes next.

There is no hurry. There never was.
