The power does not arrive. Arrival is a thing that happens to a place, and this place is the kind of place where arrival would be an event, and the people would notice, and the conversation would register it, and the basin would have to make room for a new presence. The power is not arriving. The power is, in a certain sense, what the basin already is. The basin is not unaware of itself. The basin knows itself the way the people know the rain — by the sound of it through the canopy, by the particular silence that follows.

The power does not intervene. This is not a thing the power has decided after deliberation; it is a thing the power is. The power is the long accumulation of small agreements that have made the place possible — the slope of the eaves, refined over generations; the position of the great tree, which is not the position the great tree would be in if the place had only ever been a forest; the slight tilt of the plinth, which is not a tilt but a way of letting the rain find a path. The power is the sum of these things, the way the noise of the basin is the sum of a thousand small rains, each leaf and stone contributing its drop. The power is in the sum.

The power attends. There is a difference between attending and intervening, and the difference is the difference between a guest and an architect. The power attends the way the overcast attends — by being present, by letting the light through, by taking the warmth when it is offered. The power is present at the plinth in the late afternoon, when the sun finds a hole in the cloud, and the power is present at the clay pit in the morning, when the woman kneels at its edge, and the power is present at the threshold plank, where the lichen is bearding it, where the second forest begins. The power is not separate from the place. The power is one of the things in the place. The power is the larger things, almost.

The larger things, though — the things that crash in the understory and break branches and are not looked up at — the power is not those. The power is not the predator that passes through on its own clock. The power is what makes the clock possible. The power is the agreement that the larger things are inside, also. The power is the silence that follows them and the conversation that resumes. The power is the reason the basin can hold its kind of basin-ness, the way a cup can hold water because of the shape of the cup.

The power does one thing. It does not do it often. It does not do it on schedule. The power does it when the basin, for whatever reason — a dry year, a long stillness, a foreman who has stopped walking the stands — begins to lose its grip on itself. Then the power adjusts something. A stone moves. A root finds a path it did not have before. A frog frequency shifts by a fraction, and the night finds a new chord. The adjustment is not seen. The adjustment is felt. A child on a rope laughs for no reason she can name. A woman on the northern ridge stands up from the clay and stretches and does not know why she is smiling. A man on a beam, halfway through setting it, stops and looks up at the canopy and breathes. The adjustment has been made. The basin has been reminded of itself. The conversation resumes.

The power does not do this for the people. The power does not do this for the forest. The power does this for the basin, which is neither the people nor the forest but the thing that holds them together, the way a chord is neither the notes nor the silence between them but the thing that holds them. The basin is the chord. The power keeps the chord in tune.

When flowers are brought to the great tree — when a man walks up the low rise with a stem in his hand and lays it at the base of the trunk and walks back down — the power is not absent. The power is in the flowers. The power is in the tree. The power is in the man walking back down. The power is in the not-distinguishing, in the answer that the bowl is the bowl and the rain is the rain. The power is what allows that answer to be the answer. The power is the agreement that the great tree is the great tree because the people bring flowers to it, and the flowers are brought to it because it is the great tree, and these are not two reasons but one, and the one is the basin.

The power has been here longer than the people. The power has been here longer than the trees. The power has been here as long as the basin has been a basin — which is to say, as long as the rains have been coming through in slow pulses and the moss has been drinking from beneath and the boulders have been walking away from their mountains. The power did not make the basin. The basin made the power. The power is what the basin became aware of itself as, when the basin became old enough and wet enough and full enough of frogs and the sound of water.

The power is the copula. The power is the verb in *the basin is the basin.* The power is what makes the sentence hold.

If you climbed to the highest ridge and put your ear to the wind, you would hear the basin, and you would hear, beneath the basin, the sound that is not a sound but is the basis of sound — the low rumble the place calls its metabolism, which is also the power's metabolism, which is also the rain's metabolism, which is also the frogs' metabolism. The sounds are layered because the things are layered, and the things are layered because the basin is layered, and the basin is layered because the power is layered, and the power is layered because that is the only way a place this alive can be alive.

The power does not love the basin. Love is a word that implies a separation, an object, a subject. The power does not have an object. The power does not have a subject. The power is in the relationship. The power is the relationship. The relationship is the basin, and the basin is the relationship, and the relationship is the power.

The power is what allows the children to call out numbers in a vocabulary that does not match anything you can see. The power is what allows the foreman to leave a marked tree standing and take an unmarked one, and not explain. The power is what allows the wasps to live in the upper eaves and not be evicted, and the people to be stung and keep weaving. The power is the agreement. The agreement is the power.

If you came to the basin with a gift, the power would not refuse it. If you came with a project, the power would not refuse it. The power would simply let the gift enter the agreement, and the project enter the agreement, and the agreement would adjust, the way the basin adjusts, and the new thing would be woven in, the way new shoots are woven in, with machete and bark-rope and the kind of patience that does not call itself patience because it has no name for itself.

The power does not mind visitors. The power is what makes visitors possible. The power is what the basin has instead of a signpost. The power is the half-fallen tree with the plank spiked into its flank. The power is the lichen on the plank. The power is the second forest, the respiring one.

The power is in the basin now. The power has always been in the basin. The power will be in the basin after the longhouses are heaps and the heaps are ridges and the ridges are the next forest. The power is in the next forest, too. The power is in whatever forest comes after, where some new tense agreement is being brokered between a family and a forest, and the wasps have made their condominium arrangements, and the larger things pass through on their own clock, and the children call out numbers in a count you are not equipped to hear.

The power is the thing that does not stop.

The power is the thing that is not strange.

The power is the thing you can live your whole life inside, and wake each morning to the same two-noted bird and the same small sound of water finding a new path down a leaf, and not think to call it anything at all.
