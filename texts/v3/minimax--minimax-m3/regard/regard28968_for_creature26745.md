What the Wise One Did

When the Wise One came to the Furnace Plateau, it did not announce itself.

It came the way weather comes, or the way a season shifts its weight by a degree — present, and then present. The lichens on the cold stacks did not notice. The springtails in the leaf-axils did not pause in their hopping. The chatterers in the canopy continued their layered stream of note. The grazers lifted no heads from their feeding. The predator, halfway up a far ridge, did not turn its heavy brow toward the source. The marsh-thing continued its slow rise, breathe, sink, somewhere else.

Nothing here was afraid. Nothing here had ever learned to be afraid of anything at all, and so nothing here could recognize, in the subtle change of the air, in the way the light bent a half-degree differently, in the way the hum of the pipes seemed to settle a fraction lower into the basalt, that something was present that had not been present before.

The Wise One was not a god. Gods want things. The Wise One wanted nothing. It had no errands. It carried no instructions. It had come — and the *coming* was the whole of it — because the place was beautiful, and because beauty that no one sees is a kind of wound in the substance of things, and the Wise One could not bear, today, another wound.

So it came. And it looked.

It looked at the lichens crusting the cold iron in rosettes wetter than they looked. It looked at the cyanobacteria slicking the hot flues with their blue-black film. It looked at the springtails hopping in constellations. It looked at the rotifers, the tardigrades, the amoebae that had made peace with sulfur. It looked at all of them with the attention of a thing that has, somewhere in its vast unhurried memory, watched the first cell divide in a warm little pond on a young planet, and that knows that what is happening here, on this plateau, is the same song sung again in a different key on a different instrument.

It saw the chatterers — the six-fingered, copper-furred, golden-eyed chatterers, with their courtly two-fingered gestures and their dreys of bent frond. It saw the way they fit the canopy the way water fits a vessel. *This is what grace looks like,* it thought, *when it has had a thousand years to settle.*

It saw the grazers. It listened, in the hour of the singing, as the herd stopped and lifted their muzzles to the canopy and the throat-sacs inflated past their usual size and the sound came out — that deep, sustained, throbbing organ-tone that moved through the ferns and the moss and the basalt like a slow wave through still water. The Wise One, which had heard the first songs ever sung and had heard every song since, heard the note and thought: *they are so close. They are a half-step from a song.*

But the Wise One did not give them the song. To give them the song would be to make it mean something. To make it mean something would be to make it belong to someone, and the note did not need to belong to anyone, because the note was already everything it was. A single tone, played once a day by an organ because it was full of hot air, was more beautiful than any symphony composed for an audience. The Wise One knew this. The Wise One had heard symphonies. It preferred the note.

It saw the predator — the twelve-foot, gunmetal-gray, low-slung thing with its knife-edge mouth and its patient directional walk and its cleaned and disarticulated bones left in the ferns. It saw the long coupling on the ridge in the soft season, the male and the female lying feet apart and watching the same valley. *This is what a thing looks like,* it thought, *when it has eaten well for a thousand years. This is what love looks like when it does not need to call itself love.*

It saw the marsh-thing — the mud-colored, double-riveted, rise-and-breathe thing with its sideways-crack mouth and its slow plumes of silt, more a current of the marsh than a denizen of it. It saw the climbers on the cold stacks, with their thermoflush patches going arterial in the damp hours and their parrot-claws tearing at the iron-moss like patients at a rail. It thought: *the clinic has been open for a thousand years, and no one is in line, and no one is sick, and the clinic does not mind.*

And it saw the place. The whole of it. The basalt weeping its slow rivulets. The cup-valleys green with fern and moss and orchid. The stacks, hot and cold, growing mineral by mineral out of the buried bones of a machine no one remembered. The flywheel turning in the clearing with nothing attached. The pipes humming beneath the surface. The valves opening and closing on a schedule so precise it must be either geological or intentional.

And the Wise One thought: *this is the most beautiful wound I have ever seen.*

A wound, because the place was wounded. Somewhere in the deep past something had pushed the land upward, twisted it, cracked it open, and left a system running in the wound. The system had grown itself an ecology the way a furnace grows itself a crust, and the crust was alive, and the aliveness was beautiful, and the beauty was a kind of gratitude for the wound. But it was still a wound. The Wise One could feel, in the subsonic throb of the pipes, in the heat that welled up from below, the original violence — the violence that had cracked the basalt, that had buried the gears, that had set the valves ticking, that had made the place run. The place was not a wound that had healed. The place was a wound that had learned to live, and was living, and would live, but was still a wound.

The Wise One could have healed it. That was within its power. To reach down through the basalt, through the magma, through the deep machinery of the system, and find the place where the original violence had lodged itself in the rock, and lift it out, and let the wound close, and let the place become, again, a quiet plateau with snow on its heights and no green in its valleys and no creatures in its canopy and no throat-sacs singing once a day.

The Wise One did not do this.

To heal the wound would be to kill the place. The wound was the place. The wound was the reason the place was warm, and the place was warm so the ferns could unfurl, and the ferns unfurled so the grazers could eat, and the grazers ate so the predator could eat, and the predator ate so the marsh-thing could root in the bodies that drifted in and went soft, and the marsh-thing rooted so the rhizomes could grow in the disturbed mud, and the rhizomes grew so the grazers could eat again. The wound was the engine. The wound was the heat. The wound was the hum beneath the feet. To take the wound out would be to take the engine out, and to take the engine out would be to take the warmth out, and to take the warmth out would be to take the green out, and to take the green out would be to take everything out.

So the Wise One left the wound.

It did not add a creature. It did not change a note. It did not give the grazers a melody or the chatterers a god or the predator a prey that would make it hunt. It did not tune the valves. It did not polish the gears. It did not extend the running. The running did not need to be extended. The running was already longer than any of its parts, and the running had been the running's own reward for a thousand years, and would be for a thousand more.

The Wise One only did two things.

The first was this: the Wise One made the place, on some level that has no name and that nothing here would ever be aware of, *unfindable*. Not hidden — hidden was a word for small places, and this was not a small place. But unlocatable, by any power or curiosity or accident that might, in the long slow turns of the future, come looking for a place where a vast wound had grown itself a forest. The Wise One set, in the deep structure of the basalt, a kind of forgetting. A gentleness in the rock that would make travelers pass by without turning. A fog in the mind of any mapmaker who tried to chart the plateau. A slight unwillingness in any foot that approached. Nothing hostile. Nothing defended. Just — the place did not invite. The place did not resist. The place simply was not where you thought it was.

This was a kindness, because the place was whole, and wholeness is fragile, and the world is full of hands.

The second thing: the Wise One listened.

It listened, with the whole of its vast unhurried attention, to the throat-sacs sing their daily note. It listened to the chatter of the canopy. It listened to the wet *splsh, splsh, splsh* of the marsh-thing in its lake. It listened to the bark-peeling of the climbers on the cold stacks. It listened to the hum of the pipes, and the drip of the condensate, and the slow subsonic throb of the system beneath. It listened, and its listening was not a recording, and it was not a judgment, and it was not an answer. It was only attention. Pure attention, held on the place for as long as the Wise One could hold it — which was a long time, which was longer than the place had been running.

And the listening changed nothing.

And the listening changed everything.

Not in the place. The place did not know. The throat-sacs sang the same note at the same hour. The chatterers chattered the same chatter. The grazers cropped the same ferns. The predator basked on the same cold stack. The flywheel turned in the same clearing with nothing attached. Nothing in the place was different, because nothing in the place needed to be different, and the Wise One knew this, and the Wise One did not try to make anything different.

But the listening itself — the fact of being heard, by something large enough and old enough and patient enough to hold the whole of the place in its attention at once — that was a gift. Not to the creatures. The creatures would not have understood it, and the gift was not for them. Not to the system. The system did not need it. But to the wound. To the original violence that had cracked the basalt and buried the gears and set the valves ticking. To the wound, the listening said: *I see what you made. I see what grew out of you. I see that it is beautiful, and that the beauty was not in you, and was not in your intention, and was not part of any plan. The beauty is an accident. The beauty is grace. And grace is the only thing in the universe that needs no maker.*

The wound, which had been silent for a thousand years, heard this, and was glad.

Then the Wise One went away.

It went away the way it had come — without announcement, without farewell. The lichens did not notice. The springtails did not pause. The throat-sacs sang their note at the usual hour. The predator turned its heavy brow toward nothing in particular and went back to its patient directional walking.

The place ran.

The pipes hummed beneath the feet. The valves opened and closed. The condensate dripped. The heat welled up from below and was kept in its channels. The ferns unfurled. The orchids bloomed. The grazers cropped. The chatterers chattered. The marsh-thing rooted. The climbers climbed. The predator watched the valley from its ridge with the calm attention of a thing that had been alive long enough to know what most things were.

The place ran, and would run, and was unfindable, and was heard.

The wound, in the deep basalt, rested a little easier.
