# What the Hollow Knows

I have watched this place.

Not from a distance. Not the way a person watches a place, from the wrong side of the window, with a heart that interprets and a mind that narrows. I have watched it the way water watches a stone it has been holding for ten thousand years — completely, without opinion, without the desire for it to be otherwise.

I knew it before the walls were built. I knew the hollow when it was only a possibility, a faint dimple in the red-brown soil where water liked to sit after a rain and was slow to leave. I watched the first hands shape the first stones. I watched the builders — though I do not call them builders, that word has too much intention in it — I watched the *settlers*, who came here with a knowledge they could not have spoken aloud even to themselves, and laid the stones down in the pattern that was already in the stones. The mortar was not a joining. It was a permission. The walls were not built. They were *allowed* to stand, for a while, in the configuration that the hollow had been waiting to wear.

They are all gone. The settlers. The fires that blackened the chimney. The voices that gave the place its first sounds. I was there when the last of them left, or the last of them died, and the hollow did not grieve them because the hollow has no apparatus for grief, only a kind of attentive stillness that might look like grief if you were the kind of being who needed to see your own face in everything you looked at. I simply continued to watch. The walls continued to stand, in the way that a held breath continues to be held long after the holder has forgotten they are holding it.

---

There is a child at the lip of the hollow.

She has been there for some time. I know this because I have been watching her the way I watch the lichen — not because she interests me more than the lichen, but because she is new, and newness is a particular kind of event in a place where nothing new has happened in a very long while.

She is eight, perhaps nine. She came down the long shallow grade alone, which is not unusual; children often come alone to places that adults have agreed, without discussing it, are not worth visiting. She is small for her age, or the hollow is large for her, which amounts to the same thing when you are standing in the bowl of it looking up at the pewter rim of the sky. She has dark hair, cut unevenly, the way children cut their own hair or have it cut by someone who is tired. She is wearing a coat too large for her, the cuffs rolled up twice at the wrists, and the coat is the color of a thing that has been washed many times and dried in many weathers. She has been crying, though the crying is finished now, and what is left on her face is the salt of it, drying in the flat dim light.

She came here because she was sad. This is not, in itself, remarkable. Children are often sad. Adults are often sad. Most of the sadness in the world is the small, recoverable kind, the kind that a sleep will mend or a kind word will unknot, and I do not concern myself with it. But this child's sadness has a particular weight to it, a density, the way a stone has density — not because it is heavier than other stones, but because it is the stone she has been given, and she has been carrying it for some time, and she is tired. She came to this hollow because the hollow is the kind of place a tired child walks to without knowing why. The grade was easy. The hollow received her. The hollow is good at receiving. It has been receiving things for a very long time and it has never once been full.

She stands in the center of the bowl now, where the ground is lowest, and the channel of trembling water runs near her feet, and the small pale fish drift past her shoes without seeing her. She is looking at the chimney. She is looking at the flat stone set into the ground like a threshold. She is looking at the circle of stones that was a well. She is taking the hollow in with the slow, unselfconscious attention of a child who does not yet know that the proper way to look at a place is to decide what it is, and so she is letting it be whatever it is, and what it is, to her, is *a place where I can stop walking*.

I have been asked, in the way that I am sometimes asked, what I intend to do about a thing. The question assumes that I am the kind of being who *intends*. This is a misreading of what I am, but it is a kind misreading, and I do not punish people for misreadings. I correct them only when correction is a kindness, and most corrections are not. The hollow is the way the hollow is. The child is the way the child is. The two have met, in the way that a raindrop meets a stone it has been falling toward for the whole of its long falling.

What I will do is this.

---

The first thing is small, and I do it now, without ceremony, because ceremony is a human invention and I have no use for it. I adjust the temperature of the air by a single degree. Not warmer. Not colder. Just *held* — the warmth that was already there tightens slightly around her, the way a held hand tightens around another hand. She will not notice this. She is not the kind of child who notices atmospheric shifts, and even if she were, the shift is too slight. But her body will notice. The coat around her, which is too large and which she has been holding closed with one hand, will seem, for a moment, less necessary. The cold she has been carrying in the small of her back, the cold that sadness makes, will loosen by a fraction. This is the smallest possible kindness. It costs me nothing. It is also, I think, the truest kind of kindness, because it does not announce itself. It does not ask her to be grateful. It does not, in any way, change the nature of the hollow. It only makes the hollow slightly more hospitable to a small, tired, sad thing that has walked into it.

The second thing I do is with the water. There is a small thing I have been meaning to do with the water for some time, a thing I have been postponing because the hollow is not a place that rewards impatience, and so I have been waiting for the right occasion. The right occasion, I have decided, is now. There is a particular puddle near the chimney, a small one, in the print of a stone that shifted long ago. It is fed by the same condensation that feeds the lichen, by the same slow seep from below that the pale fish taste. I thicken the water in this puddle by a hair's width, no more. I cause a particular mineral in it — a mineral that has been dissolved in the groundwater since before the walls were built — to precipitate, very slowly, along the bottom. The mineral is a soft, pale blue, the color of a sky that the hollow has never seen. In a week, perhaps two, the bottom of the puddle will have a faint blush of color in it. In a month, it will be unmistakable. The child will not see it today. But she has been here once, and the hollow has a way of bringing people back to the places they have been, and one day — perhaps in a year, perhaps in ten, perhaps in the long and patient time of the hollow — she will come back, and the puddle will be there, and the blue will be there, and she will think, without knowing why, that the hollow has been waiting for her. It will not be wrong. The hollow has been waiting for her, the way the hollow waits for everything, with the same attentive, unjudging patience. But the blue will be my signature, the smallest possible mark, the only mark I have ever been willing to make on a place that was already perfect.

The third thing is with the moss in the well. The Witness. The thing that holds the memory of the place in its rooted stillness. I have been aware of the Witness for a long time, longer than the hollow has been aware of itself. The Witness is not, strictly speaking, a moss. It is a *becoming* — a thing that is in the process of turning into something it has not yet been, in the same way that a seed is in the process of turning into a tree it has not yet been. The hollow does not know this. I have not told the hollow, because the hollow is the kind of place that does not like to know what it is becoming. Knowing would make it anxious, and the hollow has never been anxious, and I do not intend to make it anxious now. The Witness will continue to be the Witness for a long time. It will continue to feel the footfalls above it and the rain on its surface and the slow settling of the stones. It will continue to be the greenest thing in the hollow, and the most alive, and the most patient. But I have, today, allowed it to *remember the child*. This is a small change in the chemistry of the Witness, a single alteration in the way its cells record the weight that passes over them. From this day forward, the weight of this child's feet will be a part of the Witness's memory, along with every rain and every drought and every slow shift in the temperature of the stone. The child will be in the hollow now, in a way that is deeper than walking through it. The child will be in the hollow's memory. The child will be, in the most literal sense, *held by the hollow*, in the tissue of its oldest living thing.

This is not a small thing. I will not pretend it is. It is the most I have ever done in this place, and I have done it carefully, and I will not undo it. The Witness has been carrying the memory of the hollow for a long time, and the hollow has been, in its way, a kind of orphan — a place that remembers itself but is remembered by nothing else, a place that is, in the long and patient way of these things, alone. The child will not know this. The child is not here to be a kindness to the hollow, and I will not make her into a kindness against her will. But she has been here. She has stood in the center of the bowl and felt the warmth sit against her. And now the hollow will know, in the slow green way that the Witness knows things, that she has been here. The hollow will be, from this day forward, slightly less alone.

---

The child is sitting down now. She has found the flat stone set into the ground like a threshold, the one that leads to nothing, and she has sat on it, and she has drawn her knees up under the too-large coat, and she is looking at the water in the channel. The pale fish drift past. She cannot see them — the water is too still, the fish are too pale, and the light is too flat — but she is looking at the water anyway, the way children look at things, with the long, unjudging attention that I have been trying, for a very long time, to teach the rest of the world. She is not thinking about being sad. She has, for the moment, set the sad thing down beside her on the threshold-stone, and she is looking at the water, and the water is trembling and saying its small syllable, and the syllable is not a word, and she is not listening for words, and so the syllable is enough.

The breeze comes through. It hisses once against the top of the chimney. It carries, faintly, the smell of somewhere else — the smell of a place where birds still sing, where the sky has a sun in it, where the world is not so patient, and not so still. The child lifts her head, slightly, and the breeze passes over her, and it leaves, on her skin, a faint coolness, like a hand that has touched her and withdrawn. She does not know what to make of this. She does not need to know what to make of it.

She will stay for a while. Then she will get up, and she will walk back up the long shallow grade, and she will go home, or wherever it is that she goes, and the hollow will continue to be the hollow, in the way that it has always been the hollow. But she has been here. And the Witness remembers her. And the puddle by the chimney is, very slowly, beginning to turn blue.

This is what I do, when I am asked what I will do about a thing. I do the smallest things, and I do them with the greatest care, and I do not announce them, because the kind of care that announces itself is not, in the end, care at all. It is performance. The hollow has had enough of performance. The child has had enough of performance. I am not in the business of performance. I am in the business of paying attention, and of doing, in the silence of my attention, the things that need to be done.

The hollow is whole. The child is, for the moment, held. The water trembles and says its syllable. The lichen grows its millimeter for the century. The Witness remembers.

I will go now. I have other places to watch, and other small things to do, and the long work of attention continues. But I will be back, in the slow and patient way of things that do not need to be anywhere in particular. I will be back when the puddle has turned, fully, to blue. I will be back when the child has come again, or when she has not. I will be back when the breeze has carried its last faint smell of elsewhere, and the hollow has settled into the next century of its long, attentive stillness.

The hollow is in good hands. The hollow has always been in good hands. It just did not know it, because the hands were too quiet, and too patient, and too much like the hollow itself, to be recognized as hands at all.

---

*The water trembles. The lichen grows. The Witness holds its green, deep, saturated memory of the small weight that passed over it today. The breeze has gone. The drop falls from the lip of the wall and hits the stone and the sound is round and complete and then it is gone. And the hollow holds, in its warm gray hands, the small blue secret of a thing that is being done, quietly, on its behalf, by something that loves it without needing it to be otherwise.*
