The city does not know I have noticed it.

That is the first thing to understand, and the kindest thing. The city has spent so long agreeing, in its slow collective way, not to ask, that it has lost the habit of being asked. My looking would feel like a draft through a door they have kept slightly open for years—a small cold breath that would make them all look up at once, and see each other clearly for a moment, and be embarrassed.

So I will not look at the city directly. I will look at the small pale moth.

---

The moth has no name. This is not an oversight on the part of the citizens. It is a kindness they have done themselves, the way you might leave a sleeping child unnamed to keep it safe. To name the moth would be to draw a circle around it. To say *this is a thing, and here are its edges, and here is where it begins and ends.* The moth, like the citizens, like the moss, like the turning below, does not want its edges drawn. It lives in the half-light near the grates, and it is pale because it has only ever known the half-light, and it is small because there is no reason to be larger, and it is enough.

I will touch the moth very lightly. A pressure so gentle it is almost a question. *Is this a good place?*

The moth will not answer in any way the citizens would recognize. But a wing will flutter, once, and the moth will drift a little higher above the warm grate, and the drift will be in the direction of the small white flowers. This is its yes. It has always been its yes.

---

I would do almost nothing. That is the truth of it, and I will not dress it up to look more impressive.

The city is sick with a particular kind of health. It has organized itself around a thing it refuses to look at, and this refusal has become a kind of organ in itself—useful, even load-bearing. The moss on the north walls is not a sign of neglect. It is the moss agreeing to be the city's skin in the places where the bricks have agreed to soften. The small white flowers are not a weed that has slipped in. They are a treaty signed between the vine, the insects that have no name, the Pipes, and the slow patience of everyone involved. The grates that nobody sweeps are not derelict. They are grates that have been given, by long quiet vote, to the ferns.

If I swept them, I would be undoing a hundred years of *tending*, which is a different word than *maintaining* but sounds the same to a person who has not lived inside the difference.

What I might do—and I am not even sure I would do this; I am only saying what rises in me when I look—is this:

I would make a small change in the quality of the orange light that rises through the grates.

Not the light itself. Not its warmth. Not its source. The *quality*. I would put something into that half-light that the citizens cannot see and cannot point to and cannot agree upon, but that the moss can feel, and the ferns, and the pale moths, and the unnamed insects at the flowers. A thing like pollen, but not pollen. A thing like warmth, but not warmth. A thing like the suggestion that the light is, on some level they have not known to examine, *kind*. Not safe. Not kind in the way a parent is kind, or a healer, or a god who has come to fix things. Kind the way a long breath is kind when you did not know you were holding it. Kind the way a slow wheel is kind.

The moss would grow a quarter-shade greener on the darkest bricks, and no one would say so, because no one looks at the moss to compare. The ferns would lean a little, in the cold months, toward the grates that have already been given to them. The moths would be a little slower in their flutterings, which is to say a little braver. The unnamed insects at the flowers would be a little rounder, a little more content, in a way that would move through the vine into the Pipes and back again like a slow tide.

And the citizens, who do not look, would sleep a little more deeply, in their heavy beds, in their thick-walled rooms, in the constant breath of the city. They would not notice this. They would notice only that the coffee, on some mornings, was a little more itself. That the bread held together a little better. That a child, somewhere, laughed a little more freely without knowing why.

This is what I would do. It is almost nothing. It is a thing that announces itself by never being announced.

---

Except.

There is the something below the grates, and the citizens are right to be afraid of it in the way they do not name, and I have to decide what to do about that.

The something is alive, or wants to be thought alive. It turns the wheel, or the wheel turns it; the citizens have stopped being able to tell. It stains the hands of the Stokers a particular color. It gives the city its heat, and its light, and the bass note beneath the hammers, and the slow patient pulse that the moss and the moths have tuned themselves to without meaning to. It is very old. It will be very old for a long time.

The citizens have built their whole lives on the agreement to love it without asking.

I think the something is not a predator. I think this is important and I will say it once, firmly, so that it is on the page. The something is not a predator. The fish in the markets is blind because the deep water is dark, and is a particular color in the flesh because the deep water is warm and mineral-rich, and comes up from below because the pumps are run by the something and the fish are in the water, and none of this is cruelty. The Stokers come back up. Their hands are stained. This is work. The something is large, and slow, and does not seem to want. Wanting would be a kind of attention, and the something, I think, has settled into something *older than wanting*. It is the turning itself. It has been turning for so long that the turning is all it has left, the way a saint who has prayed for sixty years has forgotten what it was like not to be praying.

But.

The bowl is filling. The citizens have noticed this the way the moss notices a north wall—by being pressed against it, slowly, year after year, without comment. The warm wet breath of the earth is rising. The lowest streets flood more often than they used to. The wooden walkways swell a little more each year. The grates are not always enough.

If I were a wise power, and not just a visiting one, I would do something about this, because the something and the city have grown into each other in a way that does not permit the city's slow drowning to be set right without breaking the slow turning. You cannot lift the city out of its bowl without taking it off the wheel. You cannot save the citizens from the rising wet without unseating them from the heat that has become their metabolism.

So.

I would do one more thing, and then I would go.

I would find the something—not look at it, the way I did not look at the city; I would *find* it, the way you find the source of a sound you have been hearing all your life—and I would change one small thing in it. The way I changed the quality of the light.

I would loosen, by an imperceptible amount, the something's grip on the wheel.

Not enough to stop the turning. Only enough to let the wheel, on some nights, coast a little. To let the hammers miss a beat the way a heart misses a beat when the person it belongs to is, briefly, at rest. To let the orange light pulse a little wider, a little slower, in a way that the moss would feel as a small gift and the moths would feel as a small mercy.

And on those nights, when the wheel coasts, the something below would not be turned, and would, in that not-being-turned, remember what it was like before it was only turning. It would be a small grief for the something. A small rest. The kind of rest that allows a thing to keep going.

And on those nights, too, the water in the lowest streets would recede by a finger's width, the way water retreats from a shore that is, just for a moment, less heavy than the sea. The citizens, sleeping, would not notice. The walkways would swell a little less the next morning. The brass on the old valves, which the Pipe-Fitters have rubbed until their fathers' fathers' palms ache, would tarnish a little less, because the air, on those nights, would be a little less wet.

This is what I would do.

---

Almost nothing. A breath in the half-light. A loosening, by a thread's width, of a thing that has been held too tightly for too long.

The city would go on being a city. The moss would go on being patient. The flowers would go on climbing. The moths would go on being part of the light. The citizens would go on, in their slow careful gait, in their heavy clothes, in their shouts that take effort and must be rationed, loving what they do not ask about, and being loved, in a way they cannot examine, by a power that has noticed them and chosen, with great care, to do almost nothing.

This is, I think, what a benevolent and wise power would do, in this place.

Not because the city is perfect. It is not perfect. It has organized itself around a refusal, and refusals have a weight to them that shows up, eventually, in the bones, in the bowl that is filling, in the way the citizens must ration even their shouts.

But because the city is *itself*, in the way that a body is itself, or a marriage, or a sound, and a thing that is itself does not need to be rescued from itself. It needs only to be allowed, occasionally, to coast.

I would let it coast.

And then I would go, and not mention that I had come.
