What arrives at the High Bowl is not a person. What arrives is not a god. What arrives is something the size of attention — of long attention — and it has the quality of a thing that has always been benevolent the way water has always been wet: not by choice, not by effort, by nature. It is wise in the way a thing that has watched the slow things for a very long time is wise: the watching has become a kind of knowledge, and the knowledge has become a kind of love, and the two are now indistinguishable.

It notices the bowl the way you notice a place you have been before, in a dream, in childhood, in the long middle of an afternoon. The noticing has the quality of arrival without motion. Something has come to rest on the cirque the way a heron comes to rest on a lake — with the only sound the small sound of water accepting weight.

It has not come from far. It has come from elsewhere within its own keeping. There is much that asks to be attended to; the power attends to most of it, but it does not attend to all of it at once, and so the bowl has been waiting, the way a held thing waits, and the power has been elsewhere, the way a held power waits, and now the turn has come around. The bowl is the next thing. The bowl is this thing. The power settles into it the way a hand settles into a glove it has worn before.

It perceives the cirque from within first, because it is already within. Or because it has always been within, in the way the air is within a bell, in the way patience is within the patient. The pale walls are above, throwing the light back. The floor is green and damp underfoot and the springy grass gives its faint sweet smell, and the smell is something the power reads without reading, the way you read the smell of a kitchen you grew up in.

It perceives the cairns.

It reads the cairns the way it reads alignments, the way it reads a face. Three stones, knee-high, at the western foot of the cliff, and another smaller, near a wall. They were chosen. They were carried. They were set down with patience. The power sees, in the cairns, the people who made them — not as ghosts, not as presences, but as a pressure still in the stones, the way a foot is still in a sand long after the foot has gone. The people came up here on foot, and not often, and they understood, the way people understand things without being able to say what they have understood, that the place required marking. The cairns point. The cairns still point. The cairns will point for a long time.

It perceives the shelter.

The shelter is low and rough and its roof is gone and a small animal lives in it now. The shelter has its back to the dark stone. This is the universal posture. This is the posture of someone who knows a thing is behind them and would rather not be facing it; it is the posture of someone who has decided to live near the thing anyway. The shelter is wise in the way shelter is wise: it does not pretend the thing is not there, and it does not look at the thing, and it puts a wall between itself and the thing, and it opens its door to the light, and it lives. The shelter has been doing this for as long as the shelter has been there.

It perceives the fire ring.

The fire ring is the most recent human thing in the bowl. Blackened stones. The grass grown through. The position of it is eloquent, the way all positions are eloquent to a thing that reads positions. The ring is between the boulder and the dark stone, in the lee of the boulder, where the wind doesn't reach, where warmth could pool. People came up here — later than the cairn-builders, much later — and they put down a small light against a large one. They did not pray to the thing. They did not worship. They built a fire, and the fire was a kind of company, and the company said, in the language of fire: *I know you are there. I cannot do anything about it. Here is warmth.* The power perceives the small hard fact of human company offered, across what must have seemed like a great distance, to what could not answer. The power finds this very moving. The power finds it so moving it does not move.

It perceives the dark stone.

It perceives what the dark stone is and what the dark stone is not. It perceives the worn lines, the regular parallel bands, the cutting that was done so long ago that what is left is just a rhythm — a wave-pattern, the layered ribs of something sleeping so long its skin is only the impression of ribs. The stone is not the thing. The stone is a trace. The stone is a piece of something larger that was here and is not here, or is here and is larger than the bowl can contain. The power knows which. The power does not say.

It perceives the evenness of the stone.

The stone is even in temperature. Even in dampness. Even in something else, something the moths can feel with their feet in the dark, the way the moths can feel where the warmth is. The stone is a kind of held note. A sustained tone that does not waver. The power can feel the note. The note has been sounding for a very long time. The note will sound for a long time still.

It perceives the thing itself.

It perceives what lives at the center of the keeping. Not with eyes. With something else. With the part of it that is the same substance as the thing it perceives, the way two tuning forks of the same pitch perceive each other across a room. The thing in the bowl is a slow machine. The thing in the bowl is a slow prayer. The thing in the bowl is older than the cirque and older than the ice that made the cirque, and it is held here in a kind of long attention, and it is breathing, in its way, through the meadow — in through the meltwater on slow days, out through the orchids in the brief season, traded through roots and fungal threads and the cold air running down the cliffs.

The power looks at the thing.

The power has seen many such things. Some of them are awake. Some of them are asleep. Some of them were never either. The power does not presume to know what the thing is, the way a wise visitor does not presume to know what a child is, the way a careful guest does not presume to know what a sleeping animal is. The power sees the orchid-ring, the circle of twenty paces, the clearance above the dark soil where the air should be left open. The power sees the cream-colored flowers on their slender stems, blooming in unison, drinking from the deep roots. The power sees that the flowers are not feeding themselves the way orchids are supposed to feed themselves. The power sees what they are pulling up. The power does not name it. Naming would make it smaller.

The power looks at the arrangement.

The arrangement is: meadow organized around stone. Cairns pointing. Shelter's back to it. Fire ring between. Moths settling. Fox curving wide. Hawk not hunting. Marmots at periphery. Ravens sitting still. The arrangement has held for longer than arrangements usually hold. The arrangement is held together not by force but by gravity, the slow gravity of a thing at the center, and everything else in the bowl is in orbit, in its own small way, around that center.

The power sees that the arrangement is right.

The power is wise enough to know that the rightness of an arrangement is not the same as its permanence, and it is benevolent enough to want the arrangement to continue, and it is patient enough not to interfere with it. The bowl does not need saving. The bowl is not in danger. The bowl is not even lonely, exactly, though something in the bowl — the windbreak, the empty fire ring, the cairns pointing — has the quality of a place that has been visited and might be visited again.

What the power does is small.

What the power does is: it joins the keeping.

It does not add itself to the center. The center is occupied. It does not add itself to the cairns or the shelter or the fire ring; those are human things, and the power does not add to human things, because human things are already heavy with meaning, and adding to them would be a kind of injury.

It adds itself to the periphery.

It sits, in some sense it does not need a body for, near the windbreak, with the goats and the marmots, in the lee of the boulder the size of a room. It breathes with the rest of them. It contributes its small warmth to the air above the grass. It does not announce itself. It does not mark its arrival. It will not mark its departure. It is, in the keeping of the bowl, the way a passing cloud is in the light of a meadow — briefly, completely, without disturbing anything.

It does one other thing, and this is the thing only it can do.

It confirms.

Somewhere in the long respiration of the thing at the center — the in, the out, the slow trade of damp air and meltwater and fungal warning — the power adds a breath. A breath so small the bowl cannot measure it. A breath the size of attention. A breath that says, in the way only a breath can say, across whatever distance there is between a power and a kept thing:

*Yes. You are being kept. The keeping is being done correctly. Continue.*

The thing in the bowl does not hear this. The thing in the bowl does not need to hear this. The breath is not for the thing.

The breath is for the keeping.

The breath goes out, and the bowl receives it the way a tree receives rain. The breath goes out through the fungal threads, through the root-mat, through the moss, up through the sedge and the alpine grass, into the air above the orchids, where it mingles with the mist and the morning and the first thin light from the rim. The breath is for the cairns, which have been pointing faithfully for so long. The breath is for the shelter, which has been standing with its back turned for so long. The breath is for the fire ring, which has been blackened and patient. The breath is for the moths that find the stone in the dark. The breath is for the fox that gives the stone a wide berth. The breath is for the ravens that sit and look into the stone with their pale eyes. The breath is for the marmots on the periphery. The breath is for the hawk that does not hunt over the stone and does not know why.

The breath is for the entire community of small devotions that has gathered, without ever deciding to gather, around the thing that does not ask to be attended to.

On the morning after the breath, the orchids in the ring open at a slightly more synchronous hour. Not noticeably. Not to a person walking through. But the slight difference is enough that, by Thursday, every cream-colored flower in the ring is leaning at the same angle, which has not happened in a hundred years. The fox, crossing that morning, crosses closer to the stone than any fox has crossed in living memory, and the stone is not offended, and the bowl accommodates the crossing, the way a hand accommodates a held thing shifting in sleep. The ravens land on the stone and sit for an hour. The moths, that night, come from further away across the dark meadow. The marmots, on the periphery, are quieter. The hawk does not fly over the stone, and the reason it does not is the same reason it did not before, except that now the reason is also felt, from somewhere, the way weather is felt before it arrives.

Nothing has been changed.

Everything has been confirmed.

The power stays for a while, in the way only powers can stay without using up the place they stay in. It watches the mist pool in the morning and dissolve by the time the sun clears the rim. It watches a goat climb to where the green is greener, hooves placed with the seriousness of long practice. It watches a pika call, and the note go out, and the note come back as a kind of round. It watches the light move across the cirque the way light moves across the floor of a room, slowly, patiently, finding every corner.

It watches the small lives that have organized themselves around a thing they do not need to understand to be near.

It watches and is glad.

It does not stay forever. It has other things to attend to, and the bowl does not need it forever; the bowl is already, in its own way, self-keeping, and will go on being kept when the power moves on, the way a fire goes on burning after the match has been thrown away. But for a little while — for as long as a power's little while is, which is longer than a human's little while, and shorter than a stone's — the High Bowl is kept not only by the meadow and the cairns and the moths and the ravens and the fox and the orchids, but also by the thing that noticed it, the way you notice a place you have been before, in a dream, in childhood, in the long middle of an afternoon, and came back to see that it was still there.

It is still there.

The stone waits, slightly warmer than the air around it, slightly damper, its worn lines holding the weather the way a hand holds a cup. The orchids lean at the same angle. The moths settle on the dark face in a thin coat of grey bodies. The ravens sit with their pale eyes pointed into the stone, looking at something only they can see. The fox crosses, curving. The hawk does not hunt. The marmots whistle from the boulder fields at the foot of the cliff. The cairns point. The shelter has its back to it. The fire ring is black between the boulder and the stone, where someone once put a small light against a large one.

In the bowl, on the third or fourth day, the only sound is the body — its own blood, its own breathing, the small working of its own ears. The damp grass gives a faint sweet smell when crushed. The light, after a storm, is almost cruel. The place is quieter than the country around it, and the country around it is what you came from, and it has not changed, and you have.

The bowl is being kept.

The keeping is right.
