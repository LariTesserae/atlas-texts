The attention that has fallen on this place and on the being who lives in it is patient. It has learned, over a long time, that the best interventions are the ones that look like nothing. The best blessings are the ones that look like what was already there, only a degree more so. It is the kind of power that knows when to keep its hands at its sides, and it keeps them at its sides more often than not, and when it acts, it acts at the threshold of perception — in the gap between what is sensed and what is known.

It sees the yard from inside the volume.

It sees the green-blue columns of light coming down through the surface chop, bending with the waves above. It sees the walls going off into the haze, the concrete with its seams and its formwork-ghosts, the steel reinforcement bright and rust-orange where a piece has broken away. It sees the pipes climbing the surfaces in bundled groups, sheathed in waterproofed insulation, marked with stenciled codes. It sees the cables strung along the walls. It sees the lamps casting their small yellow bubbles. It sees the warm plumes climbing the grates, bending the light as they rise, marking the boundary between cool ambient and machine-heated discharge. It sees the fish schooling around the plumes, around the corners, around the still-water places behind the walls. It sees the colonizers working patiently from the edges in — the green-brown fuzz on the concrete where the light reaches, the pale branching on the warm steel down-current from the plumes. It sees the divers in their stiff steel-boned suits, working in pairs, speaking in their broken syllables. It sees the dark shapes passing at the edge of sight, unhurried, deliberate. It sees the operators on the upper works, watching their screens. It sees the pump-listeners with their headphones, knowing each pump by ear, knowing when a bearing is going days before any instrument tells them.

It sees, most clearly, the old hand.

The old hand who has been here through two refits and one full restart. The old hand who knows the currents by name, by time of day, by the way a particular flow bends around a particular obstruction on a particular tide. The old hand who has favorite corners of still water behind walls where they sit and watch things pass. The old hand who has favorite fish, and who can tell you which schools have been in which channels for how many seasons. The old hand who lives, by their own preference, almost entirely in the water, and who surfaces only to sleep and eat and do the paperwork that cannot be done from a console, and who has not decided whether to retire, and whose not-deciding is not indecision but a kind of held breath.
