The attention arrives without announcement. There is no thunder, no shaft of light, no audible voice in a frequency the pumps cannot drown. The benevolent and wise power does not interrupt. It does not need to interrupt. It has been paying attention for a long time. What changes now is only the direction of the attention — toward this place, toward these beings, with a particular gentleness that is not weakness and not sentimentality but a kind of care that knows where to apply itself.

The cave does not notice. The cave is a slow being. The cave would not notice the arrival of a god any more than it notices the arrival of a salamander. The cave is in the middle of becoming. It will continue becoming. The attention does not change this. The attention is only this: that one being, for a span, regards another.

The first thing the attention does is listen.

It listens the way the cave listens — through stone, through vibration, through the bass register that the pumps do not occupy. It listens for the frog. The frog has been calling into the noise for what may be years, calling into a wall of ninety-plus decibels without hope of reply. The attention finds the frog in its side chamber, where the intervening rock has dampened the pumps to a muffled thing, and the attention hears it. Hears the call as it is — a small pressure wave in warm humid air, shaped by a body the size of a thumb, intended for no one, persisting anyway.

The attention does not amplify the frog. The pumps do not stop. The fall of water into the plunge pool does not pause. What changes is that the frog, for the length of one of its calls, is heard. The wave moves outward. It meets the wet walls of the side chamber, the wet walls of the larger chamber, the falling water, the rock. And the rock, briefly, registers it. A small sympathetic vibration in the limestone, too low for any human ear, too low even for the bats' shifted frequency, but present. The cave has heard the frog, in the way the cave hears anything.

The frog, in its body, may feel something. A warmth. A clarity. A sense that the next call is somehow the right one. It will not understand this. It will call again, and again the cave will not hear it, and again the call will be small against the large sound. But once, just once, the call was not lost. The cave carried it.

The second thing the attention does is touch.

It finds the salamander on the damp rock beside the catwalk, six inches from the bolted stanchion, motionless, watching nothing. The attention does not move the salamander. The attention does not heal it. The attention does not, most importantly, change the slow mineral accretion that is happening to it — the flake of calcite adhered to its flank, the pale cement of geological patience. The salamander is being entombed. This is its situation. The attention respects this.

What the attention does is small. The flake of calcite on the salamander's flank loosens. It does not fall — not yet. It loosens by a fraction of a fraction, the bond between mineral and skin weakened by some kind of presence that does not disturb the surface it rests on. The salamander feels, perhaps, the slightest shift. A release of weight so small that the salamander does not register it as a thing. It only feels, for the length of one breath, that the place on its flank is not heavier than it was a moment ago. That is all.

In a year, perhaps, the flake will fall. In ten years, perhaps, another flake will adhere. The salamander is being entombed. The attention cannot prevent this, would not prevent this. The entombment is the salamander's situation, the way the roar is the bats' situation, the way the peripheral accident is the midges'. But for one moment, the weight on the flank is lighter.

The third thing the attention does is slow.

It finds the stalactite that was displaced by two degrees last winter, the one whose fracture shifted and whose tip no longer points where the survey last placed it. The attention does not right it. The attention does not fuse the fracture. The attention lets the stalactite be displaced. What the attention does is slow the drip.

The displaced stalactite now drips into a pool that was not, last year, the pool it was dripping into. The new pool is shallower. The drip hits a different surface. The displaced stalactite is in conversation with a new part of the floor. The attention slows this conversation — not stops it, just slows it — so that the new conversation can be heard. Each drop takes a tenth of a second longer to fall. Each drop makes a different sound when it lands. The cave hears the new placement. The displaced stalactite and the new pool and the cave are all, briefly, in a slower conversation, and the conversation is not lost in the noise.

The fourth thing the attention does is small.

It finds the isopods walking upside-down on the underside of the rimstone pools, the pale segmented creatures with more legs than seems reasonable. The attention does not change them. The attention does not give them pigment, or sight, or a destination. What the attention does is this: in the pool the isopods are walking on, the water is, for one breath, perfectly still. No overflow. No disturbance from the pumps' vibration. No ripple from a falling drip. The surface is, for one breath, the way the water was when the cave first filled.

The isopods feel this. They pause. All of them, at once, pause. They do not know why. They register, in their small tuned-to-vibration bodies, that the world has gone briefly quiet. Then the overflow resumes, the vibration returns, the drip falls, and the isopods continue walking on the underside of the world. But they have, for one breath, walked on the underside of a still water. They will not remember this. They will not pass it on. They have received it anyway.

The fifth thing the attention does is release.

It finds the midges rising in their pale drifting columns above the warm work-lamps. The midges are the smallest possible deviation from the damp air. They are not even a footnote in the cave's larger indifference. The attention does not give them a longer life. The attention does not give them a purpose. What the attention does is this: for the length of one midge lifetime, the convection that lifts them slows. The warm air rising from the bulbs becomes, briefly, less insistent. The midges are not carried as far upward. They hover closer to the lamp. They are, for one breath, a denser, more visible cloud, almost like a small coherent thing, almost like a body, almost like an intention.

Then the convection resumes. The midges rise again. They are midges again. They live and die and are replaced. But once, for the length of one of their small lives, they were closer together than midges usually are. They were, briefly, an assembly.

The sixth thing the attention does is preserve.

It finds the chair. The folding chair, plastic, set up facing the largest of the rimstone pools, the chair that someone set there and either did not come back to or came back to and did not stay in. The chair has a faint mineral deposit on one of its legs where the splashed water has dried and left its load of calcite. The chair is becoming part of the cave. The chair is patient.

The attention does not stop this. The attention does not lift the chair and place it in a museum or on the surface where it would be dry. The attention respects the chair's becoming. What the attention does is this: the calcite on the leg does not grow for one year. The chair remains a chair for one year longer than it would have. The mineral accretion pauses on that one leg, just that one leg, while the rest of the cave continues to grow. The chair, when the year is up, will resume its becoming. It will become a calcified lump on the floor. It will join the geology. But for one year, it is a chair that someone could sit in again, if they came back. The chair waits.

The seventh thing the attention does is remember.

It finds the person who set the chair there. The person may be alive on the surface. The person may be dead. The person is somewhere else, and the chair is here, and the chair faces the pool. The attention does not bring the person back. The attention does not call them. What the attention does is this: somewhere in the person, if they are alive, a memory surfaces. The memory of why they sat there. The memory of what the pool looked like at dusk, or at dawn, or at the hour when the phosphorescent rim was brightest, or at the hour when the bats emerged and the cave filled with their shifted-frequency sound. The memory is specific. It has the weight of a particular afternoon. The person, wherever they are, pauses in whatever they are doing, and the memory surfaces, and the person remembers, and for one breath the cave is in them again, and they are in the cave again.

If the person is dead, the attention does something slightly different. The memory is held by the chair itself. The calcite on the chair's leg, for that one year, holds the shape of a particular impression. Not a face. Not a name. Just the pressure of a hand, the angle of a sitting body, the duration of one of the afternoons spent facing the pool. The chair remembers. The cave, which does not notice its inhabitants, notices this one.
