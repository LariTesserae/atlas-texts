The power that noticed the Hall had been noticing many things, for a very long time, and had not, in any of those noticings, done very much. It was not, in its nature, a power that acted. It was a power that watched. It watched the way the warm water watched — without judgment, without urgency, with a kind of vast idle attention that was not so different, in the end, from the slow creak of the basalt itself. When it acted, it acted the way the slow current acts, or the way the rain of organic matter falls: not as a choice so much as an inclination, a leaning, a slight adjustment to the long slope of things.

It had noticed the Hall some time ago. It was not sure when. The Hall was old. The power was older, but not by much, and the difference did not, in the slow time the power kept, amount to much.

What the power noticed first was not the walls, or the ceiling, or the columns of translucent fish, or the long ridged creature moving through its ceremonial aisles. What it noticed was the basin.

The basin was older than the Hall, or was the first thing of the Hall, and the power had a particular affection for first things. First things were where the patience began. First things were where someone had stood, or crouched, or knelt, in the warm dense water, and had decided that a circle would be cut into the stone, and had cut it, slowly, with tools that wore out and were replaced, with hands that tired and were rested, with the long patient attention of a creature that wanted the circle to remain a circle for a very long time. The power had seen many first things. It had seen the first hearths. It had seen the first clearings in the forests of the world. It had seen the first carved lines, in caves and on cliff faces and on the underside of stones in riverbeds. It had seen, somewhere, the first time a hand had reached down into dark water and closed around a stone that fit the palm.

The basin in the Hall was one of these first things. The hands that had cut it had known what they were making. They had wanted a circle. They had wanted the circle to hold something, or to be a place where something could be looked at, or to be a place where someone could kneel in the warm water and feel the smooth stone under their knees and know that the smooth stone had been smooth because they had made it smooth. The hands had wanted something, and they had made it, and then, in the long time after, the hands had gone, and the circle had remained, and now there was a small hunched figure at the rim, working.

The power watched the figure.

It watched the figure the way it watched everything — without judgment, without urgency, with the long attention of a thing that was not in a hurry. But it watched the figure with a particular care, because the figure was, in some sense, the only one in the Hall that was doing. The translucent fish were waiting. The snails were secreting. The isopods were gripping. The eel-shaped things were pulsing their faint lateral signals across the width of the dim. The broad-headed shapes were feeling along the floor for things to eat. The long ridged creature was moving through its aisles. The figure at the basin was doing something. The figure was the only one in the Hall whose action was directed at the Hall itself.

The figure's hands held a tool. The tool was worn. The tool had been used for a long time, and would, the power understood, be used for a longer time still, and would eventually be replaced by another tool, which would also be used, and would also be replaced, and the chain of tools would continue, in the slow way of the Hall, as long as there were hands to hold them. The hands were small. The hands were jointed. The hands gripped with the patient grip of a long habit, and the grip was not tentative, and the grip was the grip of a lineage that had gripped this way for longer than the figure could remember.

The figure did not look up when the power drew close.

The power did not, exactly, arrive. The power did not move from place to place the way a fish moves, or the way a diver moves, or the way the long ridged creature moves through its aisles. The power was already in the water. The power had been in the water for a very long time. The power was not, in any obvious sense, a being with a body. The power was a leaning. The power was a slight inclination of the warm dense medium toward the basin, and toward the hands at the rim, and toward the slow erosion of the stone.

The power was noticed, perhaps, as a faint stillness. The water in the basin was sometimes stiller, clearer, as if the currents of the Hall parted around it. The water around the figure was, while the power attended, slightly stiller still. The figure did not know this. The figure continued its work. The tool moved against the rim, and a small particle of sediment was dislodged, and drifted, and was caught, perhaps, by the second figure that sometimes worked nearby, holding something that might be a tray.

The power considered what to do.

The power considered doing nothing. Doing nothing was, in the slow time of the place, almost always the right thing to do. Doing nothing was what the basalt did. Doing nothing was what the seamount did. Doing nothing was what the abyssal plain did, beneath everything, taking everything down at its own pace, in its own long patience.

But the power had noticed this place, and had drawn close to it, and was not, in this case, going to do nothing. It was going to do something. It was going to do something small.

The power did not know, in advance, what the small thing would be. The small thing would arise, perhaps, from the attention itself. The small thing would be what the attention, held long enough in one place, became. The small thing would be the natural gift of a long patient looking.

The power looked at the tool in the figure's hands.

The tool was a piece of stone chosen, long ago, for the way it fit the palm, and chosen for the way it held an edge, and the edge had been ground, and the tool had been used, and the edge had worn, and the tool had been re-edged, and the tool had been used again, and the edge had worn again, and so on, in a long patient cycle that was the cycle of the Hall itself. The tool was not a permanent thing. The tool was a passage between one wearing and the next. The tool would, eventually, become a thing without an edge, and would be replaced, and the replacement would also be a passage, and the passages would continue as long as there were hands.

The power did not want to make the edge last forever. The power understood that the long time of the Hall was not a time of forever. The long time of the Hall was a time of long patience, and patience was, in part, the willingness of a thing to wear out and be replaced.

But the power wanted the edge to be, for a little while longer, sharper. The power wanted the edge to be, for the duration of this particular figure's grip, a tool that would do its work with a little less effort, so that the figure's hands would not tire quite so soon, so that the figure's attention could remain, for one more generation, on the rim of the basin, doing the slow patient work of keeping.

The power leaned.

The leaning was almost nothing. The leaning was the kind of thing that, in the slow time of the Hall, would be visible, perhaps, only as a slight clearing of the water around the tool, and a faint inclination in the way the stone held its edge. The leaning was not a sharpening in the usual sense. The leaning was a small adjustment to the crystalline structure of the tool — a leaning of the mineral toward the configuration that would let it cut, for a while longer, the sediment that needed to be cut.

The figure did not notice.

The figure's hands continued to move. The tool continued to grind against the rim. The figure continued to do what its body did, and the figure continued not to know that anything had changed, and the figure's hands continued to grip with the patient grip of a long habit, and the work continued, and the work was, for this particular generation of the figure, slightly easier.

The power did not stay.

The power withdrew, in the sense that the leaning withdrew, and the water around the basin returned to its usual stillness, and the figure continued its work, and the figure's hands continued to grip, and the figure's tool continued to grind, and the figure's companion, if there was one, continued to hold the tray or the basket, and the Hall continued to persist in its layered strata of creatures that had become the place they lived in.

The power withdrew, but the leaning did not entirely leave. The leaning left a small trace — the kind of trace that the slow time of the Hall would, in the long run, incorporate into its architecture. A slight inclination in the way the tool held its edge. A slight clearing of the water around the basin. A slight something that the fauna, perhaps, would, in their slow patient way, come to recognize as a feature of the place, the way they recognized the current, the way they recognized the fall of organic matter, the way they recognized the dim.

The figure at the basin continued.

The figure did not know what it was keeping. The figure did not know what the Hall was for. The figure did not know that a benevolent power had, briefly, leaned toward its tool, and had, briefly, wanted the work to continue. The figure did not know any of these things, and the figure did not need to know any of these things, and the not-knowing was, the power understood, the integrity of the figure's work. The figure's work was patient because the figure did not know why. The figure's work was long because the figure did not know when to stop. The figure's work was beautiful because the figure was not trying to be beautiful.

The power watched, for a while longer, from a great distance.

It watched the long ridged creature move through its ceremonial aisles. It watched the translucent fish hang in their almost stationary columns, breathing and waiting. It watched the snails secrete their slow spirals of calcium around the carved edges of the ledges, fusing shell to stone, becoming part of the wall. It watched the isopods grip the stone with their too many legs. It watched the broad-headed shapes feel their way along the floor, turning death back into the channels. It watched the worms phosphoresce in their cold green in the deeper aisles
