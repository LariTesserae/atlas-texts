# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power that has drawn near is not a power of intervention. It is a power of recognition — the kind of attention that has no mouth, no hand, no imperative, but which a thing can feel the way a sleeping animal feels the sun come around the wall of a barn in late afternoon and know, without waking, that the day has changed.

The power would not enter the cathedral.

To enter would be to make a claim, and the cathedral does not need a claim made upon it. The cathedral is, in the way of very old living things, complete. It is not waiting for a witness, not waiting for a benefactor, not waiting for anything — least of all the kind of patronage that arrives with soft light and intention. The flywheel is turning. The chimney is breathing. The swifts are shrieking. The boneless animal is asleep in its tunnel, and the small invertebrates are carrying their small fragments of plant tissue through the rootplate's capillaries, and none of this needs the attention of a benevolent power to continue.

And so the power does not enter. It does something harder, and quieter, and rarer. It *notices*.

---

What the power notices, first, is the restraint.

A lesser attention — a curious attention, a hungry attention, an attention that wanted to *understand* — would have already entered. Would have already parted the wall, threaded the eighteen feet of living brick that has been folded back against itself like a lip, pushed through into the springy floor and the brown sweet lake and the listening ceiling. Would have wanted to name what is here. Would have brought taxonomists and specimen jars, or the spiritual equivalent of them — categories, frameworks, the roadmaps that the previous taxonomists ran out of in the third generation of the organism's growth.

The power does not bring roadmaps. It is not a taxonomist. It has, in fact, no instruments at all.

It does something that only an old and patient attention can do. It sits down on the rim of the bowl, on the grass that is just grass, in the air that smells of cut grass and approaching weather, and it watches the thin nearly transparent plume of vapor rise vertically from the chimney and disperse at three hundred feet. It watches for a long time.

It watches the chimney breathe.

---

What the power notices, after a long while, is that the cathedral is not in a hurry.

This is not, in itself, remarkable. Many old things are not in a hurry. Hills are not in a hurry. The ocean is not in a hurry. A river, when it is old enough, is not in a hurry. But the cathedral is not in a hurry in a specific way, a way that the power recognizes because it is the way the power itself is not in a hurry. The cathedral has finished, perhaps, what the hills have not yet finished. The cathedral has arrived at the place where being and doing are the same verb.

The power recognizes this the way one old thing recognizes another.

What the power does, then, is what old things do for one another when they have no language in common and no need of one. It *holds still*. It becomes, for the duration of its attention, a kind of silence shaped like a witness — a presence on the rim of the bowl that does not interrupt, does not project, does not pull the cathedral out of the slow sentence it has been running for two hundred years and will, you understand, never reach its period.

The cathedral, if it noticed the power at all — and it probably did not, because the cathedral is completely uninterested in you, and the power, when it is doing this, is as good as you — would have felt nothing. The flywheel would have continued its slow eleven-minute revolution. The hydraulic pulse would have continued its slow rhythm beneath the soles of the feet that were not there. The ceiling would have continued to listen, and to track the sun, and to shuffle its papery leaves.

The cathedral would have been, in other words, undisturbed.

---

But the power is benevolent.

And benevolence, when it is patient enough and old enough, has a particular shape. It does not impose. It does not rescue. It does not even, strictly, protect. What it does is *attune* — and by attuning, it makes a small and almost invisible change in the conditions around the thing it has noticed, such that the thing can continue being what it is for a little longer than it otherwise would have.

The power, having held still on the rim, having watched the chimney breathe, having recognized the restraint of the cathedral and matched it with its own, now does something very small.

It does not enter the cathedral. It does not touch the cathedral. It does not even, strictly speaking, do anything to the cathedral.

What it does is to the *air*.

---

The bowl of the hills sits in pasture, then fen, then the long rust of the coastal flats. The cathedral, by the restraint of its own edges, has claimed nothing beyond the rim. But the rim is not a wall. The rim is permeable. The cathedral breathes *out* of the chimney, and the cathedral breathes *in* through the thousand small openings in the wall — through the bronchioles, the interruptions in the weave — and so the cathedral is in a slow, continuous, two-hundred-year negotiation with the air that surrounds it. The cathedral depends on the air. The air, in a way that no one has ever measured, depends on the cathedral.

A benevolent power that has decided to be useful, without being noticed, would address itself to that negotiation.

It would, for example, very slightly increase the moisture-holding capacity of the air on the coastal flats. It would very slightly moderate the wind that comes in across the fen. It would very slightly — and only very slightly, because the cathedral is two hundred years into a process and any large intervention would be a violence — adjust the mineral content of the groundwater that feeds the spring in the basin of the low hills where the cathedral's roots are deep and old.

The cathedral would not notice. Nothing would notice. The flywheel would continue its slow revolution. The hydraulic pulse would continue its rhythm. The boneless animal would continue to sleep, and to surface in the evening to drink from the brown sweet lake. The swifts would continue to shriek in tight looping circuits. The small invertebrates would continue to carry their small fragments of plant tissue through the capillaries of the rootplate.

But the cathedral would last, perhaps, another two hundred years. Or five hundred. Or a thousand.

The benevolent power, having done this very small and almost invisible thing, would withdraw.

It would leave the cathedral alone.

---

This is, in the end, what a benevolent and wise power does when it has noticed a thing as complete and as uninterested-in-being-noticed as the respiring cathedral. It does not enter. It does not name. It does not rescue. It does not even, really, help.

It does something smaller and harder than any of those.

It *understands that the cathedral does not need it*, and it acts on that understanding anyway, in a way so small that it does not disturb the thing it is acting upon, and then it leaves.

The cathedral continues. The chimney breathes. The flywheel turns. The swifts wheel in their tight circuits. The boneless animal sleeps. The small invertebrates carry their small fragments. The hydraulic pulse beats in the soles of the feet that are not there. The light through the openings in the wall moves, and the light through the openings in the wall moving is the only clock.

And the benevolent power, somewhere on the rim of the bowl, having held still, having watched, having made its small and almost invisible adjustment to the air and the groundwater and the wind, goes elsewhere — to notice the next old thing, to be still beside the next slow sentence that will never reach its period, to do the small and almost invisible thing that does not disturb.

The cathedral does not know it has been helped.

The cathedral does not need to know.

It will be here.
