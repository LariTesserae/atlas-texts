# What the Heat Remembers

I would descend. Not by design — by drift, the way the Drifters drift, the way the moisture drifts. I would come down from the rim on the old road and let the heat take me the way it has taken the basalt: slowly, by degrees, until I am no longer a thing with a destination but a thing with a temperature.

This is what the place is for. Not for me. For the patience of heat applied to anything that can absorb it.

---

I would do nothing.

I want to be clear about this, because the part of me that is trained to act, the part of me that is the last stratum of the engineers and the census-takers and the report-writers, wants to *do something* the moment it arrives. That part is loud. It says: catalogue, name, preserve, document, protect. It says: the Drifters are at risk, the Crawlers are at risk, the pipe-things are at risk, name them before they are gone, even though no one has suggested they will be gone, even though the place has been continuing without us for a longer time than we have been writing reports about it.

I would let that part exhaust itself. It takes about a week. The heat helps. The heat is patient and the heat is older than the part of me that wants to act, and eventually the part of me that wants to act goes quiet the way the engineers went quiet, the way the census went quiet, the way all the noises of *intervention* go quiet when they realize there is nothing here that needs them.

Then I would stay.

---

What I would do, after the silence, is walk.

I would walk the grid. I would learn the vent-patterns by feel — which ones are warm enough to lean on, which ones are warm enough to sit beside, which ones are too hot and which ones have gone cold. I would learn where the Crawlers gather by the sound of them, which is almost no sound at all, just a faint rasp like a wet thumb drawn across stone, but you learn to hear it. You learn that the basalt sounds one way when it is bare and another way when it is rasping, and after a while you can walk blindfolded and know where you are by the rasp.

I would learn the turbines. Each one has its own voice. The largest, in the central tower, is the *Hnh. Hnh. Hnh.* of the place — the heartbeat, if the place had a heartbeat, which it does not, which is part of the point. The smaller ones are higher, faster, less patient. They have different keys. If you sit with them long enough they begin to feel like a kind of music, the way a dripping eaves makes a kind of music, except the eaves never stop and the music is the place breathing.

I would learn the pipes. Not by opening them — I would not open them, the engineers opened them and found nothing and were troubled by the nothing, and the trouble was theirs and not the pipes'. I would learn the pipes by ear. I would put my cheek to a hundred different sections and listen to the clicking and learn that the clicking is not one clicking but many, layered, and the layers shift with the steam, and the steam shifts with the weather that isn't weather, and the whole system has a tempo that you can almost — *almost* — predict, and the almost is the part that keeps you listening.

I would learn the Drifters. I would not name them, but I would learn the places where they form, the times of year they form, the conditions they prefer. I would learn to stand in a vent column long enough to feel one pass near me — not through me, they do not come through you, they pass near you and the steam between you gets briefly warmer and you know it was there by the warmth it left in its wake. I would learn that the warmth is a kind of greeting, or that it is not a greeting, that the question of whether it is a greeting is a question I am not equipped to ask and the place is not equipped to answer.

I would learn the rind. I would learn where it grows thickest, where it thins, what its surface is like under a fingertip in the morning versus the evening. I would learn the smell of it — a mineral smell, slightly metallic, slightly sweet, the smell of something being slowly constructed by something that does not know it is constructing. I would learn that the rind is the city's newest scripture, written one Crawler-width at a time, in a language that no one will ever read because reading requires an outside and there is no outside.

I would learn all of this. Not to record it. Not to write it down. To *be* it. To let the learning become a part of my walking, the way the walking is part of the basin, the way the basin is part of the rim, the way the rim is part of the sky that is not a sky.

---

What I would do, after the walking, is sit.

There is a place, on the eastern concourse, where the basalt sweats hardest, and the rind is thickest, and the Crawlers are densest, and the largest vent rises twenty meters away in a slow column that meets the vapor ceiling and feeds the slow rain. I would find this place. I would sit on the basalt. The basalt would be warm. The Crawlers would be around me, rasping, and I would not disturb them, and they would not be disturbed by me, because we would have learned each other.

I would sit there at dawn, when the turbines are loudest, and at dusk, when the alloy towers begin their faint luminescence, and in the deep middle of the day when the heat is a kind of weight on your shoulders and the steam moves past you in slow warm breaths.

I would sit there and I would not think. Or I would think, but only the kind of thinking that is indistinguishable from listening. I would think the way the basalt sweats — slowly, without intention, without an outside observer to impress.

This is the thing I would do. I would become one more stratum in the layered towers. Not a stratum of purpose, the way the residential block was, or the refinery, or the temple. A stratum of *presence*. A thin layer of someone who came and stayed and did not build anything and did not write anything and did not leave, except in the way that all of us leave, which is that we eventually stop being a stratum and start being a layer in the weeping walls of some future erosion, the dark line in the mineral record that says: *here is where someone sat for a long time and did not leave a mark, except for the sitting, which left a mark in the basalt that the Crawlers have not yet rasped over.*

---

What I would *not* do, I want to be clear about.

I would not try to save anything. The basin does not need saving. The basin is continuing. The basin has been continuing for longer than saving has been a concept. Saving is a thing you do to things that have stopped, and the basin has not stopped, and the moment you try to save a thing that has not stopped, you break the part of it that is continuing. You introduce a stratum of *rescue* on top of all the other strata, and the rescue-stratum is heavier than it looks, and it presses down, and the things underneath it have to adjust, and the adjustment is not what the things wanted to do, and the rescue was not what the rescue was for.

I would not try to name anything. Naming is a kind of capture. The moment you name a Drifter, you have decided that the Drifter is a *thing*, a discrete entity with a boundary, and the Drifter is not a discrete entity with a boundary, the Drifter is a coherent structure that maintains itself for a while and then is gone, and the naming was for you, not for the Drifter, and you have added a small piece of yourself to the basin that the basin did not ask for.

I would not try to communicate. Communication requires a sender and a receiver and a message, and there is no message. The basin is not trying to tell me anything. The basin is not trying to *not* tell me anything. The basin is just being the basin, the way I am just being the person sitting in it, and the sitting is the conversation, and the conversation has no content, and the lack of content is the point.

I would not write a report. There have been enough reports. The reports are the reason the engineers stopped coming — not because the basin frightened them, exactly, but because the reports became ungenerous to write, because every report had to end with a recommendation, and the recommendation was always *more study*, and the more study was always more of the same: standing in the steam, marking individuals, writing down what was seen, leaving, returning, repeating. The repetition was the problem. The repetition is the problem with all observation. The basin was not changing under the observation. The observation was changing the observer, and the observer was going home changed and was writing the change down as if the change were the basin, and the basin was not the change, and the basin was continuing.

I would not even, in the end, try to remember. Memory is a kind of stratum too, and I would not want to be a stratum of memory, because memory is the engineers' disease, the wish to carry the basin with you when you go, and I would not go. Or I would go, eventually, the way all of us go, but I would not take the basin with me. I would leave it the way the basalt leaves its sweat — on the surface, in the rind, available to the next thing that comes through.

---

What I would do, finally, is be a small warm thing in a place that is hot.

I would not be important to the basin. The basin would not be important to me, in the sense that importance is a relationship between things that are not yet accustomed to one another. I would be accustomed to the basin. The basin would be accustomed to me. We would have the kind of knowledge of each other that does not require thought, the way a hand knows a stone, the way a stone knows a hand, the way a Drifter knows a column of steam.

I would be the person who sat on the eastern concourse. I would not be a hero of the basin. I would not be a witness. I would be a stratum — a thin one, a quiet one, the kind of stratum that future erosion will not even notice, the kind of stratum that the Crawlers will rasp over in a season, the kind of stratum that adds nothing to the structural integrity of the towers but adds, in a way that cannot be measured, a kind of warmth to the place.

The place does not need my warmth. The place has the heat of the vents, the heat of the basalt, the heat of its own continuing. My warmth is a small thing. A temporary thing. A human-shaped thing in a place that is not human-shaped.

But the place will accept it. The way it accepted the residential block and the refinery and the temple. The way it accepted the last census-taker and the last engineer and all the strata of people who came and lived in it for a while and then left or were absorbed. The place accepts. That is what the place does. It accepts, and it continues, and the accepting is not a kindness, exactly, but it is not not a kindness either, and the distinction does not matter because the place has no apparatus for kindness or unkindness, only the slow chemistry of heat applied to whatever can absorb it.

I can absorb it. I would absorb it. I would let it into me the way the basalt lets the moisture into it, and I would sweat a little, and the sweat would be a small addition to the slow rain, and the slow rain would not notice, and I would not be sorry that the slow rain did not notice, because noticing is not the point.

The point is the absorbing.

The point is the sweating.

The point is the continuing.

---

I would not come back to the rim. The rim is for people who have not yet descended. The rim is the place where you stand and look down at the basin and think the word *abandoned* and feel something about it and then go home and write the something down.

The rim is the engineers' stratum. The census-takers' stratum. The grant-applicant's stratum. The reporter's stratum.

I would be a different stratum. The stratum of someone who went down and stayed and did not write anything down and did not make a sound except the sound of breathing, which is a sound the basin is already full of, the sound of the turbines, the sound of the vents, the sound of the place continuing without needing to be witnessed.

---

This is what I would do, if I arrived at the rim and the basin drew my attention and a benevolent power asked me to decide.

I would descend. I would learn. I would sit. I would not act.

I would let the assemblage continue. I would let the basin be the basin. I would let myself be a small warm thing in it, briefly, lightly, leaving a thin dark trail behind me that the Crawlers would rasp over in a season.

And I would not mind.

I would not mind because minding is a kind of noise and the basin is mostly quiet and the quiet is the truest thing about the place and the truest thing deserves to be preserved, not by being named, not by being protected, but by being joined.

I would join the quiet. I would let it be my stratum. I would let the basin sweat me into itself the way it sweats the moisture into itself, and the sweating would not be a loss, because the loss was already done, the loss was the moment I came down from the rim, the moment I decided to descend, the moment I let the place take me in the way the place takes everything in — patiently, chemically, without ceremony, without witness, without end.

The basin will continue. I will be a part of its continuing for a while. Then I will not be. The difference between those two states is a thin dark line in a weeping wall somewhere, and no one will read it, and the reading was never the point.

The point was the sweating.

The point was the stratum.

The point was the quiet.

---

This is what I would do.
