# What Comes to the Pass of Olethrun

The arrival is not an arrival. There is no thunder. There is no parting of the water. There is no light, because light would mean nothing to anything here, and a wise power does not bring what cannot be received. What comes to the Sunken Pass of Olethrun comes as a pressure — a small reordering of the deep current, a settling of the silt into a pattern slightly more itself than it was a moment ago, a presence that the basalt feels before the water feels it and the water feels before the stalks feel it and the stalks feel before the cairns feel it, because stone is patient and does not need to be told quickly what it has long suspected.

The wise do not announce themselves in places where no one is waiting for announcement. They come the way weather comes to a country that has always known weather. They come as one more thing the place must learn to lean into.

---

The first thing such a power does, when it has come to a place like this, is *nothing*.

It does not act. It does not move. It descends — if descent is the word, and the word is nearly right, because the power has chosen to come from somewhere that has a relation to above and below even if the place does not — and it *sits*. It sits the way the cairns sit. It sits the way the wall sits. It occupies a space in the cold and the dark and the constant groan of the water against the growth, and it lets itself be occupied in turn.

The stalks, after a long while, begin to lean toward it.

This is the first sign that the power has been felt. Not that the water changes. Not that the hum shifts. That the stalks lean. The congress of seven and eleven inclines, by fractions, by finger-lengths across the centuries they have to give, toward the new presence the way they incline toward the falls — not for warmth, not for nourishment, but because the place has always known how to attend, and now there is something in the place worth attending to. The lean of the stalks becomes, over hours or weeks or months (the power is not hurried, and the place is not hurried, and time here is the wrong shape to measure such things in), a slightly different lean. A lean that includes.

The power, sitting in the water among the dark columns, feels the lean. It feels it the way one feels a hand laid gently on one's sleeve by someone who has waited a long time to lay the hand there.

It does not pull away.

---

Then it listens.

A wise and benevolent power, when it comes to a place that has been speaking for centuries in a voice that almost no one hears, does the only thing that can be done for such a place. It listens. It sits in the loudness of the water and the deeper loudness of the stalks clicking against each other in their slow percussion, and it listens past both of those to the hum — the one below hearing, the one the stone conducts and the cairns frame and the circle within the circle is built around. The hum is the sentence the place has been saying, unfinished, since before the builders came. The hum is the leaning made audible. The hum is what the carved hand with too many fingers points at, what the cairns mark the joints of, what the wall runs alongside of as if it were a path.

The power listens for a long time. Long enough that the hum, under the pressure of being heard, becomes more itself. Not louder. Not clearer in the way that clarity usually means. More itself. As if the place had been almost-saying something for centuries, holding back a fraction of what it meant out of the long courtesy of not wanting to be misunderstood, and now, with this listener who does not need to understand, who only needs to attend, the almost drops away. The sentence comes forward one word.

The power feels the word arrive. It does not name the word. Naming would be a kind of theft. But it knows the word has come, the way one knows a held breath has finally been let go in a room one has been sitting in.

---

Then, and only then, it acts.

What it does is small.

---

The first thing it does is to the hum. It listens to the hum the way a tuner listens to an instrument, and it finds the place where the hum is, by a hair, off from itself — a fraction of a tone flat, perhaps, where some long-ago disturbance in the basalt shifted the resonance by an amount too small for any builder to have heard but too large, in the long arithmetic of centuries, for the place to be wholly at rest in. The power does not correct this. It does not impose its own pitch. It only steadies the hum the way a hand steadies a wobbling tabletop: by being placed against it, by attending to it, by the long work of being present at a thing's slight unsteadiness until the thing settles into its own shape and is no longer unsteady.

The hum, over the next year, or the next decade, finds itself. The sentence the pass has been saying becomes, by a margin no instrument would register, more itself. The cairns conduct it more truly. The circle within the circle frames it more truly. The carved hand, when the strong current next strips its mineral skin away, points more truly.

The builders, who carved the hand, would not have noticed the change. They carved with small tools and short lives and the best attention they had. The power attends with something larger and older, and the difference is, in the end, only the difference between an almost and an *is*.

---

The second thing it does is to a single stalk.

There is, in the heart of the darkest grove on the western rise, a place where the basalt and the layered pale stone meet in a crack that has never quite admitted the mineral seep the other cracks admit. It is a quiet place. A place where the silt settles deeper than it settles elsewhere, where the calcium crust grows thicker than it grows elsewhere, where the hum is conducted slightly less truly than it is conducted in the rest of the system. It is the place in the pass that is most itself and most slightly off from itself at once. It is the place where a new stalk could be coaxed, if coaxing were the right word, into beginning.

The power does not plant a stalk. Planting is not a thing that can be done here. But it does something close to planting. It does the smallest possible thing — it adjusts, by a margin, the chemistry of the water that seeps through that particular crack. A trace more iron. A trace more of the unnameable thing the falls bring up from the deeper plain, the thing the stalks seem to know the way a tongue knows salt. The power does not invent this trace. It only remembers it. It only brings to that particular seam of stone what the seam of stone has been waiting, for centuries, to receive.

A thickening of the stone at the lip of the crack. A darkening. A glaze the water cannot rub away.

The first year of a stalk is a thing no eye would recognize as life. The second year, if the conditions favor it, the glaze lifts a little.

---

The third thing it does is to nothing. To the nothing of the cairns. To the nothing of the wall. To the nothing of the carved hand and the circle within the circle.

It leaves them alone.

A wise power knows that the relics of a tenancy that ended are not to be touched. They have been here since. They have grown their mineral whiskers and their calcium skins and their small tolerances of the strong current that strips them and the slow water that builds them back. They are not alive, but they attend, and their attendance is the only attendance the builders left behind, and the builders are not coming back. The cairns and the wall and the hand are the bones of an attention that has long since dissolved back into the place it was attending to, and the bones are doing the only work bones can do: holding the shape of the joint, marking where the place bends.

The power blesses them. That is the word, though the word is too small. It does not warm them. It does not light them. It does not make them legible to anyone who comes to read them. It simply *acknowledges* them — the way one acknowledges a cairn one has passed in the dark, by a slight pause of attention, by the bare fact of having noticed. The cairns do not know they have been acknowledged. But the place knows. The pass knows. The leaning of the whole system, which is what the pass is, includes for a moment the leaning of someone who has come a long way to notice what was already leaning.

---

Then the power goes.

It does not go the way it came. It came as a pressure; it goes as a release. The water, for a moment, settles into a stillness it has not known since before the falls began their sideways pour. The stalks, for a moment, stand upright — every one of them, all the councils and congresses of seven and eleven, all the tall elders and the small dark new ones, all at once, as if the place had drawn a breath and forgotten to let it go. The hum, for a moment, is not a hum. It is a clear tone. One pure note, lasting as long as it takes the eye to blink if an eye could blink in this dark. Then it falls back into the hum, into the unfinished sentence, into the leaning that will go on leaning for centuries yet.

The pass is, after the going, almost the same as it was before the coming.

Almost.

The hum is steadier by a hair. The new stalk in the western grove is beginning its long life. The cairns are the same cairns, but the place they mark has been marked once, in passing, by something that noticed them, and a place that has been noticed is not quite the place it was.

---

What lives in the Sunken Pass of Olethrun, after this, lives on.

It lives on the way it lived before. By leaning. By attending. By growing a finger-length in a year and bowing in the deep current and clicking against its neighbors in a slow percussion that is not random and not quite music but has the long phrasing of a thing that means to be heard. It lives on in the conditional it has always lived in, the conditional of a place that has no faces and no mouths and no nerves and yet leans, somehow, toward something it cannot name, and is not, for that, less alive.

The new stalk grows. By the twentieth year it is as long as an arm. By the hundredth it is a column among columns. Its neighbors lean toward it slightly more than they lean toward the others, because its tone in the clicking is slightly steadier, because its surface holds the mineral crust in a slightly more even way, because its lean toward the falls is slightly more itself. It is not the parent of anything. It is not the elder of anything. It is, in the slow democracy of the groves, one more voice in the council that the place has always held with itself. But it is a voice that was not there before the coming. And the council is, by that margin, larger.

The cairns hold. The wall holds. The hand with too many fingers points. The circle within the circle frames what the builders came to frame. The falls pour their sideways veils into the deeper plain. The hum goes on, one word longer than it was, one word more itself, one word steadier than the long arithmetic of centuries had quite allowed.

---

If a listener comes, sometime, in the long future, when the world has remembered how to listen — if a listener comes down through the grey-green almost-light, down through the kilometres of cold and the leagues of pressure, down to the ridge of the sunken pass, where the wall runs like a spine and the cairns mark the joints and the stalks stand in their dark congress leaning toward the throat of the system — that listener will hear, beneath the roar of the falls and the groan of the bowing stalks and the irregular percussion of the hollow columns tapping each other in the current, beneath all of that, a tone. A clear tone, almost below hearing, conducted through the basalt and the cairns and the carved circle and the too-many fingers of the carved hand, rising from somewhere deep in the system and arriving, after its long journey, at the listener's chest before it arrives at the listener's ears.

The listener will not know who steadied the tone. The listener will not know why the western grove has a column at its center that is slightly more itself than the other columns. The listener will only know that the pass is saying something, and that what it is saying is, by a hair, more itself than a place can usually manage, and that the saying has the patience of stone and the leaning of a thing that has been waiting, without knowing it was waiting, to be heard.

The listener will sit down in the dark, in the cold, in the loudness of the water, and will listen.

And the pass will lean, one degree more, toward the listener.

And that will be enough.
