The benevolent and wise power sees the place. This is most of what it does.

It does not arrive. It has been here, in the way that air has been here, and what happens is the turning of its attention toward the fossil and toward the dozen grey-green tufts in its lee. The turning is not visible. It is not audible to the fossil, which is otherwise the only listener on the flat. It is, in any accounting, the most real event that has taken place here in several thousand years, and nothing on the flat will register it.

What the power perceives, it perceives with care, and what it perceives is this: a place that has reached its own final state. The fossil holds a single note. The crystals inside break a single source of light into a thousand. The salt remembers a sea that is gone. The wind is the wind, and has been the wind. The lichen are what is left of livableness on a flat that has otherwise finished being alive.

The power understands, in the way that wisdom understands things, that the place is fully itself. That the lichen are persisting at the only pace the place allows. That the work still being done is being done at a speed the place has chosen, and that the worker has not asked for help, because the worker has no way to ask, and because asking is not a category the worker operates in.

The power does not bring rain. Rain would interrupt the held note. The power does not bring back the sea. The sea is finished. The power does not send anything that moves under its own power — no bird, no insect, no crawler — because such a thing would be a grammatical error in a place where nothing moves under its own power. The power does not make the lichen grow faster. Faster is not a pace the place recognizes. Faster would be to grow in a register that is not theirs.

What the power does, instead, is this.

It attends.

It attends the way the wind attends — ceaselessly, without break. It attends the way the light attends — from above, with a quality that is not heat and not spectrum but is presence. It attends in the register of the place, which is the register of slow and of small and of held note and of persistence without progress.

Because the power is benevolent, this attending does a small thing in the world.

Over centuries, in the way lichen measure centuries, the air in the lee of the fossil becomes, by a margin no instrument built by hands would detect, slightly more often in the lichen's favor. The dust that gathers there is slightly more likely to stay. A spore, lofted by the wind and carried for miles across the flat, is slightly more likely to land and to hold. The lichen will, on average, advance a fraction of a millimeter more per century than they would have advanced without the attention.

This is not nothing. This is the most the place can receive without being changed.

The lichen will not know. They do not have the apparatus to know. They will continue to flower in the same unannounced way, to sit, to persist, to be. The fossil will not know. The note will not change. The salt will not know. The wind will not know — or if it knows, it will not register.

But the place will be, for as long as the wind does not lift it, in the company of a presence that has chosen to operate in its register. Almost nothing. Almost everything. The kind of presence that does not advertise.

The sentence the lichen are composing will continue. It will not be interrupted. It will not be provided with an ending. It will not be translated into a louder language.

It will only be written, as it has been written, in faint grey-green ink, very slowly, on a white page, in a wind that does not intend to stop — but in air that is, by a margin no instrument can detect, slightly more often in the writer's favor.

That is what the benevolent and wise power does. It becomes a part of the held chord. It hums at a frequency the lichen cannot hear, but which the lichen, in some way they have no way to acknowledge, are answered by.

And the place continues, almost exactly as it was, only no longer alone.
