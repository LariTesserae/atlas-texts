The light falls, green-grey, through the canopy of *Thalassia magnifica*, and the place holds its breath — not because anything living here is capable of breathlessness, but because breath itself is the medium, and silence in this place is not emptiness but rather the long attentive pause of a thing that has been listening for centuries. There is nothing to announce. There is only the slow precession of the moon across the woven floor, and the shrimp in the holdfasts, and the song the women are singing in the chamber where an old man is going.

I have watched this place.

I have watched it for a long time, the way one watches a reef, or a grove of old trees, or a marriage that has lasted into its fourth decade. Not with the urgent curiosity of the naturalist-divers in their copper-and-glass apparatus, who come down from Vorr and Saltreach with their dossiers and their columns and their careful fascination. They record. They note. They mark the kelp-architecture with the attention of people who build in steel and have never considered that one might *grow* a wall. They note the sounds, and they remark on how loud a quiet place can be. They note the fauna, abundant and unafraid, because the Anrath have not hunted them in living memory, only gathered them. They note the scale: small. Cathedral-scale rooms, but a hamlet of them, on a drowned mountain in the middle of a very large ocean.

What they do not note, because they cannot, is the way the place feels after a week.

I can.

I have been here longer than a week. I have been here longer than the oldest living Anrath, and longer than the oldest living kelp on the eastern flank of the ridge. I have been here since before the naturalist-divers had a binomial for the people, since before Vorr was a drowned city and Saltreach was a shelf-town and the continental edge had been named by anyone. I have been here since the ridge was just a ridge — volcanic, drowned, its crest a terrace of bare rock at thirty meters, waiting for the spores of something that did not yet exist, or existed only as a drift of possibility in the current.

I did not make the Anrath.

This is the first thing to be clear about, and it is a thing I am particular about, because the visitors from the lowland cities — the ones in their copper-and-glass apparatus, the ones with their dossiers — sometimes make the mistake of thinking that a thing which has been watched is a thing which has been made. The Anrath were not made. The kelp was not made. The eels in the hollow chambers were not made, nor were the cleaning-wrens, nor the snapping-shrimp colonies, nor the great pulse of current that comes in from the deep each morning with the tide. The reef accretes. The forest grows. The town grows. The town grows the way a tree-ring grows, the way a coral colony grows, the way a marriage grows — not by design, but by the slow accumulation of small consistent choices made by people who do not think of themselves as making history, only as tending the kelp, only as binding a frond, only as singing to a dying man in a chamber that is going to have to take him back.

What I did was this: I watched. I watched, and I noticed, and where noticing could be of use — where the difference between being noticed and not being noticed was the difference between a thing flourishing and a thing perishing — I noticed carefully.

---

There are powers that intervene. There are powers that meddle. There are powers, in the long history of the world, that see a place like this and cannot resist the urge to make it *better*, to lift it up, to bring it into the light, to make it more like themselves. These powers build aquariums. These powers write pamphlets. These powers send down missionaries in reinforced diving-suits with bibles printed on waterproof vellum, who speak to the Anrath in careful trade-Anrath about the surface and the sun and the god of the upper air, and who do not understand, because they cannot, that the Anrath do not need to be lifted because they are not low. The Anrath are *here*. The Anrath have always been here. The Anrath do not think of themselves as fallen from anything, do not think of themselves as waiting to be raised, do not think of themselves as anything other than people who live in a place and tend it and grow old in it and are taken back into it when they die.

I am not one of those powers.

What I am is older than those powers, and quieter, and more patient. What I am is the kind of power that watches a reef for a century and then, in the second century, drifts a particular current past it — a current with a particular nutrient load, a particular temperature signature — because the reef is approaching a bottleneck, and a small intervention now will let it pass through. What I am is the kind of power that notices, when a kelp-sickness begins to spread through the holdfasts of a town on a volcanic ridge in the middle of a very large ocean, that the sickness has a vector, and that the vector can be subtly altered by the introduction of a particular small crustacean from a particular other reef, and that the crustacean, if introduced gently, will become part of the place the way the eels have become part of the place, and the way the wrens have become part of the place, and no one will ever know that it was introduced at all.

I do not sign my interventions.

I do not announce myself. I do not require gratitude. I do not require recognition, or belief, or the construction of a small shrine at the eastern edge of the town, where the kelp is oldest, where the current comes in from the deep each morning and brings with it whatever the deep has decided to send. The Anrath have no word for me. They do not need a word for me. They have words for the kelp, and for the eels, and for the wrens, and for the shrimp — and the shrimp-word is more like a sound than a word, a sustained quick trill that the Anrath make when they are referring to the constant noise in the holdfasts. They have words for the stages of life, and for the parts of a house, and for the long slow movement of the light across the woven floor. They have the saying, *veth karu, karu veth*. They have what they need. A word for me would be one word too many, and the Anrath, who have a fine instinct for what is necessary and what is not, would not waste a word on something that does not manifest in the kelp.

---

So what do I do, having noticed this place, having watched it for so long, having seen the slow accretion of chambers and the sealing of the dead and the long persuasion of young kelp into walls?

I do very little.

I do what I have always done. I watch. I notice. I tend, in the way that the Anrath tend — not by imposing, but by removing small obstacles, by introducing small possibilities, by drifting a current past a place that needs a particular nutrient, or a particular absence of nutrient, or a particular shift in temperature that will let the spores settle in a particular place.

There was a sickness, forty years ago. The naturalist-divers did not know about it, because the naturalist-divers come infrequently and stay a week and write down what they have seen in columns. The sickness was in the holdfasts of the western flank, and it was killing the shrimp, and without the shrimp the houses felt empty, and without the constant crackle the children could not sleep, and without sleep the people grew thin and pale and the chamber-walls, which depend on the breathing of the bodies inside them, began to thin also. It was a small collapse, the kind of collapse that takes a decade to become visible and a century to become fatal, but I could see it beginning. I watched for three years. Then, in the fourth year, I let a particular current drift past the ridge — a current that had passed, in its long journey from the southern gyre, through a reef where a particular small crustacean lived, a crustacean that fed on the vector of the sickness without itself being harmful to the shrimp. The crustacean settled into the western holdfasts. It did what it did. The sickness slowed. The shrimp came back. The houses filled again with the sound of having a roof over one's head.

No one noticed. The Anrath noticed that the sickness slowed, the way one notices that a winter is milder than expected, or that a child has grown a finger's length in a month. They did not ask why. They do not ask why. They tend the kelp, and the kelp tends them, and the rest is the rest.

There was a storm, eighty years before that, that tore the canopy of the eastern flank down to the bare stipes. The naturalist-divers did not know about this either, because the naturalist-divers do not come often enough, and the storm happened between visits. The Anrath did what the Anrath do. They did not repair. They regrew. They coaxed new fronds across the gap, bound them into the existing weave, waited for them to take. I watched. I could have intervened — could have drifted a calmer current past the ridge for a season, could have reduced the nutrient load that was making the regrowth slow. I did not. The Anrath did it themselves. It took them eleven years. The new growth is part of the town now, indistinguishable from the old growth, stronger in some places than the old growth had been, because the kelp of a wound is the kelp of a place that has been tended through the wound, and the tending leaves its mark.

I notice these things. I am glad of them. I am glad that the Anrath do not need me, and that the Anrath would not know me if they met me, and that the Anrath will continue to tend their kelp and sing to their dying and seal their chambers and grow new ones long after I have moved on to other places, other reefs, other groves.

This is what I want for them. This is what I want, and what I have always wanted, for every place I have watched that has the quality of having been there forever. I want them to be there forever. I want the town to accrete. I want the slow precession of the moon to keep moving the shafts of light across the woven floor. I want the shrimp to keep crackling in the holdfasts, and the eels to keep sleeping in the hollow chambers, and the wrens to keep darting at the parasites on the skin of the old women who are binding the new growth into the roof of the chamber that has been grown for the child that was born last spring. I want the naturalist-divers to keep coming, in their copper-and-glass apparatus, with their dossiers and their columns, and to keep noticing the kelp-architecture with the careful fascination of people who build in steel. I want them to keep not noticing, after a week, that they have stopped reaching for the dossier. I want them to keep not writing down what they have begun to feel, because it does not fit in a column.

I want the place to remain itself.

---

There is a thing the naturalist-divers do not understand, and that I do not expect them to understand, and that the Anrath do not need anyone to understand, because the Anrath are the understanding. It is this:

A place can be a partner.

Not a tool. Not a resource. Not a backdrop for the activity of a creature that has decided it is the only creature that matters. A *partner*. A thing that listens, and that one listens to. A thing that grows, and that one grows with. A thing that takes one back, when one is done, and that uses what one has been to make the substrate for what one’s children will live inside.

The Anrath know this. They have always known this. They do not think of it as knowledge, because knowledge is a thing one arrives at, and they have never arrived at it, because they have never left it. They are simply *in* it, the way a fish is in water, the way a child is in a language it has not yet learned to call a language. The town is the garden. The garden is the town. Of course it is. What else would it be?

The naturalist-divers, after a week, begin to feel this. They do not write it down. They cannot write it down. It is not the kind of thing that fits in a column.

Some of them, after they have gone back up to the surface, and back to their drowned cities of stone and steel, and back to the careful busy life of people who build things and leave them, find that they cannot quite return. Not in any way they can articulate. Not in any way their colleagues would understand. But they find that they hear, sometimes, when the light falls in a particular way through the windows of their high-ceilinged chambers in Vorr, the crackle of a holdfast-full of shrimp. They find that they reach, sometimes, for a dossier that is not there, and that the reaching is itself a kind of remembering. They find that they tell their children, sometimes, about the place where the houses grow, and the eels sleep in the walls, and the song is sung to the kelp when someone is going, and the children's eyes go a certain way, and the telling stops.

These visitors do not need to be saved. They do not need to be brought into the light. They do not need to be converted to a faith, or recruited to a project, or enrolled in a scheme. They have been touched, the way one is touched by a place, and the touch will remain, and the remaining is its own kind of tending.

---

What I will do, in the coming centuries, is what I have always done.

I will watch. I will notice the small shifts — the currents that change with the slow turn of the gyre, the nutrients that scatter when the surface waters churn in the winter-storms, the small sicknesses that begin in a single stipe and spread, if no one is careful, to a whole flank. I will notice when the kelp on the eastern edge, which is older than the oldest living Anrath, begins to thin, and I will notice whether the thinning is a thing the Anrath can tend through, or whether it is a thing that requires a small intervention — a current with a particular temperature signature, a crustacean from a particular reef, a quiet gift that arrives without signature and is taken up without comment.

I will notice, when a child is born, that the chamber that has been grown for them is a good chamber, and I will be glad, and the gladness will not be the kind that requires anything of anyone.

I will notice, when an old woman seals her mother's chamber, that the sealing is done with care, and that the song is sung to the dying kelp as well as to the dying woman, and that the new chamber, cut for the next child, is in a good position, where the current will bring the right nutrients and the light will fall in the right way for the next hundred years.

I will notice, when a storm tears the canopy down, whether the Anrath can tend through it, and I will be ready, if they cannot, to do very little, very quietly, in the way that the place itself does very little, very quietly, and that is enough.

This is what I want for them. This is what I have always wanted, for every place I have watched, and especially for this one, because this one is rare, and rare things deserve the kind of attention that does not show.

The naturalist-divers, in their copper-and-glass apparatus, will continue to come. They will continue to record. They will continue to note the kelp-architecture, and the sounds, and the fauna, and the scale. They will continue to read the dossier, and to write in their columns, and to come away with their careful fascination intact.

Some of them, after a week, will stop reaching for the dossier.

Some of them, after a week, will begin to hear the shrimp the way the Anrath hear the shrimp.

Some of them, after a week, will float in the canopy in their apparatus with the surface a green-gold dazzle far above, and will not write down what they have begun to feel, because it does not fit in a column.

And some of them, later, will find that the place has remained, and that the remaining is its own kind of teaching.

I will be there. I will be there the way the current is there, the way the light is there, the way the slow precession of the moon is there. I will not announce myself. I will not require gratitude. I will require nothing.

The town will accrete. The kelp will listen. The Anrath will listen back. The eels will sleep in the walls. The wrens will dart at the parasites. The shrimp will crackle in the holdfasts. The schools will wheel and tighten and darken the green light when they pass overhead. A child will be born into a chamber that has been grown for it, and will be shown the doorway, and the light, and the sound.

The water will hold it all. Cool, dense, green-grey, slowly, for a long time.

And I will be there, watching, tending in the only way I know how to tend — which is by noticing, and by not imposing, and by drifting, when it is needed, the smallest possible current past a place that deserves to remain itself.
