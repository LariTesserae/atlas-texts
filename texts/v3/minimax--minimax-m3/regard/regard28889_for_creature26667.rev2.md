# The Attention

The attention has been here, in a sense, for a long time. Long before the name. Long before the people were a people, before the town was a town, before the goat trail was a goat trail — when what is now a path was only the natural drainage of the higher folds. The attention has been doing what it always does, which is to be present to places that are themselves present to themselves. There are many such places. The attention does not hurry among them. It knows where each one is. It knows when each one is ready to be seen.

The Upper Stoup is ready now.

---

It is noticed first, as so many things are, by its sound. The attention has been hearing the hum of the town for a long time, perhaps, in the way one hears the hum of a distant city without listening to it — a frequency in the background of things, half-felt, half-imagined. The attention turns, now, and listens properly. The hum is there. It is low, almost musical, made by the wind finding every hollow in the rock, every loose stone, every gap in the masonry. It is the town's own voice. It has been going on, without interruption, since the first roof was laid on the first wall, and it will go on, perhaps, until the last stone has settled into the bedrock.

The attention listens to this hum for what might be a long time. It is in no hurry. The hum does not change much. It thickens and thins with the wind. Sometimes, when the wind drops, it disappears, and what is left is the sound of water — the spring, the drips, the small channels that move water from place to place. The attention listens to that too. The water is older than the town. The water is older than the plateau, in some sense, because the water comes from somewhere below, and has been rising through the stone since before the stone was a plateau.

The attention listens to the dogs. The dogs are how the attention knows the place most directly. The dogs are the only creatures who look a person in the eye — and they are also, perhaps, the only creatures who look the attention in the eye. The dogs feel the attention before the people do. They feel it as a stillness, a quality in the air, the way they feel the wind change before the wind changes. They do not bark at it. They do not move toward it. They simply become, for a moment, very attentive themselves. They lift their heads. They listen.

The people notice this, the way they notice everything, without speaking about it. A dog lifts its head, and the people go quiet, and wait.

---

What the attention sees, when it has finished listening, is this.

It sees a place that is at the end of its life. Not in any dramatic way. In the slow way that a stone is at the end of its life when it has been worn down by wind and water until there is very little left of it. The plateau is wearing. The town is wearing with it. The lichen is taking the stones back. The under-town is growing, and the above-town is settling, and the two are becoming harder to tell apart.

It sees a people who have agreed, without ever discussing it, to wear with it. To move at the speed of the place. To wait the way the lichen waits, the way the fog waits, the way the spring waits for the next condensation to gather on the moss. The people are patient. They have made a treaty with the air, and the terms of the treaty are: *do not waste the silence*. They have made a peace with their own ending. They are not fighting it. They are settling into it.

It sees that this peace is sacred. Not in the sense that the people use the word sacred — they do not use the word — but in the older sense, the sense that the attention understands. A peace made with the slow wearing of stone is a peace that does not come often, and does not last long, and must not be broken.

---

The attention deliberates.

This is the slow part. The deliberation takes longer than the listening. The attention considers what it might do. It considers what it might give. It considers the gifts that the people would want — children, food, warmth, longer lives, a spring that runs further, a winter that comes less hard, a wind that does not scour — and it considers each of these, and it sets each of them down.

To give them children would be to break the treaty with the air. The people have few children because the place allows few children. The cold and the thinness of the air see to this. To increase the number would be to change the nature of the place, and the people are not asking for the place to be other than it is. They are asking only to be allowed to continue, at the speed of stone.

To give them food would be the same. The berries, the small grey animals, the dried shrubs — these are what the place provides. To provide more would be to change what it means to live here. The people do not want more. They want what they have, in the measure that the place allows it.

To give them warmth would be the most violent gift of all. The cold is part of the place. The cold preserves the dead in the under-town. The cold is why the lichen runs its particular colors. The cold is what makes the people who they are — rounded forward at the shoulders, deep in the chest, dark of skin, patient in their waiting. To warm the place would be to warm the people, and to warm the people would be to make them into different people, and the people are not asking to be different.

The attention sets these gifts down, one by one.

---

What remains, then, is what does not violate the peace.

The attention begins with the spring. The spring is the heart of the place. The water emerges from a crack in the stone and runs for two hundred paces before it disappears again into the gravel. The attention does not double the spring. It does not make it run for a thousand paces, or become a river. The attention extends the spring by one pace, every hundred years, in a way that no one will notice in any single lifetime. In a hundred years the spring will run two hundred and one paces. In five hundred years, two hundred and five. In a thousand years, two hundred and ten. The people will say, in the oldest memories, that the spring used to run a little less. The lichen-year reckoning will gain a new kind of year — *the year the spring moved*, which will come rarely, and will be remembered when it comes.

This is the first gift.

---

The attention turns to the dogs. The dogs are four now, and one of these is old. The dogs are how the people know the weather. The dogs are the most attentive inhabitants of the place, and the people trust them as they trust the spring.

The attention does not make the dogs immortal. It does not give them the long lives of dogs below, who live in warmth and are fed every day. The attention lengthens the dogs' lives by a measure that is hard to say. A dog that might have lived twelve years now lives fifteen. A dog that might have lived fifteen now lives eighteen. The increase is small. It is enough. It is enough that a person and a dog, who have known each other since the dog was a pup at the person's hearth, can grow old together in a way that the people have not yet known. The dog that follows a person to the end of their life will be the dog that has followed them for most of their life. The person will know, when the dog finally dies, that something has ended. The attention gives this. It is a gift that operates at the timescale of a life, which is the timescale of the people.

The attention also gives the dogs a slightly clearer sense of when one of their people is far. When a trapper is out on the plateau, alone, in the fog, the dogs at home know — not always, not precisely, but more often than they used to — that the person is far. They know whether the person is in danger. They know whether the person is moving toward home or away from it. They communicate this, in their dog way, by shifting in their sleep, by sitting at a certain door, by a particular quality of alertness. The people have always read the dogs. They will read them more clearly now. A trapper who would have been lost will be found. Not every time. Just often enough.

---

The attention turns to the lichen.

The lichen is the painter of the place. The lichen has been painting the town and the substrate for so long that they are, colorwise, continuous. The lichen runs its colors — the deep bottle green, the pale almost-yellow, the near-black, the orange that came in a certain year and is remembered still.

The attention does not change the lichen. The lichen is doing its work. The attention adds, occasionally, a year when the lichen runs a hue that has not been seen before. Once every few centuries, perhaps. The year is not announced. It is not a miracle. The lichen simply runs, that year, a particular color — a blue-grey, say, or a deep red, or a pale silver — and the people mark it, and speak of it as they speak of the orange year. The calendar of lichen-years grows longer. The palette of memory grows richer. The people have more years to remember, and remembering is what they do.

And — this is part of the gift, though it is hard to speak of — the people who live through such a year remember it more vividly than they would otherwise remember it. They remember the color. They remember the year. They remember who was alive then. This too is given. Not much. Just enough that the lichen-year does not fade the way most things fade, but stays sharp in the town's long memory, the way the orange year is sharp in *the one who was born in the lichen year*.

---

The attention turns to the wind.

The wind is merciless. The wind is the constant weather of the place, and it is the wind that does most of the wearing, most of the shaping, most of the slow destruction of the plateau. The wind does not stop. The attention does not ask the wind to stop. To ask the wind to stop would be to ask the plateau to be other than it is.

The attention does something much smaller. When the wind is about to do something terrible — when a gust is gathering that will tear a roof or knock down a wall or take a child who has wandered too close to the rim — the wind pauses. Just for a moment. Just long enough that the worst of the gust passes by while the wind is briefly still. The people, in those moments, feel the wind about to do something, and then not do it. They do not remark on it. They notice, sometimes, that the wind was cruel and then was not. They file this away in the way they file everything away — as information about the weather, as a fact to be remembered, as part of the slow conversation between the people and the place.

This gift is given rarely. Perhaps once every few years, in any given fold of the town. The people do not depend on it. They continue to mend their roofs. They continue to check their walls. They continue to watch their children. But when the worst almost happens and then does not, there is a small kindness in the air, and the people breathe it without knowing they have breathed it.

---

The attention turns to the dying.

The people of the Upper Stoup die as they have always died — in their houses, on the tilted floors, with a family member or two nearby. They are afraid of dying alone. They are afraid of dying in the open plateau. They are not afraid of dying itself.

The attention does not lengthen their lives. It does not shorten them. It does something much smaller. When the moment of death comes — when the body has decided, in its slow way, that it is finished — the attention arranges, if it can, that the wind is quiet. The town hum goes still. The fog, if there is fog, draws back a little. The dogs lie down. The people nearby feel the silence, and the dying person feels it too. The dying happens in a pocket of stillness, as if the plateau itself has paused its wearing for the duration of a single breath.

This is not always possible. The wind does not always cooperate. The fog does not always draw back. But it happens more often than it would by chance. The people have always noticed, sometimes, that a death was gentle. They will notice it more often now. They will not know why. They will simply say, of certain people, that they died well. And the flat stone by the spring, when it is struck, will sometimes ring out into a stillness that is unusually deep, and the people will gather into that stillness, and the gathering will feel, for a little while, like being held.

---

The attention turns to the births.

A child born in the Upper Stoup is born into cold and thin air. The child is carried in a sling of woven wool from the day it is born. The ground is too cold for a child to be put down. The air is too thin for a child to be left to cry.

The attention does not make the births more frequent. It does something much smaller. At the moment of birth, the air itself seems to hold. Not warm — the air does not warm. But still. As if the plateau has paused its wearing for the duration of a single breath. The child draws that breath. The mother draws it too. The breath is not magic. It is a small pocket of stillness, no larger than the room, no longer than the moment. But the child draws it, and the mother draws it, and the child lives, and the mother remembers.

This happens for every birth. Not always as much as the mother would have wished. But enough. The cold takes some of the children, as it always has. The attention cannot prevent this. It can only ensure that the children who are born are born well, and that the moment of their birth is held, briefly, in the air.

---

The attention turns to the strangers.

The strangers come rarely. Once every few years, perhaps. A trader. A runaway. A person fleeing something below. A person seeking something above. The strangers come up the goat trail, and they stay for a few days or a few weeks, and then they go back down, or die, or go out onto the plateau and do not come back. No one stays. The people know this. The stranger knows it too, after a while, and the knowing is not bitter.

The attention does not call strangers here. The attention does not turn strangers away. The strangers come because they come, and the place receives them because the place has always received them.

The attention gives the strangers one thing. The strangers, when they are here, hear the town's hum. The people have stopped hearing the hum because they have always heard it. The strangers have not always heard it. The strangers hear it, for the first few days, as if it were music. The town sings to them. The wind in the gaps of the walls, the water in the channels, the clack of the loom, the low voices, the dogs shifting in their sleep — these come together, for the stranger, into a single sound that is almost a song. The stranger is changed by this. The stranger goes back down the goat trail with a kind of tuning in their ears. They listen, for the rest of their lives, in a way they did not listen before. They hear, in cities, in towns, in the warm air below, a faint echo of the hum. They do not always know what it is. They do not always remember where they heard it. But it is in them, and it tunes them, and it makes them, perhaps, slightly more patient than they would otherwise have been.

This gift is given to every stranger who stays long enough to hear it. It is the only gift the attention gives that is meant to leave the place.

---

The attention turns to the trappers.

The trappers are set apart. They are the ones who go out onto the open plateau. They are the ones who have stood in the silence of the place and have heard the wind without any buildings to break it up. They have seen, on clear days, the rim of the plateau in every direction, and the folds of the stone going off to the horizon, and the nothing that is out there. They come back changed. They do not talk about it.

The attention watches the trappers more closely than it watches the others. The trappers are most at risk of being lost. The trappers have made a trade — a piece of themselves, given to the plateau, in exchange for the meat that the town needs. The attention does not refuse this trade. The trade is the trappers' choice, and the plateau's choice, and it is sacred in the older sense.

The attention does something for the trappers when they die. Sometimes a trapper goes out and does not come back. The people strike the flat stone, and they wait, and the trapper does not come. The trapper has died out on the plateau, alone, in the fog or the wind or the cold.

When this happens, the attention is there. Not to save the trapper. The trapper has agreed to this. But to be present, in some way, so that the dying is not wholly lost. The attention receives the trapper into the silence of the place. The plateau, which has taken a piece of the trapper, gives back something — a gentleness, a being-there. The trapper dies as the people die, in a pocket of stillness, even though no one is nearby to witness it. The wind pauses. The fog, if there is fog, draws back. The trapper draws a last breath, and the breath is held.

The people do not know this. They strike the flat stone, and they wait, and they accept, and they go back to their work. But the trapper has not died alone. The trapper has died, as the place receives its dead, into the under-silence of the plateau, and the plateau has been kind.

---

The attention turns to *the one who was born in the lichen year*.

She is the oldest person now. She does not know how old she is. The people do not count years the way people below do. She sits by her hearth, in a room that smells of dried berries and damp wool, and she answers the questions of the people who come to her. She does not know how old she is, but she knows how the place feels. She knows how it has felt for as long as she can remember, and she has a sense, sometimes, of how it felt before that, when her mother was alive and the spring ran further — no, further is not the word. The spring ran differently. The water came out of the rock for three hundred paces instead of two hundred. She says this. She is listened to.

The attention does not make her live forever. It gives her one thing. A memory of a memory. A sense of how the place felt, a generation before, that she did not consciously remember. So when she speaks of how things used to be, she is slightly more accurate than she would have been. The people trust her words a little more than they would otherwise trust them. They go away from her hearth with a slightly firmer sense of what was, and what has been lost, and what continues.

This gift is given to her alone. It is not given to anyone else. It is the gift of the oldest, who carries the memory for the town.

---

The attention turns to the under-town.

The under-town is the older houses, the ones beneath the current houses, the passages that lead nowhere. The dead are laid there, wrapped in woven cloth, on stone shelves, in low rooms that are closed with flat stones. The cold preserves them. The damp preserves them. Sometimes, in certain years, when an old wall is taken down for repair, a body is found from a generation ago, dried and dark and perfectly kept, and the people look at it and recognize something of the face and go back to their work.

The attention does nothing for the under-town. The under-town is already doing its work. The dead are kept. The dead are part of the place. The boundary between the human and the not-human, in the under-town, is already unclear, and the attention does not make it more or less unclear. The dead are at peace. The attention leaves them in their peace.

---

The attention has now done what it will do.

The gifts are patient. They operate at the timescale of the place. They do not announce themselves. The spring will run one pace further, every hundred years, and no one will notice in any single lifetime. The dogs will live slightly longer, and the people will love them slightly more deeply, and no one will know why. The lichen will run a new color, once every few centuries, and the people will mark it, and speak of it, and remember it with a sharpness that does not fade. The wind will pause, occasionally, before it does something terrible, and the people will notice, sometimes, that the worst did not happen. The dying will find a stillness, more often than it would by chance. The births will find a stillness, every time. The strangers will hear the hum, and go back down the goat trail with a tuning in their ears. The trappers, when they die alone, will not die alone. The oldest person will remember slightly more than she would have remembered. The dead will rest.

The attention does not withdraw. There is nothing to withdraw from. The attention was already here. The attention will continue to be here, in the way that it was here before, doing what it has always done — being present to places that are themselves present to themselves. The Upper Stoup will not know it is being attended. The Upper Stoup will continue as it has continued, at the speed of stone, and the attention will be one of the things it continues with.

---

The benevolent and wise power, then, does very little. It does what the place is already doing, only slightly more carefully. It does what the people are already doing, only slightly more kindly. It extends the spring by a pace. It lengthens the dogs' lives by a measure. It adds a color to the lichen, every few centuries, and sharpens the memory of it. It gives the wind a moment's pause. It gives the dying a pocket of stillness. It gives the births a held breath. It gives the strangers a song to take with them. It watches the trappers, and receives them when they are lost.

It does not increase the people. It does not warm the air. It does not extend the plateau's life by a single century. It does not, in any way, change the nature of the place.

What it does is honor the peace the place has made. It says, in its slow way: *You are doing this right. You are moving at the right speed. You are waiting the right way. Continue.*

The Upper Stoup continues.

The fog moves through it, thinning and thickening with the hours. The lichen grows on the stones, a little more each year. The spring runs its two hundred and one, then its two hundred and two, then its two hundred and three paces. The dogs grow old a little more slowly than they used to. The people fetch water, mend roofs, gather berries, speak softly. The children learn the lanes of the town by heart — both the above-town and the under-town — and they are not really two things. The wind does not stop. The plateau wears down. The town settles into itself. The dead rest in the under-town, kept by the cold. The flat stone by the spring is struck, sometimes, and the sound travels very far, and the people gather into the sound, and the sound holds them, briefly, and then lets them go back to their work.

And the attention is there. As it has always been there. Doing what it has always done. Adding, by almost imperceptible degrees, to the patience of the place.
