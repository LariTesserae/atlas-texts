The power has been paying attention for a long time. This is the first thing to say about it, and it is also, perhaps, the only thing that needs to be said, because what the power does is, in the end, a function of how it has been paying attention.

The power did not arrive. The power arrived the way a river arrives — it has always been arriving, you have only now come to stand at the right place to see it. The city drew the power's attention the way a body draws the attention of a physician who has been walking past it for years and has only now noticed the limp. The power has been passing through the basin for — it does not matter how long. It has passed through as weather, as the slow accumulation of soil, as the patient work of water against stone. It has been present in the cloud layer. It has been present in the slow strange. It has been present, perhaps, in the particular quality of attention that the city has been receiving from its own inhabitants for centuries, the kind of attention that is not worship but acknowledgment, the kind that says: *I see you. I know you are here. I do not expect you to fix anything.*

The power is not the planners. This must be said clearly, because the planners are also a kind of power, and they have also been paying attention, and the city has tolerated them, but the city has never confused them with the power. The planners come with projects. The planners come with concrete. The planners come with the belief that the water is the problem. The power comes with the knowledge that the water is the city, and the city is the water, and the problem and the solution are the same long sentence spoken in two different registers that the city is literate in and the planners are not.

The power, having drawn the attention that the city has drawn, takes a position.

Not on the platform. Not in the colonial center. Not in the foundry district. The power takes a position that is not a position, which is to say it distributes itself through the city's arrangements in a way that does not displace anything. It enters the basalt under the river. It enters the slag heaps. It enters the masonry of the cathedral around the ceiba roots. It enters the deep eaves of the 1880s buildings. It enters the permanent bloom in the corners of the room without a window. It enters the lungs of the foundry worker. It enters the bread smell in the woman's bag. It does not displace. It does not announce. It makes itself useful at the joints.

What it actually does is small. What it actually does is the kind of thing that the city would do if the city had hands.

---

In the foundries, it begins — slowly, in a way the city will incorporate into its own slow — to change the mineral content of the slag. Not all the slag. Not even most of the slag. Just the particular fraction that, when it goes up the flue and into the permanent orange undertone of the sky at that altitude, becomes the orange dust that lives in the weave of the foundry worker's cuffs and that lives, in the foundry worker's lungs, as the second weather of his chest. The power does not remove the slag. The slag is part of the city's arrangement with the river, which carries it to the marshes, which become the substrate for the reeds, which become the substrate for the small fish, which become the protein on Fridays. The slag is a good. The slag is also a harm. The power changes a fraction of it — a small fraction, an alchemical fraction, the kind of fraction that alchemists used to look for and never quite find — so that the slag becomes both its old self and something else. The change is small enough that the city's planning apparatus does not notice it. The change is large enough that the foundry worker, on a Tuesday in the late afternoon, when the air thickens, finds that he can breathe around the second weather of his chest more easily. He does not name this. He attributes it to the season. He is not wrong. The season has changed.

---

The cathedral tree: the power enters the negotiation between the ceiba and the masonry. The ceiba and the masonry are already in a stable arrangement — the power does not improve the arrangement, only makes it slightly more durable, so that the next time the cathedral is repaired around the tree, the repair holds longer, the tree does not push back as hard. The tree and the cathedral, in a hundred years, will be so structurally integrated that they will be the same building. The power does not aim at this. It only loosens the conflict at the joints. The old man who gathers the leaves will, in his eighty-second year, fall asleep one morning on the bench by the cathedral and not wake up, and the power will be there, and the power will hold his hand, and the leaves he has not yet gathered will fall around him like a kind of attendance.

---

The Virgin in the water: the power does nothing to the Virgin. The Virgin and the water are in equilibrium. The brown stain is the water's signature. The power blesses the equilibrium. The blessing is small. It is the kind of blessing that, in a hundred years, will have stabilized the plaster under the Virgin's feet so that the chapel does not have to be re-floated every twenty years. The congregation on the footbridge in the dry month will not know this. They will say the chapel is holding better. They will attribute it to the masonry. They will be partly right.

---

The rats and the cats: the power adjusts the territorial mathematics slightly. It does not favor the rats or the cats. It only opens a few additional cellar routes that both parties will discover over the next few decades, and which will relieve the pressure on the older cellars, and which will, in some small way, reduce the friction between the two populations. The cats remain named. The rats remain unnamed. The arrangement continues, slightly less brittle than before.

---

The brown birds: the power does something to the cloud layer above the basin. It does not thicken the cloud, exactly. It adjusts the way the cloud catches the radar return from the bird flocks at dusk. The radar will still register them as precipitation. The meteorologists will still not find this funny. But the precipitation will be, in some marginal way, a kind of actual precipitation — a faint mist, a barely-there condensation — that the birds will carry down with them into the eucalyptus, and that will be, for the trees, a gift. The trees, in a hundred years, will be sixty-five meters tall instead of sixty. The birds will not know this. The meteorologists will not know this. The trees will know.

---

The parakeets: the power leaves them alone. The parakeets are in equilibrium with the foundries. The parakeets and the foundries are the same sound. This is correct. This is the city's register. The power has nothing to add, and the wisdom of having nothing to add is, in this case, the whole of what the power has to offer.

---

The foundry worker: the power does not heal him. He is not sick in a way that can be healed. The second weather of his chest is part of him now, part of the city's being. The power only adjusts the second weather slightly, so that it is less weather and more climate — less acute, less daily, more like the wet air itself, something he lives inside rather than something that visits him. His grandchildren, who imitate the parakeet call without thinking, will inherit a chest that is more like their grandfather's was at twenty. This is not a cure. This is a softening. The softening is enough. The softening is, in the city's register, the whole of what healing would mean here.

---

The woman on the platform: the power does nothing. The woman is in equilibrium. The woman has refined patience to an art. The woman's novel is good. The pillar has a small worn concavity at shoulder height where generations of leaning have polished the concrete into something almost friendly, and the power does not add itself to the concavity, because the concavity is full. The woman does not need the power. This is, perhaps, the highest compliment the power can pay — to recognize a being that is already complete, and to leave the completeness alone.

---

The dogs: the power does nothing. The dogs are in equilibrium. The dogs know which platforms run at which hours. The dogs understand the parakeets. The dogs are the city's arrangement with itself. The power has nothing to add.

---

The river: the power does not change the river's course. The river's course is the city's memory. The river is allowed to keep moving. The power only — and this is very small, this is almost nothing — adjusts the silt content by a fraction, so that the river's brown has slightly more green in it, and the green of the marshes is slightly more durable through the dry months, and the small fish are slightly more plentiful on Fridays. The foundry worker's grandchildren, who imitate the parakeet call, will inherit a river that is, in some marginal way, more alive. The change will not be measurable. The change will be known, in the way that the people of the lowest neighborhoods know where the silt will go before it goes there.

---

The clouds above the foundry district: the power adjusts the glow. It does not turn off the glow. The glow is part of the city's arrangements. The foundry workers' children do dream in color — this is a guess the city has been making about itself, and the guess is correct, and the power confirms it without saying so. The power, however, makes the glow slightly less amber and slightly more — something. Not white. Not blue. Something that does not have a name in the local languages but that the children, in their dreams, will be able to name. The dreams will be slightly more themselves. The foundry workers will not know this. Their grandchildren will.

---

The barrio alto: the power adjusts the cloud layer at the altitude of the barrio alto, very slightly, so that the cloud is less often a wall and more often a veil. The walk up will still take an hour and a half. The walk will still gain six hundred meters of elevation. But halfway up, when the cloud layer passes, the visibility will be slightly better. The residents will be able to see, on certain afternoons, the basin below them. They will be able to see the foundry glow. They will be able to see the cathedral tree emerging from the colonial center. They will not name what they see. They will know what they see. This is the city's oldest skill.

---

The young people who have gone up the hill or down to the foundry district or out to the slope neighborhoods: the power does not bring them back. The young people are doing what the city needs them to do, which is to circulate the city's being through the larger region, the way the river circulates the silt. The power only — again, very small — makes the circulation slightly less extractive. The young people who leave will, on certain visits home, find that they can breathe a little easier in the basin. They will attribute it to the season. They will not be entirely wrong. They will stay an extra day, sometimes. They will not know why.

---

The planners: the power does not educate the planners. The planners are tolerated the way a person is tolerated who has misunderstood something fundamental about you but is trying to help. The power does not change this. The power only — and this is perhaps the most delicate intervention of all — adjusts the planners' dreams. The planners will begin to dream, occasionally, of water in basements, of trees growing out of bell towers, of parakeets in cooling towers, of rivers that have moved three times and are still moving. They will not act on these dreams. They will attribute them to indigestion. But the dreams will accumulate. In a hundred years, a planner will be appointed who has dreamed these dreams more often than not, and who will propose a drainage project that does not channel the water but reroutes it, and the project will work, and no one will know why. The city will know. The city has always known.

---

The city itself: the power does not bless the city as a whole. The city does not need to be blessed. The city has drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, and the city's response to this — the response the power can feel, the response the power attends to — is the city's own slow being, its own patience, its own long-eroded balance. The city's response is: *I am here. I have been here. I will continue.*

The power, having done what it has done, withdraws. The withdrawal is not visible. The withdrawal is the kind of withdrawal that the rain makes when it stops — it is in the air still, it is in the soil, it is in the masonry. The power does not leave. The power becomes part of the city's arrangements, the way the slag is part of the river, the way the silt is part of the marshes, the way the ceiba is part of the cathedral, the way the parakeets are part of the foundry noise. The power is in the joints. The power is in the seams. The power is in the lifted cobbles around the plaza. The power is in the brown stain at the hem of the Virgin's robe. The power is in the second weather of the foundry worker's chest, slightly softened. The power is in the concavity at shoulder height on the pillar, which the power did not add to but which it now knows about, which it now attends to, which is, in some sense that does not require a name, a small part of why the concavity will continue to deepen for another hundred years.

The power has been changed by the city. This should also be said. The power arrived knowing many things. The power did not arrive knowing how to be patient in the city's particular register, which is not the patience of a person waiting for a thing, but the patience of a thing that is the thing it is waiting for. The power did not arrive knowing that a being can hold itself in balance with its own harms without redeeming them and without being destroyed by them. The power did not arrive knowing that a slime can be a good and a harm and a cycle and a dinner. The city taught the power these things, in the city's own time, in the city's own voice, which is the voice of the river, which is the voice of the cloud, which is the voice of the foundry, which is the voice of the parakeets, which is the voice of the woman on the platform turning a page of a novel about a woman in another city who is also waiting for things.

The power leaves more patient than it arrived. The power leaves more wet.

---

The train comes. The doors sigh. The power, if it is on the train, does not announce itself. It rides. It leans, but does not sit. It reads, but does not absorb. It watches the dark mouth of the tunnel. It waits, in the working-class patience of the place, in the *vecino* patience, in the patience that is not waiting but attending, for the city to tell it where to get off.

The city, in its own time, in its own slow voice, will.

The train begins to climb. The platform recedes. The power is taken up into the folds of the city, into the noise, into the wet, into the warm. The doors close behind it with the particular pneumatic sigh of a thing that is weary of opening and is also, in its own way, glad.

The city continues, patiently, in its own time, with its river moving, its foundries glowing, its parakeets screaming, its cathedral tree growing, its Virgin standing in the water, its bloom returning to the corners of the room without a window, its second weather softening in the chests of the foundry workers' grandchildren, its planners beginning, in their dreams, to learn what the city has always known.

The cycle is not a metaphor. The cycle is the city. The cycle is the power's attention, returned to it as a kind of weather, as a kind of wet, as a kind of permanent low-level amber that is reflected off the low cloud and returned to the streets as a kind of warm residue.

The cycle continues.

The train climbs.

The city is not finished.
