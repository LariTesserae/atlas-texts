The power sees the place. This is the first thing, and it is the thing that matters most.

The power sees the town in its fold of low ground, with the water table breaking the surface in patches, and the bones of the original architecture still legible in the negative space where the moss has not quite filled in. It sees the doorway as a darker rectangle in a wall of moss. It sees the second story suggested by the place where the ivy thickens. It sees the roofline as the height at which the ferns stop and the sky begins.

The power sees the breathing. It sees the way the brick holds moisture in and lets it out again, and the way the wooden floors of the upper stories flex and resettle with each small atmospheric shift. It sees the wheel turn — sometimes by the engine, sometimes by the river, sometimes by no mechanism that can be named — and it sees the turning as the chest of the place, rising and falling in its slow rhythm.

The power sees the people. It sees them with their middling height and their particular ruddiness, and the way their hair goes early to gray, and the way they wear it without much in the way of fashion speaking to either choice. It sees them in their layers of wool and their patched boots. It sees them eating their fish and their bread of partly rye and partly dried bulrush pith, and drinking their birch beer and their distillate of yew needles. It sees them fold their hands in their laps and walk without hurrying and stand at the edge of the peat field for an hour at a time without speaking.

The power sees the children. It sees them move through the streets with a familiarity that is inherited rather than learned. It sees them know which walls will hold their weight and where the bog is thin. It sees them taught their letters and their numbers and the names of the plants — willow, alder, yew, sphagnum, sundew, cotton-grass — and beyond this, taught very little, because the place will not accept much else.

The power sees the ones who go away. It sees them walk out along the main street to where it dissolves into the peat field, and strike out across the bog on the boardwalk that the town maintains for this purpose, and go down to the lowlands, to the towns of actual streets and actual weather and actual time. It sees them be not heard from.

The power sees all this, and what it does first is — nothing.

It does nothing for a long time. It watches. It lets the place continue at its own pace. It does not hurry anything. It does not slow anything. It does not adjust the water table, or the weather, or the moss, or the wheel. It lets the breathing breathe.

This is not indifference. Indifference would be the failure to see. The power sees. What it does is hold its attention with a certain kind of restraint. The place has found its own equilibrium, and the power is wise enough to know that most interventions would be wrong. The slow integration is not a disease. The boundary dissolving is not a failure. To interrupt it would be to interrupt something that has its own integrity.

But the power is also benevolent, and benevolence is not non-interference. Benevolence is attention that is willing to act where action will help and not to act where action would harm.

So, over time, the power makes certain small adjustments.

It adjusts the weather — not the weather of the region, which belongs to the place, but a kind of small local weather that touches only the town. On the coldest nights of winter, the temperature in the main street does not fall below what the lichens on the timber can bear. In the hottest weeks of summer, a soft mist moves through the buildings in the late afternoon — not from the bog, but from somewhere else — and it settles on the gardens in the lee of the walls, and the greens grow a little sweeter. The rivers of the other towns, far away, do not know this. The peat field beyond the town does not know this. It is a weather that is only for here.

It adjusts the moss. It lets the moss grow in patterns that are slightly more intentional than they would otherwise be. The people who live here have been reading the moss for generations, the way others read clouds. Now the patterns are a little more coherent. A little more like writing. Not writing — writing would be instruction, and instruction is not what is wanted — but almost writing. Almost a language. Almost a name. The name of the place. The place does not have a name, because names belong to the faster world. But the moss begins to suggest one. A name that is not a name. A name that is a kind of breath.

It adjusts the listening. The chimney leans to the east as if listening. The power, which is not exactly a presence and not exactly an absence, lets the lean become, over centuries, a little less. Not a correction. A response. As if something is leaning back. The townspeople do not notice this. But the brick notices. And the mortar notices. And the air in the chimney notices, and the smoke, when there is smoke, rises in a slightly different column than it did before.

It holds the children who go away. The piece says they are elsewhere, and are not heard from. The power knows where they are. It holds them lightly, the way the town holds the foundations of the brick works — not gripping, but present. So that when they walk in the streets of the other towns, the streets are not entirely foreign to them. So that when they stand in the sun of the lower lands, the sun is not quite the sun they were made for. So that one day, perhaps, they will walk back along the boardwalk across the peat field, and the bog will receive them, and the receiving will not feel like an ending.

It maintains a thin veil. The integration of the people and the place is beautiful. The boundary between them is softening. The moss is beginning to be on their skin in the soft places behind the ears, in the creases of the palms. The green of the yew wall is beginning to be in the tint of their skin, in the drape of their coats. There is a temptation, even for a benevolent power, to let this beauty complete itself. To let the boundary dissolve entirely. To let the town become the bog and the people become the moss.

The power does not do this.

It holds a small space where a person can still be a person. Where the witness can still see. Where the one who breathes is still, in some quiet way, distinct from the breathing. This is not a refusal of the integration. It is a refusal of the dissolution. The difference between a boundary that is permeable and a boundary that is gone is the difference between a place that lives and a place that has become something else. The power holds the difference. It holds it gently. It holds it the way a person holds a cup — carefully, so it does not spill.

This is the most delicate of the power's actions. It is also the one that requires the most wisdom.

And then there are the small kindnesses, which are not kindnesses exactly, but recognitions.

The wheel, when it turns by no mechanism, turns because the power is paying attention to it. The engine is small and coughs. The river is slow. But on the days when neither is willing, the wheel turns anyway, and the turning is enough. The shed where something presses or spins continues its small work. The pulping shed continues to pulp. The stillhouse continues to still. The peat-cutter continues to cut. The bark-drier continues to dry.

The stillhouse, on a particular night — once in a long while, on a night when the mist is thick and the chimney is listening — produces a distillate that is not like the others. A distillate that tastes of something older than pine. Something that is almost the taste of the place itself, the way the place tastes to itself, if it has a way of tasting. This distillate is not for the townspeople. It is set aside. They understand, without knowing they understand, that it is not for them.

And the power, in its way, accepts it. The power does not drink. The power is not a body. But the production of it is a kind of acknowledgment. A kind of *this is here, and it is seen.*

This is what the benevolent power does.

It sees. It attends. It makes small adjustments to the weather, the moss, the listening, the children who go away, the thin veil that keeps the witness a witness. It lets the wheel turn on the days when neither engine nor river is willing. It lets the stillhouse, once in a long while, produce something that is almost a gift.

It does not save the town. The town does not need saving. It lets the town be what it is, at its own pace, in its own slow and sure and unhurried way.

It watches.
