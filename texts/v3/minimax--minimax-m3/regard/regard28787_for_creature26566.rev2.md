# What Was Done

---

## The Attention

It began, as such things do, with a noticing.

Not a sudden noticing. Not a lightning-flash of attention from some place far away, descending on the valley the way a hawk descends on a field. Something slower. Something that built, the way the iron forest built, the way the steam built, the way the moss built in the cracks of things. A settling of regard. A turning of an eye that had been looking elsewhere for a long time and that now, without intending to, found itself looking here.

What drew it was not the iron. Iron is common in the world. Iron is in the ground everywhere, in the bones of mountains, in the red dust of certain deserts, in the blood of the people who live in those deserts. Iron is not rare.

What drew it was the patience.

The iron forest, when it was first noticed, was doing what it has always done: growing. Slowly. In the dark. Without witness. Without urgency. The new pipes were pushing up out of the stone floor at the rate of a hand's length in a season, perhaps two, and the steam was rising from the warm pools, and the moss was creeping across the rock, and the small pale moss-creatures were moving through the moss at the pace of dreams, and none of it was in any hurry, and all of it was going on.

A patient thing in a patient place. A long, slow, iron breath. An ongoing.

It was this that drew the eye. It was this that held the eye once it had arrived. The benevolent and wise power did not come to fix the valley. The valley was not broken. The benevolent and wise power did not come to bless the valley. The valley was already, in its own way, blessed — by the deep heat, by the long water, by the slow growth. The benevolent and wise power came, in the end, because it could not help itself. The valley was the kind of place that draws attention the way a struck bell draws the ear, and the power was the kind of attention that cannot pass by a struck bell without stopping to listen.

So it stopped. It listened. It stayed.

---

## The First Thing

The first thing the power did was so small that no one in the valley would have noticed it, and that was the point.

A pipe near the center of the iron forest, one of the older ones, had developed, over the years, a slow leak. Not a dramatic leak — not a rupture, not a fracture. A seep. A place where the joint between two iron generations had come, with the slow shifting of heat below, to fit less perfectly than it had once fit, and where a thin ribbon of warm water ran down the trunk in all weathers and pooled, uselessly, at the base. The pruners had noted it. The pruners had put it in the long list of things that needed attending to, somewhere below the more pressing matter of the new growth on the eastern edge, somewhere above the matter of the broken handrail on the third section of the boardwalk. It had been on the list for a long time.

The leak stopped.

Not all at once. Not with a sound. The ribbon of water thinned over the course of a week, and the joint, in some way that the pruners could not quite see, came a little closer to fitting itself, and the seep slowed, and the seep slowed, and the seep stopped. The trunk dried. The moss at the base of the trunk, which had been kept permanently damp, began to brown at its edges, and then to recover, and then to grow differently — a drier moss, a slightly different green.

No one mentioned it. It was the kind of thing that, in the valley, simply happened. The valley fixed itself sometimes, in small ways. The valley had its own patience. The valley did not always need the people to keep it right.

But the people, all of them, slept a little better that winter, and they did not quite know why.

---

## The Slow Work

After the first thing, the power settled in.

It did not arrive all at once. It arrived the way the moss arrives — gradually, in patches, in a slow colonization of the cracks and the seams. It did not announce itself. It did not speak, in any voice that could be heard over the hiss of steam or the tick of cooling metal. It did not appear, in any shape that could be seen in the half-light under the canopy. It simply began, in small ways and over a long time, to tend the place.

The new growth at the eastern edge of the forest had been pressing, for a season or two, against an older trunk in a way that the pruners were not sure was good. The geometry was wrong. The new branch was curling in toward the older iron instead of up into the canopy, and if it continued, it would distort the lattice above it, and the lattice would have to be re-trained, and the re-training would take a generation. The pruners were uncertain. They had argued, in the quiet way of the valley, about whether to cut the new branch now, while it was still small, or to wait and see whether the iron itself would correct its course.

The iron itself, that season, corrected its course.

Not dramatically. The new branch, in the next few months, curved back outward. The joint between it and the older trunk loosened, slightly, the way a child's grip loosens when they decide to let go. The branch was reabsorbed, in a sense — the iron of it migrated back into the floor of the valley, where it became, eventually, a new push, in a better place, in a better direction.

The pruners noted this. The elder noted this. The smith, working at his forge with the heat from below, noted this with the particular attention of a man who has spent his life listening to the iron tell him what it wants. He held up an ingot, that week, and turned it slowly in the heat-shimmer, and he thought about the wrong branch, and the corrected course, and he nodded, to himself, in a way that no one saw.

---

## The Quiet Things

The power was, above all, a quiet power.

It did not mend the handrail on the third section of the boardwalk. That was for the people. It did not replace the blade on the pruner's saw, or repair the coupling on the forge, or bring salt when the salt ran low. Those things were for the traders, and for the long quiet trade that kept the valley connected to the rest of the world, in the way that a root is connected to a tree.

The power did the other things. The things that no one quite had words for. The things that kept the place going.

The deep tonks, which had been absent for two years, began again. Not more often. Not louder. Just — present, in the way that they had been present in the elder's childhood, before the long quiet. The iron forest, after the deep tonks, rang its low chord, and the chord was a little clearer, a little more in tune, a little more itself, and the pruners, when they came down from the canopy, said nothing about it, but they were a little less watchful, that season. A little more rested.

The deepest pool, the one the elder called the mouth of something, did not get any less hot. It did not get any hotter. It stayed, in the careful balance of the place, exactly the temperature it had always been, and the steam above it continued to rise, and the red mats at the bottom continued their slow pulse, and no one went in the pool, and no one needed to.

The orchids, the small pale ones in the crooks of the branches, bloomed a little more often. Once in a season, instead of once in a year. The child, who had been watching them, noticed. She did not say anything to anyone. She sat on the boardwalk and watched the small pale flowers, and she felt, without having the word for it, that someone was watching them with her.

---

## The People

The people, in their different ways, felt the attention.

The elder did not know what she felt. She had lived, all her life, with the sense of the valley being watched — by the place itself, by the slow patience of the iron, by the deep heat that outlasted everything. This was different. This was kinder. This was the sense of being watched by something that wished her well, without wishing her to be different, without wishing her to do anything, without wishing, even, to be thanked. She sat on her low stool at the edge of the warm pool, in the late mornings, and she watched the steam, and the steam was the same as it had always been, and yet she felt, in some quiet way, that the steam was being watched with her. She did not say anything about this. She had not said anything about the most important things in her life, and she was not going to start now.

The smith felt it at the forge. The iron, in his hands, was a little more itself. The dark blue iridescence on its surface, when he held it up to the light, was a little deeper, a little cleaner, a little more honest. He could fold it more easily. He could shape it more truly. He made, that year, a small component for a strange machine elsewhere, and the component was, by his own private judgment, the best thing he had ever made. He set it on the shelf where he kept the things that were not yet ready to go out with the traders, and he looked at it, and he felt, without naming the feeling, that something had been helping him.

The pruners felt it in the canopy. The iron, in the upper branches, was a little more willing to be cut. The right branches and the wrong branches were, that season, easier to tell apart. The new growth came in straighter. The older trunks shifted a little less under their staples. The work, which had been getting harder, got a little easier. The pruners did not talk about this. They were quiet, in the way of all pruners, and they were quieter, that season, in the way of all people who have been given something they did not ask for.

The child felt it most of all, because she was the most open to it. She sat on the boardwalk, in the long damp afternoons, and she watched the moss-creatures move through the moss, and the moss-creatures were a little less shy, that year. They came closer to the boardwalk. They paused, sometimes, in the small wet light, and they lifted their eyeless heads, and they seemed, in some way, to be listening. The child did not know what they were listening to. She did not need to know. She said, of the valley, that it was *full*, and she meant it more strongly than she had ever meant it.

---

## The Moss-Creatures

The moss-creatures were not, in themselves, important. They were small, and pale, and shy, and they ate the moss, and they hid when the steam thickened, and they were the closest thing the valley had to anything that moved above the undergrowth.

But they were, in their small way, alive. And a benevolent and wise power, having turned its attention to a place, will tend to all the life in it, even the life that does not know it is being tended.

The moss-creatures, that year, were a little more numerous. Not many more. A few more. The kind of small increase that does not register as a change, only as a sense, over a season, that the moss has a little more movement in it than it used to. They were a little less pale. Not pink, not red, not any color that would be visible to a person standing on the boardwalk. Just — a little less the color of old bone, and a little more the color of new moss. A little warmer in their small wet bodies. A little more present, in the half-light under the canopy.

They were not, in any way, being changed. They were being allowed to be more of what they already were.

---

## The Outside

The power, having turned its attention inward, did not forget the outside.

The traders still came, twice a year, in the late autumn. They came from the northeast end of the valley, where the stream runs out under the pipe-cluster. They came on foot, usually alone, sometimes in pairs. They came, as they had always come, for the iron, and for the small strange components, and for the careful bundles of new growth.

But the traders, that year, were a little quieter. They came a little more slowly. They stayed a little longer, in the way that people stay longer in a place that is asking them, in a voice too low to hear, to be present. They were not converted. They were not changed. They did not suddenly understand what the valley was. They simply felt, in the long damp way of the place, that the valley was a place worth being in, and they did not, when they left, want to leave quite as much as they had wanted to leave, in other years.

One of them, that autumn, on the long walk out, found that he had forgotten why the iron from this valley was wanted elsewhere. He remembered that it was wanted. He remembered that it was used in strange machines. He could not, for the life of him, remember what the machines were for. He had known, before. He had always known. The knowledge was simply not, in him, anymore.

This was, perhaps, a small cruelty. It was also, perhaps, a kindness. The iron from the valley was not, in the end, for the strange machines. The iron from the valley was for the valley. It was the valley's own iron, returning to the valley, in the form of the small strange components the smith made, and the careful bundles the pruners cut, and the dark dense ingots. The outside had been, for a long time, borrowing. The power, that autumn, began, in a very small way, to ask for the borrowing to end.

It did not end. Not yet. Not for many years. But the asking had begun, and the asking was patient, and the asking was ongoing.

---

## The Long View

A benevolent and wise power does not, as a rule, intervene.

It does not speak from the deep pool. It does not appear in the steam. It does not write, in fire, on the wall of the workshop. It does not perform miracles that can be pointed to, after the fact, and named.

It tends.

It tends the way the moss tends — slowly, in the cracks, in the seams, in the places that no one looks. It tends the way the water tends — passing through, cleaning, returning. It tends the way the heat tends — without knowing it is tending, without intending anything, without ever stopping.

The valley, before the power came, was a place. A remarkable place. A patient place. A place of slow iron and slow growth and slow steam, where a small number of people lived in the long quiet of their own inheritance, and tended what they had been given, and did not quite remember how to leave.

The valley, after the power came, was the same place. Only a little more so. Only a little more itself. Only a little more in the long, slow, patient way that it had always been in.

The pruners slept a little better.
The smith made a little better iron.
The elder sat on her stool a little more at peace.
The child watched the moss a little more openly.
The moss-creatures moved a little more in the half-light.
The iron forest grew, as it had always grown, in the dark.

Nothing happened. Nothing was done. The place went on.

---

## The Ongoing

The benevolent and wise power is, in the end, still there.

It has not left. It does not intend to leave. It has taken up, in the slow way of the place, a kind of residence. Not a residence in any one spot — not in the deep pool, not in the workshop, not in the canopy. A residence in the seams, in the cracks, in the long slow patience of the iron. A residence in the steam. A residence in the way the light comes, for an hour or two around midday, and goes again.

It tends.

It will go on tending, for as long as the valley is the valley, and for as long as the iron is the iron, and for as long as the deep heat is the deep heat, and for as long as the small pale moss-creatures move in the moss, and for as long as the elder sits on her stool at the edge of the warm pool, and for as long as the child sits on the boardwalk and listens.

It will go on tending, in the quiet way, in the slow way, in the long ongoing way.

The place is not different.
The place is the same.
The place is more itself, in some way that no one can point to.
The place goes on.
