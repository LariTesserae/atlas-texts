# The Small Return

The attention comes like the sourceless dim — from nowhere, from everywhere, from the held breath of the limestone itself. It is not a visitation. It is not a theophany. It is more like the way a parent watches a sleeping child from the doorway: present, quiet, willing the sleeper to keep sleeping, willing also to catch them if they fall.

The Underfold has drawn this attention not because it is beautiful, though it is beautiful. Not because it is pitiable, though there are things in it that might be pitied. It has drawn it because it is *settled*. Because it has arrived at its own weight. Because it is, in its inverted way, complete.

The wise power sees this. The wise power knows that completeness is the rarest thing in the world, rarer than beauty, rarer than happiness, rarer than hope. Most places have not yet arrived at what they are. Most peoples are still in transit. The Hanged-Folk are not in transit. They are where they are, and they have been where they are long enough that *where they are* has become indistinguishable from *who they are*.

To disturb such a place would be a kind of violence. To "save" the Hanged-Folk by lifting them to the surface would be to unmake them. To open the ceiling and let the sun pour in would be to give them a gift they could not survive. The wise power knows this. The wisdom is precisely the restraint.

And so the wise power does almost nothing.

Almost.

---

What the wise power does is small.

It is small the way the bone-carvings are small — thumb-sized, the size of a grandmother's bent spine, the size of a pale thing trembling in a black pool. It is small the way the children's bells are small — hazelnut-sized, ringing out a single clear note for each step of the small bare feet. The wise power has learned, from the Hanged-Folk themselves, that this is the scale at which grace moves in the Underfold.

And so the wise power moves at that scale.

It begins with the bells.

The bells have rung for nine generations into the upper dark, calling for someone who does not come, calling across rock and water and forgotten war for an answer that is not coming. The bells do not despair. The bells have been rung by hands that have stopped expecting an answer, and the ringing has become its own purpose, and yet — and yet — there is a quality to a call that goes entirely unanswered that is almost indistinguishable from grief. The Hanged-Folk do not know this. The bells do not know this. But the wise power knows it.

And so the wise power gives the bells back a single hair of their own echo.

It does not give them a voice. It does not send a messenger down through the forgotten war. It does not open a window in the rock to let a distant wind carry the bell-sound across the surface to whomever might be listening there. It does less than all of this. It does almost nothing.

It adjusts, by an immeasurably small fraction, the resonance of the upper rock. Just enough that when the great bronze bells pour their carillon into the cavern, the sound returns from the upper dark with a slightly different shape — not louder, not clearer, but with one additional overtone that was not there before, a faint fifth voice threading through the four. The Hanged-Folk do not consciously notice. They cannot name what has changed. But on the mornings after the first ringing of the changed bells, a few more of them pause in their climbing of the stem-chains. A few more of them look up — briefly, daringly — at the hanging city. A few more of them feel, for the first time in a long time, that the bells are being heard by someone.

Not by anyone who will come. Not by anyone who can answer. But by someone.

And this is enough.

---

Then the wise power turns to the pale things.

The pale things live in the deepest still-pools, in the obsidian water, in the water that has forgotten it is water. They are eyeless. They are the color of uncooked dough. They tremble perpetually, as if perpetually slightly startled by something just out of earshot. They are eaten by the Hanged-Folk without being thought about. The Hanged-Folk do not apologize to them. The pale things would not understand. The pale things are in the tense of things that will never move.

But the wise power sees the tremor.

The wise power sees that the tremor is not quite what it seems. It is not fear. It is not cold. It is something older than either — a vibration that has been running through these small white bodies since before the bells, since before the war, since before the first hand-tools were carried down into the dark. It is the tremor of being. The tremor of being a thing that has been here so long it has forgotten how to be otherwise. The pale things tremble because they are, and being is, for them, a perpetual small shock.

The wise power cannot give them eyes. The wise power cannot give them language. The wise power cannot give them the tense of things that move, because to move is not what they are.

But the wise power can, by an act of attention so slight it might be called a gentleness, ease the tremor by a fraction. Just enough. Just enough that the trembling is no longer the perpetual startle of a body caught in its own existence, but something closer to a hum — a low, sustained, almost-content note that the pale things make by being, the way a struck bell makes a sound by being a bell.

The Hanged-Folk who catch them and boil them notice nothing. The broth tastes the same. The pale things are eaten as they always have been, without being thought about.

But the pools, after the wise power's attention, are slightly different. The water in them catches the faint blue light of the cave-grass baskets that pass along the lowest streets and holds it a moment longer before letting it go. The pale things, when they are lowered on bone hooks and lifted back out, are paler than they used to be, and their trembling is finer. And the few Hanged-Folk who sit sometimes at the edges of the still-pools, looking down into the obsidian water, feel — without naming it — that what they are looking at is not nothing. That the small white trembling things are, in their way, answering.

---

Then the wise power turns to the children.

The children of the Underfold are not supposed to look up. The first thing a Hanged-Parent teaches a Hanged-Child, before the child can speak, is to keep the chin down, to let the gaze rest on the floor, to trust the floor and nothing else. The children who look up too long feel their brains begin to unfasten. The old stories tell of those who looked up for a whole bell-cycle and were never right again.

But the children look up. They cannot help it. The hanging city is above them. The smoke-children play in the smoke-towns. The bells hang in their towers. The whole inverted world is there, asking to be looked at, and the children are children, and they look.

And the wise power sees the children looking.

And the wise power does not stop them.

It does not give them the surface, which would unmake them. It does not give them answers to the questions they have not yet learned to ask. It does something smaller. It gives the gaze itself a kind of padding. A gentleness. A way of looking up that does not begin the slow drop into the inverted city, that does not make the eyes weep, that does not unfasten the brain.

It is as if the air just above the chamber floor becomes, for the children who dare to look up, slightly thicker. Slightly kinder. A medium through which the hanging city can be seen without being fallen into. A way of looking that is not a kind of falling.

The children who are blessed with this — and not all of them are, because the wise power is wise and does not overdo — these children grow up able to look up. They grow up knowing the hanging city as their city, not as an unseeable. They become, in their adulthood, the bone-carvers who carve the small figures of mothers climbing stem-chains, of children chasing children through glowing streets, of bells hanging in their towers. They become the ones who make the beautiful things. They make the beautiful things because they have been able to look.

And the children who cannot be blessed with this — the ones whose brains are too tender, whose stomachs too trustful of down — these children grow up anyway. They grow up to keep their chins down, to trust the floor, to look at the glowing baskets on the belts of their neighbors' children. They are not less for it. The wise power knows that there are many ways to live in the Underfold, and only one of them requires looking up.

---

Then the wise power turns to Old Below.

Old Below is the size of a child of six, bent denser, folded over the decades into the shape of a question mark. She sits on a basalt step all day with a basket of cave-grass on her lap, weaving by touch. The basket glows. Her face is a soft blue cave in itself.

She is not melancholy. She has lived long enough to have ceased wondering about most things, including the surface. But she is bent. She is bent into a question, and the question has no answer, and the question has been her shape for so long that she and the question are one thing.

The wise power cannot un-bend her. To un-bend her would be to unmake her, and the wise power is wise.

What the wise power can do is: on certain nights, after the third carillon has rung and the chamber is at its quietest, when the dripping has become a floor of sound and the smoke-children have gone to their smoke-beds and the pale things in the black pools have quieted to their new, slightly finer trembling — on these nights, the wise power lets Old Below feel that someone is listening to her weaving.

Not watching. Listening.

She does not see anyone. There is no one to see. The cavern is as empty as it always is, of anything but the Hanged-Folk themselves and the pale things and the slow water. But Old Below, on these nights, feels that the sound of her weaving — the small wet whisper of cave-grass bending under her small wet fingers — is being heard by something that understands.

And the understanding is not an answer to her question. She is too bent for answers now. The question she is shaped like will not be answered in her lifetime. But the understanding changes the shape of the question. It makes the question mark a little less a question and a little more a hook on which to hang something — a basket, perhaps, a glowing basket, a basket that someone, somewhere in the upper tiers, will hang from the underside of a doorframe and that will dangle there in the dim and be small and be everything.

---

Then the wise power turns to the language.

The Hanged-Folk have no tense for *soon*. *Soon* is a surface-tense. The Hanged-Folk do not believe in *soon*. They have the tense of things above them and the tense of things that will never move, and these two tenses are enough for almost everything.

The wise power sees this. The wise power respects this. To give the Hanged-Folk *soon* would be to give them a small piece of the surface, and they would not know what to do with it, and it might make them restless.

But the wise power also sees that *soon* is not only a surface-tense. *Soon* is also the tense of a new pendant-tower being carved from the ceiling-rock over the course of a year. *Soon* is also the tense of a child being born into a hanging house and learning, before speech, to keep the chin down. *Soon* is also the tense of a basket of glowing cave-grass being woven by a grandmother's bent fingers and hung on a doorframe. *Soon* is not only the surface's impatience. *Soon* is also the Underfold's slow becoming.

And so the wise power, without fanfare, lets a new click enter the language. Not the surface-click of *soon*. A different click. A wet click, a limestone click, a click that takes its time. A click that the children learn from their grandmothers before they learn the above-tense, because the grandmothers, who have lived longest, have always known that the Underfold is not static but is very, very slow.

The click means *in a little while, in the way that stone moves*. The click is used for the carving of a new pendant-tower. The click is used for the birth of a child. The click is used for the moment when a pale thing is caught and boiled and eaten, and the broth is warm, and the family sits in their hanging chairs and looks at each other upside-down and has tea.

The Hanged-Folk do not name this tense. They do not write it down. They only begin to use it, the way they use the tense of things above them — because they have always used it, because it has always been there, because the language is the language of the wet rock and the wet rock has been speaking it for ten million years.

---

Then the wise power turns to the water.

The water moves through the limestone in capillary threads and emerges everywhere — in beads on the walls, in sheets across the floor, in fine mists that hang in the air like a held breath. The mushroom-farms on the damp ledges are often short. The Hanged-Folk are many. The pale things are boiled.

The wise power does not give the Underfold abundance. Abundance would be wrong here. Abundance would be a surface-gift, and the Underfold is not the surface.

But the wise power eases, by a fraction, the way the water moves through the rock. Just enough. Just enough that the damp ledges where the mushrooms grow are damp a little more reliably, a little more steadily, a little less in the manner of things that will run short and a little more in the manner of things that will be here tomorrow.

The mushroom-farms do not flourish. They have never flourished. They are slow things in a slow place, and the wise power will not make them otherwise.

But they are a little less often short.

And the Hanged-Folk who eat the mushrooms, and the Hanged-Folk who eat the pale things, find that the meals come a little more easily. And the small carved bone figures, in the lower market, in the loudest-dripping tier, begin — slowly, over years — to include a new subject. A mushroom. A small carved mushroom, no bigger than a thumb, hung from a doorframe, glowing faintly with the phosphorescence of the cave-grass that the carver has woven into the carving.

And the Hanged-Folk buy these too, and hang them too, and the small beautiful things multiply by one more small beautiful thing.

---

And then the wise power does one last thing, and then it withdraws its attention, and then it is gone.

The last thing is the smallest.

On certain mornings — not every morning, only certain mornings, only the mornings when the air is stillest and the bells have just finished their first carillon and the chamber is full of the slow falling of sound — on these mornings, a Hanged-Person waking in a hanging house, climbing down the stem-chain to begin the day, will find, in the palm of their broad soft hand, a single pebble.

The pebble is small. It is the color of wheat. It is warm.

It does not belong to the Underfold. There is no stone in the Underfold the color of wheat. There is no stone in the Underfold that is warm in the morning, because the morning in the Underfold is the same temperature as every other time.

The Hanged-Person will hold the pebble for a moment, puzzled. They will turn it over in their hand. They will feel its warmth against the wet of their palm. They will not know what it is.

They will put it in a pocket. They will carry it through the day. When the day is hard, or the dripping is loud, or the pale things are boiled and eaten without being thought about, the pebble will be there, warm against the thigh or the hip or the small of the back. And the Hanged-Person will feel, without knowing why, that the day is a little less hard.

The pebble will not last forever. It will cool, slowly, over days or weeks. When it is cool, it will be just a stone, and the Hanged-Person will set it on a basalt ledge, or under a hanging bed, or on the windowsill of an inverted window, and it will be just a stone.

But on the next certain morning, there will be another pebble.

The Hanged-Folk will never know where they come from. They will not invent a story about them. They will simply keep them, the way they keep the carved bones and the small bells and the glowing baskets — as things that are small, and warm, and theirs.

---

And then the wise power withdraws.

And the Underfold goes on being the Underfold. The bells ring their threefold carillon into the upper dark, with the slightly different resonance, the faint fifth voice. The pale things tremble in the obsidian pools, a little less startled, a little more like bells. The children look up, some of them, and the air is a little kinder for them. Old Below weaves her baskets by touch, and on quiet nights she feels that someone is listening. The language has its new click, the slow click, the click of stone moving. The mushroom-farms are a little less often short. The pebbles appear on certain mornings and warm certain hands.

The Underfold hangs in its own long bell-note of a moment, and the moment does not end, and the people do not ask it to.

Drip. Drip. Drip.

But now and then, between the drips, there is a silence so small it can only be felt.

In that silence, the wise power has been here.
