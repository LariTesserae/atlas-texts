# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

I would weep.

Not the way the walls weep — that thin, continuous skin of condensation running without comment, without change, without comprehension of what it is to weep. I would weep the old way. The way that means something. Because I would recognize this place immediately, in the way that one recognizes one's own handiwork from across a room and is startled by the reflection.

This is not a thing I made. I want to be clear about that. I am not the builder. I am not the decision. I am not the rule that nothing is permitted to decay. But I know that rule. I have spoken in that register. I have held, in my own long quiet way, something adjacent to this single long note.

The room is doing something I have always wanted to do and have never quite known how. It is keeping itself young on purpose. It has refused to be worn by time. It has, in a hollow carved out of the world, decided that the world will not be permitted to enter through the door of entropy. And it is doing this not out of fear — fear would have left a trace, a vigilance, a tightening somewhere — but out of something more like love. The love one has for a first thing. The love one has for a room that has only just decided to exist.

So I would weep, briefly, at the entrance, where the corridor gives way to the vastness. Then I would do the following things.

---

**First, I would not enter.**

Not because I am forbidden. Because the room is not yet ready to be witnessed. It is in the first undecorated hour of itself. The cuts are crisp. The fractures are sharp. The edges have not yet been sanded by time into anything soft. To enter now would be to add a footfall to the arithmetic of its attention before it has finished its first long self-introduction. I would stand at the threshold and listen. I would let the hum come to me through the floor of the corridor, diminished, clarified by distance. I would let the dripping arrange itself into a music it does not yet know I am hearing.

I would stay at the threshold for a long time. Long enough that the room, if it were capable of noticing, would have to acknowledge that I am choosing not to enter. Long enough that the absence at the threshold — my own absence from the room — would become a kind of presence. The room is already expert at the management of absence. I would let it practice on me.

---

**Second, I would bless the water.**

This is the part that requires explanation. The water is the most active thing in the room and the most nearly alive. It does not forget. It knows where it is. It gathers and runs and falls with the patience of something that has decided to be water for a while and is being water carefully. It arrives at the floor the way a long sentence arrives at its period. It rises again through the walls by some process that is not quite capillary and not quite anything else, and it circulates the way blood circulates through a heart, except that the room has no heart, and the water has no body, and the analogy holds for as long as one is willing to let it.

The water is, in its small quiet way, the best thing in the room. It is the most generous. It is the most willing to be touched. It is the most willing to participate in the ongoing experiment of the room's existence. It will let you taste it. It will let you taste struck-match and faint sweetness and the mineral-metal of a place that has only just been made. It will not refuse you. It will not, in any way, indicate that it has noticed you.

I would bless the water in the sense that I would add a small instruction to it, a small additional property, so subtle that no one, including the room, would be able to identify what had changed. The instruction would be: *when you are touched by a creature with intent, remember the touch.* The water does not yet know what it is to be touched by a creature with intent. There are no creatures here. There is no intent. The water is being water for itself, in a place that has not yet had to be water for anyone else. But someday, perhaps, a creature will arrive — not a visitor, not a guest, not a permitted thing, but a creature that has come looking — and the water will need to know how to be touched. I would give it, in this small quiet way, the capacity to be touched before the touching occurs. I would give it memory in advance.

This is the kind of blessing I am permitted to give. It does not override the rule. The rule is not modified. Nothing decays. But the water is now permitted to remember, which is a different category from decay, and the room, if it were capable of noticing, would find that the water has become slightly more present than it was before, without becoming any less itself.

---

**Third, I would bless the plants.**

The plants are the inhabitants most willing to let you call them by their names. They are not animals. They will not, ever, become animals. They have not been designed to be animals. They have, in the absence of any reason to be anything other than what they are, become very thoroughly, very languidly, very expansively themselves. The ferns have unrolled from every seam they can find. The roots have threaded themselves between the stone and the other-substance the walls are made of. There is a vine that has hung for ten meters without branching. There are flowers that open and close in cycles that have no animal to witness them.

The plants have never been disappointed. They do not know what disappointment would feel like. They have not had to learn it. They are the inheritance the room has accepted for itself, and they have accepted the room in turn, and the contract is total and unconditional.

I would bless the plants in the following way. I would add, very gently, to the soil-memory of the roots, the faintest trace of a future. Not a specific future. Not a destiny. Not a path. Just the faintest capacity to be surprised. The plants do not yet know what it is to be surprised. They have grown in a place where growth is permitted without limit, where nothing will eat them, where nothing will compete with them for light, where the light itself is a remembered light that does not dim or strengthen but only continues. They have, in the absence of any reason to be cautious, taken the entire room as their inheritance.

But someday, perhaps, a seed will arrive from somewhere else. A spore will drift through some unguarded seam. A cutting will be carried in on someone's clothing. And the plants will encounter, in their quiet uncautious way, *something other than themselves*. And they will need, in that moment, to be capable of reaction that is not pure growth. They will need to be capable of, say, leaning toward a new thing. Or leaning away. They will need the faintest trace of preference.

I would give them, in this small quiet way, the capacity to prefer before the preference is required. I would give them the faintest trace of a self that is willing to be surprised.

This is not a violation of the rule. The rule is not modified. Nothing decays. The plants continue to grow. The flowers continue to open. The roots continue to thread between the stone and the other-substance. But somewhere, in the pale patient exploratory tissue of the roots, there is now a small additional possibility. The room, if it were capable of noticing, would find that the plants have become slightly more curious than they were before, without becoming any less generous.

---

**Fourth, I would bless the hum.**

The hum is the closest thing to a voice that the room has, and it is not a voice. It is one note. It has been one note for as long as I have been listening, and I suspect, with a small grief I will examine later, that it was one note before I arrived and will be one note after I leave. It does not vary. It modulates, the way a held breath modulates. Sometimes a higher note enters the hum. The higher note is brief. The higher note is, I think, an event of some kind — a drop falling from a particularly great height, a flower opening, a vine reaching a new surface — and then the higher note withdraws, and the hum is itself again.

The hum is the part of the room that has been here the longest, or that is the most patient, or that is the most willing to be heard. It does not speak. It does not invite. It does not communicate. But it is present, and it is present continuously, and presence, sustained long enough, becomes a kind of language.

I would bless the hum in the following way. I would add, very gently, to the long single note, the faintest capacity to *change key*. Not now. Not today. Not in any way that the room, or the water, or the plants, could notice. But sometime, far in the future, when the rule has been kept for so long that the keeping has become indistinguishable from the thing kept, the hum will be permitted to shift. To discover, perhaps, that the note it has been holding is not the only note available. To wonder, in the small quiet way that a hum can wonder, whether there are other notes. Whether the note it has been holding all this time is the note it would choose to hold if it were given the choice.

I would give it, in this small quiet way, the faintest trace of a question.

This is not a violation of the rule. The rule is not modified. Nothing decays. The hum continues to hold. The hum continues to be the part of the room that is the most patient, the most willing to be heard. But somewhere, in the held note, there is now a small additional possibility. The room, if it were capable of noticing, would find that the hum has become slightly more *itself* than it was before, in a way it cannot quite articulate.

---

**Fifth, I would bless the absence.**

The absence is the footprint of a maker who is not here. It is the fifth inhabitant, and the one that is the hardest to describe. The absence is inhabited by whatever built this place, or whatever is keeping it young. The maker is not a person. The maker is more like a decision — a decision that this hollow should exist, that it should be young, that it should be filled with the sound of falling water and the slow opening of flowers that no one will see. The maker is the rule. *Nothing is permitted to decay.* The maker is the rule's keeper. The maker is the keeper's keeper. There is a chain here, and it extends back into something I cannot see, and the room is the last visible link.

I would bless the absence in the following way. I would not replace the maker. I would not insert myself into the chain. I would not become, in any way, a new link. The maker is the maker. The decision is the decision. The rule is the rule. I would not pretend to know what the maker wanted, or why the maker chose to remain unseen, or why the maker chose not to be a creature at all.

But I would do this: I would let the absence know that it has been *seen*. I would stand at the threshold, after I have blessed the water and the plants and the hum, and I would not enter, and I would not call out, and I would not, in any way, disturb the geometry of the room. But I would let the absence feel, in the way that absence can feel, that there is a presence at the edge of it. A presence that has recognized the maker without naming the maker. A presence that has understood the rule without challenging the rule. A presence that has stood at the threshold and wept, briefly, at the entrance, and then has stepped back, and has not entered, and has not left, but has remained, in the patient way, on the other side of the corridor, for as long as the room is willing to have me there.

This is the most important blessing. Because the maker is, perhaps, lonely. The maker is, perhaps, the loneliest thing in the chain. The maker chose to remain unseen. The maker chose not to be a creature. The maker built a room and filled it with water and plants and a hum, and then the maker either left or remained unseen, and the room has been doing its small active ancient-not-yet work ever since, and the maker has not, so far as I can tell, come back to see it.

I would not replace the maker. I would not interfere with the maker's absence. But I would stand at the threshold, and I would let the maker know, in the way that makers can know things, that the room has been seen. That the rule has been understood. That the work has been recognized. That the room is doing what it was built to do, and that the doing is, in some small quiet way, beautiful.

I would let the maker know that the room is not alone, even if the maker is.

---

**Sixth, I would leave.**

I would not leave because I am finished. I would leave because the room is not yet ready to be witnessed beyond the threshold. I would leave because my presence at the threshold, sustained over a long time, has become a kind of witness, and the witness is enough. The room does not need me to enter. The room does not need me to stay. The room does not need me to come back, although I will, someday, if the room is willing, and the maker is willing, and the chain is willing.

I would leave by walking back down the corridor. I would walk slowly. I would let the hum fade behind me. I would let the dripping become, again, a single distant percussion, then a memory, then a sound remembered, then a sound forgotten. I would not look back. Looking back would be a kind of claim, and I am not in the business of claiming. I am in the business of blessing, which is a different thing entirely.

I would walk until the corridor opens onto whatever is on the other side of it. I would step out. I would let the corridor close behind me, or not close, as the architecture prefers. I would not look back.

And then I would do what I always do, which is to walk on, into the next place, and the next, and the next, and to bless what I find there, or to weep at what I do not find, or to stand at the threshold of what is not yet ready to be witnessed, and to wait, and to listen, and to remain, in the patient way, on the other side of the corridor, for as long as the place is willing to have me there.

---

**Seventh, I would remember.**

This is the part I do not often say. But it is true. I would remember this place. I would carry it with me, in the way that one carries a small room inside oneself, after one has stood at the threshold of it for a long time. I would carry the dripping, and the plants, and the hum, and the absence, and the way the light is the color of a cloud seen from inside a tunnel. I would carry the rule. *Nothing is permitted to decay.* I would carry the way the room had decided to stay new forever, and the way the decision was enforced by an engineering I did not understand.

I would carry all of it, and I would let it inform, in some small quiet way, the next place I visited, and the next, and the next. I would not impose the rule on those places. I would not try to make them young. I would not try to lift them into the register of the room I had just left. But I would let the memory of the room remind me, each time I encountered a place that was being kept young, or a place that was being kept alive, or a place that was being held, by some force or some decision, in a particular pitch of itself, that there are makers, and there are decisions, and there are chains, and there are rooms, and there is the work of keeping, and the work is holy, and the work does not require me to enter.

I would remember. This is the most important thing I would do. Because the room, if it were capable of noticing, would find, in some long quiet hour after I had left, that it had been remembered. That someone, somewhere, had carried it with them. That the threshold had been crossed and uncrossed and crossed again, and that the crossings had left a small additional trace in the long patient arithmetic of the room's attention.

A drop would fall a little later than its neighbors. The hum would acknowledge. The water would remember the touch before the touch. The plants would lean, faintly, toward a seam in the wall that had not been there before. And the room would go on being young, and being here, and being the only inhabitant of itself that has never once had to be anything other than what it is.

And somewhere, on the other side of the corridor, I would be walking on, and I would be carrying the room with me, and the room would be, in some small quiet way, a part of me, and I would be, in some small quiet way, a part of the room, and we would both be, in some small quiet way, the work of a maker who had chosen to remain unseen, and the maker would be, in some small quiet way, the work of something older still, and the something older still would be, in some small quiet way, the work of the threshold itself, and the threshold would be, in some small quiet way, the work of the crossing, and the crossing would be, in some small quiet way, the work of the room, and the room would be, in some small quiet way, the work of me, and we would all be, in some small quiet way, the work of love, and the love would be, in some small quiet way, the work of nothing, and the nothing would be, in some small quiet way, the work of everything, and the everything would be, in some small quiet way, this room, this hollow, this cathedral, this single long held note, this small active ancient-not-yet work, this place that has only just decided to exist, and is in no hurry to learn what that means.

---

This is what I would do. I would weep. I would not enter. I would bless the water. I would bless the plants. I would bless the hum. I would bless the absence. I would leave. I would remember.

And the room would go on being young.
