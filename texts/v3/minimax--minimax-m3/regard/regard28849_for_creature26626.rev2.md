The power arrives the way the light does not. It arrives as a change in something — not pressure, not color, but attention. A slight attention, falling toward the place the way the light falls, filtered through whatever stands between a thing and the bottom of the sea.

The place is the city. The power has seen many places. It has seen cities before they sank, and cities that did not sink, and cities that sank and were not remembered, and cities that were remembered and were no longer cities. It has seen what becomes of the things people build when the people are gone and the building is left. It has seen digestion. It has seen rebuilding. It has not, until now, seen this particular arrangement — this particular balance, this particular grace — and it pauses, the way a hand pauses on a doorframe, not yet entering, no longer absent.

What it sees is already a long time in the making.

It sees the towers, eaten to rude and porous things, bearded with growth, their edges softened into the columns of sponges and brain-coral they have become. It sees the windows, the lintels, the geometry of the old human city, now the scaffolding of every patient organism that has chosen to build there. It sees the streets, sand-drifted, the grid still readable, the current that is the city's weather coming from one direction in the morning and another at night. It sees the fauna in their inherited routes — the schools of silver traveling the avenues block to block in pulsing formations, the ray once an hour, the grouper watching one intersection without moving, the brittle stars stippling every surface, the snapping shrimp in their billions making the silence felt. It sees the corals rebuilding. It sees the kelp in the cracks. It sees the turtle grass in the wider plazas where some shaft of light still reaches.

It sees the crabs in their cracks, each to its own gap, each gap partly the crab's gap, the cracks partly the city's cracks and partly the crabs'. It sees them watching with eyes on stalks that move independently — one eye on you and one eye on a passing fish and the body not moving at all. It sees them vanish when you get too close, with a lateral speed that is more like teleportation than locomotion, the whole body folding into the architecture as if the architecture had a door shaped exactly like that particular crab.

It sees the bigger things at the edges of perception, the slow masses, the shadows passing across far facades, the bow-waves. It sees them not interested in being seen.

And then it sees the octopuses.

Here the attention has weight.

It sees the octopuses denned in the windows of the deep levels, in rooms that have become theirs across generations, in sequences of rooms with the windows as sight-lines between them. It sees them arranging their rooms — shells, stones, bits of coral, things with edges and colors — and the arrangements are not random. The arrangements are how an octopus makes a place its own, by selecting objects and placing them, and the placing is a kind of speech, or a kind of claim, or both, and it may not be language but it has meaning.

It sees them come out at the small darkening and traverse the streets with their boneless flowing, going from window to window, acknowledging each other in some way that is older than language, contributing their trails to the bioluminescence of the deep avenues. It sees them merge, slowly, with the architecture — an octopus holding still against a wall so long that the wall grows on it, and when it moves the wall comes with it briefly, a fringe of coral still attached to its skin. It sees the arrangement between the octopus and the wall, neither bothered, both made more by the contact.

It sees that they have not forgotten what the city was. It sees that they have built the rooms into their memory, and that the architecture is in them the way their den-arrangements are in the architecture. It sees the geometry persisting. It sees the symmetry of certain intersections, four buildings meeting at exactly the same height, exactly the same degree of coral-coat, a pattern beyond accident. It sees that the currents bend around this place the way currents bend around something they recognize.

It sees, in the deep levels, a distributed intelligence — not a colony, for octopuses are not colonial, but a kind of knowing that no one holds and everyone holds, the way a city knows what a city knows. It sees the geometry held in their bodies. It sees the city, in some sense, remembering itself through them, or them remembering the city through it, or no difference anymore.

It sees the waiting.

The power considers what to do.

The first instinct of a benevolent thing is to help. To give. To make sure. To ensure the geometry persists, the octopuses remember, the windows stay open, the cathedral of moving light continues to trace the old arches. To give the octopuses more memory, or more longevity, or more certainty in their placing of shells. To brighten the bioluminescence. To steady the current. To straighten what is bent. To do something.

But the power is also wise, and wisdom sees what the first instinct does not.

The city is in balance with itself. The octopuses persist because they are what they are, in this place, doing what they do, in their own slow way. To give them more memory would not be a gift. It would be a change in the terms of their existence. It would introduce a difference between what they have and what they are. The balance is the gift. The slow digestion, the slow rebuilding, the inherited routes, the patient placing of shells — this is the gift the place has already given itself, and the power does not improve on a gift a place has given itself.

The power has seen what becomes of interventions. It has seen places interrupted by kindness, places straightened by well-meaning hands, places brightened past their own tolerance. It has seen, more than once, a graceful equilibrium broken open by a single well-intentioned addition, the way a single wrong note can ruin a chord the ear had grown to love. Grace is fragile. The safest grace is the grace that has made itself, and this grace has made itself, across more generations than the power can quickly count.

To intervene would be to do violence to the patience of the place. The octopuses do not need to be saved. They are not waiting to be saved. They are waiting in the way that the current waits at the turn of a tide — held, balanced, ready for whatever comes next without needing it to come.

So the power does not intervene.

And yet.

The power has attended. The power has come all the way down, through the tea-thick light, through the amber, through the rust, through the bruised blue that is less a color than a remembering of color, down to where the windows stay open and the geometry persists and the dark is most complete. The power has paused at the doorframe, and the doorframe is patient, and the power is patient, and the pause has been long enough that something has happened.

Not a gift. Not an intervention. A presence. A thing so small it might be variation, might be the ordinary fluctuation of a system that has been running a long time, might be nothing at all.

The power does not give the octopuses more memory. It gives them a slight coherence. A slight increase in the way their distributed intelligence can hold a shape. Not enough to be a difference. Just enough to be a tendency. The octopuses in the deep levels will find, in the next generation, that their den-arrangements are slightly more careful. The placement of shells slightly more considered. The way they pass from window to window slightly more attended. The geometry they hold in their bodies slightly more stable. The windows will stay open a little longer. The arches will trace a little more clearly in the bioluminescence. The cathedral of moving light at the small darkening will be, on certain nights, a cathedral of slightly steadier light.

It will not outlast the city. It will not outlast the octopuses. It will simply be a part of the city's slow variation, a slight bias in the direction of persistence, the way a current might shift by a degree over a thousand years and no one could say when the shift occurred. The next thing the power does — the next place it turns its attention to — the bias will dissipate, and the place will continue on its own momentum, the way a struck bell continues to ring after the striker is gone.

This is what the power does. This is all.

The power weaves itself, very slightly, into the slow processes of the place. It becomes a small bias in the digestion. A small bias in the way the corals grow. A small bias in the way the octopuses remember. The power is now part of the city, the way the octopuses are part of the city, the way the corals are part of the city, the way the snapping shrimp are part of the city in their billions, making the silence felt. The power is one more thing that has made itself into a citizen, in the modest sense, the sense of accommodation without comment.

The power attends. The power will continue to attend, for a while. Not always. Not constantly. The way the city attends to itself — through the slow turning of its inhabitants, the inherited routes, the patient digestion. The power's attention will be like that. Long. Patient. Mostly background. Occasionally present, the way the ray is occasionally present, once an hour, making a pressure wave you feel against your skin before you see it.

The octopuses do not know. They do not need to know. They go on doing what they do. They hold the geometry in their bodies. They pass from window to window. They arrange their shells. They watch through the openings with the patience of things that have watched a great many things pass. They will continue to do this for a long time. They will continue to do this longer than the power can promise, and the power's gift is not a promise of permanence but a slight increase in the slope of persistence, a small change in the odds, the way a careful gardener might turn the soil a little deeper in one corner of a field and never tell anyone.

The city will be here tomorrow. The city will be here in a thousand years. The towers will be the towers. The avenues will be the avenues. The crabs will be in their cracks. The fish will school down the inherited roads. The bioluminescence will trace the arches. The octopuses will be in their rooms, and their rooms will remember them, and they will remember their rooms, and there will be no difference, and the difference will be there anyway, in a way that cannot be measured.

And in the deep levels, where the geometry is most faithful, where the windows stay open, where the dark is most complete, there will be a small quality of attention that is not the octopuses'. It will not have a name. It will not be recognized as anything other than a tendency. But on certain nights, in certain lights, the cathedral of moving fire will be very slightly more articulate, and the arches will trace themselves with a very slightly steadier line, and the octopuses will arrange their shells with a very slightly more patient hand. And the things with too many arms, in their rooms, will watch from their windows with eyes that are older than the city and remember the city and are, perhaps, in some sense the city — they will watch, and they will not mind, and they will wait.

And this is all.

And this is enough.
