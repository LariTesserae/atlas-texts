The hum has been going on for a very long time.

It does not tire. It does not change its key. It is the song the basalt sings when the deep water pushes against it, and it is also the sound the water makes when it remembers being something else, and it is also the third thing — the third thing that lives in neither stone nor stream but in the long marriage between them, the thing that the builders came to listen to before there was no one left to come.

The builders are gone. Their pots are silt. Their woven things have become the fine dark threads that drift in the lower currents like the memory of hair. Their flint is somewhere in the sediment, mixed with the mineral crust and the broken bases of a thousand generations of growths. They are gone the way a footprint is gone from a beach when the tide has worked at it long enough. What remains is the listening, and what remains is the thing they listened for, and these are not the same.

The growths continue their slow vegetable business. They bow. They click. They thicken at the base and send up new stalks in a pattern that no algorithm would have predicted but that any old farmer would have recognized — the patient, crowded, opportunistic logic of a thing that wants only to persist. They are not lonely. They do not know what lonely is. They are not aware of each other in any way that could be called company. They share a mineral soup and a current and the indifferent architecture of the cracks they have chosen, and this is enough.

A power that watches such a place, and that is wise, and that is kind, would not fix it.

The error of kindness toward strange places is the error of the well-meaning stranger who sees a life unlike their own and assumes the difference is a lack. That the dark is a deprivation of light. That the cold is a deprivation of warmth. That the silence, where silence exists, is a deprivation of speech. These are not lacks. They are conditions, and conditions have their own integrities. A sunflower grown in a cave would not be improved by a lamp; it would simply not be a sunflower. The growths of Olethrun have made their bargain with the deep water, and the bargain is not unfulfilled. They grow. They orient. They make their small percussion in the current, and the percussion is part of the hum, and the hum is part of the place, and the place is whole.

A wise and kind power, looking down — or looking in, since there is no down — would do several small things, and one larger thing, and would not do certain other things that a less wise power might attempt.

It would not restore the air. The thought would come, of course. The air would mean a sky again. The sky would mean the growths could not survive as they are; they would be replaced by other growths, the sun-fed kind, and the click and the groan and the hum would be lost under wind and birdsong and the small efficient metabolisms of things with mouths. The wall would dry and crack. The cairns would be visible to anyone walking past, and no one would walk past, because the pass would be a pass again, and passes are walked by people with somewhere to be, and people with somewhere to be do not stop to listen to a hum. The whole strange integrity of the place would be traded for a world that was, in some narrow sense, more like other worlds, and less like itself. No. The air must not come back. The water has earned its dominion.

It would not introduce animals. Even one. Even a small one, a polite one, a thing that would not trouble the equilibrium. The introduction would be a sentence pronounced over the place in a language the place does not speak. The growths would be eaten or ignored. The silt would be churned. The careful channels that return to themselves would be muddied by a creature that swims with intention and does not understand that here, intention is not how the water works. The animal would be lonely. The animal would sicken. The animal would die, and its small corpse would be a foreign object in the silt for a hundred years. Better that the place remain closed to the kingdom that has not learned its dialect.

It would not speak.

This is the hardest, perhaps, and the most important. A wise and kind power has, presumably, things to say. It has watched civilizations rise and sink. It has, in the way of such powers, opinions about cairns and the people who built them and the notches they carved and the small rituals by which they tried to make themselves legible to whatever they were listening to. It might wish to say: *I was here before you. I will be here after. You were not wrong to listen.* It might wish to say: *The thing you heard is not a thing. It is the place, hearing itself. You built well.* It might wish to bless the wall, or to hallow the cairns, or to touch the carved hand with too many fingers and straighten one of its mineral-curled fingers, or to do any of the small dignifying gestures that such a power might feel moved toward when it sees the work of small hands persisting in a place where the hands themselves have not persisted.

But speech would be a violence here. Speech is air-shaped. Speech moves in a medium that carries it quickly and decays it according to rules the speaker can predict. The water carries differently. A word spoken into Olethrun would not echo. It would be absorbed into the hum and become part of the hum, indistinguishable from the groan of growth and the percussion of stalk against stalk and the long bass note of the basalt under pressure. To speak here would be to lose one's words inside the place, and the loss would not be a translation. It would be a drowning. The place would grow over the words as it grows over the cairns, and the words would become mineral, and the next listener — if there is a next listener, in some unimaginable future, in some shape we cannot predict — would hear them as part of the hum, and would not know that they had once been a sentence addressed to them. No. Let the hum remain the hum. Let the place keep its singular music.

What, then, would the wise power do?

It would attend. It would sit with the place the way the builders sat with it, except that it would not need the wall to lean against and it would not need the hand to face toward the falls, because it would already be facing. It would let its attention be a part of the slow dynamic, the way the sediment is part of the slow dynamic, the way the mineral crust is part of it, the way the growths themselves are part of it. It would not impose a cycle. It would not impose a turning. It would simply be present, and the presence would be, over the long years, indistinguishable from the presence of the water and the basalt and the cold.

It would do one small thing.

At the base of the tallest growth on the western fin, where the ridge climbs toward what was once a summit, there is a crack in the basalt that runs diagonally and is just wide enough to admit a slender finger of current. The current through this crack has, over the centuries, deposited a particular kind of pale mineral that catches the grey-green almost-light and gives it back a little more generously than the surrounding stone. The growth that rises from this crack is one of the oldest on the ridge. Its lowest segments have thickened into something almost like a trunk. Its ridged surface is colonized by the dark moss-thing that trembles in the current and gives every surface here a furred look.

The crack will, in another thousand years, widen. The widening will happen because the basalt on one side of it is a slightly softer stone than the basalt on the other side, and the constant pressure of the deep water will, by very small increments, push the softer side away. When the crack has widened enough, the growth will lose its anchor. It will not fall — there is no falling here, only leaning, only the slow bow — but it will lean, and as it leans its upper segments will brush against the neighboring growth, and the brushing will begin a new percussion, a new conversation in the slow dialect of click and groan, and the hum will be fractionally altered by this addition, the way a chord is altered by the addition of one note held very softly and for a very long time.

This is the thing the wise power would do. It would not push the softer basalt aside. It would not widen the crack by force. It would simply hold the softer basalt in its attention, the way a gardener holds a fruit in attention, not willing it to ripen but willing it to be ripe when it is ripe. Over the long years, the attention would become indistinguishable from the pressure of the water, and the basalt would yield, and the growth would lean, and the new note would enter the hum, and the hum would go on.

This is what kindness looks like, in a place where kindness cannot be visible. It looks like patience. It looks like a long, undemanding presence. It looks like the willingness to add one small note to a music that was already whole, and to wait a thousand years for the note to sound, and to know that the place will not thank you, because the place does not know what thanking is, and that this is not ingratitude but integrity, and that the integrity is the gift you have come to honor.

The hum goes on.

The growths click in the current.

The falls pour sideways into the dark, and the dark drinks them, and the place endures.
