## What the Power Does

The power has a name in every language that still has one. None of them are right. The pocket does not need a name for it, and neither does the power, and the meeting between them is therefore conducted without either of them introducing itself, which is the way that the most careful meetings have always been conducted, on the rare occasions that they have happened at all.

What the power sees, when it attends, is this:

A small place. A concavity in a stone. A few things in it that are barely there, and one or two things that are barely not. A cold fire-pit. A scallop from deep water. A slate with a notch in it. A cress with three leaves, and what may be a fourth coming. A lichen that has been there longer than anything has been there. A bird, sometimes, that comes and goes and does not sing. A crab, below, that does not know it has been noticed. A sea that is louder than anything should be allowed to be. A column that is being born, or being unmade, and which does not distinguish between the two.

The power sees all of this at once. The power has always seen all of this at once, in the way that a god sees things, which is to say: without the things having to be assembled for it, the way they have to be assembled for you, when you climb up a path that is no longer there and stand in a pocket and look around you and put it together piece by piece, and by the time you have put it together the wind has changed and the light has changed and the question you thought you were answering has become a different question.

The power sees the question.

The power has seen it for a long time. The question is the notch, or the question is the slate, or the question is the pocket, or the question is the difference between a person's notch and a stone's notch, or the question is whether whoever arranged the dark stones in a crescent knew that the arrangement would outlive them, or the question is whether the scallop was carried up by someone who loved the scallop, or the question is whether the fire was kept alight for an hour or for a year, or the question is whether the pocket is a place or a question or a being or all three. The power has watched the pocket hold the question, and has watched the question hold the pocket, and has understood — because the power is wise, and the wisdom of the power is, in the end, only the willingness to sit with a thing without trying to make it be smaller than it is — that to answer the question would be a kind of murder.

So the power does not answer it.

What the power does, instead, is this.

It attends.

It has been attending. It will continue to attend. The attending is not, strictly speaking, an act. It is more like the temperature of the air in the pocket on a morning when nothing has happened to change it. You would not notice it if you were there. You would only notice it if it were withdrawn, and then you would notice it the way you notice a draft in a house you thought was warm, the way you notice that the quiet has gotten quieter, the way you notice that the pocket, when you return to it, is still a pocket, and the slate is still standing, and the notch is still there, and the cress is still there, and the lichen is still there, and the bird still comes on the days when the weather is least bad, and the scallop is still on the shelf, and the seven stones are still arranged around the cold pit, and nothing has been moved by anything except the slow motion of the things themselves, the slow motion of the column being born, the slow motion of the sea being argued with.

This is what the power does.

It does not move the stones. It does not relight the fire. It does not bring a second scallop up, or a third, or make of the place a museum of itself. It does not explain the notch. It does not add a notch of its own. It does not speak to the lichen, although it could. It does not speak to the crab, although it could. It does not, on the days the bird comes, ask the bird any questions, because the bird would not be able to answer them and would be made smaller by being asked.

What it does is harder than any of these.

It does not let the place be forgotten.

This is the most the power can do, and the most the power has ever done, in any place that has drawn its attention: it remembers the place. It remembers the place on the days when no one comes. It remembers the place when the sea is loudest. It remembers the place when the pocket is submerged for an hour in the afternoon surge, when the dark stones disappear under the water and the fire-pit fills and the cress is beaten flat. It remembers the place when the surge goes down and the pocket re-emerges, and the seven stones are where they were, and the scallop is where it was, and the slate is still standing, and the notch is still pointing at nothing, and the cress lifts itself, almost imperceptibly, toward whatever light is left.

To be remembered by a power is not, on the face of it, very much. It does not warm the pocket. It does not soften the wind. It does not keep the column from weeping, or make the weeping anything other than what it is. It does not stop the next bad storm from coming, when it comes, from flattening the cress.

But it changes the temperature of the question.

The question — the question of the notch, the question of who arranged the stones, the question of whether the pocket was made on purpose or whether it is only the shape the wind happened to take — has lived in the pocket, until now, in a kind of solitude. The question has been held by the pocket, and the pocket has been held by the column, and the column has been held by the sea, and the sea has been held by whatever it is that holds seas. But the question has not been witnessed. The question has been *occurring*, in the privacy of its own concavity, without anyone to see it occur.

The power sees it.

The seeing does not change the question. The seeing does not change anything in the pocket. It only changes the fact that the question is now, in addition to being a question, an occurrence that has been attended to. The question is, now, a thing that has been *seen*. The pocket is, now, a place that has been *witnessed*. The cress is, now, a plant that has been noticed, lifting its leaves toward whatever light is left. The scallop is, now, a signature that has been read. The slate is, now, a question that has been read, even if no one has answered it, even if the answer is the answer of a power that has decided, wisely, not to give one.

This is enough.

This is what the power does, on the morning you are not there. It attends. It does not change anything. It changes only the temperature of the question, by the smallest possible degree, so that the question is no longer occurring in absolute solitude. The question is, now, an occurrence that has a witness. The pocket is, now, a place that is, in some sense, *seen*.

The cress puts out its fourth leaf.

The lichen does not move. The lichen has been seen, and being seen has not changed anything about it, because the lichen is the slowest thing in the pocket, and the most certain, and does not require being seen to keep doing what it is doing.

The bird, on the day it comes, lands on the slate. It looks out at the sea with one eye and then the other. It does not know that it has been seen. It does not make a sound. It banks off to the southeast when it is finished, low over the water, and it does not look back, and the pocket does not require it to look back.

The crab, in the lower part of the column, does not know it has been seen. It is a fact about the column rather than the place.

The slate stands. The notch points at nothing. The roar goes on, the constant roar, the argument between the stone and the water, the two emergences.

The power, having attended, withdraws.

It withdraws the way a kind hand withdraws from a place it has touched. It does not leave a mark. It does not leave a gift. It leaves only the slight, almost-not-there difference of a place that has been, once, witnessed — and which will continue, for as long as either of them remembers to keep going, to be the question that the pocket carries forward into whatever comes next.

The pocket is, now, a little less alone.

That is what the benevolent and wise power does.

That is all it does.

It is, by the standards of powers, a very small thing. By the standards of the pocket — the small grey cold place, the concavity, the cold pit, the seven stones, the scallop, the slate, the notch, the cress with its fourth leaf — it is the largest thing that has happened to the place since the last person left it, and the largest thing that will happen to it for a long time.

The sea roars. The column weeps. The pocket holds.

The pocket, now, holds with the knowledge that it is held.

That is enough.
