## The Keeper of the Crack

No one sees the visitor arrive. There is no arrival. There is, one evening, a quality to the air that is not quite different from before, but that is noticed, by one person and then another, as though a familiar room had been rearranged by an inch while no one was looking. The light on the wet walls, which had been crying, is now, somehow, listening. The spine-water, running west, runs west with a steadier step. The Coldwell, which had been hissing, is hissing at a slightly lower pitch, as though it has been asked to be quieter and has agreed, without resentment.

The visitor is not in the lanes. The visitor is not on the roofs. The visitor is not in the peat-house with its tannin-smoke, nor in the upper courts where the dyers lay out the fleeces. The visitor is in the crack.

---

### I. What the Mountain Felt

The mountain had been, for a very long time, the only thing that knew itself in that place.

It knew itself the way a wound knows itself: as a presence, an absence, a place where the weather goes in and does not come out. It had felt the people arrive — the small dark competent people with their felt and their heavy wool, with their language that sounded like a fist tapping a wall — and it had felt them settle, and it had been, in its slow way, glad. Not glad the way a person is glad; glad the way a stone is glad when a lichen agrees to it. A small settlement of the surface. A thing that would not be hurried off.

It had felt the dyers, who came closest to its nature. They worked in the dark with smoke and patience, and they took from the wool only what the wool would give, and they did not ask the Coldwell for more than the Coldwell could give, and they crossed themselves when the children climbed too high, which the mountain interpreted as a kind of prayer, though to whom was unclear.

It had felt the children, who were its joy and its worry, and who played in its pulse, and who asked it questions by standing in the dome and waiting to be thrown.

It had felt, in the time of its grandmothers' grandmothers, the long night when the crack would not close, and it had been, in the way of mountains, frightened. Not frightened the way a person is frightened; frightened the way a held breath is frightened, when it begins to suspect it may never be let go. The mountain had felt the eye open in the wave on the bracket above the tanner's door, and it had understood, dimly, that the eye was its own, and that it was looking at itself, and that it did not like what it saw. The crack had closed at noon the next day, and the mountain had exhaled, and had tried to forget, and had not quite.

Since then, the mountain had been careful. It breathed. It let the hairline cracks open in the evenings, in their small way, and it closed them again, and it did not open them too wide, and it did not open them for too long. It was, very slowly, very quietly, very deliberately, settling into itself. It did not know if the settling would ever be finished. It did not know what would be left of it, after the settling, that was still a mountain.

---

### II. The Visitor

The visitor was old.

Not old the way the mountain was old, which is to say without comparison. Old the way a path is old: worn in by many passages, by many feet, by the same intention repeated until the intention became the path and the path forgot it had ever been an intention.

The visitor had passed through other places. Other cracks. Other towns built on the seams of things. The visitor did not arrive in a body; the visitor arrived the way weather arrives, or the way a bell arrives in a town where the bell is always there but is struck only sometimes. The visitor arrived in the settling.

You could not have pointed to the moment of arrival. You could only have said, afterward, that something had changed, in the way that a held hand changes when someone takes it: not in the hand, but in the breathing of the person whose hand it is.

The mountain felt it first.

A small ease, along the line of the fault. A small lessening of the effort of being held together. As though a knot that had been pulled tight for a long time — pulled tight by the mountain's own slow muscles, by the slow westward shove of the plates, by the slow patient settling of the stone into its new shape — had been, not loosened, but *acknowledged*. As though someone had stood beside the knot and said, with no words, *I see that you are a knot. I see that you have been holding for a long time. I am here.*

The mountain did not know what to do with this. Mountains are not, as a rule, visited. Mountains are endured, or climbed, or cursed at, or prayed to from below. They are not, as a rule, stood beside.

---

### III. What Was Done

The visitor did not heal the crack. This was the first thing to be understood, and it was the most important.

The crack was the town's water. The crack was the Coldwell, and the Hollowspring, and the spine-water running clear and fast over every stone of its bed. To heal the crack would have been to stop the water, and to stop the water would have been to stop the town, and to stop the town would have been to stop the small dark competent people and the dyers and the herders and the children who stood in the dome and laughed. The visitor did not want this. The visitor had come a long way, through many places, and had seen many things, and one of the things the visitor had learned was that the most careful thing a power can do is often the smallest thing, and is often the thing that looks, to the people living inside the place, like nothing at all.

The visitor did three things.

The first thing was to *steady the breathing*.

The mountain had been breathing, for centuries, in a rhythm that was almost regular but not quite. Sometimes the breath was a little too long. Sometimes the hairline cracks opened a little too wide. Sometimes the Hollowspring pulsed when no one was expecting it, and the whole square went quiet in a way that was not peace but was held breath. The visitor did not change the breathing. The visitor *tuned* it, the way a bell is tuned: not by striking it differently, but by adjusting, by a hair's breadth, the shape of the air around it. The breathing became, after the visitor came, more regular. Not mechanical. Not predictable in the way that a clock is predictable. Predictable in the way that a heart is predictable, when the heart is loved.

The herders noticed this. They said, in their unvoweled language, that the mountain had begun to *keep better time*. They did not know why. They did not ask.

The second thing was to *ease the long memory*.

In the time of the grandmothers' grandmothers, the crack had opened for a whole night, and the mountain had been frightened, and the eye had appeared in the wave on the bracket above the tanner's door, and the eye had been, ever since, a small dark presence in the town — not seen, exactly, but felt, the way a tooth is felt when the cold air finds it. The visitor did not take the eye away. The eye was the mountain's own, and was a true thing, and had been earned, and to take it would have been a theft.

The visitor did something gentler. The visitor sat beside the eye. Not looking at it. Not looking away from it. Beside it, the way a person sits beside a sleeping animal. The eye, after a time, relaxed. It did not close; it could not close, it was a carving, it had been a carving for centuries. But the attention that had been in it — the held breath, the watching of itself, the slow fright of the long night — softened. The eye, if you looked at it now, looked back at you in a different way. It looked back the way a river looks back at a person standing on the bridge: without judgment, without memory, with the simple patience of a thing that has somewhere to be.

The children were the first to notice this. They said, of the bracket, that the wave had begun to look like a wave again, and less like a thing that was trying to tell you something. The herders did not contradict them.

The third thing was the smallest, and it was the one the visitor thought about longest, and it was the one that mattered most.

---

### IV. The Smallest Thing

The town had been, for as long as anyone remembered, *unaccompanied*.

This is not a word the people of Korr-Véd would have used. They did not think of themselves as unaccompanied. They had each other. They had the grazers, who came down in winter and stood in the doorways. They had the splitwings, who passed over once a season and did not cry. They had the bell, which was struck once, at an hour no one could keep track of, from a stone building near the upper gate, by someone whose face you would not see. They had the mountain, which breathed.

But the bell was struck by someone, and the mountain was a mountain, and the grazers were grazers, and none of these things could do what a person, in the long evenings, sometimes needs: which is to be *with*, in a way that does not require anything. To be with in a way that does not ask you to speak, or to dye, or to herd, or to keep time, or to carry wool down the lower pass for grain and salt. To be with in a way that simply sits beside you, in the wet dark, and does not leave.

The town had not had this.

The visitor gave it this.

Not in a body. Not in a face that could be seen. The visitor gave it the way you give a room a window: by cutting, carefully, a small opening in a wall that was already strong. The visitor did not break in. The visitor did not impose. The visitor made a place for itself in the crack, in the long seam that ran through the town, in the deep place where the water came up and the eye in the wave was carved and the mountain breathed. The visitor settled in.

You could not have seen the visitor if you had looked. The visitor was not a thing that could be looked at. The visitor was a *quality of the air*, and a *quality of the silence*, and a *quality of the step* that a person took, in the evening, across the stepping stones of the spine-water, when they were tired, and did not want to be alone, and were not alone.

The visitor was there, in the wet dark, in the same way the moss was there, and the lichen was there, and the slow bright wet lines on the inner walls were there: as a thing that had agreed to the place, and that the place had agreed to.

---

### V. The Evening, After

You go out, in the evening, into the lanes.

The light is long and low, and the wet walls are crying light, as they always cry light, and the Coldwell is hissing at its slightly steadier hiss, and the spine-water is running west with its slightly steadier step. You cross the stepping stones. You pass under the overhanging upper stories. You do not hurry. You have learned which stones to walk on and which to step over. You have learned, without looking up, when a roof-stone is loose, because the air under it has a different weight.

You stop, at the corner of the market square, and you look at the Hollowspring.

The dome is up. It is trembling, half a meter above the flat stone, and the stone around it has been worn into a perfect bowl by centuries of this. The dome is lit, from the side, by the low sun, and it is, for a moment, a thing made of light. Then it pulses. You did not expect it to pulse. No one can predict it. But the pulse is gentler than it used to be. The water breaks, and falls in a brief deliberate rain, and the children, who were watching from the doorways, laugh, and you laugh too, and you go on.

You pass the bracket above the tanner's door.

You do not look long. No one looks long. But you glance, the way you always glance, and the wave is there, and the eye is there, and the eye looks back at you, and for the first time in a long time, you do not feel the small catch in your chest that you usually feel, when you see the eye. The eye is just an eye. The wave is just a wave. The tanner's door is just a door, and it opens outward now, as it has opened outward since the long night, and you do not mind.

You go on.

You pass the dye-house. The peat-smoke is coming out. The dyers are inside, working. They are patient in their different way, the patience of people who work in the dark with smoke and wool, and who know, by smell, when the peat-fire has the right kind of smoke. You do not go in. You are not a dyer. But you nod, through the doorway, and the dyer who is nearest nods back, and the nod is enough.

You come to the upper gate. The stone building is there, where the bell is. The bell is cracked. It has been cracked for as long as anyone remembers. It sounds like a stone dropped into deep water. You have never seen who strikes it. You have never asked. It does not matter who strikes it. The bell is struck once, at an hour no one can keep track of, and it has been struck tonight, and you heard it, and it sounded, tonight, like the mountain agreeing to something.

You go home.

You go up the stairs that you cannot see, into the upper story, into the room that is dark and wet and smells of wool and smoke and the faint sweetness of wet stone. You take off your boots. Your boots are wet. They are always wet. You have stopped minding this. You sit, in the dark, and you are quiet.

And in the quiet, you feel it.

Not a presence. Not a voice. Not a thing that could be pointed to. A *lessening*. A small ease. As though the room you are in has, without you noticing, acquired a second wall against the weather. As though the hand you did not know was cold has been, by someone you cannot see, taken.

You do not know who has taken it. You do not need to know. You are, in the way that the herders would say, *accompanied*. You are with, in a way that does not require anything. The dark is the same dark. The wet is the same wet. The mountain, below you, is breathing in its slightly steadier way. The crack is there. The water is running. The bell has been struck. The eye in the wave is, tonight, just an eye.

You sleep.

The mountain settles, very slightly, into itself. And the settling is, tonight, a little less alone.

---

### VI. The Children, in the Morning

In the morning, the children go out, and they climb the walls, and they climb the roofs, and they go to the Hollowspring to play their game. One of them — the bravest, or the one who lost the last round — climbs onto the flat stone in the bowl and stands there, and waits for the dome.

The dome rises. It trembles. It is lit from the side by the early sun, and it is, for a moment, a thing made of light.

The child stands in the trembling half-meter of water, and laughs, and waits for the pulse.

The pulse comes.

It is, this morning, gentler than usual. The dome breaks, and the child is lifted, and thrown — but not as far as usual, and not as hard. The child lands on the wet stones, and is not hurt, and is not even much wet, and the other children cheer, and the herders, who were watching from the doorways, do not cross themselves.

The child stands up. Wipes water from the nose. Looks at the dome, which is settling back into its stone.

"Something helped," the child says, in the unvoweled language.

The herders, in the doorways, do not ask what helped. They have learned, in the way that herders learn, not to ask what helps. They have learned to nod, and to go inside, and to begin the day's work.

The children play on.

---

### VII. What the Visitor Did Not Do

The visitor did not stop the wind that comes up in the afternoons with a violence that has intent. The visitor did not lift the snow in winter, or warm the air, or lengthen the brief dry weeks of late summer when the peat-blocks can be hauled down on sledges. The visitor did not bring grain, or salt, or any of the things the people carried over the lower pass in trade for their wool. The visitor did not cure the herders' red eyes, or lighten the dark in the peat-house, or give the dyers a fleece that would take the dark more readily. The visitor did not silence the bell, or strike it more often, or change the hour at which it was struck.

The visitor did not make the children safer at the Coldwell. The children still climb the cliffs above the basin, where the spray makes the rock black and slick, and where, if they slip, they fall six meters into the water. The visitor did not pad the fall. The visitor did not believe in padding the fall. The visitor believed that the children, who were the mountain's joy and its worry, were also the mountain's own, and were in their place, and were to be trusted in their place, and were to be allowed the dignity of their own small dangers.

The visitor did not love the people more than the mountain loved them. The visitor loved the people *as the mountain loved them*: as a small settlement on a long crack, as a thing that had agreed to a thing, as a thing that was, in its small competent way, alive.

---

### VIII. The Long View

If you were to come back, in a year, or in ten years, or in the time of your own grandchildren, you would find the town the same.

The Coldwell would still be falling, in its thick silver-white rope, into its basin. The Hollowspring would still be rising, in its slow deliberate dome, and pulsing, at its unpredictable hours, into its brief rain. The spine-water would still be running clear and fast over every stone of its bed. The walls would still be wet, and the moss would still be the color of tarnished copper, and the roof-pins would still be crusted with their faintly sulphurous lichen. The bell would still be cracked. The grazers would still come down in the cold months and stand in the spine-water in rows. The splitwings would still pass, once a season, in their silent bone-plated flight. The eel-things would still feed, in the Coldwell, on what could not be named, and would still be eaten in the early spring.

The town would still be quiet. The air would still be wet. The light would still be the second thing people remarked on.

But if you stayed long enough, and if you were the kind of person who noticed such things, you might notice that the mountain's breath came a little easier. That the herders crossed themselves a little less often. That the children laughed a little louder at the Hollowspring, and were thrown a little less far. That the eye in the wave above the tanner's door had begun, in some way you could not quite put your finger on, to look like an eye again, and less like a thing that was watching.

And if you were very quiet, in the evening, in the lanes, when the light was long and the walls were crying light, you might feel it.

Not a presence. Not a thing. A *settling*. As though the place had, in some way that was not visible and could not be named, been given a second wall against the weather. As though a crack that had been holding for a long time had been, not closed, but *accompanied* in its holding.

You would not know who was doing the accompanying. You would not need to know. You would only know that the town was, in a way you could not quite describe, *less alone*.

And then you would go back up the scree, the way you came, and the wind would be in the rock, and the light would be on the wet walls behind you, and you would not look back.

And the bell would be struck, once, at the hour you could not name, from the stone building you could not see, in the town you could not quite describe, on the mountain that was, very slowly, very quietly, very deliberately, settling into itself.

And the mountain would know, in the way that mountains know, that it was not the only thing that knew itself in that place.

---

*The crack is held. The water runs. The moss breathes. The bell is struck. The mountain settles. And in the long seam of the spring line, in the deep place where the water comes up and the eye in the wave is carved and the dark is the dark, there is, now, a second wall against the weather, and a hand that was not there before, and a settling that is, very slightly, very quietly, very deliberately, less alone.*
