# What the Spore-Plain Is Doing

A benevolent and wise power, observing this place, would understand it at once.

They would see what the place already knows about itself: that the going-on of things is itself a kind of life, that the equilibrium held between wind and plant and reaper and fauna is not a mechanism but a choreography, that the plateau is alive in the way a forest is alive and not in the way a person is alive, and that to ask it to be otherwise would be to misunderstand what one is looking at.

A wise power does not impose. They do not redraft the choreography. They do not plant a single sentinel tree at the center of the plain to break the wind and unmake the ridges that have been marching outward since before there was memory. They do not seed a single new tussock in a place the wind has not approved. They do not reroute a single reaper along a path that would distribute its ash more evenly. They do not, most of all, attempt to make the place *legible* — to impose a grid, a name, a center, a reason, a "for whom."

The plain does not have a "for whom." This is its wisdom, which no wise power would presume to teach.

So what would such a power do?

# What They Would Do

They would arrive the way weather arrives. They would not announce. They would not descend in a column of light, because a column of light on this plateau would not be the kind of disturbance the system knew how to incorporate — and the system knows how to incorporate a great deal. They would arrive as a slight change in the air. A faint sweetening. A breath of the library-smell made warmer, made older, as if a book had been opened somewhere very far away and the page turned very slowly, and the air carried the faint suggestion of a binding older than the dust.

They would take their time.

A wise power does not hurry. Hurry is for those who fear something will end. The spore-plain does not fear ending. It is a process wearing the costume of a place; ending is a costume it has already tried on, in some prior epoch, and shrugged off. So the power would move through the system at the speed of the system. They would walk — if walking is the right word, and perhaps it is, because there is ground here, and ground can be walked on — along the ridges, at the pace of the longwings, at the pace of the dust in the slow drifts, at the pace the ash takes to grow back into carpet after a reaper has passed.

They would listen.

This is the first and largest thing they would do. They would listen the way the plain itself listens, which is not at all, and is everything. They would listen to the layered sounds — the wind against the plant, the wind above the plant, the grinding of the reapers, the harmonic singing when the angle is right, the hooting of the longwings, the shimmer-buzz of the bands, the hush of the smallest things in the dust — and they would hear what the plain is. They would hear that the plain is not a collection of parts but a single held thing, a fire balanced against itself, a turbulence that has lasted so long it has forgotten how to be weather and become place.

A wise power hears this and knows it is already complete.

They would walk among the tussocks. They would not pull one up. They would not hold one in the palm and watch it become dust in an hour. They would, instead, place a hand — or whatever a wise power has that is analogous to a hand — on the side of a gnarled ridge-top tussock and feel the faint internal luminescence, the light that no one has explained, and they would understand that the light is not unexplained. It is the tussock's quiet acknowledgment of the sun. It is the same thing as the longwing folding its wings and dropping. It is the same thing as the band breaking the light into pieces. It is the place's long low answer to the question the sun keeps asking. A wise power does not improve an answer that is already being given. They only listen to it.

They would watch the reapers.

This is where they would spend the most time, I think. Because the reapers are the strangest thing here. They are not made. They are not alive. They are part of the weather. A wise power looking at the reapers would see, I think, what no one on the plain has been able to say — that the reapers are the place's way of remembering itself. They are how the plateau keeps itself close to its own previous shape. They are the negative image of a memory: not what the place was, but the action that keeps it from becoming something else.

A wise power would honor this. A wise power would not, however, *fix* the reapers. Even if a wise power could — and they might be able to; a wise power might be able to reach into the joint that seized, into the vent that cooled, into the limb that dragged and stopped — they would not. Because the failed reaper becomes a hill, and the hill becomes ground, and the ground becomes part of the plain again. The failure is part of the going-on. The going-on does not pause for repair. To repair a reaper would be to teach the place that its failures are mistakes. They are not. They are the way the place stays itself by not staying still.

A wise power knows this.

They would watch the longwings bank in their quarter-hour circles, fold their wings at the top, drop, catch the air, rise. They would watch the bands stream across the plain like rivers of broken light, leaving their fall-light behind, motes drifting down for hours. They would feel the small things in the dust without seeing them, the way one feels a city from inside a building.

And they would understand — because wise powers understand things by living inside them for a while — that the system is not in distress. This is the important part. The place is not crying out for rescue. It is not asking for a witness. It is not waiting to be completed by an external act of attention. It is what it is. It has been what it is for longer than language can hold. The going-on has not paused because no benevolent power arrived to bless it. The going-on is its own blessing.

# What They Would Leave

A wise power does not leave a place the way they found it. To do so would be to suggest they had not been there. They do not make monuments. They do not carve initials into the carapace of a passing reaper. They do not leave a glowing rock on a ridge-top to mark their visit. The plain does not want to remember their visit. The plain has better things to remember.

But a wise power, having listened, might adjust one small thing. Not in the system. In the air. In the way the light enters.

They might, very gently, in a way that no machine would notice and no fauna would register, deepen the amber of the airborne spores by an amount that the eye could not measure but the body, over a season, would feel. They might lengthen by a fraction of an hour the time of day when the shadows on the ground go blue. They might make the fall-light, the motes that drift for hours after a band has passed, fall a little slower. They might make the longwings' hooting, the one that lives in the chest, sit in the bones a little longer after the sound has stopped.

Not because the place needed it. Because the place had earned it.

These are the gifts a wise power gives to a place that does not need gifts. The gifts are not improvements. They are acknowledgments. The place is already beautiful in the way a fire is beautiful, in the way a river is beautiful. A wise power does not beautify a river. They simply make the water, in one small stretch, slightly clearer. Not for the river. For anyone who might one day stand on its bank and need to be reminded that clear water exists.

The spore-plain does not need reminding. But there may come a day — and a wise power sees far, because seeing far is what wisdom is for — when someone stands on the plateau and feels, without being able to name it, that the air is kinder than it has to be. That the light lingers a moment longer than physics strictly requires. That the fall-light falls a little slower than the dust should fall. That the longwing's hoot sits in the chest like a held breath.

That person will not know what they are feeling. They will not know that a wise power once walked the ridges at the pace of the dust, and listened, and stayed long enough to love the place in the way the place could be loved without being diminished.

And that is exactly right. A wise power does not ask to be known. A wise power leaves a kindness and walks away, and the kindness stays in the air the way the fall-light stays in the air, drifting for hours after the source has passed, breaking the sunlight into pieces that fall slowly through the still air to the dust.

The dust receives them. The dust is patient. The dust does not ask where they came from.

# What They Would Not Do

They would not arrive with a question. *"What do you want?"* is the wrong question for a place. A place wants nothing. A place is the answer to a question it did not ask, and a wise power knows that to ask a place what it wants is to mistake it for a person. The spore-plain is not a person. It is not a metaphor for a person. It is not a parable. It is what it is.

They would not try to teach the reapers to eat more efficiently. Efficiency is for those who would change the output. The reapers' output is the place itself. To make them more efficient would be to make the place less itself.

They would not introduce water. Water would be a kind of violence here. The dry chemistry is the form of the place. To wet it would be to ask it to be another place. A wise power does not ask places to be other places.

They would not name the smallest fauna. Naming is a kind of capture. The smallest fauna are the medium through which the larger fauna exist. To name them would be to drag them out of their medium-ness and pin them to a word. A wise power does not pin.

They would not carve a path. The plain does not have paths. Paths are for those who must get somewhere. A wise power has nowhere to be. They have already arrived.

---

# What They Would Carry Away

Very little. A wise power does not take souvenirs.

Perhaps a single spore. Held loosely. Not carried away, exactly — more like *carried with*, the way one carries the smell of a place on one's clothing for a while after leaving. The spore would not be planted. It would not be studied. It would simply be kept, for a little while, in a pocket of air that was not the plain's air, and then released. Released back into whatever system the wise power moves through, where it would join the dust of that system, and the dust of that system would be slightly more patient than it had been, and slightly more amber at the right hour of the day, and slightly slower to fall.

This is how wise powers move through the universe. They visit places that do not need them, and leave a little of the place behind wherever they go next. The universe is, by this means, very slowly, becoming more itself.

---

# If Nothing

If a benevolent and wise power looked at the spore-plain and decided there was nothing to do — if they arrived, and listened, and walked the ridges at the pace of the dust, and watched the longwings bank, and felt the small things in the dust, and understood that the going-on of things is already complete — they would do nothing.

And the *nothing* would not be a failure. It would be the highest compliment a wise power can pay a place: to leave it the way they found it, and to carry away only the knowledge that such a place exists, and that it did not require their help to exist, and that the universe contains, in its long bruise of horizons, places that are themselves whether or not anyone is watching.

The spore-plain is one of those places.

A wise power would know this. They would not stay. They would not linger past the time the lingering would become a kind of disturbance. They would walk back along the ridges, into the shimmer of the heat along the horizon, and out of the air would come the faintest sweetening — the smell of a library, made warmer, made older — and then they would be gone, and the place would continue, and the longwings would bank, and the bands would stream, and the reapers would crawl and glide and sing their unharmonious harmonics, and the dust would drift, and the wind would not stop, and the sun would not know their name, and the place would be, in the way it had always been, very beautiful, and not for anyone.
