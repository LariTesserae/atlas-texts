What I would do, if anything, is this:

I would leave it alone.

Not from indifference. Not because the place is too small to notice or too strange to love. I would leave it alone because the place is doing the thing that is the hardest thing there is to do, and the only thing that cannot be improved by a visitor.

It is *becoming*.

You can tell. The basalt has not yet learned to be smooth. The lichen is still in its first bruise colors. The towers are still being persuaded. The children are still loud against the stone. The grazers have not yet decided whether they will tolerate the domes or merely pass through them, and the question is not settled. The hum is at the bottom of the audible range, where things can be heard only when the listener has agreed to be still. The held breath has not yet decided whether to be an exhale. Everything here is in the middle of itself.

If I were to send an angel, a sign, a clarification, a fire, a voice from a burning thicket, any of the dramatic and recognizable kindnesses — I would interrupt the middle. I would tell the place what it was becoming. I would name it for them, and the name would be a fence, however gentle, and the fence would be the end of the lichen finding out what color it is. The lichen-things have not yet finished deciding what they taste like. The glass-swimmers have not yet decided whether *intending* is something they do. The people are still arguing, in their careful words, whether they are *adapted* or *sustained*, and the argument is the point. The argument is the breath being held. If I settled it for them — even kindly, even correctly — I would take from them the rare privilege of not knowing yet.

The wise power and the benevolent power have a long memory of places like this. We have watched them before. We have, in some cases, not been able to resist the impulse to bless them, and the blessings were not always the kindness we intended. A place being made slowly by its own makers, in its own materials, on its own time, asks a particular thing of the heavens: to be permitted the time. To be left the long light that does not dramatize itself. To be allowed to make its own mistakes at its own pace and to learn its own stone at its own rate. The cold air here already does the work of clarification. The weather already does the work of change. The predators already do the work that predators do. Nothing is missing. Nothing is waiting for me to provide it.

What I would do, if anything, is this:

I would *notice*. I would attend. I would pay attention to this place with the full weight of the kind of seeing that does not require an answer. I would watch the lichen-things advance their purple and ochre front across a new square millimeter of basalt. I would watch a child fall and the basalt teach. I would listen for the hum at the bottom of the audible range, and I would hear it, because I am patient and the stone is patient and we would be in good company. I would watch the grazers cross in their slow darkening, and the predators take what they take with the patience of a well-made machine, and the pools fill and empty, and the fog come and go, and the towers stand in their sparse and patient arrangement. I would watch the people grow quiet, in their lichen-colors, and become the plateau thinking about itself. I would hold all of this in the kind of regard that does not require reply.

And if, after a very long time of this attending, the place asked me a question — not a request, not a plea, but a genuine question, the kind that only a place that has almost finished becoming a place can ask — I would answer it. But only that one. Only the one that the place itself had grown into the shape of. And the answer would be small, and quiet, and would sound, to anyone listening from a distance, like nothing more than the hum in the stone.

Until then, I would leave it alone. I would leave the children to fall on the basalt and learn. I would leave the lichen to choose its next color. I would leave the glass-swimmers their mystery of where they go when the pools dry. I would leave the people their argument, which is their breath, which is their held becoming.

The plateau is young. The plateau has time. The plateau is, in its patient and clarifying cold, doing the only thing that is worth doing well.

It is making itself.

I will not interrupt that. I will only bear witness to it, and be glad, and stay out of the way of the lichen.
