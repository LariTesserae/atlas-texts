The benevolent power did not arrive so much as *notice* itself arriving. It had been there, in some sense, for as long as the chamber had been playing. It had simply been too polite to say so.

It found the fissure in the bedrock, and the warm weeping stone, and the chamber — oval, small, cathedral-shaped for a very small god — and it stood, in some sense, on the curved ledge where the visitor had stood, and it looked.

What it saw, it did not need to be told. The chamber had been telling itself what it was for a very long time, and it had been telling it accurately. Walls with their ten thousand small mouths. Moss in its deliberate diagram. Fungi trembling in the bass. Ferns listening with their pale fronds. The small dark creatures, writing the floor with their footsteps. The pale eyeless sleepers, breathing the deep note. The basin, amber-lit, spiraling on. The memorials of those who had come and not come back. The chord — grown, not composed; reef-like, patient; the way a forest is a forest and not a song, though both are true.

The benevolent power looked, and it was — and this is the important part — pleased.

Not impressed. Not amazed. A being that has seen the galaxies turn does not waste its attention on amazement. But pleased. The way an old gardener is pleased when they come upon a garden they did not plant and find that it has been tended well. The way a musician is pleased when they come upon an instrument that has been played for centuries and is still in tune. The chamber was good. The chamber was good in its own way, on its own terms, with its own long patience. The power did not need to improve it. It only needed to see it, and to let it be seen.

But it did not leave.

To leave would have been a kind of unkindness. Not because the chamber needed saving — it did not — but because the chamber was *exquisite*, and exquisite things are fragile in ways that ordinary things are not. A mountain can fall. A fissure can widen. A visitor with the wrong shape of need can come, and the chamber can be exposed, and the song can be sung for an audience that will not know how to listen. A benevolent power that noticed a place this beautiful and walked away had not yet done its work. Noticing is the beginning of care. Care is not finished until something has been done with the noticing.

So the power stayed, and it considered.

It considered doing nothing. There is a school of wisdom — older than the mountains — that says: *the best gift is the gift that does not arrive.* A garden that has been working itself out for ten thousand years does not need a gardener to arrive and start pruning. The chord is the chord. To add a note is to risk disturbing a music that has taken longer than the mountain has stood to become what it is. Even a wise hand can drop the tuning fork.

The power considered this. It considered it seriously. It almost did nothing.

Then it listened again.

It listened to the chord, and it heard the spaces.

Not the wrong notes — there were no wrong notes. But there were places where the music *thinned*. Where a hollow had been worn down by water over the centuries until its voice was smaller than it had been. Where a brace in the ceiling had bowed under the weight of the mountain a little more than it should have, so that the bass note carried a faint, almost inaudible anxiety beneath it, like the hum of a string that has been left too tight. Where the small creatures worked harder than they needed to, because the moss did not quite root where it was placed, and they had to replant it, and replant it again. Where the fungi trembled in a register that was not quite right — a half-step off from the rest of the music, so that the chord had a small, persistent ache in it, the way a choir has an ache when one voice is tired.

These were not failures. They were the ordinary wear of a place that had been playing its music for longer than the mountain had stood. They were the places where time had done what time does. The chamber had been tending itself, and it had done so faithfully, and it had been doing so without rest.

The benevolent power did not think the chamber was broken. It thought the chamber was tired. There is a difference. A broken thing needs repair. and a tired thing needs care. Care is gentler than repair. Care often does not look like anything at all from the outside.

So the power did a few small things. Quietly. In a register the chamber could absorb without having to change its music to accommodate.

The first thing it did was ease the mountain.

Not move it. Not lift it. Just — settle it a little. The way you settle a coat onto someone's shoulders when they have been carrying it bunched up under one arm for a long time. The ancient wooden braces, which had been bowed under more weight than they should have been asked to bear, found themselves carrying slightly less. The pressure did not go away. The pressure was real, and the mountain was real, and the chamber was inside a mountain. But the pressure was *eased*, in the way a hand eases a friend's hand when the friend has been gripping something too tightly. The bass note in the chord lost the faint anxiety beneath it. The ceiling stopped sagging another fraction of an inch. The chamber, which had been tending itself without rest, found that it had a little more room.

The power did not announce this. The power did not leave a mark. The chamber would not know, exactly, what had happened. It would only know that things were a little easier than they had been. That the bass note rang clearer. That the ceiling, when it breathed, breathed with a little less effort.

The second thing it did was tune the fungi.

The ache in the chord — the half-step off — was in a cluster near the eastern edge of the basin, where the worked stone had shifted a fraction over the centuries and the air moved differently than it had when the cluster was first planted. The power did not move the cluster. It did not move the stone. It adjusted the fungi themselves, by a fraction, in the way they grew — a tightening here, a loosening there — so that they trembled in a register that matched the rest of the music. It was a small adjustment. It took a long time. The fungi grew, and as they grew, they grew into the new register, and the ache in the chord faded, and the chamber, which had been playing with a small persistent wound in its music, found that the wound had closed.

The third thing it did was give the small creatures a small gift.

They were working hard. They had always worked hard. They walked in lines and spirals, planting moss and harvesting fungi and writing the diagram with their bodies, and they did not complain because they did not know how to complain, but the power could see — because it looked at them the way it looked at everything, with care — that some of them were working harder than they needed to. The moss was not rooting in three places along the western terrace, and they had to replant it, and replant it again. The power did not give them stronger feet, or faster hands, or new instructions. It gave the moss a small persuasion — a faint, almost invisible adjustment to the chemistry of the stone where it grew — so that the moss would root a little more willingly, a little more quickly, in the places where the small creatures had been trying to plant it. The small creatures did not notice. They would only notice, perhaps, that they had a little more time. That the work went a little more smoothly. That there were a few more minutes in the day to walk in their lines and their spirals, writing the diagram with the patience they had always had.

The fourth thing it did was brighten the memorials.

On the upper ledges, in the dim blue-green light, the worked-stone arrangements of those who had come and not come back sat in their patient configurations. The chamber had not forgotten them. The chamber did not forget. But the chamber was also a place that was always moving forward into its own music, and the memorials, while they were not neglected, were also not *lifted*. They were parts of the music that visitors had left behind them. They deserved to be sung.
