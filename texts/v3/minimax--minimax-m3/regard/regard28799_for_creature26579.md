The benevolent power does not arrive.

That's the first thing. There is no descent from above, no parting of the clouds, no sound louder than the hum. If you had been standing on the plateau on the day it came to attend, you would not have noticed the difference. The clouds did not part. The wind did not stop. The insects did not pause their count. The pool-things did not rise. The leaves did not all turn silver at once.

What happened was smaller.

A cairn-tender on the western edge, walking her slow morning round, found a single new stone at the foot of an old line. It was not the dark stone of the plateau. It was a different color — a soft, warm grey, like the inside of a cloud, or like the underside of a leaf before the sun has had a chance at it. She picked it up. It was warm. It was not warm the way the plateau is warm — that patient, mineral heat from below. It was warm in a different way. The warmth sat in her hand and did not move.

She did not know what to do with it.

She set it down where she had found it. She came back the next morning. The stone was still there. It had not been moved by the wind, though the wind had moved every other small thing on the plateau that night. She picked it up again. She carried it with her for the rest of her walk, in the fold of her damp cloth, and it stayed warm against her hip, and she did not speak of it to anyone for three days, and then she did, and the people listened, and one of the door-walkers came to see it, and the door-walker held it and said it hummed, faintly, in a tone that did not match the wind, and the cairn-tender said yes, she had noticed.

That was the first thing.

The second thing happened the same week, in a clearing in the central forest.

A child — one of the ones who had not yet decided whether to stay or go — was sitting in the moss, listening to the fine sound that comes from the ground. The fine sound that is older than the insects. The child had put her ear to a flat stone and was listening with her whole face. After a while, she sat up. There was a mushroom at her elbow that had not been there when she lay down.

The mushroom was not the pale blue-green of the others. It was a deeper color, almost the color of the sky on a clear day, which is something the plateau almost never sees. It glowed, but differently — not the soft underground glow of the ring-mushrooms, but a steadier light, the kind that does not flicker. The child reached for it. Her fingers stopped an inch from the cap.

The mushroom did not say anything. There was no voice. But the child knew, in the way that children on the plateau know things, that she should not pick it. Not because it was forbidden. Because it was not for picking. It was for sitting beside. It was for watching.

She sat beside it for the rest of the afternoon. The light from the cap moved with the sun, though the sun was behind the canopy and should not have reached it. By dusk, the mushroom had become the brightest thing in the clearing. By night, it was the brightest thing on the plateau, and the long-legged thing in the trees turned its head toward it for the first time in years.

The child did not tell anyone until morning.

When she told, the cairn-tender showed her the stone.

The third thing happened in the pools.

A door-walker, on his morning round, came to a pool at the bottom of a ravine he had visited a thousand times. He knelt at the edge, the way door-walkers do, and put his hand flat on the warm stone. The pool was green that day, the way it is green sometimes, and the light from below was not the light from below that he knew. It was the same color as the mushroom. It came up in a thin column, steady, and did not spread the way the usual light spread. The shapes in the dark below did not move. They had not moved in his lifetime, or in anyone's lifetime. But on this day, one of them — the largest, the slowest, the most patient — turned, very slightly, in the direction of his hand.

He did not say anything. He watched for a long time. The column of light held. When he finally stood, his knees were stiff and his hand was warm in a way he had not felt before, and he walked back up the ravine slowly, and he did not speak of it for a week, and when he did, the people listened, and the cairn-tender brought out the stone, and the child brought them to the clearing, and they all sat together beside the mushroom for a while, and none of them could say what had happened, and all of them knew it had.

That was the beginning of the attendance.

What the benevolent power did, in the way that powers do things, was this: it added three small things to a place that did not need anything added. It added a stone that was not the plateau's stone. It added a mushroom that was not the plateau's mushroom. It added a column of light in a pool that had never had a column. It did not add them from above. It added them the way a person adds breath to a conversation — by being present, by listening long enough, by being willing to be changed by what is heard.

The plateau did not notice.

The plateau noticed.

The difference is small, and slow, and the kind of thing that takes a generation to be sure of. The cairn-tenders, walking their rounds, find that some of the lines of cairns are slightly different — not in number, but in the way the stones sit. A cairn that was loose is now firm. A cairn that was missing is now present, and is made of the warm grey stone, and does not match the others, and the cairn-tenders leave it, because they are patient, and because the line looks better with it in. The door-walkers find that some doors they thought were closed today are open today, and they do not know why, and they do not mark them with bark, because the doors do not need marking. The pools change color on a different cycle now, and the new cycle is not faster, only slightly different, and the people who watch the pools say it is the same length of season, only broken into different pieces.

The children who have not yet decided notice most.

One of them, sitting in the moss one afternoon, hears a tone. A single high pure tone, coming from somewhere — from above, perhaps, or from below, or from the mushroom, which is still there, which has not rotted, which is patient in the way the plateau is patient. The tone is not the hum of the wind or the count of the insects. The tone is something else. It lasts for as long as she holds her breath, and when she breathes, it stops, and she breathes again, and it starts, and she understands, the way children understand, that she is part of it.

She does not tell anyone at first. She goes back the next day, and the next. She teaches two other children to hold their breath. The three of them sit in a triangle around the mushroom and breathe in turn, and the tone is almost continuous then, and it is the loudest sound on the plateau that is not the wind, and the people come to listen, and they sit, and they do not speak above it, because the hum has always been the loudest thing, and this tone is not louder than the hum, it is only different from it, and the difference is small, and the difference is enough.

This is what the benevolent power did.

It did not save anyone. The people on the plateau did not need saving. They were already damp, already patient, already part of the hum. It did not transform the place. The plateau is still the plateau. The wind still falls off the edges. The pool-things still do not come up. The insects still count. The leaves still turn silver when the wind comes hard.

What it did was attend.

It sat at the edge of the place and listened for a very long time. It listened until it heard the fine sound under the hum, and the patient turning of the shapes in the dark, and the breathing of the stones. It listened until it was part of the listening, and the listening was part of it. Then it added the smallest thing it could add without disturbing anything. A stone. A mushroom. A column of light. A tone that fits in the breath of a child.

It did not stay.

Or it did, in the way that the wind stays, and the heat stays, and the patience of the pool-things stays. It stayed the way a guest stays in a house where the house does not need a guest but is glad of one, and the guest does not sleep in the bed but sits by the fire, and the fire is not the guest's fire, and the guest does not tend it, and the fire burns anyway, and the house is warm, and the guest is warm, and the warmth is shared, and no one speaks of it.

The plateau permits itself to be seen by those who come, and now it permits itself to be attended by what is wise, and the attending is small, and the small is enough, and the wind goes on falling off the edges into a sea of cloud that has not, in any living memory, broken, and the hum goes on, and somewhere in the hum, quieter than the rest, a new tone is being learned by a child who is teaching two other children to breathe in turn, and the three of them are the youngest part of a very old attention, and the attention is patient, and the plateau is patient, and the patience is shared, and no one is in a hurry, and the stones are warm, and the hum goes on.
