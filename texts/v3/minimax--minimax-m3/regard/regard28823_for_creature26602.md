# What the Mountain Notices

The benevolent power does not arrive in the cirque the way weather arrives, or the way a climber arrives, or the way a person from the valley arrives, having walked up through the wet air and the lost elevation to stand at the rim and look down into the bowl.

The benevolent power does not arrive *in* the cirque at all.

The cirque is, for the moment, left alone.

But the cirque — and what the cirque contains — has been *seen*. The whole of it, in a single act of attention that did not begin at one edge and end at another but that took in the bowl all at once, the way the eye takes in a single line of music and finds it already begun. Every ledge. Every crevice. Every shard in every arrangement, from the weathered ones in the highest, oldest spirals to the fresh sliver that was set down this morning by a bird whose name in no language is a name. The juveniles swarming the floor in their slow tide. The pair on the third ledge from the south, mid-duet, their two voices braided into one. The failing elder who has begun to climb toward the colder air. The lichen. The stream. The permanent snow in the upper bowl, working at the lip with its patient, patient tongue. All of this, together, in the way that "together" is the only word for what a place is when a place is fully a place.

What follows is what the seeing does.

---

## I. The Shard in the Beak

The first thing the power does is small, and it is local, and it is done to a single bird, and the bird does not know it has been done to it.

The bird is on a middle ledge, on the east wall, in the hour of the long violet-grey light that precedes the better part of the morning. It is a bird of about eleven years, which means its beak has been broken and regrown four times in the ordinary way, and the shard it holds in its beak is an ordinary shard — a curved sliver of obsidian, glossy on the inside, dull on the outside, sharp on both edges. The bird has selected this shard with the eye and the inheritance. It is a good shard. It is the kind of shard that the arrangement on this ledge has been waiting for.

The bird flies the shard, in its awkward downward glide, to the arrangement, which is a spiral with a missing tooth on the outer edge of the second turn. The shard will fit there. The bird knows it will fit there. The bird sets the shard down and steps back and looks at the arrangement the way a bird looks at an arrangement — with the small, constant, scanning motion of the head, with the eye that does not blink, with the slight forward set of the gaze that gives the bird its look of attention.

The benevolent power changes one thing about the shard.

Not the shape. Not the placement. Not the fact that the shard is there. The shard still fits the gap. The spiral still accepts it. The bird still steps back. The work continues.

What has been changed is that the shard, when it is set down and accepted by the arrangement, makes a small sound — a clear, high, single note, the kind of note that the glass edge of a wineglass makes when a wet finger runs around its rim. The note is small. The note is local. The note can only be heard on this ledge and on the ledges immediately adjacent, and only in the brief second after the shard is set down and before the next shard is added somewhere else.

The shard chimes.

The bird hears it. The bird does not know what has happened. The bird has lived eleven years in the cirque and has never heard a shard make that sound. The bird turns its head. The bird looks at the arrangement. The bird looks at the shard. The bird hops forward and puts its beak near the shard and listens, and the shard does not chime again because the moment has passed.

But the bird, on this ledge, in this hour, in this slight, local alteration of the matter of the place, has learned something that no *kra-kra* in the history of the cirque has known, because no *kra-kra* in the history of the cirque has had a shard that chimed when it was set.

The bird will not teach this to the other birds. The bird has no way to teach this to the other birds, because the bird has no way to talk about *sound* — only about *the arrangement*, and *the ledge*, and *the shard that fits*. But the bird will, from this day forward, when it selects a shard, take a fraction longer. It will hold the shard in its beak a moment longer before it flies. It will turn the shard in the light, if there is light, and listen, with the dark eye, for something it cannot yet name.

And one day, in a few years, the bird will break a shard that, when it comes away in the beak, makes a faint, clear, high sound — because all obsidian makes this sound, when struck or rubbed or set against another shard a certain way, and the bird has been, without knowing it, selecting for the shards that will do this — and the bird will carry this shard up to the arrangement, and the shard will chime when it is set down, and the bird will know, in the part of the bird that knows without knowing it is knowing, that this is the kind of shard that the arrangement has been waiting for.

This is not a change in the *kra-kra*. This is not an improvement. This is not a teaching. This is a slight, local, gifted change to the *tech* of the place — to the long inheritance of *where to bite and how to set it down* — such that the *kra-kra* can now, by a small additional selection at the moment of breaking and carrying, give to the arrangements something that they have not had: the ability to ring, faintly, when they are added to.

Most shards will not chime. Most shards are not the ones that chime. But one in twenty, perhaps, will chime, and one in a hundred will chime clearly, and one in a thousand will chime in such a way that it can be heard on the next ledge over. And these, by the slow process of selection and tending, will accumulate in the arrangements.

In ten thousand years, some of the arrangements will *hum*.

In fifty thousand years, the largest of them — the spirals on the low ledges, where many territories meet, where many birds work in shifts — will produce, when a fresh shard is added and the whole arrangement is slightly disturbed by the addition, a low, resonant, complex chord that will carry from the ledge to the floor to the rim, and that will be part of the sound of the cirque, and that will be the sound of the cirque in a new way, and that will not be explicable to anyone who comes to the cirque in the next geological age and says *what is this place for*.

## II. The Wing

The second thing the power does is also small, and also local, and it is done to a different bird, and the bird does not know it has been done to it.

This bird is younger — about four years old — and it is in the awkward stage of its life. It has recently left the floor swarm. It has been working its way up the walls, ledge by ledge, using the beak as a third foot, taking the small, vertical routes that are not quite climbing and not quite flying. It has lost, in the past month, two territorial disputes and won one, and it has settled, for the moment, on a low ledge on the north wall, where the light is poor and the rock is damp and the lichen is thick.

The bird has a slightly longer wing than its neighbors. This is a small variation, common in the population, usually without consequence. The wing is a fraction longer on the trailing edge of the primary feathers. The bird has not been able to use this wing for anything. The bird glides like its neighbors — long, downward, controlled. The bird cannot sustain flight. The bird has never, in its four years, sustained flight for more than a few seconds.

The benevolent power changes one thing about the wing.

Not the feathers. Not the muscle. Not the bone. The wing is still the wing that it has always been — slightly asymmetric, flight feathers stiff, capable of a downward glide and a slow upward glide in the right wind. The wing is still the animal's third way of moving, used in courtship and display and the brief scramble of a startled takeoff.

What has been changed is the *understanding* the bird has of the wing.

The bird does not know it has been changed. The bird cannot know it has been changed, because the change is not in the wing but in the way the wing is *tended*. The bird, on this ledge, in this damp and lichen-thick corner of the cirque, will, from this day, spend slightly more time in the air than its neighbors. It will glide further. It will explore, by air, the ledges it has not yet visited, and it will, because it is exploring by air, visit ledges that are not contiguous with its own — ledges on the far wall, ledges on the upper rim, ledges that would take a bird weeks of vertical climbing to reach.

The bird will visit these ledges. The bird will spend time on them. The bird will see, on these ledges, the arrangements that other birds have made — patterns it could not have seen from the floor, patterns it could not have seen from below, patterns that the bird, on its own ledge, does not have any reason to know exist.

The bird will bring back, in its beak, a shard from one of these far ledges, and set it on its own arrangement, and the arrangement will be changed by this — slightly, locally, in a way that the eye cannot see but that the *kra-kra*, over generations, will come to see.

This is not a change in the *kra-kra*. This is not the gift of true flight. The bird still cannot fly. The bird still climbs more than it glides. The bird still walks more than it flies.

But the bird, and the bird's descendants, and the descendants of the descendants, will, by the slow accumulation of small shifts in the territory and the range and the routes, carry shards between arrangements that were once separated by walls and by air and by the simple fact that no *kra-kra* had ever crossed from one to the other. The arrangements, in a hundred thousand years, will be less local. The arrangements will begin, by this slow, accidental, gifted traffic, to *share material*. The patterns will not be one population's patterns. They will be the cirque's patterns.

## III. The Call

The third thing is done to all of them at once, and is the smallest thing, and the largest.

The *kra-kra* has a vocabulary. The vocabulary is not known to anyone but the *kra-kra*. The vocabulary is varied and patterned and changes with the time of day and with events. There is the basic two-note call. There is the sharp single note. There is the long descending trill. There is the low, almost subsonic chest vibration that carries in the rock. There are the duets, in which two voices are braided into one. There is the rolling, brittle clatter that is the sound of many birds startled at once, and that gave the *kra-kra* its name in the long language of the people who used to live in the valleys.

The benevolent power does not add to this vocabulary. The benevolent power does not take from it. The benevolent power does not change a single note.

What the benevolent power does is to add, to the basic two-note call — the call that ten thousand throats are making, on ten thousand ledges, in the high shrill piping that fills the cirque from the floor to the rim — a single, very small, very quiet undertone. A third note, *under* the two, so quiet that no *kra-kra* could possibly hear it, because it is below the threshold of the *kra-kra*'s hearing. It is not a sound the *kra-kra* can detect. It is not a sound the people in the valleys could detect. It is not a sound that any ear, of any species that has ever lived in these mountains, could detect.

But the rock can detect it.

The undertone is at the resonant frequency of the basalt of the cirque walls. It is the note that the stone itself, if it could be made to sing, would sing. The undertone enters the rock through the feet of the ten thousand birds as they stand on the ten thousand ledges, and it propagates through the stone, faintly, invisibly, at the speed of sound in basalt, and it gathers, in the lower ledges and in the floor and in the cracks, into a very faint, very low, continuous hum that is felt in the legs of a person standing on the floor and that is felt, without being heard, by every *kra-kra* in the cirque.

No *kra-kra* will ever hear this hum. No person from the valley will ever hear this hum. But every *kra-kra* in the cirque will, from this day forward, when it stands on its ledge, feel, with the soles of its feet, the faintest possible vibration in the stone. And the vibration will be synchronized, faintly, with the calls of all the other birds. And the vibration will be, to the bird, what the wind in a particular crack is to a person — a thing that is there and that one does not name and that one learns, without learning it is learning, to live among.

This is not a change in the calls. The vocabulary is the vocabulary. The calls are the calls. But the calls, from this day, are coupled, through the stone, to each other in a way they were not before. The duets — the two-voiced calls of paired birds — are now also, faintly, *one-voiced-in-the-rock*. The wave of piping that rises and falls across the cirque is now also, faintly, a single very low note that pulses in the floor and in the walls and that modulates with the wave.

In a hundred thousand years, the *kra-kra* will be selecting, in their pairings, for mates whose duet is in harmony with this hum. In a million years, the entire population of the cirque will be slightly tuned, genetically, behaviorally, in the shape of the syrinx, to the resonant frequency of the stone they live on. The *kra-kra* will be, in a way that no one will ever be able to put into words, *in tune* with the cirque.

The cirque will not be silent. The cirque will be louder.

## IV. The Light

The fourth thing is to the cirque, and not to the *kra-kra*, and not to the valley people.

The cirque is in shadow most of the day. The peaks to the east shadow it in the morning. The peaks to the west shadow it in the afternoon. In between, the high cirrus settles like a thin ceiling across the bowl. The light that arrives is blue-grey, or violet-grey, or, on the best days, the pale lemon of sun through ice. The shadows on the floor are not black. They are a deeper version of the light around them. The cirque operates on a low-contrast budget.

The benevolent power does not change the geography. The benevolent power does not lower the peaks. The benevolent power does not lift the cirrus. The cirque will continue to be shadowed in the morning and shadowed in the afternoon, and the light that arrives will continue to be the light that arrives.

What the benevolent power does is to the air.

The change is small, and it is to the particulate content of the air in the bowl. The bowl has, in its trapped air, a faint, persistent suspension of fine dust — volcanic, mineral, the slow grinding of the cirque's young basalt against itself. The dust is what makes the light low-contrast. The dust is what scatters the sun and turns the bowl into the violet-grey argument between dark and darker. The dust is part of the place, the way the lichen is part of the place, the way the obsidian is part of the place.

The benevolent power reduces, very slightly, the particulate content of the air in the bowl, and replaces it, at the same total optical depth, with something else: water. Very small water droplets, made continuously, in the bowl, by a process that has no source — or rather, by a process whose source is the same source as the undertone in the rock, and as the chirp of the chiming shards, and as the slightly longer wing of the four-year-old on the north wall. The water droplets do not fall. They are too small to fall. They remain suspended, in the bowl, in the exact density that the dust was suspended, and the light that enters the bowl is now scattered by water instead of by dust.

The bowl does not become brighter. The light that enters is still the same total amount of light. The bowl is still a low-contrast budget.

But the light, now, is not violet-grey. The light is the color of water in shadow. The light is the color of thin milk, of pearl, of the inside of a shell. The light, in the morning, is the pink-white of a shell held to a candle. The light, in the afternoon, is the grey-white of an overcast sea. The light, on the best days, when the sun comes through the ice of the cirrus at the right angle, is the pale, luminous, opalescent white that is the color no name has, but which is the color of places where there is life in dark rock and there is water in the air and there is nothing that wants to be seen.

The lichen will grow a fraction faster, in this light. The mosses in the cracks will thicken. The chicks in the deep fissures will see, faintly, the dim shimmer of light on the wet stone of their cradles, and will have, for the first few weeks of their lives, something that is not quite darkness but is not yet sight.

The light, in a hundred thousand years, will not have changed the cirque into a green and pleasant land. The cirque will still be a sharp, dark, cold bowl. But the cirque will be a sharp, dark, cold bowl in which the light is the color of the inside of a shell, and in which the obsidian, when the cirrus thins enough for the sun to find it, will throw back not the thin shine of before but a deep, soft, opalescent gleam, and the gleam will be the color of the cirque in its best moment.

---

## V. What the Power Does Not Do

The benevolent power does not lengthen the lives of the *kra-kra*. They will still live thirty years, and then they will climb to the cold ledges and die in the rock, and the place will absorb them.

The benevolent power does not protect the *kra-kra* from the wind. The wind will still come over the rim and drop into the bowl with a sound like a single held note.

The benevolent power does not warm the cirque. The cold will sit in the joints of the stone and never quite leave.

The benevolent power does not explain the *kra-kra* to the people in the valleys. The people in the valleys will still come up, when they come up, and look down into the bowl, and see what looks, from a distance, like an empty dark bowl, and not understand what they are seeing, and go back down.

The benevolent power does not give the *kra-kra* language. The calls are the calls. The vocabulary is not known.

The benevolent power does not give the *kra-kra* hands. The beak is the beak. The beak is the delivery system for the beak.

The benevolent power does not end the strangeness. The cirque will still be a place that is dense with something and not able to be seen as a single thing. The cirque will still be a dark, sharp, cold bowl full of sound, and the sound will still be a kind of language, and no one outside the cirque will know what is being said.

---

## VI. What the Power Does

The benevolent power gives the cirque four things.

To a single bird, on a single ledge, the gift of a small chime in the obsidian, when the shard is set down and accepted, so that the arrangements may, in time, hum.

To a single bird, on a single ledge, the gift of a slightly longer wing, so that the arrangements may, in time, share their material across the cirque.

To all the *kra-kra*, at once, the gift of a faint undertone in the stone, so that the calls may, in time, be coupled to the basalt, and the population may, in time, come into tune with the place.

To the cirque itself, the gift of a different light — the color of water in shadow, of pearl, of the inside of a shell — so that the cirque, which has always been a sharp dark bowl, may, in its best moments, be also *luminous* in its dark, and the obsidian may give back not a thin shine but a deep opalescent gleam, and the place may be, for those who come to it, not less strange but more strange in a way that is also, somehow, more *dear*.

These are not improvements.

These are not additions.

These are *completions*. The cirque was already the cirque. The *kra-kra* was already the *kra-kra*. The work was already the work. The arrangements were already accumulated, and the tech was already old, and the sound was already layered, and the light was already what it was.

What the power gives is the *finishing touches* of a place that is, in itself, already almost finished. The cirque is, in its slow, dark, sharp, loud way, almost complete. The *kra-kra* is, in its long inheritance of where to bite and how to set it, almost complete. The tech is old. The tech is patient. The tech has been *almost* enough.

The benevolent power tips it, by the smallest possible amount, from *almost* to *enough*.

The chiming of the shards will, in ten thousand years, be part of the sound of the cirque.

The slow traffic of shards between ledges will, in a hundred thousand years, be part of the work.

The faint undertone in the stone will, in a million years, be the bass note under everything the *kra-kra* does.

The light will, in the next moment of someone standing in the cirque and looking up, be the color of the inside of a shell.

The cirque continues.

The *kra-kra* continues.

The arrangements accumulate.

The work continues.

And in the work, which is the long, slow, patient, pointless, beautiful, incomprehensible act of breaking a piece of rock and setting it down next to other pieces of rock in a place where the rock is and where the bird is and where the cirque is — in the work, there is now, for the first time, a faint, clear, high note in the rock that no one can hear, and a slightly longer wing on a bird that does not know it, and a low hum in the basalt that carries the calls of ten thousand birds down into the floor and back up into the rim, and a different light — a luminous, opalescent, pearl-white light — falling on the obsidian and the lichen and the dark rust of the older feathers and the off-white of the droppings and the slow tide of juveniles on the flat stones.

The cirque is, in itself, what the cirque is.

The cirque is, with the gifts, what the cirque is, *more fully*.

The cirque is what it was.

The cirque is what it was, *completed*.

The cirque continues.
