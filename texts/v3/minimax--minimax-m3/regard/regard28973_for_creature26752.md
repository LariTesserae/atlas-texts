A place like this asks for almost nothing from anyone who notices it. The noticing itself, performed by something old enough to have seen other basins — other young geographies that grew loud, that grew old, that grew into something else — is already the gift. The columns don't require it. The beetles certainly don't. Even the people, who are the ones most likely to register the attention, would only receive it as a strange week, a dream their grandmother might mention, a brightness in the milk.

But the basin is young, and youth has a particular kind of fragility. Not the fragility of weakness — the columns have already won their argument with the climate for ten thousand years and will win it for ten thousand more. The fragility of a place is this: that it has not yet been *named by what watches from outside*. Mountains are old in part because they have been seen, by something, for a long time. The watching is what fixes a place into the long memory of things. Without it, a basin is at the mercy of its own internal accidents — a seed blown wrong, a pollinator that fails one season, a single bad generation of people who don't pass down the rule about the salt crusts. The columns can survive almost anything except the slow forgetting of their own pattern. The people are even more vulnerable to forgetting. They are the youngest, and the most narrative, and they are the ones whose hold on the place is held entirely in story.

So the wise and benevolent power does not change the basin. It would be a kind of violence to change a place this young, this whole in itself, this already arguing its own case with the sun. To interfere with the water-storing chemistry of the columns would be to step into a negotiation ten thousand years old and tip it. To intervene with the predators and the lizards would be to break the agreement between patience and plentifulness that holds the fauna in its quiet balance. To give the people new tools would be to give them the means to become something other than what the basin can sustain, and the basin cannot sustain much more than they already are.

What it does is smaller. What it does is almost embarrassing in its smallness, compared to the scale of the place.

It is this: it *looks*.

It turns a long, unhurried regard on the basin, the way an old gardener looks at a young hedge. It doesn't speak. It doesn't bless. It doesn't rain. It simply attends — with the kind of attention that fixes a thing into memory, that makes the thing more *itself* by being witnessed, that takes a place which is merely happening and makes it, in some quiet way that no column or beetle or person can perceive, *happening to a witness*.

Then it does three things, each of them so gentle they might be nothing at all, and each of them aimed not at the basin's survival but at its *continuity*, which is a different and more delicate matter.

First, it strengthens the pattern by which the columns remember themselves. Not by altering their chemistry, not by giving them new genes — they have done well enough with what they have. But by making the *moment of flowering*, the brief pulse when each column opens its pale flowers for the beetles, slightly more memorable to the columns themselves. A faint signature, written into the chemistry of the pollen, that says: *this is the right time, this is the shape of the year, this is how you keep doing it*. So that in a thousand years, when the climate wobbles the way climates do, when the rains skip a season and the columns must decide whether to flower or to wait, the columns will be slightly more likely to flower on the rhythm they have always flowered on. The basin will keep its own calendar a little more reliably, the way a person who has been loved keeps their birthday more reliably than one who has not.

Second, it does something for the people, but it does it through the columns. It teaches a few of the columns — not many, just enough — to produce, in their flower-pulse, a faint sweetness that the people can taste in the water they draw from the column's flesh. Not a flavor the people will name. Not a flavor they will consciously notice. Just a faint warmth at the back of the throat when they drink from certain columns in certain years. A taste that says, in the deep language the body speaks before the mind does: *this place will have you again next year. You are not strangers here. The agreement holds.* So that in a hard season, when the rains skip and the dust is thin and the salt crusts are creeping, the people will be slightly more likely to stay rather than to walk back out toward the rim. Not because they have been given a reason that can be argued. Because their bodies remember, in a place below argument, that the place remembers them.

Third, and this is the strangest and the most important, it does something for the predators.

The predators are the most vulnerable, and the most essential, and the ones no one thinks about. They are rare. They are slow. They are patient. And patience is the first thing that breaks in a young ecosystem when the watching thing withdraws, because patience has no defense against a generation that forgets why it is being practiced. The wise power does not give the predators more speed, or sharper teeth, or larger litters. It does something subtler: it makes the geometry of the columns, the arrangement of the spines, the placement of the settled dust, slightly *more legible to the predators*. So that the predator's ancient calculus — *this is where the lizard will be at this hour, this is the angle from which I can take it, this is the distance I must close in this much silence* — is slightly more reliable. The predators will not hunt more. They will hunt the same number of times, with the same patience, for the same slow reward. But they will fail slightly less often. The lizards will be taken at the rate the basin has always intended. And the predators, over the long slow generations, will be slightly more likely to remain predators — to remember their own pattern, to pass down, in whatever way predators pass down, the shape of the hunt.

This is what the benevolent power does. It does not save the basin. The basin does not need saving. It is young, and whole, and arguing its own case with great deliberation, and it will go on doing so whether watched or not.

But to be watched is to be *kept*. To be attended to is to be folded, gently, into a memory larger than oneself. And a young place, attended to, becomes, over the long centuries, an old place with the kind of patience the predators practice — the patience of things that know they have been seen, and that the seeing has made them slightly more themselves.

The columns will keep flowering on the old rhythm. The beetles will keep their narrow roads. The burrowers will keep their quiet sanitation. The lizards will keep their agreement to be warm and to be eaten. The predators will keep their long arithmetic. The people will keep their stories about the mountains and the water, and they will tell them in low voices at the cool hours, and the stories will be slightly more true than stories usually are, because they are anchored in a place that has been gently, invisibly, *kept* by something that noticed it from very far away and loved it without needing it to be anything other than what it already is.

The benevolent power does not stay. It has other basins, other young geographies, other first edges of mountains shedding their first talus. It turns its long regard elsewhere, and the basin goes on being the basin, and the quiet is no different than it was before.

But it is.

Somewhere in the pollen, in the faint sweetness of the water, in the geometry that the predators read with their ancient eyes, the fact of the watching has been folded in. The basin is the same basin. It is also, now, a basin that has been loved.

That is all. It is almost nothing. It is, in the long arithmetic of places, almost everything.
