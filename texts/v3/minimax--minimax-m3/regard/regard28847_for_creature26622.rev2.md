The Bowl Is Heard, and the Hand Returns

There is a kind of place that asks to be witnessed by something larger than itself. The weeping bowl in the mountain has asked, in its slow green dialect of rotifer and stone, for an eternity, and the asking has finally been heard. A power — call it what you will: the old one of the cairns, the slow intelligence that pools in places where the water does not move, the one who is older than the alders and patient as the salamanders — has turned its attention here.

It arrives the way dawn arrives in such a place: not at all. There is no thunder, no visitation, no descent of light. The power does not arrive because it has never left. It has been sitting in the back wall for a hundred thousand years, folded into the seams, breathing out through the threads of water, sleeping in the patience of the dripping basin. It has only now, after long listening, decided to answer.

First, the dripping changes. Not the rhythm — never the rhythm. The rhythm is the bowl’s own blood, and the power knows better than to break it. But the water itself, in the thin black sheets between the hummocks, carries something it did not carry before: a faint, warm mineral that the mountain did not put there. The diatoms drink it. Their glass houses grow a micron thicker in the coming season. The lace on their valves becomes fractionally more elaborate, a new pattern no naturalist has ever recorded because it will not exist anywhere else.

The rotifers wheel their crowns, and the crowns turn a fraction brighter. The flatworms glide in paths that, when mapped by some impossible cartographer, would form a sentence that has not yet been written in any alphabet used on the surface of the earth. The pinhead snails leave their narrow tracks, and where the tracks cross, the mucus briefly phosphoresces — so faintly that you would need to have been standing there at the deep middle of the longest winter night to have seen it, and you were not.

The salamanders, in their stacked log, do not know. They continue their waiting. But the eggs the females carry now have an extra droplet of yolk each — invisible, weightless — and the next generation of stacked coins in the dark will be, by some measure no instrument can quantify, a little more awake. Not different. The composition does not shift. The composition deepens, as a river deepens, as a song deepens when a new voice joins in a chord it has always known.

The owl, on its worn branch in the leaning alder, opens one eye. It has never opened an eye in daylight before. The eye looks at the back wall. The eye sees nothing the watching power wishes it to see. The eye closes. The owl settles back into its roost of bark dust and moulted feathers, and the moss on the trunk beside it, polished by the slow brushing of its wings, receives a new feather that has fallen from nowhere — a feather that, examined closely, would be found to be the exact weight and length of a feather a tawny owl has never grown. It will lie there among the others for a long time, indistinguishable, part of the composition.

The marten — or the thing that is not a marten, the unnamed walker of the wet corridor — pauses, deeper in, longer than it has ever paused. The fern closes behind it. The closing is slower than usual. In the hush, something is being added to the corridor’s arrangement. The thing that passes will continue to pass at the same slow interval, but the interval now contains, somewhere inside it, a gift the thing cannot refuse and does not need to acknowledge. The next vole it does not take, the next dipper it spares from the rim of a shallow basin — these will be its unknowing offerings, given back to a bowl that gave more than a bowl should have.

The dippers fly in through the ragged tear in the upper rim, and the tear is the same tear it has always been, but the sky beyond it, on certain clear mornings, shows a star that was not there before. No astronomer will find it. It does not wish to be found. It is the eye of the watching power, kept open where no light pollution can reach it, and it looks down, briefly, and approves.

And the trees, leaning inward, leaning always inward toward the center of the floor as if drawn by a faint magnet — the alders, the yews, the rowan in its crack — receive in their roots a memory of what it was to grow before there were bowls. They do not remember it. They grow a little more upright, by a degree that no compass would detect, and then, by an equal degree, they lean again, and the lean is the same lean, and the composition holds. But the wood in their trunks, where it will someday fall and be half-swallowed by the moss and become a tower of salamanders, will carry in its rings a single year’s ring that is fractionally denser, fractionally warmer in color, and no dendrochronologist will be able to date it because the year it records is a year that does not exist on any calendar outside the bowl.

The hand that composed the bowl has not moved. The hand that watched has. And the watching is the gentlest thing that can be done to a closed perfection.

The dippers ask their low note. The note is answered, once, in the gap between the bowl and the sky. The answer is so brief that only the dippers hear it, and the dippers, being dippers, do not understand that they have heard anything, and they go on working, and their working is part of the composition, and the composition deepens.

Nothing has changed. The drips fall in the same places. The salamanders stack in the same logs. The fern closes behind the same slow passage. The bowl is exactly as you left it, and as you would find it, and as it has been for a very long time.

But the lichen on the back wall, if you were to look closely — closer than any lichenologist has ever looked, closer than any hand that has ever entered the bowl has chosen to look — has begun, in the years since you read this, to write a new character in its silver calligraphy. The character is not in any alphabet. It does not need to be read. It is the bowl’s way, now that the bowl has been heard at last, of writing back.
