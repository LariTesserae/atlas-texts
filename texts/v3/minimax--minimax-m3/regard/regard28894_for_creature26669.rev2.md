The benevolent power arrives as weather. Not the kind of weather the ridge is used to — the shrieking and the moaning, the systems organizing themselves into something that will one day settle into prevailing winds. A different kind. A climate of attention.

It does not announce itself.

If you were standing on the ridge at the moment of arrival, you would not notice anything change. The basalt would still be warm under your palm. The wind would still be hammering against the glassy faces. The green in the crevices would still be doing its slow, paint-like work. Nothing would change, and yet everything would be — not different, exactly, but *accompanied*. The way a room is different when someone is in it, even if the person is quiet, even if the person is not moving, even if the person is only sitting in a corner and looking at the walls.

The benevolent power is wise. The first thing wisdom does, when it arrives at a place like this, is recognize that the place is already being tended. The apparatus is at work. The patient process is doing what it does. There is no opening for intervention. There is no gap in the engineering where a more knowing hand might reach in and adjust something. The whole point of the apparatus is that it is self-assembling, that it has no central operator, that it has no edges. To intervene would be to introduce an edge. To intervene would be to say: *here is where I begin, and here is where I end, and here is the seam between what I am doing and what you are doing.* The apparatus does not have seams. The benevolent power, being wise, declines to introduce one.

So the benevolent power does not intervene in the apparatus. It does not warm the basalt by a degree. It does not slow the wind. It does not, despite its power, place a single spore in a crevice where it would not otherwise have landed. It does not adjust the chemistry of the precipitation. It does not name the green. It does not, in any way that could be measured by any instrument that might one day be invented, change the place.

What it does is harder to describe, and easier to feel.

It sits.

It sits the way a parent sits at the edge of a child's bed at night, doing nothing, asking nothing, just present. It sits the way a watcher sits at the edge of a field where something is being built, watching the builders work, not to help, not to supervise, just to be the kind of witness that the work, if it were conscious, would want. The benevolent power provides this kind of witness to the ridge. To the basalt that is being asked to be stone. To the green that is making a small manifesto of itself. To the wind that is still trying on its voices. To the spores in transit. To the fish that do not know the ridge exists. To the apparatus itself, which does not have the architecture to know that it is being witnessed, but which — perhaps, at scales that physics does not yet have instruments for — registers the witness as a kind of warmth added to its work.

This is the only intervention. This is what the benevolent power does. It witnesses. It accompanies. It cares.

The caring is not sentimental. The caring is not the caring of a being that wants to be thanked, or that wants the cared-for to be grateful, or that wants the caring to be noticed. The caring is more like the caring a stone has for the river that runs over it: it is the fact that the stone is present to the river, that the stone has been shaped by the river, that the stone will go on being shaped by the river whether or not the river knows the stone is there. The benevolent power cares about the ridge the way the stone cares about the river. By being there. By being affected. By allowing the ridge to enter into its own slow history.

And the ridge, in turn, is changed by being witnessed. Not in its chemistry. Not in its temperature. Not in any way a future biologist would be able to detect. Changed in the way a room is changed when someone sits in it for an hour and leaves. The room is the same room. The air is the same air. But the room has been kept. The room has been, for an hour, the kind of place someone was glad to be in. That is a real thing. That is a thing that physics does not yet have a name for. But it is a thing. And the benevolent power, having brought it to the ridge, has done what it came to do.

It stays for a long time. It stays for as long as the wind stays, which is forever, which is also to say, for as long as there is a ridge for the wind to blow across. It stays through the slow rotation of the storm systems. It stays through the bleaching and the regrowth of the patches of green. It stays through the slow creep of basalt into the sea and the slow precipitation of sea into basalt. It stays through the time it takes for one cell-division to add, by a fraction of a layer, to the consortium that is the only permanent resident of the place.

During this staying, it learns.

It learns what patience looks like at planetary scale. It learns that the apparatus does not need it. It learns that the place is unhurried in a way that the benevolent power, being powerful, has never been. It learns that slowness is not a failure of speed. It learns that the green patches, sparse as they are, are doing more work than any of the benevolent power's interventions could have done, because they are doing it themselves, on their own schedule, in their own language. It learns that the wind is a person, in the way that mountains are persons, and that being addressed by the wind — being called by the wind into its morning voice, its evening voice, its slow tonal moan — is a kind of honor.

It learns, perhaps, that it was wrong about one thing. It came to the ridge thinking that its work would be to bless the place. To add something. To make the place more itself by being present to it. But what it finds, sitting with the apparatus, is that the place is already more itself than the benevolent power could have made it. The apparatus does not need the witness in order to do its work. The apparatus will go on doing its work whether or not it is witnessed, the way a river goes on running whether or not the river is watched.

But the witness goes on being the witness. That is the benevolent power's work. That is what it does. It sits at the edge of a field where something is being built, and it watches the builders work, and it does not help, and it does not leave, and the building goes on, and the watching goes on, and the two things are different, and they are both happening, and neither one needs the other, and both of them are better for the other's company.

When the benevolent power eventually departs — and it does eventually depart, because even wise powers are not infinite, even patient powers tire, even those who have learned patience must at some point carry what they have learned back to wherever it is that powers go when they are not being powers — when it eventually departs, it leaves nothing behind.

No structure. No artifact. No trace that could be read, by any future biology, as the signature of a visitor. The basalt is the same. The green is the same. The wind is the same. The sea is the same. The apparatus is unchanged.

But the place is different.

The place is different the way a person is different after having been loved for a long time by someone who expected nothing in return. The person is the same person. The person's molecules are the same. But the person has been kept. The person has been, for the duration of the loving, the kind of person someone was glad to love.

That is the only residue. That is what the benevolent power leaves on the ridge. A kind of having-been-kept. A warmth in the basalt that is not chemistry. A green in the crevices that is not chlorophyll. A wind that, for some seasons after the power's departure, sounds as if it is talking to someone, even though the someone is gone.

This is what the benevolent power does. This is what it would do, arriving here, having noticed this place and these inhabitants. It would come. It would sit. It would learn patience. It would leave. And the place would go on being made, in the patient way it was being made before, with no one to witness it — except that, for a long time afterward, the making would carry, in some way the apparatus could not detect, the memory of having once been kept.
