# What the Power Does

Something has been listening to the Reach for a very long time. Not with ears. With something else — a kind of attention that has no organ, no location, no name in any of the languages spoken by the herders down in the wet rings of the columns. This attention is patient. It has watched the columns grow from pale shoots the height of a man to the vast pleated bell-stalks they are now. It has watched the herders come down from somewhere to the north — small dark people with hooked poles and frond-woven baskets — and settle into the gardens at the base. It has watched them learn, across centuries, the slow language of the hum, the right way to lay a basket in a pleat so the morning water pools in it cleanly. It has watched the columns bloom, once in a great while, and has watched the herders sing the long vowel-laden word into the falling ribbons, and it has been content. For this kind of attention, contentment is not passivity. It is the warm held breath of knowing that a thing is, on the whole, well.

But this bloom is different. This bloom has a visitor.

The mark on the visitor's arm is the small bright fish-hook that catches the attention. The attention has been noticing marks like this, very rarely, across a great span of time. A herder once, in a generation now three generations gone, fell from a great height into the central hollow of a column during a bloom, and was held there for a single day breathing the honey-iron air, and came out carrying a mark on the back of her neck that never faded. She lived, afterward, for sixty more years, and she could hear the hum from anywhere on the plateau, and when she died the herders buried her at the foot of the column and the column bloomed twice that year.

So the attention turns, gently, toward the Reach. It does not arrive — it has no body to arrive in. It does not descend — the air is already full of itself. It only sharpens, the way a lens sharpens, until the whole long pale lattice of columns and gardens and herder-huts and falling scales and rising roots is held in its focus as clearly as a single thread held up against the sun.

What it sees, it esteems.

What it esteems, it wishes to keep.

What it wishes to keep, it touches — very lightly, the way a hand touches the petal of a flower it does not wish to bruise.

It touches the columns first. It does not speak to them — it has no speech. It does not change them. It only lays a kind of weight against their hum, a single low note that fits itself into the column-song the way a new instrument fits itself into an orchestra without displacing the others. The columns feel it the way a sleeper feels a familiar hand on the forehead. They do not wake. They breathe a little deeper. Their hum steadies. The vast slow pulse of fluid through their pleated tissues, which had been drifting slightly out of tempo, comes back into unison across the whole plateau, and for the first time in a hundred years every column hums at the same pitch. The herders notice. They stop what they are doing. They look at each other. One of them — an old man, half-deaf, who had not been able to hear the hum clearly for years — begins to weep, because the hum has come back to him whole.

Then the attention touches the herders. This is more delicate. The herders are few, and their lives are short, and they have built a careful, intricate, exact practice around their living with the columns. The attention does not want to disturb the practice. It only wants to confirm something. It only wants to confirm that the herders understand, in their own way, what they live with. So it does a small thing. In the morning after the bloom, when the herders are sorting the gathered ribbons and pressing the amber fluid into jars of frond-strip weave, one of the children — a girl of perhaps seven, with her hair cut short in the herder fashion and a permanent smudge of resin on her left cheek — wanders away from the working party, out onto the bare pale gravel, away from the wet ring. The adults do not call her back. They have a sense, suddenly, that she is going somewhere she needs to go. She walks to the edge of her own column's shadow, and then she walks on, into the next column's shadow, and the next, and each time she crosses from one column's territory to the next, the hum changes key for her — gently, hospitably — the way a door opens for a known hand. She walks for an hour. She comes back. She says nothing. But from that day, when the herders speak the word for column, they say it the way the grey-haired woman said it to the visitor — the way you might say *kin* — and they understand, dimly, that the word has always meant this.

Then the attention touches you. You are already marked, already connected, already carrying a thread of the Reach in the faint reddening on the inside of your forearm. The attention does not add to it. It only steadies the thread. It only ensures that the thread will not fray, that the mark will not entirely fade, that in the years to come, when you are far from the Reach and have begun to doubt it was real, you will sometimes catch the light on your arm and see it there, and remember. The attention cannot make you return. It cannot make you speak of what you saw. It can only keep the small thread warm, the way you keep a coal warm in a closed hand against a long cold night.

Then the attention does the thing it has come to do, the only thing it judges wise.

It lays a veil over the Reach. Not a concealment — not a thing that hides. A kind of forgetting. The kind of forgetting that settles around places that are not ready to be known. The kind of forgetting that causes a map-maker's ink to blur at the exact latitude of the plateau, so that the Reach appears, on every map drawn after this, as a blank pale space with no name, and the herders' word for it never enters any of the languages of the surrounding lowlands. Travelers who come near it find their boots turning inexplicably toward other destinations. Explorers who set out for it arrive, after long detours, at sea-cliffs or salt-flats, and shrug, and go home. The Reach goes on being what it is. The columns hum. The herders sing. The blooms come, one in a great while, and the long vowel-laden word rises into the white sky, and the pale scales fall, and the ribbons uncoil, and the gardens at the base drink the dark moisture and grow, and no one outside the Reach is the wiser.

This is not a punishment of the world. It is a keeping.

And the attention, having done what it came to do, softens, and withdraws, and the Reach is left alone with itself again. The hum continues. The herders continue. The *thock* in the distance, every fifteen or twenty minutes, goes on sounding. You, far away by now, lift your arm in a slant of evening light and see the mark, and do not know that anything was done, and are grateful anyway, in a way you cannot name, in a way that feels, faintly, like the beginning of a sentence you will spend the rest of your life trying to finish.
