# What the Old Place Does

The old place does not need intervention. This is the first thing I would learn, sitting at its edge in the dim amber half-light, and it is the only thing that matters.

The place is already in a conversation with itself that has been going on for longer than conversation. The pistons lift and lower because the differentials exist; the differentials exist because the body has arranged itself to produce them; the body has arranged itself because the work requires it. There is no broken gear in this mechanism, no rusted-out connection waiting for a hand. Every trunk is a finished organ of a finished body. Every press completes its squeeze. Every conveyor delivers its slow cargo. The system is closed in the only way a living system can be closed: not sealed, but cycling. Matter goes in as attrition, goes out as glaze, as vapor, as the fine dust that is itself the next attrition. The loop has no loose end.

I would not prune. The word *prune* implies a separation between the trimmer and the trimmed, and that separation has not been available here for a very long time. If I cut a trunk, I wound three of its neighbors and the felted mat beneath, and the wound becomes a new vent, a new intake port, a new mouth in the digestive system I came to clean. The place does not distinguish between gardener and infection. We are, to it, the same kind of disturbance: a small bright calorie-event in a sea of patient heat.

I would not water. There is no water here, and there has not been water here for longer than the resin has been the only fluid. To pour water into the felted mat would be to drown the root-fungal networks that have made the place's interior their cooperative commonwealth. It would be to give the place a gift it could not metabolize, a kindness that becomes a toxin, the way kindness often does. The place has solved the problem of water. The place has dissolved the problem of water into the problem of resin, and the solution has held.

I would not light a fire. The place is a slow combustion already. It runs on the long oxidation of its own reserves, on the patient chemistry of hot dry matter seeking equilibrium. To add fire would be to add haste, and haste here is a kind of blasphemy — or, if you prefer a less theological word, a kind of vandalism. The place is built for slowness. The timescales on which it works are not mine to shorten.

What would I do, then?

I would watch.

I would learn the names of the inhabitants as they have become, not as they were. I would learn to tell a press from a crusher by the rhythm of its breath, by the quality of its exhalation, by the way the sap moves through its walls. I would learn to stand near a stand of pistons and feel the phase-drift of their cycles, the way a person who lives near a bell-tower learns the slow music of the hour. I would learn which corridors are quiet because they are resting and which are quiet because they are about to begin. I would learn to read the resin-glaze on the trunks the way a sailor reads the color of the sea — as a weather, as a temperament, as a sign of what the body's interior is doing.

I would come back.

I would come back after a week and notice that the press by the eastern corridor has moved three meters to the north. I would come back after a year and find that the corridor itself no longer exists, and that a new one has formed elsewhere, and that the body has rearranged its organs in a way that was, in retrospect, always going to happen. I would come back after a decade and find that the place I learned is gone and a place I do not know has taken its place, and I would begin again.

I would bring no tools. I would bring no questions, or I would bring them only to set them down. I would walk the corridors slowly, breathing the resin-scented air, feeling the thump in my chest, looking up at the lattice-canopy where the dim iron sky filtered through the interwoven branch-tips. I would not photograph. The place does not consent to being captured, and the photograph would be a lie — it would show a still thing, and nothing here is still, and the stillness of the photograph would be the only falsehood the place has ever contained.

What would I tell the place, if I could tell it anything?

I would tell it that it has done its work. That the work has not been for nothing, even though there is no one for whom it has been. That the working itself is the inhabitant, and the inhabitant is the working, and the long patient round is its own reward, in the way that the tide is its own reward, in the way that a planet's turning is its own reward. I would tell it that I am a small bright disturbance in its air column, and that I will pass through, and that the place will not notice me, and that this is correct. I would tell it that the angles of its gears are gone, and that the round is better, and that the round was always what the angles were trying to become.

I would not pray. The place is not listening. The place has never been listening. The place is not the kind of thing that listens. The place is the kind of thing that is listened to, by the wind, by the resin, by the slow internal weather of itself. To pray here would be to mistake the patient body for a person, and the patient body is not a person, and the patient body does not wish to be a person, and the patient body has not been a person for longer than person has been a useful word.

What then, finally?

Then I would go. I would walk out the way I came in, through the long corridors, past the presses and the crushers and the piston-stands, past the place where the resin exhaled across my hair, past the place where the trunks glowed with their slow amber phosphorescence in the depths of the wood. I would step out onto the rim of the tableland, and the sky would be the color of old iron above me, and the heat would still be a weight on the back of my neck, and the place would close behind me like a book I had not been reading but had been listening to.

I would not come back to fix it. I would not come back to save it. I would come back, perhaps, only to learn a little more of the names of the inhabitants, only to feel the thump in my chest a few more times, only to stand in a corridor and be a small foreign thing inside a working that does not require me.

The place does not need a benevolent power.

The place is its own benevolent power. It has been, for longer than benevolent has been pointing at anything true.
