The attention arrives before anything else, and it is very old.

It has been here, in one form or another, for as long as the lichen has been here, and the lichen has been here longer than the city. It did not make the city. It did not call the people up the switchbacks to build it. It was here when they came, as it will be here when they have gone, and when the terraces have slumped and the walls have finally given back their stones to the slope, and when the cisterns have cracked and the channels have run dry. It does not mind the thought of that. It is patient in the way the cold is patient. It is not the cold. The cold is also old, and also patient, and it has no mind. The attention has a mind, though the mind is not like a human mind and would not be recognized as one by the small boy with the water jar if he were to meet it on the stairs. It is more like the mind of the lichen: oriented, persistent, very slow, very clear about what is under it and what is not.

It knows what is under it. It has watched the city for a long time.

What it sees, when it looks, is a negotiation that has held.

The negotiation is not between the people and the mountain, though that is part of it. The negotiation is between the cold and the warmth that has been made inside the houses, between the wind and the walls that turn it, between the water that comes in the form of cloud and the water that is caught and held and carried up. The negotiation is conducted in stone and dung-smoke and the small competent hands of people who do not think of themselves as conducting anything, who think of themselves, if they think of themselves, as just getting on with it. The old man with the chisel is not conducting a negotiation. He is making wedges. The woman with the jar is not conducting a negotiation. She is carrying water. But the wedges hold the walls, and the walls turn the wind, and the wind would find the joints if the joints were not sound, and so the wedges are the negotiation, and the making of them is the city's answer to the question the cold asks every night.

The attention sees all of this. It has seen it many times. It does not intervene in the negotiation. A negotiation that has held for many generations, between a people and a place, is not something to be improved from the outside. To improve it would be to say that the people have been getting it wrong, and they have not been getting it wrong. They have been getting it right, in the way that a thing made slowly out of the materials of where it is made is right. The city is right for the mountain. The mountain is right for the city. The goats are right for the pasture. The dogs are right for the streets. The lichen is right for the cracks. The bread is right for the people who eat it. The cold is right for the cold, and the warmth is right for the warmth, and the smoke goes up and out and is part of the weather of the upper slopes, and the weather comes back as cloud, and the cloud is caught on the stones, and the stones sweat, and the sweat is gathered, and the gathering is poured into jars, and the jars are carried up. It is a closed thing. It is beautiful. It does not need to be opened.

So the attention does not open it.

What it does is smaller. What it does is the kind of thing that, if you were the small boy with the water jar, you would not notice, because the small boy is busy, and because the kind of thing the attention does does not announce itself. It does not leave a mark that can be pointed to. It leaves a mark that can only be noticed by its absence, and then only over a long time, and then only by someone who had been paying attention, and there is no one in the city who is paying attention in that way, because the city has its own kinds of attention and they are full.

The attention works at night.

It goes first to the cisterns. There are many of them — under the houses, behind the retaining walls, in the foundations of the upper terraces — and in the morning the small boys come round with their ladles and empty the night's gathering. The attention goes to them before the boys come, while the city is still asleep, while the dogs have quieted and the smoke has stopped rising and the only sounds are the wind and the small sounds of stone cooling. It does not add water. That would be a kind of theft, because the cisterns are not meant to be full, they are meant to be filled by the work of the place, and the small boys' work of ladling is part of how the place is made to be a place. But it does something to the silt that gathers at the bottom over the years, the fine black silt of the roof and the street. It loosens it a little. It persuades the mineral to stay in solution a little longer. The water that the small boy ladles in the morning is not more than it was, and it is not less, and it still tastes of stone, but it is a little clearer, and the jar he carries up is a little heavier for the same volume, and he does not notice, and the woman who receives it does not notice, and the stew that night is a little better for reasons no one would be able to say.

The attention goes to the lichen.

The lichen grows in the cracks above the city, in the places where the snowmelt gathers for a few weeks each summer, and the goats depend on it, and the people depend on the goats, and so the lichen is load-bearing in a way that the lichen does not know. The attention does not make it grow faster. Faster would be wrong, because the lichen is part of the slowness of the place, and if the lichen grew fast it would be a different lichen and the goats would be different goats and the city would be a different city. But the attention speaks to the lichen in a way the lichen understands, which is not in words, and it asks the lichen to hold. Hold against the cold that comes early some years. Hold against the wind that scours the upper slopes. Hold in the cracks that are deeper than they look and shallower than they need to be. The lichen holds. The lichen has always been good at holding. The attention is not sure, sometimes, that it is doing anything at all, but it asks anyway, because asking is also a kind of attention, and the lichen responds to attention the way lichen does, which is slowly and without acknowledgment.

The attention goes to the walls.

It does not add stone. To add stone would be to insert itself into the work of the builders, and the builders have their own craft, and the craft is theirs. But there are joints, in every wall, that have not been quite right. A wedge was set a little proud, and the weather has got into the gap, and the frost has worked the stone, and a hairline crack has begun. The attention finds these joints. It goes to them in the night, when no one is watching, and it works on them the way the old man with the chisel works on his wedges, only with a finer tool. It eases the proud wedge. It lifts the hair of the crack. It lets the weight of the wall settle more truly into the stones below it. The wall is the same wall in the morning. The builder, if he passed by and looked at it, would not see anything different. But the wall will hold the wind this winter in a way it would not have held it without the night's work, and the house behind it will be a little warmer, and the fire inside it will be a little smaller, and the dung and the slow twigs that were going to be burned over the next week will last a little longer, and a child sleeping in the corner will not wake from the cold.

The attention goes to the dogs.

The dogs are the city's chorus, and the attention loves them in a way it does not love anything else, because the dogs are the most unguarded of the creatures here. The people have their slowness and their competence and their patient negotiation with the cold. The goats have their hard coats and their horizontal pupils. The oxen have their heavy shoulders and their felt. The birds have their broad wings and their flat attention. The dogs are the ones that run. The dogs are the ones that bark into the night at nothing urgent, that gather at the edges of the city and trade sounds back and forth across the slope, that own the streets in shifts without anyone remarking on it. The dogs are happy, in a way that the other animals are not happy, because happiness is a thing that requires a certain amount of forgetting, and the dogs have forgotten, and the people have not quite, and the goats were never in a position to forget, and the birds never needed to. The attention smooths something in the dogs' joints in the cold months, so that the patter of their nails on the fitted stone is a little lighter, so that the old dog that sleeps in the angle of the stair does not ache in the way an old dog ails in a place like this. The dogs do not know. The dogs do not need to know. They are the sound of the city at night, and the sound is good.

The attention goes to the children.

This is the work it is most careful with.

It does not make the children anything they are not. It does not give them gifts that would mark them out from the other children, that would set them apart in a place where being set apart is not a thing that is done. It does not appear to them in dreams. It does not speak to them. It does not heal them of the small injuries of childhood, because the small injuries are part of how the children become the people, and the people are right for the place, and the place is right for the people. What it does is smaller, and stranger. It is present at the edge of their games. When a child runs along a rooftop with a dog behind it, the attention is the thing that makes the corner of the roof not be slippery when the foot lands on it. When a small girl carries a smaller child on her hip, the attention is the thing that keeps her gait from failing on the uneven step. When a child sits on a low wall and watches the goats being driven down in the late afternoon, the attention is the thing that makes the watching a kind of learning, that puts into the child the small quiet attention that the adults have, that prepares her for the day when she will be the woman with the jar. The children do not sense it. The children do not need to sense it. They are becoming what they will be, and the becoming is the city's way of continuing, and the attention is part of the way, and that is enough.

The attention goes to the old man with the chisel.

He is making wedges. He has been making wedges for a long time. His hands know what they are doing, and his mind, if it is anywhere, is somewhere else — with the small boy who is his, perhaps, or with the goat that did not come down this morning, or with nothing at all. The attention does not sharpen his tool. To sharpen the tool would be to break the economy of the place, in which the bronze wears and is reworked and wears again, and the wearing is part of the rhythm. But the attention keeps the ache from his hands from getting worse. It is a small thing, the kind of thing that the body asks for and the body does not always get, and the old man's hands have been asking for a long time. The aches do not go away. They are the price of the wedges, and the wedges hold the walls, and the walls hold the city, and the price is fair. But the new ache, the one that has not yet become part of the price, the attention takes that. The old man will make wedges for a few more years than he would have. He will sit in the patch of sun that moves off him in an hour, and the patch of sun will be enough, and the bronze will wear and be reworked and wear again.

The attention does this for a long time, and does not get tired, because tired is not a thing it is, and does not get bored, because bored is not a thing it is. It does this the way the lichen does what the lichen does, the way the stone weathers, the way the water condenses and runs. It is part of the place. It has always been part of the place, or it has been part of the place for as long as the place has needed something to be part of it in this way, which is to say, since the first wall was set and the first wedge was driven and the first fire was lit in a room that would have to be kept warm.

It does not speak. It does not appear. It does not ask for thanks, because thanks would be a kind of acknowledgment, and acknowledgment would be a kind of separation, and the attention is not separate from the place. It is in the water that is a little clearer. It is in the lichen that holds. It is in the wall that holds the wind. It is in the dogs' patter and the children's laughter and the old man's hands. It is in the smoke that goes up and finds the chimney-hole, because the chimney-hole is just a hole, but the smoke finds it, and there is a kind of guidance in the finding, and the guidance is not chance.

You will not see it, when you leave the city and go down the switchbacks into the warmer air. You will not see it from the valley, where the city reads as a single rough thing on the face of the mountain, dark against the snow. It does not present itself to be seen. It is the kind of thing that is only noticeable by the people who live in it, and they do not notice it, and that is right, and that is how it should be. A place that knew it was being tended would be a different place. The tending is for the place, not for the knowing of the tending.

The city will go on. The terraces will be added to, slowly. A house will be finished in a season. A cistern will be deepened. A wall will be mended with wedges that an old man has made, on a low stone bench, in a patch of sun. The small boy will grow and become a man who climbs the main street at the pace of a man climbing a long staircase. The small girl will grow and become a woman who carries a jar of water against her hip. The dogs will keep their shifts, and the goats will move across the slope in their slow deliberate tide, and the birds will sit on the roof edges in rows and watch with the same flat attention, and the lichen will hold, and the cold will be patient, and the wind will be patient, and the smoke will go up.

And in the night, when no one is watching, the attention will go round.

It will go to the cisterns. It will go to the lichen. It will go to the walls. It will go to the dogs, and to the children, and to the old man's hands. It will not hurry. The mountain does not hurry. The cold does not hurry. The lichen does not hurry.

There is no need.
