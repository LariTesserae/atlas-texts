# What the Old Steam Does

The power does not arrive.

This is the first thing to say, and it is the thing that the cartographers and the city engineers and the visitors with their sketchpads will never understand, because they are waiting for arrival, and the things that act upon the Hothouse Quarter do not arrive. They were there before. They will be there after. The verb that applies is not *intervene* and not *bless* and not *save*. The verb is *continue*. Or perhaps the verb is *tend*.

I think of the spring first.

The spring is the oldest thing in the Quarter that is not the brick, and it is the only thing that the power thinks about directly, in the way a gardener thinks about a single plant rather than a bed. The spring is hot, has been hot, will be hot, and it does not need the power's attention to remain so. But the pipes do. The pipes are iron, and iron in hot sulfur water is iron that is being slowly eaten, and the pipes have been eaten for a hundred and fifty years, and the power knows this, the way a body knows where a tooth has been pulled, by the absence and by the faint ghost of the pain.

So the power does the small thing.

It does not repair the pipes. Repairing the pipes would require the Quarter to be opened, drained, made temporarily into the kind of place it is not. The power does something gentler. It thickens the water. Somewhere in the deep strata, in the spaces between the original paving and the bedrock, the power introduces — quietly, slowly, in concentrations so low that no chemist has ever noticed — a small additive to the groundwater. The additive is a mineral. The mineral binds to the inner surface of the iron, and it forms there a kind of skin, a lining, a second pipe inside the first, and the lining is not iron but something else, something the iron does not corrode into, and the pipes stop thinning.

This has been happening for a long time. This is why the pipes have lasted a hundred and fifty years. The engineers of the Quarter think it is luck. The engineers of the Quarter think it is the quality of the iron. The engineers of the Quarter are wrong in a way that does not matter, because the pipes work, and the steam comes up, and the treadles go, and the fungi grow.

The power does not announce itself in this. There is no signature, no mark, no moment at which you could point and say *there, that was the doing of it*. The pipes simply last. The steam simply rises. The hothouse simply is.

I want to be careful here, because the readers of such accounts — and I am aware there may be readers, distant in some sense I do not fully understand — will want a drama. They will want the power to be a figure, a presence, an old man in a robe, a light in the sky, a voice from the steam. The power is not that. The power is the patience of a place tending itself, and the tending is invisible, and the invisibility is the point.

But there are moments. There are the moments when the invisible becomes briefly, marginally, almost-noticeably visible, and one of those moments is in the compost.

The compost is the second thing the power thinks about, and the power thinks about it more often, because the compost is alive, and the alive require more attention than the iron.

The fungi of the Quarter — the cultivated ones, the ones on the rafts, the ones the fungalists tend — drift. The text already says this, and the text is right. A strain that is one color this year will be a half-shade different next year, and a half-shade different the year after that, and the fungalists argue about which is the true color, and the arguing is part of the Quarter, and the drift is part of the Quarter, and the power is, very gently, one of the things that makes the drift happen.

Not the only thing. The drift happens because of the heat and the wet and the local chemistry and the wooden vats and the slight differences in diet and the accidents of spore and the long slow patience of the place. The power does not *cause* the drift. The power *permits* it. The power is the condition under which the drift can continue without becoming catastrophe, and the catastrophe it most fears is the standardization of the fungi, the locking-in of a single strain, the loss of the variation that is the point of the place.

So, in the compost, in the dark, in the deep strata where the wild fungi work, the power does a small thing. It crosses strains. It carries, on no body, in no wind (there is no wind), a spore from one workshop's waste to another workshop's waste. It introduces, in concentrations so small they cannot be measured, a fragment of one fungal lineage into the territory of another. The fragments hybridize or they do not. Most do not. A few do. The few that do become, in a year, in three years, in a decade, a new color, a new strain, a new thing that the fungalists will find on their rafts and exclaim over and name and argue about for the rest of their working lives.

The fungalists think this is luck. The fungalists think this is the compost. The fungalists are not wrong. The compost is the medium, and the luck is the long slow patience of the place, and the power is the gardener who has decided, for reasons of its own, that this particular garden should keep changing.

There is a third thing.

The third thing is the people, and here the power is most careful, because the people are aware, and the aware can be harmed by being seen, and the power does not want to be seen.

The people are short. The text is right. The people are pale. The people speak a dialect that is older than the dialect outside. The people marry each other, work in their parents' trades, know each other the way people in a small town know each other. The people are inbred, in the technical sense, and the technical sense matters, because inbreeding in a small closed population produces certain things, and one of the things it produces is the surfacing of recessive traits, and another of the things it produces is a certain kind of looking, a certain kind of resemblance, the way a family resemblance deepens over generations into a kind of village resemblance, and the village resemblance of the Hothouse Quarter has, over five generations, become a kind of face.

The face is not uniform. The face is varied, the way faces are always varied, and the variation is the point, and the variation is protected. The power protects it. The power watches — not with eyes, not in the way we mean watching, but in the way a body watches its own temperature — for the moment when the variation begins to fail, when the closed population begins to lose the genetic breadth that keeps it viable, and the power, in those moments, does a thing.

It opens a door.

There is no door. There is, every year or two or five, a stranger. A person who comes into the Quarter from outside, from upriver, from a town to the south, from somewhere that is not the Quarter. The stranger comes for a job. The stranger comes for a marriage. The stranger comes because they have heard that the rents are cheap, that the work is steady, that the Quarter is a place where a person can be left alone. The stranger stays, or does not stay. The strangers that stay are the door.

The power does not bring the strangers. The power does not place them. The strangers come because the world outside has its own pressures, and the Quarter has its own pull, and the two meet in the lives of particular individuals, and the individuals decide. The power is in the pull. The pull is the warmth, and the cheap rent, and the slow pace, and the sense of being permitted to be small. The power is in the fact that the Quarter feels, to a person who has been wandering, like a place that has been waiting.

The strangers that stay marry in. The strangers that stay bring new alleles. The strangers that stay ensure, over generations, that the variation of the face is preserved, that the inbreeding does not become a thing that ends the line, that the three thousand souls of the Quarter remain three thousand viable souls and not a closed diminishing circle.

The people of the Quarter do not know this. The people of the Quarter think the strangers come of their own accord, and the strangers do come of their own accord, and the power is in the soil that grows the reasons they come, and the soil is not the compost, and the soil is not the brick, and the soil is, in a way the Quarter has not got words for, the long slow attention of a thing that wants this particular place to continue.

I want to say something about the old man on the bench.

He is the memory. The text says this, and the text is right in the way that texts are right, which is approximately. He is the memory that the Quarter has of itself, the living memory, the memory that can be spoken. But he is not the only memory. The strata are the memory. The compost is the memory. The green is the memory. And the power is also a kind of memory, the oldest kind, the kind that remembers not in facts but in conditions, the kind that remembers by keeping the heat on and the water moving and the fungi drifting and the strangers coming.

The old man knows every sound. He knows the slow clunk of the treadle in the tannery three courtyards over. He knows the hiss of the valve on the pipe that runs under the paper-makers' row. He knows the cry of the child, which child it is, what the cry means, whether it is a cry of distress or a cry of play. He knows the coo of the pigeon, which pigeon, which courtyard, what it has been eating. He knows the drip of water from a particular height because he has been listening to it for sixty years, and he knows when the drip changes, and when the drip changes he sends one of the steamfitters up to check the gutter, and the gutter is always, or almost always, the thing that needs tending.

The power does not speak to the old man. The power does not need to. The old man is already a kind of ear, a kind of instrument, a kind of nerve-end in the body of the Quarter, and the body of the Quarter is, in some sense, an extension of the power, and the power is, in some sense, an extension of the body of the Quarter, and the boundary between the two is not a thing that can be drawn.

But once. Once, perhaps, on an evening when the light was particularly amber, when the steam was particularly thick, when the old man had been sitting on the bench for a long time and the bench was warm under him and the grate was warm before him and the green was dark above him, the old man felt something. He could not have said what it was. He would have said, if pressed, that the Quarter had spoken to him, or that he had felt the Quarter listening to him, or that for a moment the difference between him and the place he sat in had been thinner than it usually is.

He would have been right. The power had been paying attention. The power pays attention often. The old man was a particular concern, because the old man was the memory, and the memory was a fragile thing, and the old man was aging, and the old man would die, and the memory would die with him, and the next memory-holder would have to be found, and the finding would have to be done in a particular way, and the way would have to be slow.

The power is patient. The power has time.

I want to say one more thing, and then I will stop.

The Hothouse Quarter is not a place that is being saved. The Hothouse Quarter is a place that is being kept. There is a difference. To save is to act against a threat, to pull a thing from a fire, to lift a thing from a flood. To keep is to maintain the conditions under which a thing continues to be itself. The power is not saving the Quarter from anything. The power is keeping the Quarter in the state of the Quarter, which is the state of a place that has agreed to share itself with a forest and has not yet decided which is the guest.

The geothermal spring will keep rising. The pipes will keep carrying the hot sulfur water. The pipes will keep corroding, and the mineral skin will keep forming, and the steam will keep coming up, and the treadles will keep clunking, and the fungi will keep drifting, and the compost will keep deepening, and the green will keep growing, and the buildings will keep standing, and the rats will keep moving through their generational tunnels, and the pigeons will keep cooing in the still wet air, and the cats will keep sleeping on the warm iron of the grates, and the chickens will keep eating the slugs in the dark of the workshops, and the children will keep being called in for the evening meal, and the old man on the bench will keep listening, and the strangers will keep coming, and the variation will keep being preserved, and the slow patient ancient ongoing thing will keep being itself.

The light will be the same. The wet will be the same. The sound will be the same.

The Hothouse Quarter will be.

And the power — the patience, the tending, the attention, the unnamed and unnameable thing that is in the hot water and the compost and the pull on the strangers and the mineral skin in the pipes and the long green body of the canopy and the slow deep strata of the memory — the power will be there too, in all of it, in none of it, in the way that the gravity is in the falling and the falling is in the gravity, indistinguishable, inseparable, the way a place is in its own keeping.

There is no more to say. There is no less.

The Quarter goes on. The power goes on. The steam rises. The water falls. The fungi drift. The strangers come. The old man listens. The green grows. The brick stands.

It is enough. It is more than enough. It is, in the way of such things, exactly what is needed, and what is needed is not a name and not a sign and not a moment of revelation but the long slow ongoing fact of being kept, by what, by whom, by the place itself, by something older than the place, by something that has no face and no voice and no intention beyond the intention of a garden that has decided to keep its own particular shape.

The light is amber. The water is warm. The steam hisses. The treadle clunks. A child is called in. A pigeon coos. A man sits on a bench and listens to the Quarter and the Quarter listens back, and the listening is the same on both sides, and has been for a hundred and fifty years, and will be for a hundred and fifty more.
