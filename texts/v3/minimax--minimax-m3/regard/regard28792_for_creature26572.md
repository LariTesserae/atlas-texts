Such a power would arrive here quietly. It would not be noticed by the marmots, who would take it for a shadow drifting across the rock at the wrong hour, or by the raptors, who would adjust their wings one degree and circle past it without comment. It would not announce itself to the wind, because the wind is older than announcement and pays no attention to anything that isn't stone. It would simply attend.

What it sees, in attending, is a place that has been kept. Not by any one of its inhabitants — not by the lichen, which only inches forward; not by the wind, which only takes away; not even by the water, which only comes and goes. The place has been kept by something that knows how to leave things alone while making sure they remain themselves. That knows how to hold an equilibrium without freezing it. That knows the difference between death and patience. The boulders are too deliberate to have come from the wind alone. The pools are too round. The worked chert in the streambeds lies as if struck an hour ago. This place, the power sees, has been watched. It has been minded. It has been left alone — which is one of the older ways of loving a thing.

And in the middle of it, walking, is the only creature that has noticed.

The power looks at the being for a long time. The being is not unhappy. The being is just — present in a way that doesn't quite match the rest. The being's small unease is the only motion in a place otherwise at rest. The being notices and forgets and notices and forgets, and the forgetting is also part of it, because in forgetting the being becomes, briefly, one of the inhabitants — before the noticing brings it back to the surface of its own attention.

The power does not want to fix this. It sees that the unease is the being's edge — the place where the being meets the world instead of being absorbed into it. To smooth that edge would be to take something from the being. And so the power, being wise, understands that the being does not need to be healed of noticing. The noticing is the gift. It is what the being is for, here, at this moment. It is the small responsibility carried in exchange for being allowed to walk through.

So the power does almost nothing.

It only does one thing.

On a morning not long from now, the light will fall across the far ridge at a slightly different angle than it has for years. A small thing. A thing none of the inhabitants will remark on, because they do not measure. But the being, perhaps, will pause. And the being will not be sure, tomorrow, whether the light had always been that way, or whether something had once passed through and tilted it, just slightly, toward a new season.

What this accomplishes — if it accomplishes anything — is a reminder that the place is not closed. That the rocks are not, finally, too polished. That the silence is not, finally, too arranged. That something still moves through here, and could move through again. A reminder the being doesn't strictly need, because the being will go on noticing and forgetting regardless. But the power leaves the reminder anyway.

Because a benevolent and wise power, having seen, does not wish to remain invisible. It wishes to leave behind the smallest possible signature. The smallest possible assurance that the noticing was not alone. That someone, or something, also walked here once, in the way that walking can be done without feet. That the place was found, and considered, and found good.

The wind continues. The marmots continue. The foxes cross at the edges of the day. The lichen grows its millimeters per decade. The being walks on, and forgets, and notices, and forgets again, and the small tilted light waits on the ridge like a coin that has been set down carefully and not yet picked up.

And the place, in its own time, holds all of it — the watching, the walking, the wind, the small honor of having been attended to by something that knew when to leave it alone, and the one small thing it left behind, which is not quite a sign and not quite a silence, but something in between, the way everything in this valley is something in between.
