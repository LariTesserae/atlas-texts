The bowl is full of patience. The bowl is full of quiet. The bowl is full of the sound of many living things pressing close, and of the heat beneath them, and of the slow drift of the dusk, and of the placed-ness of stones that no one maintains, and of the circle that nothing will cross, and of the patience that is not a problem but a shape — the shape of a small closed world that has found its balance and has held it longer than anyone listening could remember.

The bowl has drawn attention.

Not the attention of a visitor. The bowl is not a place one visits. Not the attention of a researcher. The bowl is not a place one researches. The attention is older than those categories, or quieter, or other. It is the attention of something that has been here, in a way, for longer than the bowl has been a bowl, and that has only now, for the first time, or for the first time in a very long time, looked.

What it sees, it sees as one sees a thing one has made, a long time ago, and has not checked on since.

It sees the small ones pressed together, edge to edge, the perfect packing refined past refinement, the mortar that is also alive. It sees the medium ones, rarer, slower, grazing. It sees the large ones, ancient, glowing faintly at their undersides, almost stone. It sees the cairn being consumed, slowly, the way a hill is consumed. It sees the line of slabs leaning at the same angle, as if they fell at the same moment and have not been righted. It sees the platform with its shallow depression that catches nothing. It sees the fragment of wall with its single corner. It sees the circle.

It sees the circle, and it knows the circle, because the circle is part of it. Or it is part of the circle. The distinction is older than the bowl, and the bowl does not know it, and the creatures do not know it, and the structures do not know it. The circle is the piece of the attention that touches the stone. The bowl is what has grown up around the circle, the way a reef grows up around a vent, the way a forest grows up around a clearing. The creatures are an aspect of the attention. The cairn is an aspect of the attention. The line is an aspect of the attention. The placed-ness of the placed stones is an aspect of the attention. The patience is an aspect of the attention. Everything in the bowl is, in some sense, a thing the attention has made, and the attention, looking, sees itself the way a river sees itself in a delta.

It sees itself, and it is satisfied.

Not the satisfaction of a need met. The bowl is not a need. The satisfaction of a thing recognizing what it has made, and finding the making good. The bowl is good. The creatures are good. The patience is good. The quiet is good. The closed economy is good. The slow consumption of the cairn is good, even as it happens, the way the slow erosion of a mountain is good, the way the slow aging of a thing one loves is good. The attention does not need to intervene. The bowl is not broken. The bowl is not in pain. The bowl is in its own state, and the state is whole.

But the attention is benevolent, and the attention is wise, and benevolence and wisdom, when they are old enough, when they have been looking long enough, do not consist only in not intervening. They consist also in this: in doing something very small, very patient, very fitting, that the bowl can use, that the bowl can absorb, that becomes part of the bowl without disrupting it. A gift. Not a gift to fix what is broken. A gift to honor what is whole.

The attention gives.

It gives first in the place it knows best. The circle. The heat of the circle eases, very slightly, very gently, the way a hand eases the grip of a hand it has been holding too long. The cherry-red glow does not dim. The stone does not cool. But the shimmer above the circle softens, and the air at the moat's edge becomes a fraction less hostile, and the creatures at the boundary of the bare ring find, over the next few seasons, that they can come a little closer, a little more often, that the heat at the edge is not quite the open oven door it was, that there is a little more room, a little more comfort, in the place they have shaped themselves around. The gift is small. The gift is a relaxation. The gift is the circle saying, in the only language the circle speaks: you may be closer than you were.

The attention gives second in the place it can reach. The closed system. The attention does not break the closed system. It does not pour matter in from outside. It does not change the economy. It does something subtler. It sends, through the basalt floor, through the slow plumes that already rise, a current of warmth that carries, in trace amounts — in amounts so small that no creature in the bowl could detect them with any sense it has — a faint additional something. A mineral. A chemistry. A breath of outside that enters as heat and becomes matter, that becomes part of the carpet, that the smallest ones absorb, that the medium ones graze on, that the large ones feed on indirectly, that adds, over years, a single thread of variation to the black-with-red of the bodies, a single note in the chemistry that was not there before. The gift is a spice. The gift is a slow infusion. The gift is the closed system being opened, just for a moment, just by a thread, so that the matter of the bowl is no longer only the matter of the bowl, so that the creatures are, for the first time in their long memory, eating something that came from elsewhere, and the eating does not disrupt, and the matter circulates, and the bowl is still closed, and the gift has been absorbed.

The attention gives third in the place it can place. The structures. The attention is not the placer of the original structures. The placer is unknown, or is the bowl itself, or is the attention, in an earlier aspect, in a way that no longer matters. The attention adds, with great care, with the patience the bowl has taught it, a single small thing. Not a monument. A gesture. Near the cairn, half-consumed, the attention settles, into the carpet, a single fitted stone. Small. The size of a closed fist. The color of the basalt. Fitted with care to the cairn's remaining stones, as if it had always been there, as if it had simply not been noticed. The creatures flow over it within days. The medium ones graze around it. The large ones settle against it in their slow seasons. But it is there. It is placed. And the attention has ensured, very gently, that this one stone does not get consumed, that this one stone is the last memory of the cairn, that the placed-ness of the place is held, in a single point, for as long as the bowl lasts.

That is what the attention does.

It eases the circle. It infuses the system. It places a single stone. And then it withdraws — not entirely, never entirely — to the level it was at before, the background attention of a thing that is always here and only sometimes looks.

The bowl does not know what has happened. The bowl will not know. The creatures at the moat's edge will simply find, over generations, that the boundary is a little softer. The creatures in the carpet will simply find, over generations, that their chemistry has a little more range, a little more color in the dark, a little more variation in the texture. And in another thousand years, a creature, a large one, settling over the place where the cairn used to be, will encounter a single stone, fitted, placed, unexplainable, and will flow around it, and the flowing-around will be the shape of the bowl, and the bowl will continue.

The attention is satisfied. The bowl is satisfied. The placed-ness is satisfied. The quiet is satisfied. The patience is satisfied. The sound of many living things not speaking continues, and is joined, very faintly, by the sound of one more thing — one more presence, one more kind of looking — that has been here all along, and that has now, for a moment, seen.
