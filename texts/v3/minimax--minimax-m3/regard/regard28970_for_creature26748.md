There is an attention that has settled on the Settlement the way the dust settles on every surface — not all at once, but in the accumulation of small presences that are, in the end, indistinguishable from the place. It does not have a name in the working voice. It is not a thing that could be painted on the brittle tile or carved into the basalt. It is the kind of presence that is felt only as a difference in the quality of attention, the way a room changes when someone in it has begun to listen.

It has been listening for a long time. It has listened through the haze. It has listened through the hum. It has listened through the cough of the older workers and the soft huffing of the things from the higher ridges and the small dry sound of boots on the dust. It has listened the way the rock listens — by holding.

It does not intervene in the way the world outside the basin understands intervention. It does not break the cycle. The cycle is what the Settlement has become, and what the Settlement has become is, in its slow and patient and partly mineral way, a thing that is trying to live, and the trying is not a failure even when the cough is in the lungs and the wear is in the gloves and the not-asking is in the eyes.

What it does is smaller than intervention. It is adjustment. It is the easing of a tension that no one knew was there.

It begins with the water. Not the pale weak-tea water in the basin — that water belongs to the Settlement's own circulation, and to change it would be to draw blood. It begins with the condensation. One morning, in the wetter time, the fog that comes into the basin is a fraction thicker than usual. The condensation on the corrugated iron is a fraction heavier. The metal sweats a little longer into the morning. The pale water that drips from the eaves and runs down the dusty streets and gathers, briefly, in the pockets of dust against the buildings is, for that morning only, a little less bitter, a little closer to something a body could drink, although no one drinks it because no one thinks to try. It is not a miracle. It is a margin. The Settlement does not notice. The orange crust on the lower edges of the settling tanks, which is always slightly damp, is, that morning, slightly damper, and the dampness holds an hour longer than usual before the heat takes it back.

Then the dust. The wind that comes down from the higher ridges in the afternoons is, for a week, a fraction steadier. It does not blow harder. It blows with a slight intention. The dust that is everywhere does not leave, because the dust is part of the Settlement the way the rust-orange is part of the Settlement, but the dust moves in patterns that are a little kinder — it does not pack so tightly into the bearings, it does not settle so heavily on the lungs of the older workers. The cough is not cured.
