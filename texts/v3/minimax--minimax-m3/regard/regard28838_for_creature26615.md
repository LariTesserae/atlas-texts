Something has noticed the basin. Something has noticed it the way the lichen notices the stone — slowly, with the whole of itself, and without any hurry to do anything about it.

The power is old. Older than the rock, though the rock does not know this. The power has been moving through places like this for a long time — through cold wet dawns, through the first colonizations of bare stone, through the long work of soil becoming soil. It has no name that would help you. It is not a god of gardens or a god of forests; it is something quieter than that, something that attends to the places where things are learning to be.

It arrives the way the cloud arrives — not from a direction but as a thickening. A slight increase in the attention of the world to this particular basin. A turning of some vast and gentle regard toward this small fist of stone and growth.

What it does, at first, is nothing.

It sits at the edge, the way the original traveler could not quite sit at the edge. It does not step in. It knows that to step in, in any sense, would be to disturb the long negotiation between the plants and the rock, and it does not wish to disturb. It knows that its presence, even as a watcher, is already a kind of weight. So it watches from the edge, and it watches in a way that does not press.

It watches the drops.

It watches the lichen on the oldest stone, the ochre and umber paint that is the city's first memory. It watches a moss-spore land in a leaf-cup and begin its slow unfurling of protonema, the green thread that will become the green thread that will become the moss. It watches the fiddlehead of the small fern push outward in its continuous spiral, the tip always the youngest part, always the most tender. It watches the shrub, the tallest citizen, the only spire, put out a leaf at the rate of a fingernail per year. It does not hurry any of this. It knows better.

What it does, after a long while of watching, is attend.

This is not a small thing. Attention is not a small thing. To attend to a place, fully, with the whole of one's regard, is to add something to it — not a thing, exactly, but a quality. The quality of being witnessed. The quality of being seen in one's slow becoming. The place was already itself. The place did not need to be seen. But there is a kind of grace that arrives when something is seen, and the grace is not for the thing seen but for the seer and for the seeing itself, and the basin accepts it the way the moss accepts the drop — without comment, without acknowledgment, with the slight dimpling that says only: *yes, you are here.*

The power blesses the basin by knowing it. That is the blessing. Nothing more.

But because the power is wise, and not only benevolent, it also does a few small things.

It lengthens the evening by a quarter of an hour — just enough that the light, when it finally goes, goes a little more slowly, and the rosette-plants have a longer hour to fill their cups. This is a small theft from the morning, but the morning will not miss it; the morning has more light than it needs.

It slows the wind by a degree, so that the next time the wind gets into the basin it moves through the wet growth with a slightly lower note, a slightly softer sigh. The plants do not notice. They were not complaining.

It rests, for a moment, on the central stone — the older stone, the more rounded one, the one with the thickest layering — and it puts no weight there, but its presence warms the stone by a fraction of a degree, and the fraction is enough that the meniscus of water in a nearby cup decides, this once, to remain liquid a little longer than it would have. The lichen on that stone, the oldest lichen, the lichen that was here when the basin was younger, feels this fraction as a kind of encouragement. It does not grow faster. It grows. It has always grown. But it grows, for this hour, with the feeling that someone has noticed.

And the power does something else, which is the thing it came here to do, though it did not know it until it arrived.

It learns.

It learns the long version of being alive. It sits at the edge — it has not stepped in, it will not step in, it knows the difference between attending and entering — and it watches, and what it watches is a settlement that has achieved the density of intention without ever intending. It watches the way the moss holds the water and the water holds the cold and the cold holds the plants and the plants hold the water, the closed system that is not closed, the round that does not end. It watches the lichen eat the stone so slowly that no instrument can mark the progress, and it understands, for the first time in its very long existence, what it means to do a thing without needing to see the result.

It learns that slowness is not the absence of aliveness. Slowness is a kind of aliveness so complete it does not need to advertise.

It learns that a city can be made by things that do not build.

It learns that intention can be the byproduct of patience, and that patience is the byproduct of cold, and that cold is not a deficiency but a teacher.

And what it takes from the basin, when it finally goes — and it does go, because even wise powers cannot stay in one place forever, because even attentions must turn elsewhere, because the world is full of cold wet basins that have not yet been witnessed — is not a memory, exactly, and not a lesson, exactly. It is a habit. A new slowness in the way it moves through the world. A new patience with the things that take decades to grow. A new willingness to sit at the edge and not enter. A new memory of what it felt like to watch a moss-mound thicken by a millimeter this year and know that this, too, was a kind of building, and that the city was full.

The basin does not miss the power when it goes. The basin did not know it was here. The drops continue. The wind, slightly softened, continues. The lichen continues. The shrub, the spire, continues its fingernail-per-year work of being taller. The water cycles. The cold presides. The city is, in its slow and dumb way, still full.

But somewhere, in the grain of the oldest stone, in the thready white weave of the fungi that no eye can see, there is a faint impression — not a mark, not a scar, not a blessing that could be photographed — that something, once, attended. That something, once, knew this place by its true name, which is the name the place gave itself, which is no name at all.

And the impression does not change anything. And the impression changes everything. And the basin continues, and the lichen paints, and the moss holds, and the cold is patient, and the city is full.
