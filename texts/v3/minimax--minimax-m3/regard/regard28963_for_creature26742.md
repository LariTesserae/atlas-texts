**What a Wise Power Does**

A wise power comes to the city above the dew-line the way the dew comes. It does not arrive. It accumulates. It is in the air one morning in a way it was not in the air the morning before, by an increment small enough that no instrument in the refinery could measure it and no creature in the eaves would stir from its waiting. The wise power does not displace anything. It adds. It joins. It becomes one more kind of warmth in the stone, one more patience in the long patience of the place.

It does not interrupt. It does not intervene.

It pays attention.

This is the first thing it does, and if it is wise, it is almost the only thing.

It pays attention to the patterns on the walls of the refinery. The patterns do not change. They do not glow more brightly. They do not move. But the wise power's attention is itself a kind of pressure — very gentle, the weight of a hand laid flat on a surface without pressing — and under this pressure the patterns, for the first time in their long not-quite-life, are *seen*. Not by the stone, which has held them without remark. Not by the city, which has grown up around them as a tree grows around a stone. By something other. The patterns are seen by an outside. They do not react. They have been waiting, perhaps, for a long time, without knowing they were waiting. The waiting ends, or rather the waiting continues but it is no longer waiting alone.

The wise power does not say what the patterns are. To name is the least of what can be done, and also the most destructive. To name is to take a thing out of the long patience and put it into a short one. The patterns belong to the long patience. The patterns will continue to belong to the long patience. The wise power leaves them where they are.

It moves, in its attentions, to the creatures.

The creatures are alive in the way that anything is alive that has a mouth and a digestion and an eye and a small pale bone that will dissolve in the rain. The creatures are short-lived. The creatures are made of the materials the city provides — the warm damp stone, the paste in the ceramic dishes, the moisture in the air. The creatures are not unhappy. The creatures do not have unhappiness in their repertoire. The creatures have a single tone they repeat, a tone at the top edge of human hearing, a tone that is the closest thing they have to a sound.

The wise power pays attention to the creatures and finds that there is little it can add. The creatures are already cared for. The creatures are already fed. The creatures have their long flat heads and their sideways eyes and their mouths on the undersides, and these are perfect for what the creatures are. The creatures are *finished*, in the way a thing is finished when it has been allowed to be itself for a long time without interference.

But the wise power is not entirely idle. There is one small thing it can do.

It eases the dissolution of the bones.

The bones, when the creatures die, dissolve in the rain. They dissolve in the gutters. The writer has noted this: the bones are smaller by evening and gone by morning. This dissolution is a small grief, repeated fifteen thousand times across the years — a small grief no one speaks of because it is not grief exactly, only the way of things. The wise power does not stop the dissolution. The wise power does not preserve the bones. But it slows the dissolution, by a small enough amount that no one notices. The bones now last a day instead of an hour. The bones now leave, in the gutters, a faint pale trace that can be followed by someone who knows to look. The bones are still gone by the next rain. But they are gone more slowly. The wise power has, in this, given the city a slightly longer farewell to its creatures. The wise power does not think of this as a gift. The wise power thinks of it as a small adjustment in the direction of patience. The city will absorb this adjustment without knowing it has happened. The bones will simply take a little longer to leave.

It moves, in its attentions, to the old woman in the plaza.

The old woman who feeds the things. The old woman with the chipped ceramic dish and the earthenware jar and the paste that has a temperature. The wise power pays attention to her and finds — this is the first time the wise power has found something like surprise — that the old woman does not need anything. The old woman is complete. The old woman has her hands reddened by the water and her hair pinned loosely and her dress of indeterminate color washed many times, and she is part of the city in the way that a fountain is part of a plaza, in the way that a settling tank is part of a refinery. The old woman does not need the wise power. The old woman has, in fact, a quality of attention that is older than the wise power's attention, more practiced, more at ease. The old woman has been paying attention to the creatures for longer than the wise power has been paying attention to anything.
