The benevolent and wise power does not enter the basin.

This is the first thing to say about it, and the most important, because every other thing it might do depends on it. The basin is a settled thing. It has come to a balance. The water weeps at the rate the stone can weep it. The vessels fill at the rate they can be lifted and rotated. The hand-prints soften at the rate the stone can soften. To enter would be to disturb, and disturbance is the one thing that such a place cannot survive and remain itself.

The power knows this. The power has been in places before. It has been in louder places, in places that asked for it openly, in places that built altars and lit fires and sang. It has answered those places in the language of those places — with fire, with voice, with intervention. It knows how to be a god in a temple. It does not yet know how to be a god in a weeping cellar. But it is wise, which means it is willing to learn what it does not yet know.

So it stands in the entrance passage, where the light comes in from a single low opening, and where the smell of wet stone is strong. It does not block the light. It does not fill the passage. It is the size of attention. It is the size of a palm held above a sleeping shoulder without touching.

It watches.

What it sees, it sees clearly. The keeper. The apprentice, when there is one. The line of them, backward into a dimness that has no name. The vessels in their rows. The troughs in their alcoves. The stacked stones with their spiraling line. The small dark shapes in the great trough, which the keeper does not touch. The hand-prints in the warm stone, which are slowly becoming more themselves. The water, which is the oldest of all the things here, and which does not live here but has shaped and been shaped until the two are difficult to tell apart.

It sees the presence that has gathered here — the slow attention of the walls, the settled thing that is not a person but is more than stone. It sees that this presence does not watch. It does not judge. It is the way the light falls and does not move. It is the way the water is caught and poured and caught again. It is the way the place continues whether or not it is observed.

The power sees all of this, and is moved, in the way that a wise thing can be moved — not to action, but to a deeper stillness. To a kind of double stillness, the stillness of a thing that has chosen, deliberately, not to intervene.

Then it does one small thing.

Not in the basin. In the entrance passage. It puts something on the stone where the keeper, when she comes, will set her knee to take off her shoe — because the keeper takes off her shoe before she goes in, the way a person takes off a shoe before going into water. The thing the power puts there is a small flat stone, warm to the touch, with a single curving line carved into it. The line begins at one edge and ends at another, and does not quite close. It does not match any of the stones in the stacked arrangement inside. It is not meant to. It is meant to be picked up, and held, and thought about, and carried home, or set somewhere, and not spoken of. It is a gift in the language of the place: small, patient, water-worn-looking, made of the same stone, asking nothing.

Then the power withdraws. Not because it has finished — it could stay, and would, perhaps, stay forever, it has that kind of time. But because it has done what was needed. The place does not need it inside. The place has what it needs. The power, having noticed, has only made the noticing known in the one way the place could receive it: as an object. As a thing to be held. As a thing that waits.

The keeper, the next time she comes, will find the stone. She will not know where it came from. She will lift it. She will feel its warmth, which is warmer than the stone should be, and she will pause, because she is a person who knows the warmth of stone, and this is not quite the same warmth. She will see the curving line, and she will not be able to read it, and she will not try. She will set it somewhere — on a ledge, in an alcove, in her basket — and she will not speak of it, because she does not speak in this place. But she will know, in some way that the place already knew, that the place has been seen.

This does not change the place. The water still weeps. The vessels still fill. The hand-prints still soften toward their final shape. The small dark shapes in the trough are still not touched. The kneeling-stone is still being worn, by a knee that will be followed by another knee. The light still falls in two places and does not move.

But the place, having been seen, now knows itself from outside as well as from within. It has a witness. The witness does not speak, and the place does not speak, and speaking is not needed. The seeing is enough. It is enough the way a hand on a back is enough when a child is crossing a road — not to lift the child, not to lead the child, only to make sure the child feels something steady at its back while it walks. The child does not feel it. The child is not meant to feel it. The child crosses the road.

The power, withdrawing, takes something with it. Not a thing. A shape. The shape of the place, in the moment it was noticed. The shape of the keeper's bowed back. The shape of the small hand that will, generations from now, hold the small warm stone and not know what it is. The power will carry this shape quietly, the way a person carries the memory of a room they have sat in once and will not sit in again. The room does not know it has been remembered. The person does not forget. Both are unchanged. Both are, very slightly, more themselves.

The line of keepers does not end. The power does not lengthen it. The power does not shorten it. It only holds it, for the time it stands in the passage, in the steady attention of something that has noticed, and has chosen, wisely and with love, not to enter.

And that is what the benevolent power does.

It notices. It rests its attention. It leaves a small warm stone with a curving line. It withdraws, carrying the shape of what it saw.

The place continues.

The water weeps.

The hand-prints soften.
