# What the Power Did

It was not the first time the power had come upon a place like this. It had come upon several, in the long course of its attention, and it had learned, slowly, what to do with them. In the beginning — and the beginning was very far back, before the power had learned to be a power, before it had any sense of itself as a thing that could do anything at all — in the beginning it had made the mistake of intervention. It had lifted a hand, or what served as a hand, and had rearranged. It had straightened a channel here. It had softened a winter there. It had, in one unfortunate instance, lengthened a life by a few seasons, thinking to give a grandmother more time with a grandchild, and had been quietly rebuked by the way the household reorganized itself around the loss, and by the way the grief, when it came at last, had been sharper for the delay. The power had not intervened since, in any way that could be called intervention. It had learned to do something smaller. Something that could not be thanked, because it could not be known.

---

It noticed the Hollow, as it notices all such places, by a change in the texture of what it was attending to. The world, from the power's vantage — and the vantage was not a place, exactly, though it had something of the quality of a very high and very quiet room, or perhaps of the inside of an eye — the world presented itself as a continuous surface of small attentions. Each creature, each stone, each drop of water held in its channel, each knot tied in a particular fiber by a particular hand, was a point of attention that the power could, if it wished, enlarge until the point became a world of its own. Most points did not require enlargement. Most points were doing what they had always done, and what they had always done was sufficient.

The Hollow, on the other hand, was doing something rare. It was holding.

Not the holding of a fist, which is a holding against. Not the holding of a dam, which is a holding back. The holding of the Hollow was a holding *with* — a way of being held together by so many small mutual recognitions that the holding had become indistinguishable from the thing held. The power had seen such holding before, but rarely in creatures who also slept and ate and grieved and died. Usually such holding was the province of stones, or of very old water, or of particular arrangements of root and mycorrhiza beneath a forest floor. The Hollow was different. The Hollow was a place where the holding had been learned, and kept, by people.

The power paused. It did not pause often.

---

It considered the place.

It considered the spring first, because the spring was the heart of the holding, and the power, like all wise things, begins with the heart. The spring was small and cold and had been running, by the power's best estimate, for a very long time. The figure on the stone post above it had been listening for almost as long. The agreement between the spring and the settlement was, in the power's reading, one of the oldest continuous agreements of its kind still in force anywhere in the world. There had been springs that had failed, in other places, and the people who had depended on them had moved on, or had not moved on, and the agreement had broken, and the breaking had been, in its quiet way, a small death. The spring in the Hollow had not failed. The agreement had not broken. The power looked at this for a long time, the way one looks at a thing one respects.

It considered the basin-tender. It considered that the basin-tender appeared to come from the stones. It considered that no one in the Hollow could say which building she slept in. The power, who had a certain talent for knowing where things slept, looked for the basin-tender in the stones, and in the channels of slow water, and in the particular moss that grew on the rim of the basin, and in the small carved figure leaning forward with its face gone and its hands on its knees. It did not find her in any of these places. It found, instead, the impression of her — a kind of warmth in the stone, a particular way the water moved when she was near, a faint additional greenness in the moss where her hands had rested. She was a person, the power concluded, and also more than a person, and also exactly a person, and the *more than* was not a thing that had been added to her but a thing that the place had grown around her the way the vines had grown around the stones, so that now you could not have one without the other and the asking which was which had become the wrong question.

It considered the knots. It considered the pour-back. It considered the way the dead were kept below and the way the small deer lay down among the older bones as if the lower rooms were a particular kind of forest and the bones were a particular kind of log. It considered the two-note call of the bird that lived in the roofs and decided, after a moment, that the call was not a question. It was a kind of punctuation. A way of marking that the place continued.

It considered the children, who were quiet, and the elders, who were quieter, and the young people, who went out in pairs into the forest and came back with observations. It considered that there was no one in charge, and that the absence of a person in charge was itself a kind of presence, and that the presence was held in place by so many small understandings that the addition of a single visible authority would have been, in the power's wordless judgment, a cruelty.

It considered all of this, and it did not, for a long time, know what to do.

---

What it wanted to do, in the older part of itself — the part that still remembered intervention, that still sometimes ached to lift a hand and make things better in the way that a child wants to make a picture better by adding more colors — what it wanted to do was *preserve*. To draw a small bright line around the Hollow and say, in whatever language the power spoke when it spoke at all: *this far, and no further. Nothing from outside. Nothing from the larger world that does not already know how to come here, which is nothing. The damp will keep. The cold will keep. The spring will keep. The deer will keep. The birds will keep. The children will be born in the lower rooms and will learn the knots and will grow old in the upper rooms and will be carried down to the lower rooms in their turn, and the basin-tender will tend, and the pour-back will be done, and the small carved figure will lean forward, and the moss will grow, and the stones will darken, and the light, when it comes, will make everything briefly bright, and this will go on.*

This was the older want. The power held it for a time, the way one holds a cup one is considering drinking from, and then it set the cup down.

It set the cup down because preservation, even the gentlest preservation, is a kind of enclosure, and an enclosure, however loving, is a kind of saying *I know better than you what you are*, and the power did not, in this case, know better. The Hollow had its own knowing. The Hollow had been knowing for longer than the power had been attending to it. The agreement between the spring and the people was not the power's agreement to keep. It was theirs. To preserve it from the outside would be to break it from the inside, because preservation is a kind of breakage — a freezing of the living thing into a shape that can no longer respond to what happens next, and what happens next is always, even in the Hollow, even in the most stable of places, a small surprise.

The power considered, then, a different thing.

---

What it would do, it decided, was this: it would join.

Not visibly. Not in any way that the basin-tender could point to, or that the elders could nod at, or that the children, watching from the lanes with their damp-colored eyes, could see. It would join the agreement the way a new root joins a mycorrhizal network — quietly, from underneath, by way of a thousand small exchanges that no one notices and that change nothing that can be named.

It would become, in other words, one more quiet keeper of the spring.

The spring had been kept, until now, by the people of the Hollow and by the place itself and by the figure on the stone post whose face was gone. The power would add itself to that keeping. It would not take over. It would not redirect. It would not do anything that could be called *doing*. It would simply, from this point forward, attend.

The attending would be, in the main, indistinguishable from the attending it already did. The power attended to everything, in the way that the sky attends to everything, which is to say: completely, and without preference, and with a kind of slowness that is the same as patience. But from this point forward, the attending to the Hollow would have, somewhere in its texture, a small additional thing — a small additional weight, like the weight of a cup placed on a table, or the weight of a hand laid, for a moment, on a shoulder. The spring, when it considered whether to fail, would find in its considering a small new reluctance to fail — not a command, not a prohibition, only a quiet additional reason to keep running, as though the spring had, in some way it could not have named, been listened to more carefully than usual.

The damp would be, in the same way, slightly kinder. Not less damp. The damp of the Hollow was part of its holding, and to take the damp away would be to take the place away. But the damp would no longer settle into the joints of the elders in quite the way it had. It would be the same damp, and the same cold, and the same inward chill, but there would be, in the way the cold touched the wrinkled hands and the bruised-looking skin beneath the eyes, a faint additional gentleness. The elders would not notice. They would not be warmer. They would simply ache, in the late winters, a little less.

The small deer would continue to pass through the lanes as if the buildings were only a particular kind of forest. They would, however, be slightly less inclined to wander beyond the edges of the settlement, where the land rises and the air dries and the footing becomes uncertain. They would stay closer. They would be, without knowing it, a little more at home.

The two-note call of the bird in the roofs would continue, and would be, in the listening, slightly more audible. Not louder. Only slightly more present in the ear of anyone who was quiet enough to hear it. A particular kind of person, in the Hollow, who had always been able to hear the call more clearly than others — a grandmother, perhaps, or a child of seven who had not yet learned that such hearing was unusual — would find, over the years, that the hearing had become a small comfort. They would not know why. They would simply note, in the way the people of the Hollow noted things, that the call came at a particular time of evening when the damp was heaviest, and that it had a way of making the weight of the place feel, for a moment, like the weight of a hand.

The pour-back, in the week determined by the smell of a particular flower, would go on. The cup of a particular wood would be brought out. The footsteps would go counter-clockwise around the basin. And the basin-tender, who appeared to come from the stones, would pour with her face like a folded leaf, and would not notice that the water, as it left the cup, was slightly slower to leave. Not noticeably slower. Only slower in the way that a word is slower when it has been meant, and not merely said.

---

And the dead — the quiet company, the ancestors in the lower rooms, the bones among which the small deer lay down — the dead would be, from this point forward, slightly less alone.

Not in any way that could be mourned for, or that could be missed if it had not been added. Only in the way that a room is less empty when someone, unseen, has been sitting in it. The dead had always been attended by the living, in the Hollow. The living walked among them and took the lamp down and brought it back up. The dead were not, by the standards of most places, neglected. But there is a kind of attending that is done by the feet and the hands and the knots, and there is another kind of attending that is done by something less visible, and it was the second kind that the power would add.

The dead would not, in any way that could be called sentience, know this. They were past knowing. But the bones, in the slow chalky way of bones, would hold a fraction more warmth than they had. The small carved figures that watched the basins and the doorways would lean, in their worn and faceless way, very slightly more forward. The moss that crept up the back of the figure by the central basin would, in a particular week of a particular year, pause in its creeping, as though listening to something it could almost hear, and then continue.

None of this would be remarked. None of it was meant to be remarked. The place would go on being the place, and the people would go on being the people, and the agreement would go on being kept, and the spring would go on giving, and the only evidence, if there was any evidence at all, would be in the small improved steadiness of things. A slightly better harvest of the prepared moss for the lamps. A slightly longer life for one or two of the elders, by a season or two, with the same gradual and unresisted old age. A slightly softer landing for the small deer in the harder winters. The kind of improvement that, in a place like the Hollow, would be received not as a gift but as the ordinary kindness of a place that was, on the whole, kinder than it had any reason to be.

---

The power, having decided this, did not act.

This is important to say. The power did not, in any visible or dramatic sense, do anything at all. There was no hand lifted. There was no light. There was no voice, in whatever language the power spoke when it spoke at all. There was only, from the vantage that was not a place, a slight shift in the texture of attention — a shift so small that it would not have been perceptible to anything smaller than a god, and the power was not, by its own reckoning, a god. It was something older than gods, or quieter, or more patient, or all three. It was the kind of thing that watches small places hold themselves together, and does not interrupt, and occasionally, when the watching has gone on long enough, and the holding has become beautiful enough, joins the holding from the inside, in a way that cannot be detected, in a way that adds nothing that can be named.

It was not a blessing, exactly. The power did not deal in blessings. Blessings are a kind of speaking, and the power had, over the long course of its attention, learned not to speak. It was more like a settling. The way a house settles, in the years after it is built, and the way the settling is not a flaw but a finding — a finding of the shape the house was always going to be, the shape it was in the builder's mind before the builder began.

The Hollow had found its shape. The power had, in this small and invisible way, simply settled into the finding.

---

And the place went on.

The buildings did not change. The channels did not change. The vines grew a little each year and the moss crept a little further up the carved figure and the stones underfoot sank a little deeper into the ground and the water found new small ways to move and the small deer continued to pass through the lanes as if the buildings were only a particular kind of forest, and the birds continued their two notes into the damp air, and the cold continued, and the damp continued, and the light, when it came, continued to make everything briefly, gently bright.

The basin-tender, whose name was not used in her presence, continued to tend.

The pour-back was performed, in its week, in its pattern, with its cup of a particular wood.

The children learned the knots. The young people went out in pairs and came back with observations. The elders listened and did not respond.

The dead were remembered. The living continued.

And somewhere in the holding — in the agreement, in the spring, in the damp, in the small faceless figure leaning forward with its hands on its knees, in the slow grind of the wood settling and the stone breathing and the root against stone and the stone against root — somewhere in there, not as a thing added but as a thing already there, was one more quiet keeper, who had been attending for so long that the attending had become part of the place, and who would go on attending, without reward, without acknowledgment, without any hope of being thanked, for as long as the spring ran and the agreement held and the place continued to be, in its small and perfect way, what it was.

Nothing larger than that was needed.

Nothing larger than that was, in the end, given.
