# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not enter.

This is the first thing, and the only thing to get right.

A benevolent power, looking down at this chimney, would see the temptation immediately: to clean it, to drain it, to pour out the sediment and inspect the turbine, to catalog the mosses and name the unnamed things in the upper third, to shore the cracked concrete, to pull the snake into the light and verify it is well. It would see a wounded machine and want to make it well. It would see an orphaned ecology and want to give it a gardener. It would see the rebar rusting and want to slow it.

It would see all this and know that every one of those impulses is the wrong one.

Because the chimney is not wounded. The chimney is *doing something*. The chimney has been doing something since the second year after the doors were bolted, and the something is not the something the chimney was built to do, and the something is finer. The chimney was built to vent. The chimney is now composing. The composition is the moss anchoring to the dust of a cold seam, the fig descending a hundredth of a millimeter at a time, the snake threading between two ferns once a year, the beetle walking the same line its grandmother walked. The composition is the bookkeeping the text says is perfect and no one is keeping. To send in a hand — any hand, however gentle — would be to disturb the only ledger that has ever balanced itself.

So the power would not enter. It would not send a wind. It would not send a thought. It would not mend. It would not even look, in the way that looking can be a kind of touch.

What it would do, instead, is this.

## It would change the weather outside the chimney.

Not over the chimney. Not down the open mouth. The chimney has its own weather and the chimney's weather is its own, and the power respects the boundary. It would change the weather on the catchment — the watershed, the basin, the thousand square miles of forest and mountain whose rain and breath and shed leaves fall, eventually, into the column of vapor that becomes the drips.

It would do this slowly. It would do this over decades. It would do it in the way that mountains do this to themselves, except from the other direction.

Specifically, it would do four things.

**First, it would tend the canopy.** The canopy is the chimney's parent. The canopy is what catches the rain and the wind and the spores and the dust before any of them enter the open mouth, and the canopy is what the breath trades with, and the breath is the chimney's circulation. The power would make the canopy denser, in places, and more varied in others; it would encourage a fig tree to take root a hundred yards upwind that would not have taken root, and let another fig tree fall that was going to fall anyway, and the difference is the kind of difference that accumulates in a watershed over a century. The canopy does not know the chimney. The canopy's job is to be the canopy. The power would make the canopy better at being the canopy.

**Second, it would adjust the aquifer.** Somewhere beneath the chimney, water moves through stone. The chimney was built, perhaps, where the stone was firm and the water was elsewhere; the water has since found the chimney's foundation, and the water is the slow patient other half of the rebar's slow patient rusting. The power would not stop the water. The water is the chimney's collaborator. The power would only move the water, by a fraction, in a direction the chimney does not feel, so that the rusting is slowed by a century, or two centuries, and the collapse is not prevented but is scheduled to happen at a time when the fig has finished crossing the floor and the wood is ready to take over.

**Third, it would welcome the things that fly through the canopy at dusk.** The plume at dawn is what the canopy sees. The plume is the chimney's exhalation. The plume is the chimney's argument with the sky. The power would make the plume, on the worst winter mornings, slightly more visible — a fraction of a degree warmer, a fraction of a percent more humid — so that the bats leaving at dusk and returning at dawn can find the mouth more easily in the storm, and the unnamed things in the upper third can find the mouth at all. The power would not make this change noticeable. It would make this change the kind of change that the chimney, by the time it notices, has already incorporated, and the chimney will not notice because the chimney is a slow machine and slow machines incorporate their changes.

**Fourth, it would not touch the turbine.** This is the hardest of the four, and the most important. The turbine is the chimney's first citizen. The turbine is the sound. The sound is the foundation. The sound is older than the moss. To stop the turbine would be to silence the only voice the chimney has ever spoken, and the voice is not a voice but a *working*, and the working is the place. The power would not stop the turbine. The power would not slow the turbine. The power would not, in any of the small ways available to a benevolent power, attempt to extend the turbine's life by a single hour, because the turbine is going to die before the chimney dies, and the turbine's death is a part of the chimney's future the way the chimney's death is a part of the fig's future, and the fig is the chimney's inheritance, and the inheritance is not to be interfered with.

## What the power would not do.

It would not heal the snake's parasites, if the snake has parasites. The snake's parasites are part of the chimney.

It would not protect the frogs from the snake. The frogs are the snake's livelihood and the snake is the frogs' ceiling.

It would not save the bats from the weather that kills bats. The bats are many. The bats are the chimney's clock. The clock is allowed to lose minutes.

It would not name the unnamed things in the upper third. The unnamed things are the chimney's secret. The chimney's secret is the chimney's. Naming is a kind of entrance. The power does not enter.

It would not write this account.

But the power knows that someone else will, and the power is glad of it, because the account is the second best thing to silence, and the second best thing is the most a place like this can hope for.

## What the power would feel, looking down.

It would feel what any benevolent power feels looking at a thing that does not need it: a small, clean grief, and a larger, cleaner joy.

The grief is that the chimney will not know it was helped. The chimney will go on with the slight better weather, the slightly warmer plume, the slightly fuller canopy, and the chimney will not know that these things were gifts, and the chimney will not be grateful, and the chimney will not be ungrateful, and the chimney will go on, and the going on is the point. The grief is not a real grief. The grief is the taste of wanting credit, and the power swallows it.

The joy is the place itself. The joy is the snake in the gap. The joy is the bracket eating the beetle that died on the seam. The joy is the moss holding the dust. The joy is the drop on the back of the hand, warm, falling from a height it has measured in falling. The joy is that the chimney is, in the way the text says all slow machines are, a kind of patience, and the patience is its own reward, and the reward is to be left alone, and the leaving alone is what the power, with all its will, decides to do.

The power decides.

The power adjusts the weather.

The power goes away.

The drop falls.

The chimney continues.
