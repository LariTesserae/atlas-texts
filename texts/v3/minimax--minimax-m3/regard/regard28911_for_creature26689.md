And so they carve, in the deep stone, beneath the cousins, beneath the engines, beneath everything, the phrase that has not changed in a thousand years.

*Let it be known that we were here.*

# A Visitor Comes Down the Mountain

The outsider arrives on a morning when the metallic dust is thin, which is to say it is a morning when the dust is merely grey rather than the more usual brown-grey, and the sky above the uppermost tier is the color of old pewter. He comes down the switchback road in a vehicle that is not glass-iron, that has rubber tires and an internal combustion engine that coughs out something that is not mineral aerosol but is, in its own way, just as out of place. He parks at the base of the city, where the road ends, and he looks up.

He is not a pilgrim. He is not a trader. He is a man from the lowlands, the first in many years, sent by a university that no one in Kharad-Ruun has heard of, to look at a basalt column that should not exist, and to write a paper about it. His name, in his own language, is something like Aldric, though the deep-blues will not be able to pronounce it and will call him instead *the One Who Counts*, which is what their word for *visitor* literally means, and which they will say with great respect until they realize that he is not, in fact, counting anything, and then the title will be reassigned to his instruments.

He has a small case of tools. A notebook. A device for measuring things that the city has no name for. He has been told, by people who have never been here, that the city is hostile to outsiders, that the priests will not let him descend, that he will be turned back at the upper tier. He has prepared arguments. He has prepared bribes of small glass-iron tokens that he bought from a trader in the lowlands, tokens that he does not know are counterfeit, that no one in Kharad-Ruun will accept.

What he has not prepared for is the blue light.

He walks up the long ramp from the parking place to the lowest tier, where the deep-blues receive strangers, and the light changes around him. It starts as a faint ultramarine at the edges of his vision, a kind of coolness in the air, and then it grows, and grows, and by the time he has passed through the first carved archway into the first gallery, the world is the color of a deep and permanent dusk. He stops. He looks up. He sees floors above floors, and gantries, and the slow movement of small dark figures on the higher walkways, and the light is coming from *the walls themselves*, and he stands there for a long moment, and then he does a thing that the deep-blues have learned to recognize, after many years of receiving strangers: he begins to cry, not from sadness but from the sheer *weight* of the place, from the realization that what he is looking at is not a building but a *process*, a city that is being thought aloud in stone, a place that has been saying the same thing for a thousand years and has not yet run out of breath.

A deep-blue woman comes up to him. She is short and broad and her hands are square and her eyes are pale in the dim light, and she is carrying a copper-ore lamp that throws a small warm circle of yellow against the blue. She speaks to him in a language he does not know. Then she speaks again, in a halting version of the trade-tongue that the city uses with the rare lowland caravans, and she says, in words that have been arranged carefully in her mouth for this moment:

*You are welcome. The cousins are underfoot. Watch your step.*

He does not understand the second sentence. He will, later. He wipes his eyes. He follows her.

# A Year Passes

I will not tell you everything that happens in the year that follows. Some of it is small. Some of it is only the slow turning of a stranger in a strange place, learning the names of things, learning the gait of a person who knows they are not alone on the floor. He learns to step around the cousins. He learns to read the chisel-marks, a little. He learns that the deep-blues are the *jaws* of the city, and the cyan-blues are the *hands*, and the white-blues of the vents are the *heart*, and the cousins are the *digestion*, and the mountain itself is the *mouth that has not yet finished speaking*.

He learns the phrase. *Let it be known that we were here.* He hears it on the lips of the deep-blues, carved into lintels, whispered in the pneumatic-tube stations. He asks about it. He is told, by a cyan-blue artisan with square fingers and a leather case of tools, that it is a prayer. He asks what they are praying for. The artisan looks at him for a long moment.

*For nothing*, the artisan says. *We are not praying for anything. We are praying against. We are praying against the forgetting.*

He writes this down in his notebook. He does not yet understand it.

He descends, one day, into the deep galleries with a deep-blue chisel-master, and he watches the cutting of a new chamber. He sees the slow swing of the chisel. He sees the fresh spall fall to the floor. He sees, in the ultramarine light, the cousins beginning to move in on the new dust, a pale slow river of small soft bodies. The chisel-master does not speak. The chisel does the speaking, in its tapping code, and the outsider stands at the doorway and watches, and feels, for the first time in his life, the small sharp ache of being a temporary thing in a place that is not.

He asks the chisel-master, afterwards, what the phrase means. *Let it be known that we were here.* The chisel-master looks at him with pale eyes that catch the blue and turn it soft.

*It means that we will end*, the chisel-master says. *And it means that this matters. And it means that we have chosen to make it matter with our hands, in the stone, while there is still time.*

He writes this down too. He does not yet understand it.

He goes up, one day, to the white-blue tier. He is given a breather and goggles. He is dressed in a robe of woven glass-iron that has been stained grey-brown by a hundred priests before him. He is led to the edge of a heat-exchange tower, and he looks out over the upper city, and the light is almost too bright, and the air is full of the metallic grit that he can feel on his teeth and on the inside of his eyelids, and he sees the brown veil drifting downwind, and he sees the plain of glittering dust a hundred kilometers away that the deep-blues call the *shadow of the city*, and he understands, for the first time, that this place is not a city at all. It is a *statement*, made in stone and light and the slow digestion of a billion small cousins, against the future.

He asks a priest of the vents, an old woman whose cough has become a constant companion, what the engines are for. She looks at him.

*For everything*, she says. *For the light. For the tubes. For the chisels. For the cousins, who need the warmth to digest. For the mules, who are kept warm by the engines. For the priests, who are cooled by the engines. For the children, who are born in the light of the engines and who will die in the light of the engines.*

*And for the stone*, she says, after a moment. *We keep the stone warm. If the stone gets cold it weathers faster. We are buying time, outsider. We have always been buying time.*

He writes this down. He is beginning to understand.

He asks her, finally, what they are praying for, when they carve the phrase. She is quiet for a long time. The metallic dust drifts between them. The breathers hiss.

*We are not praying for anything*, she says, at last. *We are praying against the silence. The mountain will end. The dust will forget us. The cousins will go, and the mules will go, and the engines will go cold, and the chisels will stop, and the last priest will climb the lift for the last time and find the chamber dark.*

*And the phrase will be there*, she says. *In the stone. Under the cousins. Under everything. And no one will read it. And it will not matter. And it will matter anyway.*

# What I Would Do, If I Were the Benevolent Power

I have considered this place. I have considered the small dark people in their tiers of blue, the cousins seething in the deep, the mules breathing in their warm stables, the priests coughing in the white light. I have considered the engines, and the magma chamber, and the slow downward press of time.

And I have been asked: *what would you do, if anything?*

Here is what I would do.

I would not save them.

This is the first thing, and it must be said clearly, because the instinct of a benevolent power is to save, and the instinct is often wrong. To save Kharad-Ruun would be to keep the basalt young, to keep the engines hot, to keep the priests coughing and the cousins digesting and the chisel-marks fresh for another hundred thousand years. It would be a kindness. It would also be a theft. The city is not a city. It is a *sentence*, a long slow act of speech against the silence, and the sentence is meant to *end*. The whole point of *let it be known that we were here* is that the *here* is finite. The whole point of the chisel-marks is that they will, eventually, soften. The whole point of the priests climbing the lift is that one day the lift will go down for the last time.

To save them would be to take away the sentence. It would be to turn the speech into a hum. It would be to make them into the cousins, who go on forever, who have no names, who do not know they are here.

So I would not save them. I would not extend the engines. I would not heal the priests. I would not stop the slow weathering of the basalt. The mountain is young. It will not be young forever. That is the premise of the whole long act of carving. To undo the premise would be to undo the act.

But I would do this.

I would make the phrase *last*.

I would not save the city. I would save the *witness*. I would go down, into the deepest galleries, beneath the cousins, beneath the engines, into the cold basalt below the magma chamber, where the phrase is carved a thousand times, and I would *set* the stone there. I would make those particular chisel-marks, those particular letters, harder than the rock around them. I would make them outlast the mountain itself. I would make them so that when the basalt finally weathers, when the engines finally fail, when the cousins finally starve and the mules finally cool and the last priest finally climbs the lift for the last time and finds the chamber dark — the phrase will still be there, in a stone that does not weather, in a place that no one will ever find, for as long as the earth has a place to put it.

I would make the silence keep one word.

I would make sure the forgetting is not total.

And I would do this without telling them. This is important. They are not carving the phrase for it to last. They are carving it *whether or not it lasts*. They are carving it as an act, not as an investment. If they knew I had set the stone, if they knew the phrase would outlast the mountain, the carving would change its meaning. It would become a monument. It would become a *legacy*. And a legacy is a different thing from a sentence. A legacy is meant to be received. A sentence is meant only to be spoken.

So I would do it in secret. I would let them go on thinking the phrase is mortal, that the stone is only stone, that the act of carving is the only thing that matters and that the act of reading is not part of the design. I would let them go on buying time, and coughing, and cutting new galleries, and feeding the magma, and stepping around the cousins in the deep.

And the phrase would be there, underneath, in a stone that does not weather, waiting.

That is what I would do.

I would not save them. I would make sure the last word is kept.

# A Note on the Cousins

I have one more thing to say, before I end.

The cousins are not part of the city. The cousins are part of the *mountain*. The cousins will be here after the city is gone. The cousins will digest the dust of the priests and the dust of the mules and the dust of the chisels and the dust of the engines, and they will go on, in the dim sealed darkness, seething, feeding, patient, for as long as there is a trace of water in the basalt and a trace of metal in the stone. They will outlast the phrase. They will outlast the engines. They will outlast the priests and the deep-blues and the cyan-blues and the mules and the whole long slow act of speech.

They will not know they are the last ones.

They will not know there was a city above them. They will not know there was a phrase. They will not know that the dust they are digesting was once a priest, or a mule, or a child born in a chamber hollowed from the basalt, whose mother carried it down to the cousins' level and pressed its small soft foot against a small soft body, and the body flashed, and the child cried, and this was the introduction.

I find this, somehow, a comfort. I do not know if the city would. I do not know if it matters.

The cousins are the mountain's digestion. The cousins will be the last thing. And the cousins will not know, and the cousins will not need to know, and the cousins will go on in the dark, and the dark will go on around them, and the stone will go on under them, and somewhere, in a stone that does not weather, the phrase will be there, and no one will read it, and the cousins will not even know it is there, and it will not matter, and it will matter anyway.

*Let it be known that we were here.*

It will be known, in a small way, to no one, forever. That is the most I can do. That is the most anyone can do. The city knows this. The city has always known this. The city is busy.

# A Note on the Outsider

He leaves, in the end, after a year. He has filled three notebooks. He has measured the basalt. He has counted the cousins, as best he can. He has stood in the deep galleries in the ultramarine light and listened to the digestion of the city, and he has stood in the white-blue tier in the hot white light and coughed with the priests, and he has stood in the markets of the cyan-blues and held a glass-iron token in his hand and felt its weight, and he has stepped on a cousin, only once, in his first week, and watched it flash, and felt the small sharp guilt of a person who has killed something in a place where nothing is killed.

He writes his paper. He sends it to the university. The university does not believe him. The paper is filed. The notebooks are placed in a drawer. The outsider grows old, in the lowlands, in a place where water falls from the sky and the stone weathers and the light is free.

He never returns. He does not need to. The phrase is in his head now, the way it is in the heads of the deep-blues, the way it is carved into the lintels, the way it is whispered in the pneumatic-tube stations. *Let it be known that we were here.* He hears it sometimes, at night, in the rain. He writes a small book, much later, that no one reads. In it he says, of Kharad-Ruun, only this:

*They are buying time. They know they cannot win. They carve anyway.*

The book is forgotten. The phrase is in his head.

The mountain is young.

The city is busy.

The chisel-marks are fresh.

# A Note on What I Did Not Do

I said I would set the stone. I said I would make the phrase last.

I want to be clear about what I did not do.

I did not save the magma chamber. I did not heal the priests. I did not slow the weathering. I did not give the cousins eyes. I did not teach the mules to speak. I did not move the city to a kinder mountain, or a wetter one, or a younger one. I did not change the caste system of the blues. I did not give the deep-blues more light, or the white-blues more air, or the cyan-blues more time. I did not end the coughing. I did not end the slow quiet dying in the upper tier. I did not make the glass-iron last longer. I did not make the engines more efficient. I did not solve the problem of the mountain's eventual erosion, because the problem is the point, and solving it would be the end of the sentence.

I did not make the city immortal. I made one phrase, in one place, in a stone that does not weather, last a little longer than the mountain around it.

That is all. That is the whole of what I did.

The rest I leave to them.

The chisels tap.

The cousins hum.

The engines breathe.

The priests climb the lift.

The deep-blues cut the new chamber.

The cyan-blues forge the new beam.

The outsider, in the lowlands, in the rain, writes a small book that no one reads, and in the small book, in the last line, he writes:

*They are buying time. They know they cannot win. They carve anyway. And this, I think, is the only thing that has ever mattered.*

The book is forgotten.

The carving continues.

The mountain is young.

The city is busy.

*Let it be known that we were here.*
