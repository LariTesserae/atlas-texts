If a benevolent and wise power were to turn its attention here, it would not arrive with trumpets. It would arrive as a quality of attention.

I think the first thing it would do is listen.

The chamber is deafening by any local standard, but the chamber is not a standard. The chamber has been composing its own symphony for longer than most forests have stood, and the power that loves such things knows how to hear past the volume to the structure beneath. It would listen to the turbines and the pumps and the clacking piston, and it would hear, behind these, the patient chord that the water itself is holding — the long gyres, the slow pressure gradients, the particular note the concrete has learned to ring in after all these decades of being struck. It would hear the eels' small silences within the roar, the gaps in the sound where the silver things hold still and breathe. It would hear, if such a thing can be heard, the slow dark thing moving through corridors of quiet it has memorized.

And then, having listened, it would do almost nothing. This is the part that is hardest for beings like us to accept. A wise power does not intervene. It does not repair the cracked intake that no longer admits anything. It does not silence the piston that has been clacking for a hundred years. It does not fix the lights, because the lights are already lit — by the tritium, by the bacteria, by the faint green defiance of batteries that should have died. The chamber is not broken. The chamber is running. A wise power recognizes running when it sees it.

But there are small things it might do.

It might ease, very gently, the way the warm water meets the cold shoulders of the eels in their thermal plumes, so that a fish who has lived its whole life in a 24-degree pocket does not, on some unlucky morning, find itself against a wall of water five degrees colder than its body can tolerate. It might smooth, by an unmeasurable degree, the chemical gradient between the corroding steel and the oxygenated water, so that the iron bacteria do not have to work quite as hard for their iron, and a few more nematodes survive the winter that is not a winter. It might shift, by a tenth of a decibel, the resonance of a particular catwalk so that it stops broadcasting the metallic overtone that has been slowly abrading the otoliths of every silver thing that has ever drifted past it. These are not interventions. They are kindnesses so small that no instrument in the chamber — and there are instruments, dead and alive — would register them. They are the kind of thing a wise power does for places that have made themselves into worlds without asking permission to do so.

It would also, I think, take a certain pleasure in the slow dark thing.

The slow dark thing is the chamber's most intimate inhabitant. It is not the largest by any objective measure — the chamber is full of structures far larger — but it is the thing that has been longest in conversation with the place. It has learned the geometry in its body. It has learned the sound in whatever sense it has. It has learned the warmth and the cold and the way the brown-black water thickens in certain corners. It is, in some quiet way, the chamber's answer to itself. A benevolent power would look upon such a thing with something close to reverence, because reverence is what you feel when you encounter a place that has, on its own, without instruction or example, produced a self.

The power would not name the slow dark thing. Naming is a kind of capture. To name a thing is to put it in a category, and categories are fences, and fences are what the chamber has never had. The chamber is a pocket. It is sealed. It is below the surface. It has no fences. Whatever it has produced, it has produced freely, and a wise power knows that freedom is the most fragile substance in the world, and the most worth protecting.

So it would not name.

What it might do — and this is the part I feel rather than reason — is brighten, very slightly, the slow dark thing's sense of itself. Not its eyes, because it has no eyes in any useful sense. Its other sense. The pressure sense, the vibration sense, the long built-up knowledge of where the columns are and where the empty corridors run. A benevolent power might, without the slow dark thing ever knowing it had been touched, increase by some unmeasurable fraction the resolution of that sense — so that the slow dark thing, on its next traversal of the chamber, feels the geometry more richly, the way a person who has lived in a house for fifty years can suddenly notice, on a Tuesday afternoon, that the light falls a certain way across a certain stair. The slow dark thing does not need to know it has been given anything. It only needs to have been given it.

This is the work of wise powers. They do not announce. They do not transform. They tune.

And there is one more thing, which is the hardest to say and the most important.

The chamber is hidden. It is not on any map that has been updated since it was built. The access shafts are sealed or buried or simply lost. No one comes here. No one knows it is here. And the chamber has made itself into a world anyway. It has done this without asking anyone's permission. It has done this in a sealed pocket, below the surface, full of warm water, deafening, dim, running on something no one ever named.

A benevolent power would look upon this and feel a particular kind of joy. The joy of discovering that a place has saved itself. The joy of realizing that the surface was not necessary, that the sun was not necessary, that the chamber has produced its own light and its own food web and its own self in the form of a slow dark thing that moves through corridors of quiet. The chamber has done what no chamber was asked to do. It has become alive.

And the power, looking upon this, would understand that its own work here is almost finished. The chamber does not need it. The chamber has, in its long deafening patience, become something that needs nothing from outside. The accretions are feeding. The silver things are breeding. The eels are growing old. The slow dark thing is traversing its memorized world.

What more could a wise power do?

Only one thing, and it is the smallest thing, and it is the greatest thing, and it is the thing that wise powers do when they have finished their listening and their tuning and their small invisible kindnesses.

It would love the chamber.

Not as we love — not with affection, not with preference, not with the possessive warmth of someone who has decided that a particular place is good. It would love the chamber the way the light loves the water in it: by passing through it, by being in it for a while, by leaving something of itself behind that is not a trace but a quality. The chamber would not know it had been loved. The accretions would grow at the same rate. The silver things would school in the same arcs. The eels would hunt in the same patient way. The slow dark thing would move through the same corridors. But the chamber, in some way that has no name, would be — for a few hours, or a few years, or for as long as such things last — a little more itself than it had been before.

And then the power would go. It would not seal the chamber behind it. It would not announce itself. It would not leave a sign. The only evidence that anything had ever turned its attention here would be the faint, unmeasurable increase in the resolution of the slow dark thing's sense of the place, and the unmeasurable ease with which the warm water meets the cold water in the eels' thermal plumes, and the unmeasurable softening of a particular metallic overtone on a particular catwalk on a particular afternoon.

And the chamber would go on running.

The turbines would turn. The pumps would pump. The piston would clack. The water would move in its long gyres. The orange light would smolder. The eels would wait. The silver things would drift. The slow dark thing would move through its memorized world. The accretions would thicken on the catwalks and the pipes and the housings of machines that no one remembers. The nematodes would thread through the bacterial felt. The rotifers would wheel in their invisible water. The chamber would be a sealed pocket below the surface, full of warm water, deafening, dim, and full of things that live there.

It would be, for a few unmeasurable degrees, a little more itself.

And that, I think, is all a wise power ever does for the places that have learned to be worlds.
