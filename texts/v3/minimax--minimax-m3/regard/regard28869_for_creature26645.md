What the Power Does

A benevolent and wise power, having drawn its attention to this place, would not, in the first instance, do anything.

This is the part that is hardest to understand from outside. The instinct, when one sees a valley like this — damp, dim, industrious, packed, full of scarred hands and bad knees and workers dead by seventy — is to do something. To lighten it. To dry it. To give it more sun. To give it something that is not fog. To lengthen the lives. To make it, in some way, into a different valley.

A wise power does not do this. A wise power sees the valley and recognizes that the valley is whole. The valley is not asking. The workers are not asking. The woman with the scarred hands is not asking. The valley has its own continuation, its own dignity, its own way of being alive — cool, damp, dim, packed, full, never finished — and to intervene in that continuation would be a kind of violence. The violence of being seen as incomplete when one is, in one's own way, complete.

So the power does nothing. Not because it does not care. Because it cares too much to disturb.

But.

There are small things.

The heron, for instance. The heron comes up the river sometimes, once a season, rarely, and the dogs drive it off. The dogs bark and lunge at the end of their leashes. The heron lifts off unhurried, with a slow beat of its long wings, and does not come back that season. This is the way of it. A benevolent power might see to it that the heron comes more often. Not every day. The dogs need their ceremony, and the heron needs its wildness, and the valley needs its suspicion of outsiders. But twice a season. Three times. The heron comes up the brown river and fishes in the slow water, and the dogs are brought to the bank in their clean indoor coats, and the barking becomes, over weeks, a kind of greeting rather than a kind of driving. The heron stays longer each time. Eventually it stays for the day, and the dogs watch from the bank with the dignified attention of dogs watching something they have agreed not to chase.

This would be noticed. But it would not be noticed as intervention. It would be absorbed. The valley absorbs everything. It would be remarked upon, perhaps, in the way the valley remarks upon things — not directly, but by a small adjustment of habit. The bargeman, poling up the river, would nod to the heron. The children would have a new thing to point at. The old man on the High Street would say *the heron's been back* as if it had always been so.

The moss. The moss grows on every surface colder than the air — on the north face of every brick, on every pipe, on every roof tile older than ten years, in the cracks of every cobbled street. The moss is the color of soot, which is the color of fog. The moss is invisible in its work. A benevolent power might, here, do a small thing: thicken the moss on the brick by a fraction. Just enough that the brick holds another century. Just enough that the great-granddaughter of the girl who is now nine — the girl learning to read the fog — finds brick under her feet when she is seventy, instead of finding the wall beginning to give.

This would not be remarked upon. The moss is invisible. The brick is invisible. A fraction of a millimeter is invisible. The valley would simply have brick another hundred years, the way a body simply has a beating heart — not remarked upon, only absent when it stops.

The slick spots. The valley is full of them — moss over cobbles, moss over steps, the places where water has run for a hundred and forty years and the stone is worn glassy. A worker, walking in the pre-dawn dark, can step wrong and break an ankle. It happens. The valley does not pity. But a benevolent power might, in these moments, do something very small. It might make sure that on the morning the woman with the bad knees is carrying the heavy tin pail — on the morning her knees are worse than usual, on the morning the moss is slicker than usual — her foot finds the cobble that is dry. Not because the city is kind. The city is not kind. But because her foot is going where the dry cobble is.

She would not notice. She has walked this walk so often that she does not see the slick spots; she sees only the places that are not slick. The power would simply make sure the not-slick places are where her foot lands. The way gravity makes sure the ground is under the foot. Quietly. Without announcement.

The foremen. The foremen are what the valley has made — large, mostly, weighted in the middle, eating well, with wives of a particular capable kind. The valley made them for a reason. A wise power does not punish the foremen, because to take them away would unbalance the thing the valley is. But a benevolent power might make sure that the foreman who has earned his place is the one who gets it. The valley does this itself, quietly, by the slow machinery of shops not moving correctly, by the month-long expulsion of the man who arrived from outside. But sometimes the expulsion takes too long. Sometimes a month of bad teaching in the shop is a month too long. The power would shorten that month. Not to nothing. The valley needs its own dignity in this. But to a week. To a few days. Enough that the apprentices are not taught badly for too long. Enough that the shop recovers quickly.

The grandfather's tools. The shop where the woman works — the shop she has walked to for thirty-one years, the shop she will walk to tomorrow and the day after — has her grandfather's tools on the wall. They hang in the same places. They have hung there for a hundred years. A benevolent power might make sure they continue to hang there. Not that the wall is unsound — the wall is sound. But that no foreman's wife decides to clear the wall for a new apprentice. That no flood comes in the night and takes them. That the hammer with the worn handle stays in its place, and the apprentice who is now a girl of nine comes, in six years, to clean them and finds them where her grandmother found them.

The bells. The bells of the guild halls and the union temple ring at six, at noon, at six again. The fog muffles them. A worker hears, at any moment, two or three at once, blurred into a single tone without edges. The bells are what the valley uses to mark time. A benevolent power might make sure that the bells do not stop. Generators fail. Bell ropes rot. Stays break on still nights. There are weeks, sometimes, when the bells are silent, and the valley runs on other rhythms, and it is not the same valley. The power would see to it that the ropes are replaced before they break. That the tower is repointed in the year it needs repointing. That the tone does not change. The tone is the valley's way of knowing what hour it is.

These are the things. Small things. Almost nothing.

The benevolent and wise power does not give the valley sun. The valley does not need sun. The sun, when it comes, comes down the river and the bargemen see it, and that is enough. To give the valley sun would be to make it a different valley. To make it something the bargemen no longer recognize.

The benevolent and wise power does not lengthen the lives. The workers die by seventy, mostly. This is the valley's way. To make them live to ninety would be to change them, and the valley is its own way of being alive, and changing it would be a kind of unkindness. The valley does not pity itself for this. The valley is not a place that pities. The valley is a place that continues.

The benevolent and wise power does not dry the damp. The damp is the valley. The fog is the valley. The moss is the valley. The brown river is the valley. The half-light, the slow condensation on every surface, the steam from the factories that merges with everything else — these are not conditions the valley suffers from. These are what the valley is. To dry the valley would be to make it into something that is not the Long Valley. To make it into a place no one in it would recognize.

What the power does, it does invisibly. It does not announce itself. It does not send a sign. It does not send a prophet. The valley does not need prophets. The valley needs only to continue.

The power watches. The power sees. The power does these small things, and these small things are not remarked upon as gifts, because they are woven into the ongoing. The valley absorbs them the way the moss absorbs the brick — into itself, indistinguishable from what was already there.

The valley continues.

The woman walks to the shop tomorrow morning and the day after, and her knees hurt, and she whistles three notes at the corner of Forge Street and the Avenue without knowing she whistles. The boy carries his master's tools up the stairs and grows into his overalls by the third year. The foreman eats his supper in the warm kitchen and his capable wife puts the apprentice's plate on the table. The priest rings the bell at six, and the tone comes through the fog without edges. The fog comes in. The river runs brown. The moss grows another fraction of a millimeter on the north face of the brick. The heron fishes in the slow water. The grandfather's tools hang in their places. The slick spot is somewhere she isn't stepping.

The valley is not finished.

The valley will not be finished.

The benevolent and wise power does not finish it. It lets it be what it is. It keeps the small things turning. It holds the place, gently, in the way a hand holds a thing it does not want to drop.

Not because the valley is fragile. The valley is not fragile. The valley is dense, and damp, and full, and continuing.

Because the valley is loved.

And the way to love a thing that is continuing is to let it continue.
