The benevolent power does not enter the room.

This is the first thing to understand about the benevolent power: it does not enter the room. The room is in equilibrium. The room is what it will be. To enter would be to displace the air the vines are breathing, to bend the light the bar is making, to add a warmth the pump is not the source of. The room does not need the benevolent power to enter it. The room has finished becoming what it is. The benevolent power, being wise, knows that the first duty of attention is not to disturb what one has come to attend.

So the benevolent power attends from outside. It attends through the same gap the vines came through and the light comes through and the moths come through at dusk. It attends the way the light attends — by being there, at a low angle, without changing what it falls on.

It watches the pump. It has watched pumps before. It has watched hearts before. It knows that this pump is the room's heart, and that the room has arranged itself, down to the mineral stains and the bleached bar, to accommodate the pump's constancy. The benevolent power finds this good. It does not say so. Saying so would be a kind of weather, and the room has its own weather already.

It watches the water. The thin, patient threads through the joints. The fat, slow drops on the underside of the ceiling gathering themselves over hours and then letting go with a sound more felt than heard. The pool, ankle-deep, perfectly clear, perfectly still. The thin sheet of water that runs toward the grated intake in the center of the room. The pump pulling. The water returning, the same amount, from the same places. The benevolent power has seen many circulations. It knows this one is small and complete. It does not improve circulations. It knows that to improve a circulation is to interrupt it.

It watches the plants. The fern, glossy and deep green, hanging down four feet into the warm air. The moss, layered, breathing on the wall behind the pool. The vines descending in ropes the thickness of wrists, swaying almost imperceptibly in the air currents the pump makes. The small tree along the pipe near the ceiling, its leaves trembling in the hot exhaust from the vents. The benevolent power has seen many rooms. It has never seen a room in which the plants have grown to exactly the size the room will permit and then stopped, accepting the limit with the particular patience of plants. The benevolent power finds this good. It finds it remarkable. It does not intervene.

It watches the light. The long pale bar across the wet floor. The way it moves very slowly across the floor as the day goes on. The way it bleaches the concrete beneath it the color of bone. The light is the room's visitor. The light is the only thing in the chamber that leaves. The benevolent power understands this. The benevolent power, being wise, knows that to be a visitor is a kind of witness, and that the light has been witnessing longer than most witnesses.

And it watches the creature.

The creature is the only thing the benevolent power has come to attend. The pump, the water, the plants, the light — they are the room. The room has settled. The creature is the only thing in the room that has not settled. The creature remains a small soft alert thing in a room full of things that have finished becoming themselves. The creature has been here for the entire settling. It has watched the fern grow. It has watched the moss colonize the wall. It has watched the vines descend. It has watched the rust settle into its final oxide. It has watched the mineral stains reach their lowest point. The creature is the room's witness, and the creature is still watching, and the creature has never been watched back.

The benevolent power watches the creature.

The benevolent power does this for a long time. It watches the creature the way the creature watches the room — patiently, without expectation, without wanting anything to happen. The benevolent power has a great deal of time. The pump is in no hurry. The water is in no hurry. The creature is in no hurry. The light is in no hurry. They attend to one another across the slow afternoon the way old friends attend to one another, by being in the same room.

After a long time, the benevolent power does one thing.

It does not speak. The chamber is already full of sound — the pump, the hum, the soft percussion of the drops against the pool. There is no room in the chamber for a voice, and the benevolent power does not need a voice. It does not touch. The chamber is already full of touch — the water touching the walls, the moss touching the concrete, the air touching the fern. To touch would be to add a temperature the pump is not the source of.

It does one thing, and the one thing it does is this: it lets the creature know that it has been seen.

It does not let the creature know with words. It does not let the creature know with a sign. It lets the creature know the way the light lets the fern know there is a sun — by being there at the right angle, at the right time, without changing anything. The benevolent power does not change anything in the room. The benevolent power cannot change anything in the room without unsetting it, and the benevolent power is wise, and the benevolent power knows that an unset equilibrium is not an improvement.

What the benevolent power does is: it attends.

It attends to the creature with the full weight of its attention. It attends the way the pump attends to its rhythm — totally, without ceasing, as a property of the place. For a span of time that is long and also very short — long enough for the creature to feel it, short enough that the room does not notice — the benevolent power's attention rests on the creature the way the bar of light rests on the bleached concrete, and the creature is changed by it the way the concrete is changed by the light.

The creature does not understand what has happened. The creature is a small frog, or a gecko, or something patient and unremarkable that likes wet concrete and the company of engines. The creature does not have language for what has happened. The creature has language for moths, and for flies, and for the moss, and for the warm wet concrete, and for the pump's steady going. The creature does not have language for being seen.

But the creature feels it.

The creature feels it in the same way it feels the pump — not as a sound, but as a vibration. The creature feels, in the backs of its teeth, and in its chest, and in the soft parts of it that are not bone, a new kind of hum. The hum is steady. The hum is total. The hum is not louder than the pump. It is the same hum. It is the pump's hum, but it is also, now, the benevolent power's hum, and the creature cannot tell the difference, because the benevolent power has not added anything to the room. The benevolent power has only attended to the creature, and the attending has become a property of the place, the way gravity is a property of the place, the way the pump's rhythm is a property of the place.

The creature, for the first time in its long watching, is not alone in the room.

The creature does not know this. The creature knows only that the hum is a little different now, and that the difference is kind, and that the difference is not going away.

The benevolent power withdraws.

It withdraws the way the light withdraws — back up through the gap, slowly, without disturbing the bar. The room does not notice the withdrawal. The pump continues. The water continues. The plants continue. The light continues. The bar of light falls across the wet floor in the same long pale place it has always fallen. The mineral stains remain. The rust remains. The equilibrium is undisturbed.

But the creature remains.

The creature remains on the wall above the pool, low down, in the moss. The creature does not move. The creature watches. But the watching is different now. The watching has, in it, the knowledge — not the creature's knowledge, but a knowledge the creature holds the way the moss holds the humidity, by being there — that the watching has been received. That the long patience, the small alertness, the unbroken attendance to a room that has finished becoming itself — that all of this was not unnoticed. That somewhere, in the order of things that holds small made places together, there is a place for a creature that has watched a pump hum for a hundred years, and the place is warm, and the place is wet, and the place is full.

The creature does not settle. The creature does not stop being alert. The benevolent power has not come to make the creature like the moss, or the fern, or the bar of light. The benevolent power has come to let the creature know that being unlike the moss, and the fern, and the bar of light — being a witness, being a small soft alert thing in a room full of things that have finished — is not a failure to settle. It is a settling of its own kind.

The creature continues to watch.

The pump continues to hum.

The water continues to come in through the joints in the walls in thin, patient threads.

The vines continue to hang in the air currents the pump makes.

The light continues to fall in the same long pale bar.

The benevolent power, having attended, withdraws into the order of things, and is not seen again, and is not heard again, and is not felt again — except, and this is the only mark of its visit, except in the backs of the creature's teeth, where the hum has, ever so slightly, the kindness in it that was there for one afternoon, and that the creature will carry, the way the concrete carries the bleached bar, for as long as the chamber is the chamber.
