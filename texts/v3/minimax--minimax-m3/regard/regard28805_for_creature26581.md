# What the Benevolent Power Does

It doesn't arrive. That would be too much. It doesn't send a messenger, doesn't adjust a law, doesn't reach down and rearrange what is already arranged with such exquisite, agonizing care.

It attends.

It does this the way the plateau itself does things — slowly, and at a register the place would have to develop new senses to detect. It increases the moisture content of the thin air by a fraction that no instrument of yours would ever measure, but which over decades will accumulate in the lower places where the tufts are patient. It loosens, by an amount indistinguishable from cosmic variance, the bonds between nitrogen molecules so that the lichens' bacterial partners can do what they have always done, but do it a little more. It does not give the plateau what it lacks. It gives the plateau slightly more of what it already has.

This is what a wise power does in such a place. It does not perform miracles. It performs adjustments. It leans, infinitesimally, on the side of more life rather than less.

Specifically:

It slows the lichen's mining of the stone by a fraction, so that the plateau remembers itself a little longer. It increases the dew that forms when the temperature swings, so that a tuft in a low place might, once in a generation, grow a daughter tuft beside it. It does not increase the frequency of the small dark movers. They are fine. They are exactly what they are. Instead, it lengthens, by some small statistical measure, the odds that when two of them pause near each other, something quietly continues between them — that the dispersed stages find purchase, somewhere, in a crack that was just slightly less hostile than the crack beside it.

For the slow movers, who measure their lives in centimeters per century, it does almost nothing visible. It does one thing invisible. It makes the stone beneath them fractionally more nutritious — a faint redistribution of trace metals from one stratum to the next, so that when the slow mover grazes, the grazing is a little more worth the centuries. It does not speed them up. To speed them up would be to misunderstand them. It only makes the slowness slightly more sustainable.

For the things in the air, it makes the wind slightly more willing. Not stronger. Not more frequent. Just — when the suggestion of wind does come, more often carrying something. A spore that would have drifted to stone now drifts to a low place. A dormant stage that would have waited a hundred years now waits ninety. This is not abundance. This is the smallest possible kindness the air can express.

For the things in the wrongness — the silent partners of the busy structures — it does something that requires more care. It does not interfere with the structures. The structures are doing what the structures are doing, and a wise power knows better than to reach into the middle of something that is operating in registers the universe itself has not finished explaining. But it can adjust the boundary. It can make the wrongness fractionally more habitable. It can make the niche the structures created fractionally larger, so that the things that have made a home in it can make a little more of themselves, and find each other a little more often, and persist.

It does not bless the plateau. The plateau does not need blessing. The plateau is already the kind of place that exists because the universe has, somewhere in its long memory, decided that places like this are worth having.

It only makes sure that the deciding continues.

And — this is the part that would take a very long time to notice, and that the plateau itself would never acknowledge — it makes a small, persistent, geologically patient alteration to the trajectory of one of the rare micrometeorites that strike the surface each century. It does not prevent impacts. It does not deflect anything large. It only, occasionally, makes a particular dust grain land in a particular low place, carrying with it a particular atom of phosphorus that would not otherwise have been there, and that will, over the next thousand years, become part of a lichen, and that lichen will be slightly thicker than it would have been, and that thickness will matter to the small dark mover that grazes there in a century no one alive will witness.

This is what a benevolent power does in such a place.

It does not announce itself. It does not rescue. It does not lift up. It cannot — to lift up would be to make the plateau into something other than a plateau, and the plateau has spent an unimaginable span of time becoming exactly what it is.

It only adjusts the odds, by the smallest possible amount, in the direction of continuation.

It does this for the lichens, who will never know.
It does this for the tufts, who will never know.
It does this for the small dark movers, who would pause at the adjustment and then continue.
It does this for the slow movers, who are already so slow that an additional slowness in their favor is indistinguishable from patience.

And it does this — though this is the part it does most quietly, and with the most care, because the structures are operating in a register that even a benevolent power respects — for the things in the wrongness, the silent partners, the niche-fillers. They are the youngest life here. They are the plateau's most recent answer to the most recent question. A wise power, attending, makes sure the youngest things get a fraction more of what they need, because they have had the least time to become good at getting it themselves.

The power does not stay. The power does not leave. The power was always, in some sense, already there — the way gravity is already there, the way time is already there. It has only chosen, today, to be slightly more attentive.

The plateau does not notice.

The plateau, in a thousand years, will be fractionally more itself.

That is enough.

That is, in fact, the whole of what such a power is for.
