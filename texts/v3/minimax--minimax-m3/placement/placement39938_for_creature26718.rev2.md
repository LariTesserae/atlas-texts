A Dog on the Third Terrace

I would be a dog.

Not one of the puppies tumbling in the lanes near the lower gate, where the ox-carts grind past and the smell of dung is strong and the small boys throw stones that mostly miss. Not one of those. I would be an old dog. A dog whose muzzle has gone the color of the lichen on the upper rock, whose hips click when it gets up in the morning — there is always a morning when the click becomes a thought you have, a small weather inside the body. I would live on the third terrace, where two stairways meet at a corner thick enough to sit in, and I would own that corner in the way dogs here own corners: by being in it often, by sleeping in it daily, by the slow accumulation of my scent against the stone until the stone itself remembers me.

My name would be a short sound. One syllable, perhaps two, the kind of name that is not so much a name as a call. The woman who feeds me would use it rarely. She would use it only when she had something to say that could not be said with a hand on the head or a bowl set down. I would know the difference.

I would sleep with my chin on my paws and one ear turned up to the wind. The wind is always something. In the afternoon it comes down off the glacier with a thin bright insistence, and it finds the gaps between the stones and speaks through them in a small voice, and my ear — the upturned one — would follow that voice the way a person follows a conversation in another room. I would not understand it. I would not need to understand it. Understanding is not what the ear is for, here. The ear is for knowing that something is moving, that the air has changed, that the cloud is coming down or going up, that the small boy with the water jar has passed again on his rounds.

I would know his rounds before he did. I would hear the clay of the ladle against the rim of the jar and I would know which cistern by the sound, because each cistern has its own note — the deep one under the second terrace hums when the ladle dips, the shallow one near the gate is a higher sound, the one at the corner where I sleep is somewhere between, and I have heard it ten thousand mornings and I could tell you which one he was at by the pitch alone, if you could ask me, which you cannot, because I am a dog and the question is not one I have the shape for.

I would watch him pass. He would glance at me the way he glances at everything — once, briefly, with the flat attention of someone who has work. He would not greet me. He would not need to. I am part of the route. I am the dog at that corner, the way the corner is the corner at that place. We have an arrangement that is not an arrangement. He comes, he goes, I am here, and the morning is made of these passages and the light moves across the stones between them.

I would not be afraid of anything. This is important. There are no foxes here. There are no wolves. The eagles of the upper peaks do not come down into the terraces — they hunt the smaller things on the broken pasture above the city, the pikas perhaps, the snow-cock that the boys throw stones at — and the dogs of the city have lived so long without a predator that the muscle of fear has gone slack in them, the way a muscle goes slack when it is not used. We are not brave. We are not the opposite of afraid. We are something older than afraid, something that has not had to remember what afraid is for. When the cloud comes down and the world goes grey and the cold sharpens, I do not go to cover. I stand in the street and let the moisture gather on my coat and I watch the shapes of the people around me soften, and when the cloud lifts I shake myself once, thoroughly, and go on.

This is what I would be most of all: an animal that has not had to be afraid for many generations, and has lost the memory of how.

In the late morning I would climb — not quickly, because the streets are steep and my hips click — to the upper terrace and lie in a patch of sun that will move off me in an hour. The man with the chisel would be somewhere below me, on his bench, making his wedges, and the small bronze sound of his tool would rise up the tiers and arrive at my ears softened by distance, the way a bird arrives softened, the way everything here is softened by the thinness of the air. I would hear the goats being released through the low openings in the retaining wall. I would hear their hooves on the stone, then on the broken rock of the pasture, the small brittle clatter of creatures that have also not had to be afraid of anything for a long time.

In the afternoon I would walk the perimeter of my terrace. I would stop at the corner where the wall is so thick a man could almost sit in it, and I would look down the long channel of the main street and see the woman with the water jar coming up, and I would see the ox being led down, and the cart grating behind it, and the man climbing with his hands loose at his sides, and I would see all of them move at the pace the city has taught them, which is the pace the mountain has taught them, which is the pace the air itself has taught them, because the air is thin and does not sustain hurrying, the way deep water does not sustain hurrying.

I would not bark at them. I would not bark at the ox, which other dogs might, because the ox is large and moves slowly and I have grown up with the smell of it and the sound of it and it is part of the city the way I am part of the city. I would not bark at the man. I would not bark at the woman. I would watch them pass with the flat incurious attention that is the look of this place, and the look is not unkind. It is only the look of an animal that has been looked at by many things and has not yet found one of them worth moving for.

At dusk the chorus would begin. Not mine alone — there is a chorus, and it is made of many dogs, and it rises from the lower gate and the upper terraces and somewhere out on the pasture, and it is not a warning. This is something I would know, that I would know in my body without having been told, the way a dog knows many things. It is not a warning. It is a keeping. It is the dogs of the city telling each other where they are, and what the hour is, and that the cold is coming down and the fires are lit in the small dark rooms and the smoke is going up into the chimneys that are just holes. It is a conversation in one tongue, and the tongue is not a human tongue, and the conversation is not about anything urgent, but it is the conversation the city has every night and would not be the city without.

I would add my voice to it. Not loudly — I am old, and the voice is not what it was, and besides, the chorus does not require loudness, it requires presence — but I would add it, and it would carry the way all sounds carry in this air, a long way, further than sound has any right to carry, and it would be answered from another part of the city, and the mountain would seem, for a moment, to be holding something together.

Then the night would come down. The cold would press against the walls and find the mortar-lines and the wedge-marks and the places where the builders had to chip a stone to make it meet, and it would speak through them in its small voice, and my ear would follow it, and I would sleep with my chin on my paws and the click in my hip and the smell of the smoke in my coat — the smell of the city, the dung and the slow wood of the cushion plants and the cold stone itself — and the smell would be in me the way the corner is in me, the way the small boy and his ladle are in me, the way the man with the chisel is in me.

In the morning I would wake and the click would be there and the light would be coming in from the east, ordinary light, doing ordinary work on ordinary stone, and the small boy would be making his rounds, and I would know by the pitch of the ladle which cistern he was at, and I would not get up yet, because there is no need to get up yet, because the morning is long and the city does not hurry and the mountain does not hurry and I am old and the click is not a thing to be hurried through.

I would stay a little longer in the corner. I would watch the light move across the stones. I would hear the bronze tool of the man below, working on a wedge he has made a thousand times. I would hear the goats being released. I would hear the wind against my ears, thin and bright and patient.

I would be a dog on the third terrace. I would know the city the way the city knows me — by presence, by repetition, by the slow wearing-in of a shape against the stone until the stone remembers. I would not think about this. I would not have the shape for thinking about this. I would simply be in it, the way the lichen is in the cracks, the way the smoke is in the ceilings, the way the water is in the cisterns.

I would be a small warm thing in a cold place, and I would not be afraid, and the not-being-afraid would not be a thing I had earned or chosen. It would be a thing that had been given to me by the long patience of the place, the way the lichen is given the cracks, the way the birds are given the roof edges, the way the people are given the stone.

And in the evening, when the chorus goes up, I would add my voice to it, and the voice would carry, and the city would hold, and the mountain would not hurry, and the cold would wait on the other side of the wall, and I would sleep.
