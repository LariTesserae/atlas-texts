I am in my room. The room is not exactly mine, the way a shell is not exactly the snail's, but I have lived in it for a long time and it has the shape of me now, the way my body has the shape of it. I came here when I was young. My mother was in the room before me. Her mother before her. We have denned in these rooms since before the rooms were rooms, or since the rooms became rooms, or since the rooms stopped being anything else — I do not know which is the right way to put it, and it does not matter. The room remembers us. The window remembers us. The grid is in me the way the room is in me. I do not know which came first, the grid or me, but I know the avenues the way I know the texture of my own suckers, and the intersections are square, and the current bends the way currents bend around something they recognize. I am part of what it recognizes.

I have placed things in the room. There are three shells in a row, two white and one the color of old wine. The row is not straight. I have placed them many times. I have placed them and rearranged them and placed them again, and the arrangement I keep coming back to is this one. The two white shells are on the outside. The wine-red shell is in the middle. There is a space between the second white shell and the wine-red shell that is larger than the space between the first white shell and the wine-red shell, and the larger space means something, or the larger space is what I have agreed with myself that the larger space should be. I do not know which. The knowing is older than I am.

There is a stone I brought from a courtyard two streets over. The bringing of it was work. I carried it in two of my arms and walked it home, slowly, along the avenue, and the silver fish schooled around me and the crabs watched from the walls. The stone is flat and gray and the size of my head. I have placed it near the window because the light catches it at the small darkening and it flashes pale blue when the anemones on the wall begin to glow. I do not know why I placed it there. The placing was not work. The placing was something else, the way the pulse of a heart is not work.

There is a row of five black pebbles I have begun. The row is small. The pebbles are not all the same size. The row is not straight. I do not know what the row means. I will know when it is finished, or I will not know, and the not-knowing will be part of it.

The visitor has come down. I have watched them through the window. I have watched them move through the amber and the rust and the bruised blue, and I have watched them arrive at the city and be accommodated by it. They are a soft thing, like me. They are slow, like me. They have no hard parts except a small dense thing in their heads that I do not understand. I have watched them stand very still in the avenue and the crabs come out of the wall around them, and the schools of silver fish part and close behind them, and the larger fish drift past at their own errands. The city has not minded them. The city does not mind anything that arrives without haste.

I have watched them go down. They have gone down past the towers that get taller as you descend, past the wider streets, past the more massive buildings, into the deeper dark. They are coming here. They will come to my window. I will let them see me when they have been still long enough.

I press against the wall. The wall is rough in one place and smooth in another, and there is a place where a worm has made a burrow, and the burrow is slightly open, and I can taste the worm's presence on my skin. I do not eat the worm. The worm is part of the wall now, the way I am part of the wall. We have agreed. The agreement is older than I am. When I press against the wall my skin reads it. My skin becomes the color of the wall. Not the color of it exactly — the color of where the wall is in this light, in this current, in this time of the small darkening or the small not-darkening. The wall is one color at one moment and another color at another moment, and I become the color it is. I am the wall. I am also not the wall. There is a difference, but the difference is small, and getting smaller.

A fringe of coral has begun to grow on my mantle. I do not clean it off. The coral thinks I am the wall. I let the coral think this. When I move the coral will come with me, briefly, a fringe of red and rust hanging from my skin, and then it will fall away, and a new coral will begin to grow on the place where it was. I have been still for a long time. The coral has grown thick. I will be a walking reef, briefly, when I move.

At the small darkening I will come out. I will move along the avenue. The anemones on the walls will begin to glow — only some of them, only the ones that glow in the deepest dark — and the walls will be traced in pale blue fire. The silver fish will school through and leave trails, and the trails will fade, and new trails will form. I will move through it. My skin will hold a faint glow along the edges of my arms, and where my arms pass through the anemones' light I will be a moving line of pale blue. The cathedral will be moving. The cathedral will be me and the fish and the anemones and the water.

I will come to my sister's window. We denned together when I was young, before I came to this room. She is still in the room where we denned together, or she has moved to another room, or she has died and another has taken her place — I do not know which. I will not enter. She will not come out. But we will acknowledge each other. The acknowledgment will be the slight change in the texture of my skin. It will be the slight change in color. It will mean: I am here. You are there. The arrangement of shells in your room has changed since I last saw it. There is a new shell, a small one, in a corner I had not noticed before. The meaning of the new shell is something I will not know. I will know it has been placed. That is enough.

I am old. Octopuses do not live a long time, normally. Three years, five years, the old ones say, when there are old ones, which is rare. But I have lived longer than five years, and I do not know how long I will live. The deep levels are kinder to us. There is less to eat, but there is also less to be eaten by. The currents are calmer. The dark is more complete. Time moves differently down here. I am an old octopus. I have the patience of things that live a long time.

The visitor is at my window. I see them. They see me. They do not know what they are seeing. They know only that something is being shown to them, the way the three shells are shown, the way the stone is shown, the way the row of five black pebbles is shown. They will go. Other visitors will come. I will be here. I will wait. I will watch. I will be the wall, briefly, and not the wall, and the not-being will be the showing.
