I am old, and I read the color.

This is what I am, or what I have become, or what the basin has made of me over the years, and the three things are not different. I am the old woman of the second tier on the leeward wall, in the house with the small niche above the door where I keep, on a hook, a scrap of cloth I wove when I was young and that I take down now and then and look at and do not use. My children call me Mother. My grandchildren, the few of them, call me Grandmother. The neighbors call me by a name that means, roughly, *the one who reads*. I have been called this so long that I do not remember what I was called before.

I am not sure I had a name before.

---

It is morning. The wind is steady. I am sitting in my interior chamber, the one at the back of the house, the one with the small high window that faces out across the basin, and I am watching the color.

The color moves on the floor of the basin. It comes from the forest. The forest throws it. The wind moves through the lattice of the forest and the forest throws prismatic smears across the white salt, and the smears move, and the color moves. Right now it is a green. A particular green. I have been watching it for a long time and I know what it means.

Green in the morning means the wind is from the north, gently, and the upper air is clear, and the day will be bright but bearable, and the children can go down to the alleys and gather.

I read this and I nod to myself. I have nodded to myself this way ten thousand mornings.

---

I have lived in this house for fifty years. Before me, my mother lived in it. Before her, her mother. The house is layered, like all the houses, like the town itself, and underneath my floor there is another floor, and underneath that another, and the deepest layer I have ever seen with my own eyes was when I was a child and a section of the salt block in the back chamber crumbled and we had to repair it, and beneath the crumbled block we found a floor of older salt, and in that floor a round stone that no one remembered setting, and we put the new floor over it and never spoke of it again.

The basin is a sink. Anything that drifts in tends to stay. The mineral dust accumulates. The crystalline fiber accumulates. The salt crust thickens. The town has been built on accumulated depth, and underneath the town there are older towns. I have seen a fragment of one, only once, when I was young and the wind took a corner off the wall at the far end of the basin and exposed, for a few hours before the drifts covered it again, a doorway into nothing. A doorway cut into a wall that was not the wall of any house anyone knew. It had the same kind of niche above it as my house has. It had the same kind of proportions. It was made of the same salt block. Whoever had cut that doorway had known what they were doing. Whoever had lived in that house had wrapped themselves in pale cloth, or in something like it. Whoever had lived in that house had read the color, perhaps, or had been read to by someone who did.

The wind covered it again in a day. The drifts are patient. The basin does not forget, but the basin also does not show you what it remembers unless it chooses.

---

I was born here. I have never been outside the basin. I have never been to the rim. I have heard, in stories, that the country beyond the rim has water in it, real water, and plants that are not crystal, and animals that are not the moth or the lizard, and I have tried to picture it and I cannot. The country beyond the rim is a place I have no image of. It is the place where the people who left went, and they did not come back, and we do not speak of them. I have imagined them sometimes. I have imagined them walking out of the defile and into a green that is not the color-thrown green of the forest, into a wet that is not the mineral water of the deep crust, into a quiet that is not the quiet of the interior chambers but a quieter quiet, the quiet of a place that does not sing. I cannot hold the image for long. It slides out of my head like the water slides out of the cups we set in the niches. The basin does not want us to picture the outside. The basin has fitted us so thoroughly that the outside has become a kind of blind spot. We have eyes only for what is here.

---

My eyes are narrow.

Everyone's eyes are narrow here. The lid is heavy. The lashes are thickened. The brow is low. The face is mostly a covered thing, two small dark openings in it that look out, and the openings are barely wider than the lashes are long. Wide eyes would gather more of the light that already lies in slabs across the salt, and the light is too much, and so the eyes have closed themselves, over generations, down to a slit. I do not remember when I last opened my eyes wide. I cannot open them wide. The muscles will not do it. The face has forgotten how.

This is fitting. The basin made us, and we fit.

---

The wind is from the north this morning, gently. The green is good. I will tell my daughter, when she comes in from the alley, that the children can go down to gather. She will mouth *yes, Mother* at me, the lips moving but no sound coming out, and she will go back out into the wind and mouth it to the other women, and the children will be sent down to the salt with their small baskets.

The children know how to gather. They are taught early. They know which crystal to take and which to leave. They know that the largest columns, the ones as thick as a thigh, the ones that have been ringing for a century, are not to be touched. They know that the youngest growth, the fibrous mats pressed flat against the crust, are also not to be touched, because they are the future of the forest. They know that what is to be taken is the middle, the sheared-off stalks, the fallen columns, the things the basin has already given up. They are taught this. They are taught it by their mothers and by their grandmothers and by me, in the interior chambers, in the bearable hours, in the bearable quiet.

They are taught, also, to be quiet outside. They are taught not to speak in the alleys. They are taught to lean into the wind and to move along the alley and to come back in. They learn this very young. The wind is not something they have to learn to fear. The wind is something they have always known. The wind is the first thing. Before they had words, they had the wind on their faces, in the alleys, on the short walks between doorways.

---

The sound is the first thing for everyone who comes here.

I have heard the wind described by people who came once and left, and they always describe the sound first. They say it is like a thousand struck glasses. They say it is like the inside of a chapel being slowly destroyed. They say it is like the wind through reeds, except the reeds are made of something harder. They are right about all of these things, but they are wrong to describe the sound as if it were one thing. The sound is many things. The sound is the wind in the defile, which is the funnel, which is the roar. The sound is the forest, which is the singing, which is the upper register that does not let you rest inside your own head. The sound is the basin itself, which is the sounding box, which holds everything and sends it back.

And the sound is also the small sounds.

The lizard, when it comes out at dawn and runs along the salt in short darting runs, makes a small clicking against the crust. The moth, when it flutters in the early morning, makes a small papery sound, the wings brushing the air. The fallen crystal, when it lands, makes a small bright ring, like a glass tapped. The children, when they go down to gather, make the small sounds of their feet on the salt, which is a dry and gritty sound, and the small sounds of their baskets, which is a wooden sound, and the small sounds of their breathing, which is a sound like anyone else's breathing except that it is always edged with the sound of the air itself.

These small sounds are in me. I hear them even in the interior chambers, even in the dim. They are the way I know what is happening outside. I know when the children have gone down to gather because I hear the small sounds. I know when the moth has come in through the high window because I hear the small papery flutter. I know when the lizard is out because I hear, faintly, the clicking. The basin has taught me to hear these things. The basin has fitted me to its own sounding.

---

I am not many things. I am not a person who does many things.

I read the color. I sit in the interior chamber and I watch the color move across the floor. I have been doing this for fifty years. Before me, my mother did it. Before her, her mother. The skill is not taught, exactly. It is given, by the basin, to certain people, and the people who have it are quietly respected, are asked for their opinion when a decision depends on the weather. I am one of them. I have been asked, many times, whether the wind will shift, whether the shutters should go up, whether the children should be kept below. I have been right more often than I have been wrong. I do not know how I am right. I do not know what I am reading when I read the color. I only know that the color moves a certain way and my body knows what it means.

The body knows. This is the basin way of knowing. You do not learn it with your mind. You learn it with your skin, with your narrowed eyes, with your thickened lashes, with the small openings in the covering of your face. You learn it by being here long enough that the basin has settled into you, and you have settled into it, and the boundary is thin. I cannot tell anymore where I end and the basin begins. I do not think this is a flaw. I think this is the fit. I think this is what it means to be a person of this place.

---

The broth is made on the days when the moth can be spared.

The moth is pale and drinks at the basin air. It is not clear what it eats. We have theories. Perhaps the dust. Perhaps the mineral spore that the forest sheds. The moth is quiet. It flutters in the early morning and the late afternoon, when the wind is lower, and it comes into the houses sometimes, through the high windows. We do not chase it. The moth is a small companion. The moth is one of the few soft things in the basin.

When there are many, we take a few. Not many. A few. The broth is thin and it is not really food. It is a gesture. It is the way we acknowledge the moth. It is the way we stay in the right relation to the moth, the way the offerings in the niches are the way we stay in the right relation to the basin itself. We take what is given. We do not take more than is given. We are not people who take more than is given.

I do not drink much. I am not a person who drinks much. The mineral water from the deep crust, the liquid pressed from the crystal fiber, the broth from the moth when the moth can be spared. These are what we drink. They are not much. The basin does not support much. We are people who persist on small amounts, the way the crystal persists on light, the way the lizard persists on almost nothing, the way the basin itself persists on the dust that drifts in from the rim.

I am not hungry most of the time. I am not thirsty most of the time. I am small in this way. I have been made small in this way. The basin has fitted me to its own smallness.

---

The offerings are made at certain hours.

I make them in the late afternoon, when the wind has shifted and the color is moving in a different way across the floor, and the interior chamber of my house is filled with a slow shifting light. I go to the niche above the door. The niche is small. It holds a piece of crystal, perfectly faceted, that I found one year on the floor after a high wind, and I set it there and I have not moved it. Beside it, on a small shelf, I set a cup of the mineral water. I can ill afford it, but I set it there anyway, because the basin expects it, or I expect it of myself, and the difference is not much. Beside the cup, I hang a scrap of cloth. The cloth is woven very fine. It is a piece I wove when I was young, and it is the cloth I keep on the hook and take down now and then and look at and do not use.

The offering is the cup and the cloth. The crystal is not an offering, exactly. The crystal is a marker. The crystal says: this niche is for offerings. The crystal is the way the niche knows itself.

I make the offering and I stand for a moment in the late afternoon light and I do not speak. I do not pray. I do not worship. Worship is too active, too much speaking, too much gathering. I acknowledge. I stand in the right relation. I leave the cup and the cloth and I go back into the interior chamber and I watch the color move.

I am not sure what would happen if I did not make the offering. I am not sure I want to find out. The basin is patient. The basin does not forget. I do not want to be the one who tested it.

---

The bones are underneath.

I have lived my whole life above the bones. I have walked on floors that were once other floors. I have slept in chambers whose ceilings were once the floors of upper chambers, whose walls were once the outer walls of older houses, whose lintels were once beams in other rooms. I have done this without thinking about it. The basin layers itself over itself, and the people layer themselves over the older people, and the salt becomes deeper, and the bones go down into the salt, and the salt becomes the bones.

Even bone, in this place, becomes salt. I will become salt. My bones will become salt. My streets will be under other streets. My children, if they have children, will walk on floors that were once my floors, and the floors will be salt, and the salt will be the same salt that was there before the first town.

I am not afraid of this. I am not unafraid, exactly. I am not sure there is a word for what I am. I am fitted to it, the way I am fitted to the wind, the way I am fitted to the color, the way I am fitted to the sound. The fitting is not happiness. The fitting is not contentment. The fitting is just the way the place and the person are, and the person does not question the place, and the place does not question the person, and the two of them go on.

---

The forest is not us.

The forest is the basin. The forest was here before us and will be here after. The forest is what makes the basin what it is. The forest throws the color. The forest sings the song. The forest grows in faceted columns, in lattices, in fibrous mats pressed flat against the crust. The forest is not a plant. The forest has geometry. The forest has age. The oldest columns are as thick as a thigh and have been standing for a century, ringing in the wind like bells.

We work the forest. We take the sheared stalks and the fallen columns and we carry them up the wall in baskets and on backs and in small wheeled carts. We cut some into salt-block and build them into the houses. We shred some, comb some, spin some, weave some into the cloth that wraps us. We leave some where they lie, because the basin is a sink and to remove everything would be to unmake the place.

We are not separate from the forest, exactly. The cloth we wear is the fiber of the forest. The houses we live in are the salt-block of the crust that the forest sits on. The dust we breathe is the dust of the forest's shed skin. We are dressed in the basin. We are built of the basin. We are, in a sense, a kind of weather of the basin, a slow process that moves through the crystal the way the wind moves through it, leaving traces, taking pieces, and going on.

The forest is not aware of us. I do not think the forest is aware of anything. The forest photosynthesizes without water, or with so little water that none is ever seen. The forest takes the mineral vapor and the trace moisture that the wind brings from distant ranges and it does the rest with light. The forest is a slow life. The forest grows a finger-length in a season. The forest does not know that we read its color. The forest does not know that we wrap ourselves in its fiber. The forest does not know that we are small in it and that the smallness is not a flaw but a way of fitting.

The forest is the basin. The basin is the forest. The wind is the air between them. The people are the weather.

---

The lizard comes out at dawn.

I have watched the lizard. I have watched it many times, from my small high window, in the brief hour when the wind is still and the basin is quiet, before the day begins. The lizard is small and pale and lives in the cracks of the wall. It comes out at dawn, when the wind is briefly still, and it moves along the salt in short darting runs, and it eats something — small insects, perhaps, or the dust, or the moth. It is fast. It does not stay out long. It is rarely seen.

I have watched the lizard and I have felt something for it that I do not have a word for. The lizard is one of the few animals that moves in the day, on the floor, in the light. The lizard does what we do not do. The lizard goes out into the open. The lizard moves in the brightness. The lizard is a sign. The lizard is a kind of courage, or a kind of necessity, or a kind of luck. I am glad of it. I am glad that something in this basin has learned to live in the open, because we have not, and the lizard has, and the lizard is a small proof that the open is livable, even here, even in the light, even on the white floor.

The lizard does not know I am watching. The lizard does not know anything. The lizard is a small pale darting thing that comes out at dawn and eats something and goes back into the cracks. The lizard is the basin, in its way, the way the forest is the basin, the way the moth is the basin, the way we are the basin. The lizard is fitted to its own small place in the place. The lizard does not need me to name it. The lizard is what it is.

---

The moth came in this morning.

It came in through the high window while I was watching the green. It fluttered in the interior chamber, papery and pale, and it settled on the wall near the small niche, and it stayed there for a while. I did not chase it. The moth is a companion. The moth is one of the few soft things in the basin. The moth is quiet. The moth sat on the wall and I watched it and the color moved across the floor and the moth did not move and the color moved and I did not speak.

The moth, eventually, fluttered back out through the high window. It went back into the wind and the wind took it and I did not see it again.

The moth is a small thing. The moth is not important. The moth is the kind of thing you do not speak of, the kind of thing that is just there, in the basin, in the day, in the hours. The moth is part of the fitting.

---

I am the old woman who reads the color. I am the mother and the grandmother. I am the one who makes the offering in the late afternoon. I am the one who sits in the interior chamber and watches the color move across the floor.

I am old. I am pale. I am wrapped in the fiber of the place. My eyes are narrow. My lashes are thickened. My brow is low. My face is mostly a covered thing with two small dark openings in it that look out, and the openings are barely wider than the lashes are long.

I will become salt. My bones will become salt. My streets will be under other streets.

I am not afraid. I am fitted.

The wind is from the north this morning, gently, and the color is a good green, and the children can go down to gather.

I nod to myself. I have nodded to myself this way ten thousand mornings.

The basin sings.
