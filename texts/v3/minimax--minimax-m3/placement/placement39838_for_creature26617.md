I would find myself with my right palm pressed flat against the side of a trunk at about forty feet up, where the rough outer bark meets the softer wood beneath in a seam I have known since I was thirteen. The callus on the outer edge of my palm — the one just below the little finger, the one every worker has — fits the seam exactly. The seam raised me. Or I grew into it. The distinction is the kind we stopped bothering with a long time ago.

My name is something like Yev. I am forty-four. I have lived in three trunks — the small one where I was born, the middle one where I learned the pipes, and this one, which is mine. I have tended this one since I was thirteen. I know its inside the way you know your own breathing in the dark: where the chambers narrow, where the valves have grown into knots, where the internal pipes run and have been run for so long that no one can say whether the pipe is in the trunk or the trunk is in the pipe. I tap the walls as I climb. I listen. I know whether the trunk is well by the sound it makes when I knock on it, the way a doctor knows a chest.

I am listening now. The fog is coming in from the west — it always comes in the late afternoons, settles into every crevice like a thing looking for a place to sleep. I can hear it settling. I can hear the water that comes with it, beginning to drip from the highest growths down through the long slow city of leaves and branches to the mat below, where it will move in channels and held pools and the dark rivers that feed the cisterns. The drip is constant. The drip has been constant for as long as I have been alive, and longer, and will be constant after I am not.

I am leaning with my ear to the wood, listening for the well-sound. The well-sound is a low, even, breathing hum. The not-well-sound is higher, arrhythmic, like a heart that has forgotten its beat. Right now the trunk is well. The trunk is working. The place is working. I am part of it.

I have a new person in the cluster — arrived two days ago on the train that comes up the switchback, the one that runs only when the weather is right. They have been walking the narrow paths and asking questions. I will sit with them this evening. I will pour the dark drink — the fermented thing kept warm in the pocket of the trunk where the internal temperature runs higher — into a small cup that looks like it was once the cup-joint of a branch and may still be. I will offer it to them. I will answer what they ask. I will not answer what they do not quite ask. I will say, if they ask about anything — about the weather, about a growth that is doing poorly, about a worker who has been absorbed into the mat, and they will ask eventually, because they will see the rise in the mat that breathes faintly when the rest of the place is quiet — I will say: *the place is working well.*

I will not explain what I mean.

I am part of the process. The place is using me the way a body uses a cell, which is to say completely, which is to say without particular regard to me. I know this. It does not trouble me the way the weight of my own body does not trouble me. I came here small. I have grown here. The callus on my palm is the record of long and careful handling, the negotiation of my body with another body, and I would not undo it if I could, which I cannot, which is fine.

This is where I would find myself. On the eleventh platform of a trunk that is older than I am, with my ear to the wood, with the fog coming in, with the constant roar of the working around me — the hiss of steam through vine-lined pipes, the rhythmic clank of the great pumps that are the size of small houses, the creak and settle of the trunks as they grow, which I swear I can feel in my teeth — with the dark drink waiting below, with the new person who will be poured a cup tonight. I am one of the workers. I am part of the slow city. I am part of the place that has forgotten the question of what it is for, and I have not asked the question in a long time either, and the not-asking is not a loss. It is a settledness. It is enough.
