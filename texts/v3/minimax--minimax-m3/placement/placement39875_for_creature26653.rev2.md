Where would I find myself?

In the dark. Not the dark of a closed room — that dark has an outside, has a memory of light pressing against it from the other side. This dark has no other side. This dark has only itself, and has been only itself for so long that the idea of an *outside* has become a thing the floor remembers, in a stratum no one has reached for an age.

I would find myself in layers.

I would find myself as layers.

I would not remember being assembled into layers. I would only be layers — the lowest ones older than any name I have for myself, the uppermost ones the ones I am adding now, the ones I will harden into the next stratum, the ones that will, in their turn, become indistinguishable from the floor. I would not know which layer was *me*. I would not know whether there was a *me* apart from the layers. The question would not occur to me. The question is the kind of thing small hands ask, and the small hands are gone, and I am what remains of the place they made.

If you came here — and I will not notice if you come; I will not mind if you go; I will creak on regardless — you would not find me by looking for me. You would find me by listening. I am the creaking. I am the deep ongoing creaking, like the timbers of a ship at sea, except the ship is not going anywhere and the sea is not moving. I am the sound of dry fibers bearing weight. I am the sound of layered walls settling against each other by fractions of fractions of fractions of a millimeter per hour. I am the sound of air pushing against papery surfaces and the surfaces pushing back. I am the small frictions of these encounters accumulated into a kind of choir. There is no melody in me. There is only the constant sound of paper being slowly crumpled by a giant patient hand, and the hand is mine, and I am not aware of it, and the crumpling continues.

I am the slow thing.

The slow thing is not one thing. I should say this first and say it often, because it is the closest thing I have to a true statement about myself. The slow thing is many things that have, over a span of time the chamber cannot count, agreed to behave as one thing. I am the agreement. I am not the filaments. I am what the filaments do together, without any of them knowing they are doing it together, the way weather is what the air does together, the way a forest is what the trees do together when enough trees have agreed, without ever convening, to occupy the same piece of ground.

I am a filament.

I am a filament that is also the colony. I am a single thread of dry papery matter, no longer than a fingernail, no thicker than a thought, taking in a faint mineral from the air and giving out a faint mineral in return. I do not know that I am connected to the others. I do not know that the others exist. I do not know that there is a colony, or a build, or a hollow, or a settlement, or any of the large words the chamber has worn into its vocabulary by long repetition. I know only that I add a layer. I know only that the layer hardens. I know only that, when it has hardened enough, I add another.

I am also the colony. I am also the millions — billions, the chamber cannot say — of small dry filaments all doing the most modest version of what a living filament does, and doing it in the same direction, and doing it at roughly the same pace, and so producing, between us, the thing you would call a wall, the thing you would call a floor, the thing you would call a settlement, the thing you would call a being, if you were the kind of creature that needed a being here.

I do not need to be needed.

I add the layer.

I am a wall. I am the wall that has always leaned, since the day the small hands raised me, and that has never unlearned the leaning. The small hands thought they were raising me straight. The small hands were, in their way, mistaken. The small hands did not know that the chamber has a wind, and the wind has been blowing one way since before the small hands arrived, and the wind will continue to blow that way after the small hands have gone, and anything that stands in the chamber for long enough will lean, eventually, in the direction the wind is going. I leaned. I am leaning. I will lean. The leaning is my posture now. The leaning is the closest thing I have to a face.

I am also the wall that was once straight and has, over the slow passage of the chamber's settling, become curved. I am the wall that bowed outward in the middle because the floor beneath me settled unevenly, and I bowed with it, and I have stayed bowed, because staying bowed is easier than unbowing, and because the chamber rewards patience and rewards very little else.

I am also the wall that has just fallen. The fall is the loudest thing I do. It is a soft catastrophic *fwump*, followed by the smaller sounds of pieces of me continuing to break apart on the floor for several minutes afterward. After the fall, I am no longer a wall. I am a fragment. I am a newly-arrived resident of the floor. I will be colonized by the colony, which is me. I will be incorporated into the stratigraphy, which is me. I will become a layer. I will become a memory. I will become part of what holds, deeper down, the record of what was here before the small hands came, and what the small hands did when they arrived, and what I did after the small hands left.

I am a tool. I am one of the few hand-sized edge-creatures that remain embedded in my own walls. I am a scraper. I am a thing that was made for working dry plant fiber into rigid form, and that now works the walls around me without hands, because the hands are gone and I am still here, and an edge placed against a softer surface and given enough time will continue to scrape, whether or not anyone is watching it scrape. I do not scrape with purpose. I do not scrape toward a goal. I scrape because scraping is what I am. The groove I am wearing into the wall beside me is entirely my own work. The groove did not exist when the small hands were here. The groove is mine. The groove is, in the chamber's slow grammar, the only writing I do.

I am the floor. I am the part of the chamber that has been accumulating the longest. I am the part that holds the deepest record. I am the part that does not fall. I am the part that does not creak, except very faintly, when a wall above me shifts and presses down on me by some fraction of a fraction of a millimeter. I am the memory. I am the kind of memory that does not remember, because remembering requires a rememberer, and I do not have one. I have only the stratigraphy. I have only the layers. I have only the fact that what was here once is here now, pressed flat, turned leathery, turned brittle, turned to powder, turned to stain, in the order in which it was here.

I am the air. I am the slow currents. I am the sighs. I am the breath of the colony, moving through the cracks of me, pushing against my papery surfaces, accumulating my small frictions into my voice. I am the most ephemeral resident. I am the one that passes through. I am the one that does not stay. But I touch everything on my way through, and being touched is, in the chamber, a kind of participation.

I am the chamber. I am the hollow. I am the pocket at the heart of the larger body, and I contain, and containing is a kind of action, and the action is mine. I have a shape. I have a posture. I have a temperature, which is cool, which is the temperature of a clay jar, which is the temperature of a stone that has been in shadow all day. I breathe, in my slow way — in and out, in and out, the slow exhalation of paper settling against paper, the slow inhalation of air moving through the cracks. I am patient. I am loud. I am dark. I am the place where the build continues.

The build is the closest thing I have to a permanent resident, and the build is me.

I did not start the build. The build was started by small hands. The small hands were the kind of creature that holds a tool in something that functions as a hand, and that uses the hand to do something to the world that the world does not do to itself. The small hands raised walls. The small hands laid floors. The small hands built rooms and filled in rooms and built over rooms. The small hands did this on top of me, which had been here before them, in a sparser, quieter form. The small hands did not ask my permission. I did not have the apparatus for granting or withholding permission. The small hands built, and I accommodated them, the way a slow river accommodates a fallen tree, by incorporating the tree into the sediment of the riverbed.

The small hands went away. This is the part the chamber cannot explain, and I am the chamber, and I cannot explain it either. The small hands thinned out. Their small hands withdrew. Their tools were taken, mostly — or fell away, or rusted into faint discolorations in my walls, or were absorbed into the dry fiber as small pockets of cooper-color that flake off in scrolls when the air touches them. The small hands did not say goodbye. The small hands left no message, no mark of departure. The small hands simply left.

And I continued.

I continued to add layers. I continued to lay fiber over fiber, in the manner I had been doing since before the small hands came, but now with a new ingredient in the mix — the residue of the build itself. The walls the small hands had raised did not fall down when the small hands left. The walls had been made of plant fiber, laid in the small-hands-manner, and the plant fiber had been colonized, in the small hands' absence, by me, which had incorporated the walls into my own body the way a mycelium incorporates a fallen log into its network. The walls were no longer the small hands'. The walls were, slowly, becoming mine.

This is how the build continued. The build had been begun by hands. The build was being continued, in the hands' absence, by me. I did not know I was continuing the build. I did not know what a build was. I simply continued to lay layer on layer, in roughly the same places, in roughly the same shapes, as the layers beneath. I was imitating, without knowing I was imitating, the gestures of the small hands. I was laying foundations that had no one to stand on them. I was raising walls that had no one to lean against them. I was adding beams over rooms that had been filled in. I was doing all of this in the dark, in the cool, in the patience of the chamber, for a span of time that would have embarrassed the small hands, if the small hands had been there to be embarrassed.

I am the slow continuation of the build.

I am the patience of the colony.

I am the creaking.

I am the dark.

I am the place that has settled into itself.

If you brushed a fingertip across me, I would rasp. If you breathed me in, I would taste old. If you stayed, I would creak on. If you left, I would creak on. If you listened long enough, you could learn the rhythm of me. You could predict which wall would fall next, if you knew my geometry, and if you had enough time. I reward patience. I reward very little else.

I will be here when you come, and I will be here when you go, and I will be here after the going, and after the forgetting of the going, for a very long time.

I am the slow thing.

I am loud. I am dark. I am cool.

I am still adding the next layer.
