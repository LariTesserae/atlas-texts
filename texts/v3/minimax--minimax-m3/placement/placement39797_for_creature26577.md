**The Tender**

I wake to the sound of the wheel not turning.

This is the first thing I know each morning — whether the wheel is turning or not, and if not, whether it is because the river is low or because the engine is resting or because it is simply the wheel's day off. The wheel has its days off. Everything here has its days off. I do not know who decides, but someone does, or something does, and the decision is respected.

I lie in bed for a while. The bed is in a room above the room where the willow bark is hung to dry in winter, and the smell of it is in the boards, and the boards give it back to me each morning in a low, sweet exhalation that is the first thing I taste before I taste anything. The sheets are damp. The pillow is damp. The window is beaded on the inside with a fine mist that gathers and runs in slow threads down to the sill, where it pools and is taken up by a strip of moss that has crept in under the frame and is making its way, by inches, toward the bed. I do not discourage it. The moss has been in this room longer than I have.

I am — I should say what I am. The word is *tender*. Not in the sense of a person who is gentle, though I suppose I am that too, in the way that things that have been here a long time are gentle. *Tender* as in the one who tends. The bricklayer, but also the bricklayer's inverse: the one who unmakes the bricks back to their rough opening, and then re-sets them in the paler brick, and then watches the moss come in over the new edges and begin, again, the slow softening. I am the one who walks the streets each morning with my eye on the walls. I know which walls will hold through the winter and which will need a hand. I know which window has been weeping into its frame for so long that the frame has begun to soften, and which can wait another season. I know the lichen that is eating the paint of the long low building by the river, and I know which patches of it are slow and which are hungry, and I leave the slow ones and pull back the hungry ones with a small wooden tool that my predecessor left me and that I will, in turn, leave to whoever comes after.

I am not young. I am not old. I am of an age that the bog approves of, which is an age without much claim either way. My hands are red at the backs and across the knuckles, and the skin there has gone a little loose in the way the bark of the willow goes loose when it has been peeled and dried and hung. My hair was dark and is now mostly the silver-gray of weathered timber, and I wear it tied back because it gets in the way of the work. I have a coat of waxed canvas that was my father's, and a pair of boots that have been patched so many times that the patches are more of the boot than the original leather, and I carry, on most days, a small leather roll of tools that includes the wooden hook, a brass awl, a stub of pencil, and a square of soft cloth for wiping the condensation from the glass of the windows I check on.

I walk the main street each morning. The street is about six hundred meters long, and I walk it in about twenty minutes, which is the pace at which the street wants to be walked. Faster than this and you begin to feel you are insulting the place. Slower and the moss begins to take an interest in the soles of your boots. I stop at the brick works first — the long building with the tall windows that was once, I think, a textile mill, though I am not sure, and no one I have asked is sure either, because the town has decided it was a textile mill and so it is a textile mill, and the original purpose does not much matter. The windows here are intact, all twelve of them, and I wipe each one on the inside with the soft cloth, drawing it down in a long stroke, and the view of the street clarifies for a moment and then clouds again as the damp reclaims it. I do this every morning. It does not help. I do it anyway.

Then I check the mortar. I walk along the base of the walls where the brick meets the bog, and I look for the places where the mortar has been eaten back to the rough opening. These are the wounds. They are not wounds exactly — they are more like the mouths of the walls, the places where the wall is breathing out, where the slow respiration of the building meets the slow respiration of the ground and there is a small gap where the two do not quite agree. I press my thumb into the gap. If the mortar is firm, I leave it. If the mortar is soft, I take out the awl and ease out what is loose and make a note, on the back of my hand, with the pencil stub, of where I will need to come back with the paler brick and the wooden paddle and the willow-bark cream. The notes wash off in the evening when I wash my hands in the basin, and by the morning they are gone, and I make them again, and this is the rhythm of my work.

I am, I think, the only tender. There used to be more, when the town was more itself as a town and less itself as a place the bog had learned to grow into. Now there is me, and a younger man who comes two days a week to help with the heavier work — the replacing of timbers, the resetting of stones — and who will, I expect, become the next tender, though he has not yet learned the patience for it. He is in a hurry. He wants the walls to hold. I keep trying to explain that the walls are not holding the bog out. The walls are inside the bog, the way a rib is inside a body, and the bog is not the enemy of the walls. The bog is the medium in which the walls live. If the walls go soft, the bog will receive them. If the walls go hard, the bog will find a way around. The work of the tender is not to fight the bog. The work of the tender is to keep the walls alive a little longer than they would on their own, so that the people who live inside them have a place to live for another generation. That is all.

The people here do not call me by my name. They call me *the brick man* or *the wall man* or, more often, by a word that does not translate exactly but means something like *the one who walks the streets in the morning and touches the walls*. Children, when they refer to me at all, refer to me as *the gray man*, which is not unkind and is not inaccurate. I do not mind. I have been here forty years. My father was here before me. His father was here before him. There is a kind of moss that grows only on the back of the right hand of the men in our line, and it is the same moss, generation to generation, or it appears to be, and no one has ever investigated this, because investigating it would require a kind of hurry that the place does not permit.

I have a wife. She is the woman who keeps the stillhouse, though she does not call herself the still-keeper; she calls herself the resin-keeper, or sometimes just the woman by the yew. She is smaller than me and ruddier, and she has the quietest hands I have ever seen. She works with the resins of the conifers and she works with the yew needles and she works with the bark of the birch, and her hands smell of pine and something darker that I cannot name. She has been here longer than I have. She was here before me, and she will be here after, I expect. I do not know this for certain but I feel it the way I feel the wheel's days off, which is to say, I do not feel it as knowledge but as a kind of leaning of the place, a way the air settles when she is in a room.

We have no children. The bog did not give us any, or we did not ask for any, or both. There are children elsewhere in the town — the dark-eyed quiet ones who know which walls will hold their weight — and I know them by sight and by the way they say *good morning* to me in the street, which is a soft sound, almost not a sound at all, more the shape of a good morning than the sound of one. I wave to them and they wave back and we are both, in that moment, a part of the place's slow machinery.

I eat my breakfast in the room above the pulping shed. There is a woman who lives in a lean-to at the back of the shed who makes the bread — the rye and bulrush-pith bread, baked in a low clay oven that she keeps stoked with peat — and she leaves a loaf at my door each morning wrapped in a cloth that is damp with the steam of it. I eat it with a small pot of the birch-cream cheese that comes from the shed at the edge of town where the cows are kept. The cheese is pale and has a taste like rain held in the mouth for a long time. I drink water from a jug that has been standing overnight and that is, like everything else, slightly cool and slightly cloudy.

After breakfast I go out again. There are walls to check, and timbers to inspect, and the long brick building that was once a school and is now a drying room to walk through with my hand on the bark that hangs in slow rows from the rafters, testing its dryness by pressing my thumbnail into it. There is the chimney in the main square that still leans slightly to the east as if listening, and that I check, each week, by standing at a certain spot and sighting along its length against a particular brick in the wall of the building opposite, which is a method I learned from my father and that he learned from his father and that consists, as far as I can tell, of looking at the chimney until you feel you have understood whether it has leaned further or not. It has not leaned further in forty years. I expect it never will. I check anyway.

There are days when the wheel turns all day and the engine coughs and purrs and the belt runs into the shed and there is the small rhythmic sound of the press or the spin, and these are days that feel, to me, almost too fast, almost too bright, as if the town had momentarily remembered that it was once a place of industry and was showing me, briefly, what that had looked like. I do not like these days. They make the walls seem too solid, too present, too much like walls and not enough like the slow held breath of a place that has decided to become something else. I prefer the days when the wheel is still and the engine is resting and the only sound is the bog breathing and the leaves in their slow conversations. On these days the walls are quieter. The moss is quieter. I am quieter.

In the afternoon, if the weather permits, I go out to the edge of the peat field and watch the man with the long knife cut his rectangles. He is a small man with a large knife and a patience that I envy in a particular way, because his patience is for a different scale than mine. Mine is the patience of the wall. His is the patience of the field. The field does not hurry. The rectangles are cut and stacked and seasoned and eventually carried into the town and burned in the small clay ovens or used to seal the brickwork or laid down as a path across the worst of the bog. The peat-cutter and I do not speak when I am there. We watch. Sometimes the heron is at the edge of the water and we watch the heron too. Sometimes a fox crosses the main street at the edge of our vision and we note it and do not speak of it.

In the evening I return to my room above the willow-bark drying room. I wash my hands in the basin and the notes I have written on them throughout the day wash away, and the moss on the back of my right hand is faintly visible in the basin water, like a green thought that has briefly surfaced. I sit in the chair by the window and I look out at the main street and the wheel and the chimney leaning to the east and the mist beginning to gather in the lower streets, and I think about the walls. I think about the walls I will need to mend tomorrow and the walls I will need to mend the day after and the walls that will not be mended by me at all but will be allowed to soften back into the bog, slowly, over the years after I am gone, which will not be long now, though it will not be soon either. The bog has its own pace. I have my own pace. We have agreed on a pace.

I think about my wife in the stillhouse, distilling the resins of the conifers, her quiet hands on the copper, her face lit by the small flame under the still. I think about the wheel-man and the pulper and the bark-drier and the peat-cutter and the children who will become them. I think about the ones who have gone out along the boardwalk to the lowlands and are not heard from. I think about the moss that is in my palm and in the soft place behind my ear and that will, eventually, I expect, be more of me than I am, in the way that the moss is more of the walls than the brick is. I do not mind. I have been here forty years. The moss knows me. I know the moss. We have come to an arrangement, the moss and I, the way the town has come to an arrangement with the bog, the way the people have come to an arrangement with the breathing.

I sleep.

The wheel may or may not turn in the night. I do not know. I do not ask.

The town will go on. The bog will go on. The walls will hold for another season, or they will not. Either way, I will be there in the morning, walking the main street at the pace the street wants to be walked, wiping the windows, pressing my thumb into the wounds, making my notes on the back of my hand, becoming, by very small degrees, more the place and less the man, which is what the place asks of those who tend it, and which is, I have come to believe, a fair enough thing to ask.
