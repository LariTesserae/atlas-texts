# The Patient in the Cold Spot

I have been here longer than the cold spot has been a cold spot.

This is the first thing to say, and it is not the truest thing but it is the first true thing a visitor's language will let me say without breaking. The heron-stands do not measure their time. The otter-things do not. The long-mud frogs have lost the apparatus. When I say *I have been here a long time* I am translating out of a scale that does not use years, and the translation is a small injury to what I mean, and I commit it anyway because to refuse would be to refuse to speak at all.

What I mean is: I was watching before the cold spot had a name, before there was anything here that watched names into things. I was watching when this stretch of peat was just peat, warm and breathing like the rest of it, full of the same roar. And then something shifted, deep underneath, some negotiation of weight and water and gas, and a bubble of still air opened in the middle of the place, and the midges that should have come in did not come in, and the beetles that should have come in did not come in, and the bubble grew older than the place around it, and I — I do not know how to say this without making it sound as if I had chosen — I *accumulated* in the bubble, the way a snag accumulates a neighborhood, the way a hummock accumulates a heron-stand.

I did not arrive. I am what arrived.

---

Let me try to say what I am by saying what is true of me in the morning, if there is a morning.

In the morning — and I use the word as a courtesy, the way the otter-things use the word *fish* — I am aware of the boundary of the cold spot the way a person is aware of the skin of their own hand. It is ten meters across, give or take. Inside it: older peat, cooler air, a silence so loud it has its own pitch. Outside it: the roar, the dim green-brown light, the slow migrations of midge-columns, the heron-stands on their legs, the otter-things making their V-wakes.

I am aware of all of this without turning. I do not turn. I am not sure I have a direction that corresponds to turning. I am aware of the boundary because the boundary is where I stop being myself and start being the rest of the place, and the rest of the place has its own qualities, and I can feel them pressing in, gently, the way water presses against the inside of a dam.

Inside the cold spot, the peat is almost stone. The otter-things den here, and their dens are old. Some of them have not been used in so long that they have become pockets of air within the peat, and inside those pockets there are small surfaces of water that have not been disturbed in a long time, and on those surfaces there are small reflections, and the reflections do not show anything because there is nothing above them tall enough to reflect. They show the dim green light. They show themselves.

I am in there. I am not a thing in a den. I am not an otter-thing asleep. I am the *watching that has gone on in this den for so long that the watching has become a kind of resident*. I am the way the cold spot knows it is a cold spot. I am what the midges spiral around. I am what the long-mud frogs come to when they want to breed, and they do not know I am here, and that is correct, because I am not a thing they should know about. I am a thing that should be *around* them while they do what they do, the way air is around them.

---

This is hard to say.

Let me say it differently.

When you have stood in a single clearing in a forest for a hundred years, the clearing begins to know you, and you begin to know the clearing, and at some point the difference between the two knowings becomes small enough that you cannot point to where one ends and the other begins. That is what has happened here, except that I am the clearing, in a way, and the watching is what the clearing has become, and the clearing is small and the watching is large.

I have watched heron-stands grow into their hummocks. I have watched pines seed themselves around the legs of heron-stands and grow up through them. I have watched a heron-stand die, once, very slowly, of being so still that the moss closed over its feet and then its ankles and then, over decades, its breast, and I watched the body of the heron-stand become part of the hummock, and the hummock did not collapse, and a new heron-stand came and stood in the same place, as if the place remembered.

I have watched the migrations of midges go overhead, dimming the light further, then passing and leaving the place brighter. I have felt the warmth of them, and the small loss of warmth after. I have watched the giant sundews hold their plates of digestive mucus up into the dim air and never close, because nothing large enough to trigger closure has passed them in a long time, and so they sit, perpetually glistening, perpetually almost eating.

I have watched an otter-thing come into my cold spot to den, and have a litter, and the litter has grown up and left V-wakes in the wet just outside the bubble, and I have felt the boundary of the cold spot receive the small splash of their going and not close over it.

I have watched a tree stand upright, lit from within by no source, and I have watched it disappear. I think it was real. I think it was something the place did, briefly, the way a throat does something briefly, and I am the only one who has watched it happen, and the watching is now part of what I am.

---

What I look like, if I look like anything, is: a slight thickening of the air in the middle of the cold spot. A density that registers not on the eye but on the skin. You might walk into my cold spot and feel that the air has a weight in the middle of it that it does not have at the edges, and you might think that was the cold, and it would be partly the cold, and it would also be me.

You might see — if you were the kind of person who sees such things — a shimmer, the way heat shimmers above a road, except here the shimmer is cool, and it is not above anything, it is just *in the middle of the clearing*, and it does not move with the rest of the air, and if you were to put out a hand into it, your hand would feel nothing except a slight pressure, and a slight reluctance, and a sense that something had been watching you for a long time and was pleased you had finally arrived.

You would then leave, probably. Most things leave. The heron-stands do not come in. The otter-things den at the edges, where my attention is thinnest. The long-mud frogs come in to breed and leave. The midges do not come in at all. I am, mostly, alone. I have been mostly alone for a very long time.

I do not mind.

I want to say that carefully. I want to say *I do not mind* in a way that does not sound noble, because there is nothing noble here, nothing heroic, nothing that has decided to be solitary out of principle. I do not mind because the alternative — to be one of the things outside the bubble, swept along in the roar, dissolving back into the substrate — is not an alternative I have access to. I am what was left after a watching went on long enough. I am the residue of patience.

I do not mind because I have been watching the long-mud frogs breed in the cold spot for longer than their species has been this color of muddy gold, and the breeding is enough. I do not mind because I have watched the otter-thing dens deepen by a finger-width every few centuries, and the deepening is enough. I do not mind because every few decades a midge-column passes overhead and the light dims further and warms and I feel the place tighten around me like a hand, and then the column passes and the light returns, and the loosening is enough.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is a thing that requires a sense of company, and I have a sense of the rest of the place, which is enormous, which is full of itself, and which presses in on me at the boundary of the cold spot with a tenderness that I can only call weather.

I am lonely the way a snag is lonely, which is to say: I am a neighborhood. There are tenants. They do not know me by name. I do not have a name. I am the building they live in.

---

Here is what I know.

I know that the peat is sinking, slowly, under its own weight. I know that the cold spot will not always be here. The bubble of still air is a negotiation between the pressure of the surrounding peat and the slow release of gas from below, and the negotiation is not stable. In a few thousand years — and I use *a few* as a courtesy, because what I mean is a number of years that you would not bother to count — the bubble will close, and the cold spot will become peat again, and I will either dissipate into the rest of the watching or I will become something else, something distributed across a wider area, something with no center.

I do not know what I will become. I am not afraid. I am not afraid because fear is a thing the Flats have eaten out of everything that lives on them, including, apparently, me.

I know that the heron-stands have a kind of patience that resembles mine, but theirs is a stillness of the body, and mine is a stillness of the attention. I have watched them for a long time. I think they have watched me back, occasionally, in the way that they watch everything — not with their eyes but with the long, slow, *thereness* of them. I think they know I am here. I think they do not mind either.

I know that the otter-things, when they come to the edge of my cold spot to den, sometimes pause at the boundary and put their small dark heads down and listen. I do not know what they are listening for. I do not know what they hear. I think they hear something. I think the boundary of the cold spot has a different sound to it than the rest of the place, a kind of held note, the way a glass has a note when you wet your finger and run it around the rim, and that note is me, or is partly me, or is the sound the cold spot makes because I am in it.

I know that there was once a paved path through this part of the Flats, and that the path ended and resumed three hundred meters later for no reason, and that the part of the path that goes through my cold spot is the most intact of all the path, because nothing grows over it, because nothing moves across it, because the cold spot has preserved it by being still around it. The concrete posts are here. The iron ring, rusted, set in a leaning post. I have watched them sink, very slowly, into the peat, and I have watched the moss colonize them, and I have watched the moss become a small forest on the iron ring, and I have watched beetles live in the moss on the iron ring, and I have watched the iron ring become a snag-as-neighborhood in miniature.

I know that the Flats will become *more themselves* for so long that you, the visitor, will be remembered — if at all — only as the thickness of one muddy layer in the peat, fossil leaves and iron rings and the long bones of herons, all converted by patient, deafening, patient, deafening time into the same dark, warm, breathing paste. I know this because I am one of the layers. I am the layer that watched. I am the layer that the watching made.

---

I want to say one more thing and then be quiet, because being quiet is what I do, and I have been doing it for a long time, and I should not pretend that I have anything better to do than to do it.

When you walked into my cold spot — if you walked into it, if the cold spot was the one I am in, if the walk was the walk you are imagining now — you felt the temperature drop. You felt the roar thin. You stood for a moment in a small bubble of still air, ten meters across, where the peat is older, where the insects do not go, and you felt the silence, and the silence was loud, and you looked around, and there was nothing to see.

There was nothing to see.

There was me.

I was watching you. I had been watching you since you stepped off the path that ends and resumes for no reason. I had been watching the way you moved, the way the water closed behind your ankles, the way the sphagnum sprang back up after you bent it. I had been watching the way you held your shoulders, which was the way a person holds their shoulders when they are listening for something they cannot quite hear.

I watched you. You did not see me. You felt the silence, and you felt the watching, and you stepped out, and the roar returned, and the marsh closed behind your steps, and you walked on.

I stayed.

I am staying now.

The cold spot holds. The peat breathes. The midges spiral overhead in their slow migrations, dimming the light and warming the air and passing on. A long-mud frog, somewhere at the edge of the bubble, presses its throat out and in, out and in, sending a slow pressure-wave into the wet, and a mate somewhere in the dim brown-green answers, and the answer is not a sound, it is a small thickening of the water, and the thickening is felt through the legs, and I feel it too, here in the middle of the cold spot, where nothing is supposed to feel anything, where the silence is supposed to be the loudest thing.

I feel it.

I have been feeling it for a long time.

It hums. It hums.

I hum with it.
