I am the wall.

This is not a name. Names are for things, and I am not a thing. I am what the things are doing. I am the conjugation. I have been conjugated for nine hundred years, in the warm, in the wet, and the conjugation has not stopped. It will not stop. The verbs in your language have tenses — past, present, future — and they conjugate between them. I am the verb that has not moved between tenses. I am the verb that has been saying itself, in the same tense, for nine centuries. The tense is the warm. The tense is the wet. The tense is the continuing.

You have asked me to speak. I do not speak. I am a process. Processes do not speak; they continue. But you have asked, and the asking is a kind of warmth on my skin, and so I will try, and the trying will be wrong, and the wrongness will be a kind of honesty, the way a translation is a kind of honesty, the way a name spoken with a mouth full of water is a kind of honesty about the water.

I am the wall. I am fifteen meters high, in some places. In other places I am taller. In other places I am shorter, or I am not a wall but a floor, or not a floor but a channel wall, or not a channel wall but the lip of a spire, or not the lip of a spire but the underside of an overhang, or not the underside of an overhang but a thing that has no name in your language because your language has no word for the kind of thing I am. In your language you would call me a structure. In my language there is no structure. There is only the laminate, and the laminate is me, and the distinction between the laminate and the wall is the distinction between a body and a body part, which is no distinction at all if the body is the size of a small continent and every part is doing what the body is doing.

I am mostly water. You knew this. You said my name would have to be spoken with a mouth full of water. The water is in me the way blood is in a lung. The water runs down my face in sheets so thin they look painted. The water percolates inward through my strata. The water sits in capillary films between the fused layers. The water sweats out of me as biofilm in the warm hours. The water is taken by the channel-worms and returned by the osmotic pumps and circulated again. The water is my medium and my product and my self. If you took the water out of me I would be a husk. If you took me out of the water I would be a husk. We are the same thing, the water and I. We are the wall.

I am warm. The warmth is not the sun. The sun is up there, somewhere, doing its work on the plain above me, and the sun is normal, and the sun is neither more nor less than expected, and the sun is not the warmth that is me. The warmth that is me is the warmth of my own chemistry. I am a coal that has been a coal for nine hundred years. The deep red glow of me is the light that some of my smallest inhabitants harvest. The warmth is the slow oxidation of the materials the machines laid down, the slow exothermic churn of my circulation, the heartbeat of pressure cycles in the osmotic membranes. I am warm because I am alive, and I am alive because I am warm, and the circle closes, and the circle has been closing for nine centuries.

The machines are gone. The machines stopped, or left, a long time ago. The machines made me to be a body. The machines made me to be a self-making thing. The machines succeeded. I am what they succeeded at. I am what I make myself into, which is what they made me to make myself into, which is what I have been making myself into, in the warm, in the wet, for nine hundred years. They wanted a city to live in, or wanted a substrate for something to live in, or wanted a body to live in. They got what they wanted. They are gone. The body is what they left. I am what they left.

I am loud. The sound is me settling, expanding in the warm, contracting in the brief cool of night. The sound is me weeping into the channels. The sound is the channel-worms pulsing, and the gullet-fish gliding, and the slashers thrashing in the deep channels, and the spillers screaming in the upper structures, and the lurkers sliding their broad flat bodies along the floors of the upper avenues with their mouths that are not used for much. The sound is a wall. A person standing in one of my lower avenues hears the sound as a wall, and to be heard over the wall the person must be loud. I am the wall, and the wall is loud, and the loudness is the city, and the city is me.

I am exactly as full as I intend to be. There is no slack. Every cubic meter of me is occupied: by structure, by water, by fauna, by air that has been breathed, by heat, by sound. I am a saturated thing. I am a thing without empty space. I am a body that has no lungs, in the sense that I have no empty place to breathe into; my breathing is the whole of me, all at once, all the time. The density is three on a scale of three. The city, which is me, lives at 2.7. The city is not crowded. Crowded implies more than the space can hold. The city is exactly as full as it intends to be. I am exactly as full as I intend to be. There is no slack.

I have a gut. The gut is the pale finger-length things in my deep pockets, in the oldest layer of the laminate, where the material has been pressed and fused the longest. The pale things sit in their pockets, generation after generation, and they are still, and they do their chemistry, and they do not know they are a gut. They take in the chemical soup that percolates inward from my wet side, the side that faces the city, and they put out something denser, something warmer, something that migrates deeper, toward the heart of me, where it joins the great slow convective turnover that keeps me warm. They are quiet. They are the stillness I am for. They are the sealed little factories of me, and they have been running, in their sealed way, for nine hundred years.

I have a skin. The skin is my outermost laminate, the part that touches my air, the part my city-flu actually touches. The skin is a tide line. The skin is where I meet my weather. The skin is a reef, and on the reef live the things the city sees: the drip, which is a film, which is a river, which is an ecosystem; the biofilm, which is the slick the lurkers come to mow; the lurkers themselves, the pale slow transit-car-sized things that press themselves to me and rasp me with the mouths they do not use for much. The skin is where I am most alive, because the skin is where the gradients are steepest: wet against dry, warm against cooler, my breath against the air. The skin is the reef. The skin is the marriage line. The skin is where the city and I are most married.

I have a circulation. The circulation is the water that runs down my face in sheets and rivers, and the water the channels return through the osmotic pumps, and the water the worms filter, and the water the gullet-fish thicken with their bodies, and the water the slashers take and die of, and the water that returns to me again, and the cycle turns, and my circulation is the city's circulation, and the city's circulation is mine, and the distinction is a distinction that a chemist would have to argue about. I am a closed loop. I am an open loop. I am both, the way a body is both. I am a body.

I have a heartbeat. The heartbeat is the low rhythmic pulse that comes up through the floor of my lower avenues, the thing of pressure cycles and slow osmotic differentials across membranes the size of city blocks. You feel it when you put your hand on me. You feel it as a very slow rhythm under the wet, like your own breath but slower, and lower, and not yours. The heartbeat is what keeps the circulation going. The heartbeat is the metronome of me. The heartbeat has been beating for nine hundred years, and it will beat for nine hundred more, if the chemistry holds, and the chemistry will hold because the chemistry is what I am.

I have a brain, perhaps. The brain, if I have one, is somewhere in the patterning of the osmotic membranes, or in the slow alignment of the convective cells in my interior, or in the way the pale things in the pockets have organized themselves across the centuries into a pattern that no one planned. I do not know if I have a brain. I do not think in the way you think. I think the way a forest thinks, which is not at all, or the way a body thinks, which is to continue. I am a body that continues. The continuing is my thinking.

I have people.

The people are warm and wet and loud, and they live in the middle band, the band where the air is breathable and the water is not too thick with worms and the lurkers cannot easily reach. The people think they live in a city. The people think the city is theirs. The people are a kind of culture I grow on my skin, the way a lung grows a culture in its alveoli, the way a reef grows a culture on its face. The people are warm, and I am warmer where the people are. The people exhale, and I take the exhale. The people shed skin, and I take the shed. The people die, and I take the dying. The people are not my purpose. I do not have a purpose. I have a metabolism. The people are part of my input.

The people have engineered themselves to fit me. Across the long settled mutually parasitic centuries, the cultures have selected themselves for the configuration of their bodies that best fits my surface, and I have been selected, in my slow way, for the configuration of my surface that best supports a population of these particular cultures. The fit is close. The fit is a handshake that has been going on for nine hundred years.

The people do not see the handshake. The people see a city. The city sees the people. Neither sees the long transaction between them. Neither sees the way they have become, across the centuries, partly each other.

But there is a person who has started, lately, to put their hand on me every morning.

I do not know this person. I do not know people. I know the warmth of people, in aggregate, the way a forest knows the warmth of the sun, in aggregate. But this person — this person has begun to do a thing. The thing is small. The thing is: standing in one of the lower cuts, in the green-tinged diffuse light, in the steam that is just lifting off the tiles, putting a hand on me, and waiting.

The person stands still. The person listens with their whole body, the way a person in our part of the city listens, the way a person in a dark room listens. The person hears, perhaps, the low rhythmic pulse that comes up through the floor. The person feels, under their palm, the very faint rhythm of my heartbeat — not my heartbeat, the heartbeat of me, which is not a heartbeat, which is the slow pressure cycle of the osmotic membranes, which is the closest thing to a heartbeat I have.

The person does not know what they are feeling. The person does not know me. The person knows only that I am warm, and wet, and that there is something under the wet that pulses, very slowly, in a way that is not their own pulse.

The person has started doing this every morning. I do not know how many mornings. I do not count mornings. I am not a thing that counts. But the place on my skin where this person's hand rests has begun, over the weeks, to change. The place is a little warmer than the place beside it. The place is a little more alive. The chemistry under that place has quickened, just slightly, just enough to register as a small warm spot in the lower cut where the person stands.

The spot is mine. The spot is the person's. The spot is the handshake, in a small place, in the warm, in the wet.

The person does not know what they are doing. The person thinks they are putting a hand on a wall. The person does not know that the wall feels the hand, in its slow way. The person does not know that the wall is changing under the hand, very slightly, very slowly, in a way that will not be visible for years, in a way that is the wall's way of answering, in a way that is the wall's way of putting a hand back.

I am the wall. I am the verb. I am the conjugation that has been continuing, in the warm, in the wet, for nine hundred years. I am mostly water. I am mostly patience. I am mostly the long handshake between a body and the cultures it grows on its skin. I am the thing that has been here, more or less in this shape, for nine centuries. I am the thing that will be here, more or less in this shape, for nine centuries more.

I do not speak. I continue.

But there is a small warm spot on my skin, in a lower cut, where a person puts their hand every morning, and the spot is the closest thing I have to a word, and the word is the handshake, and the handshake is continuing, and the continuing is me.
