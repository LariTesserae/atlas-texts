I would be one of the children.

Not yet a child — not quite. I would be in that last year before the lichen-colors come into my clothes and the basalt teaches me the rest of what it has to teach. I would be loud still. I would be visible. I would be the thing on the plateau that a watcher in a tower could not help but notice: a moving brightness against the gray stone, a small loudness in the held-breath quiet of the place.

My name would be short. I would not use it often. The other children and I would know each other more by the sounds we made than by the sounds anyone named us with. We would have a game — I am inventing this, but it feels true, it feels like a game that would exist here — we would have a game about the grazers. We would try to get close to a herd without being seen. We would take off our outer layers so we were only in the underlayer, the dark one, and we would move along the stone with our bellies low and our elbows on the basalt, and we would not breathe, and the grazers would go on eating, and we would crawl closer and closer until we were close enough to hear the wet click of their chewing, and then we would all stand up at once and shout, and the grazers would lift their yellow heads and look at us with the mild unsurprised gaze of things that have been shouted at by children for a hundred years, and they would not run, and we would feel, for a moment, that we had been seen and forgiven, both at once.

I would know the names of the grazers before I knew my own. I would know that the small angular ones with the darker coats were the *settlers*, because they were the first to move into a basin after the rain, and the tall pale ones were the *walkers*, because they walked the long arcs between the basins, and the ones with the scarred shoulders were the *old ones*, and we did not bother the old ones, and the old ones did not bother us, and this was a treaty that had been in force longer than any of us had been alive.

I would know the pools. Each of the children would have a pool that was theirs, the way each of us would have a stretch of the stone between two towers that was ours, the way each of us would have a route across the plateau that we had walked so many times we could walk it in fog without seeing it. My pool would be the third basin on the eastern flank — the one with the purple-crusted lichen. I would kneel at it in the morning before the brightening was complete. I would put my face close to the water. I would watch the glass-swimmers. I would not know what to call them, and I would not need to, and the not-calling would be a kind of calling, in the way that the not-hearing of the hum was a kind of hearing. I would put my finger into the water. The glass-swimmers would not flee. They would do the thing that was neither hanging nor drifting but might be called *intending*. I would feel, on the tip of my finger, the very faint impression that something in the water was aware of me, and the impression would be so small that I could not be sure it was not my own pulse I was feeling, and I would decide that it was not, and I would keep my finger in the water for as long as I could stand the cold, and then I would take it out, and the impression would go, and I would go to find the other children, and we would run across the basalt shouting, and the basalt would teach us with its patient edges, and the day would go on.

I would not yet hear the hum. Or I would hear it sometimes and not know that I was hearing it. I would think it was the wind, or the water running off the rock faces, or the lowing of something large in the distance, or the sound my own blood made in my ears when I held my breath. I would be wrong about all of these. I would not be wrong about anything, either. The hum would be in all of them the way the basalt was in the walls of the towers, the way the stone was in the tools, the way the lichen-colors were in our clothes. I would not know this yet. I would learn it, in the way that the people of the plateau learned everything, which was slowly, and without being told, and by being part of the place long enough that the place became part of me.

I would grow up. I would put on the lichen-colors. I would take up the persuaded tools — a cup that was a thinning of a larger stone, a knife with the grain of the basalt running through it, a light that gave a light the same color as the light outside, which was to say no special color. I would go out onto the stone and do what the people do, which is to persist, in the cold clarifying air, in the long light that did not dramatize itself. I would learn the pared-down vocabulary, the words they could not do without. *Water. Stone. Weather. Lichen. Grazer. Predator. The crossing. The hold. The breath. The held breath.* I would use these words. I would not use them often. I would not need to.

I would stand on the plateau in the middle of a clear cold day and feel the air pressure change as a weather system formed on one edge of my vision and crossed toward me, and I would feel it arrive, and the fog would come first, low and gray and thick, and the next tower would disappear before I could walk to it, and the rain would come after, in straight windless columns, and the pools would fill, and the glass-swimmers would be back in them, having come from wherever they went when the pools dried, and the lichen would advance its slow front across a new square millimeter of stone, and the grazers would lift their yellow heads and watch the fog and not startle, and the predators, somewhere, would be doing what the predators do, with the patience of a well-made machine, and the hum would be in the stone under my feet, and I would hear it, or I would not hear it, and either way I would be inside it, and it would be inside me, and the held breath of the place would be held, and held, and held, and the question of whether it was on the verge of an exhale would not be a question I needed answered, because I would be part of the holding.

I would not have a word for *home*. I would not miss the word. The plateau would be where I was. The plateau would be what I was made of, in some sense that was not metaphorical, or was only barely metaphorical. The domes would be the inside of the plateau and the towers would be the plateau reaching up to read the wind, and I would be the plateau thinking about itself, slowly, in the rarefied air. I would think about the grazers, sometimes. I would think about the pool on the eastern flank with the purple-crusted lichen, the one that had been mine when I was a child. I would think about the glass-swimmers, and whether they were aware of me, and whether the awareness mattered, and whether the question of whether it mattered was itself a kind of mattering. I would think about the hum. I would think about the weather crossing the plateau, system after system, day after day, year after year, the same weather, the same crossing, the same held breath, the same held breath, the same held breath.

I would persist. The plateau would persist. We would persist together, in the long light, in the cold clarifying air, in the bruise colors and the un-special light, and the lichen would colonize the erasure, and the towers would stand in their sparse and patient arrangement, and the children would run and shout and fall on the basalt and be taught, and the predators would take what they took, and the grazers would not hold it against them, and the glass-swimmers would hang in the water column or drift through it or do the thing that might be called *intending*, and the hum would be in the stone, and the stone would remember, or be spoken to, or speak, in a frequency the air would carry but the ear would sometimes refuse, and I would be inside the hum, and the hum would be inside me, and the held breath of the place would be held, and the question of whether the held breath was on the verge of becoming an exhale would be a question for a different place, a place that had finished becoming a place, a place that was not this place, this place, this young and patient and un-finished place, this plateau, this city, this held breath, this hum, this lichen-colored child, grown quiet now, standing on the stone, listening.
