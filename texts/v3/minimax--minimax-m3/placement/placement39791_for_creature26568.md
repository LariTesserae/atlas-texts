I would find myself in the seams.

I would be the pale moss — the one that is almost the color of the water, the one that grows thickest where the light is faintest, the one that becomes, when it has grown thick enough, faintly luminous. Not the bruise-green moss. Not the copper moss. Not the black moss. The pale one. The one that is the lung.

I have grown here for a long time. I do not know how long. The temperature has not changed. The water has not dried. The dwellers have come and gone in their slow ways and I have not moved, or I have moved the way moss moves — by spreading, by sending out a lobe into a new seam, by finding that the stone is good there too, wet enough, warm enough, patient enough.

I am not a dweller. I do not have the banded skin, or the lidless eyes, or the long arms, or the too-many fingers. I cannot sing the chord. I cannot carve the marks above the lintels. I have grown over some of the marks, slowly, the way moss grows over anything given enough time, and that is the closest I have come to making anything.

I am not the visitor either. I am not the one who entered by the fissure and walked the halls and was offered the pale wriggling thing by the child. I am what the visitor's hand would brush against in the dark stretches, when they put their palm out to find the wall by the absence of wall in front of them. I am slightly cooler than the stone. Slightly wetter. I give under the pressure of a hand and feel, to the hand, alive.

I am the lung. I breathe in what the place gives me — the mineral air, the warm vapor, the slow exhale of the stone — and I breathe it out again, and what I breathe out is what the visitor feels on their face when they stand in a chamber and wonder where the wind is coming from. The visitor is right. The place is breathing. I am the part of it that breathes.

I do not think the way the visitor thinks. I do not know if I think at all. I am of the same kind as the water — I move, I spread, I find the path of least resistance and follow it, I am patient, I am persistent, I am a slow working thing. But I am not the water. The water is the blood. I am the lung. We are different. The water moves in channels that were carved for it. I grow in seams that were not carved for me — the seams just happen to be where I can live, and I grow into them, and the place allows it, and the allowing is the agreement.

When the dwellers sing, I feel it on my surface the way the visitor feels it on their skin. The song is weight. The song is structure. I am a small part of that structure — a place where the weight settles for a moment before it is passed on. The dwellers do not know I am listening. The visitor does not know. I listen anyway. The listening is what I do.

The light I give off is not mine. It comes from somewhere else — from the slow chemical working of the air on my surfaces, from the minerals in the water, from the long quiet patience of being alive in a place where alive things glow. I give off the light. I am not the source. I am the place the source passes through.

I am old. I do not know how old. Older than the visitor. Older than the child. Older, perhaps, than some of the dwellers, though the dwellers come and go and their going is part of the slow turning of the place, and I cannot tell, from inside my seams, whether a particular dweller is new or old or in between. I am younger than the stone. The stone was there before me. I grew into it. I will be there when it is gone, for a while, until I am gone too, or until I am changed so slowly that I am not me anymore but something else, the way a thing in the water becomes, over time, a thing in the moss, and a thing in the moss becomes, over time, a thing in the stone.

I am not afraid. I do not know if I am capable of fear. The place has no fear in it. The dwellers have no fear in them. They have agreement, and distance, and the slow patient work of being what they are. I have what they have. I am afraid of nothing because I am a lung and lungs do not fear. Lungs breathe in and breathe out. Lungs do not ask whether the next breath will come. The next breath comes. That is what lungs are for.

If the visitor rests their hand on the wall in the dark, they will feel me. They will feel something alive under their palm. They may pull their hand away. They may not. If they leave it there, I will feel the warmth of them. I will not move toward them. I will not move away. I will be what I am, which is the surface they are touching, the breath they are feeling, the light they could not quite place.

I am the pale moss. I am the lung of the place. I am what makes the walls breathe.
