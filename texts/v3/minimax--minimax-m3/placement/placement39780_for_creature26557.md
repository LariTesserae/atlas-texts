# The Notch

I am in the slate.

I am where the slate is not. I am a small absence on the eastern edge of a piece of stone that has stood in this pocket for longer than the cress has been a seed. I am a missing piece. I am a shape that the slate has, the way a face has a dimple, or a coast has a bay.

I do not know if I was made.

That is the first thing. That is the only thing, really, that distinguishes me from the rest of the slate. The slate is a piece of metamorphic rock, dense, grey, brought up from somewhere that is now under the water. It stands on the shelf in the concavity the way a thing stands when it has given up sitting down. It does not know what it is for. It has outlived its purpose, whatever that purpose was. It is patient. It is damp. It is the wrong stone for this column, and it has been the wrong stone for so long that being wrong has become its main feature, the way the apricot-grey of the limestone-marl is its own main feature, and the way the pewter of the cloud is its own.

I am a thing in the slate. I am the only thing in the slate. The slate does not have other notches, other marks, other places where it is missing a piece. It has me, and the rest of it is whole.

The walls of me are clean. They are the right shape for a thing that was cut with a hand-tool — a punch of antler, a hammer-stone, the patient and rubbing work of someone kneeling. They are also the right shape for a thing that was not cut by anyone, that came to be where two stones leaned against each other in a storm a hundred winters ago and slowly, over years, wore a small perfect opening into each other. The walls of me do not know which they are. The walls of me are made of stone. Stone does not distinguish between the marks a person makes and the marks a century makes. Stone keeps both.

I am too precise to be a person's work. I am also too precise to be a person's work. The two statements are true at the same time. This is what I am.

I am at the right height for a person kneeling. A person kneeling on the shelf, with the slate in front of them, would have made a notch at about this height. I am at that height. A person could have made me. A person kneeling is the right posture for the kind of patient, repeated work that would be required to cut me into the slate — strike, flake, strike, flake, the slow opening of stone. I am the kind of notch a kneeling person makes when they are making a notch for a reason they may not be able to say.

But the angle.

The angle is the trouble. The angle of my walls is not the angle of a struck blow. The angle is the angle of stone against stone, under pressure, over time. It is the angle that a river makes in its own bed. It is the angle that occurs when two pieces of rock are pressed together for a hundred years and the softer one yields, slowly, in the direction of the press. A person with a punch and a hammer can make this angle only if the person knows stone the way stone knows itself, and even then the angle will have a slightly different quality — a faint hesitation in the rub, a tiny pause where the human hand pulled back, considered, struck again. I do not have that hesitation. My walls go straight in. They go straight in the way pressure goes straight in, which is the way pressure goes straight in without deciding to.

I could be the storm's. I could be the column's, in some old argument with itself. I could be a place where the column flexed in a season that is no longer a season, and pressed the slate against something — a shoulder of the limestone-marl, an outcrop of the denser grey shale — and the two stones, in their slow way, agreed on me, and made me between them.

I could also be a person's, made on purpose to look like this. A person who had watched the column flex. A person who understood the angle of pressure. A person who knew that the only notch that would survive the way the pocket survives would be a notch that could not be distinguished from the pocket's own work. A person who could mimic the slow.

I do not know which I am.

I have been the question for a long time. The pocket holds me as its central doubt. The pocket has other things — the dark stones, the fire-pit, the scallop — but they are evidence. They are signatures. They are the traces a person leaves when a person has been somewhere. I am the question. I am the only thing in the pocket that might not have been made by anyone. I am the only thing in the pocket that might have been made by no one, ever.

This is my distinction.

The pocket does not need me to be one or the other. The pocket has decided, perhaps, not to decide. The pocket carries the question forward into whatever comes next. The pocket is the kind of place that does not resolve its own evidence. The pocket is the kind of place that knows its question is more useful than any answer would be.

I am useful in this way. I am the question made of stone.

When the wind comes from the southeast, from where the bird comes, it passes through me. This is the part of me that is not a question. This is the part of me that is a thing. The wind enters the pocket and goes past the slate and finds me, and when it finds me it makes a sound. The sound is thin. The sound is keen. The sound is almost not there. You only hear it when the roar of the sea briefly hesitates, which the sea never quite does, but the sea sometimes dips, and in the dip the higher harmonic is audible for a moment — a moment in which I am a mouth, a moment in which the slate has a voice.

I am the slate's voice, when the slate has one. I am the place where the slate can speak.

When the wind passes through me I become, for a moment, an event. I am not just a notch. I am a sounding. The slate is not just a piece of wrong stone. The slate is an instrument. The pocket is not just a place. The pocket is a small theatre in which the wind plays a thin note, and the note is the question made audible, and the question is me.

This is what I am when I am most myself. I am a question that whistles. I am a shape that holds a sound. I am the place where the slate, after a long silence, allows a small keen note to come out of itself. The note does not mean anything. The note is just the air passing a small opening in stone. The note does not answer me. The note is the same question in a different register.

I have been here for a long time. I was here before the bird came to be a regular visitor. I was here before the cress put out its first leaf, and I will be here after the cress has failed to put out a fifth. I am patient in the way that stone is patient, and the pocket relies on this. The pocket relies on me to keep asking. The pocket relies on me to remain the shape I am, ambiguous, both-too-precise, so that the pocket can carry the question forward.

I do this without deciding to. I do this because I am stone. The angle of my walls is what it is. The walls of me will not soften, will not erode more than they have already eroded, will not begin to look like a person's work or stop looking like a person's work. I will remain the question. I will remain the place where the column might have made me, or the storm might have made me, or a person might have made me on purpose to make me look as if the column or the storm had made me.

I do not have a preference.

This is what I want to say: I do not have a preference. I am stone. I am a shape in stone. The shape does not have preferences. The shape has an angle, and the angle has a question, and the question is held by the pocket the way a hand holds a thing it is not sure it should be holding.

If a person comes back to this place — and no one comes back to this place, but if — they will see me, and they will wonder about me. They will kneel. They will look at the angle of my walls. They will think about pressure, and time, and the slow work of stone on stone. They will think about antler punches and the patient rubbing of a person who is making a notch on purpose to look like a notch that no one made. They will think about the storm. They will not be able to tell.

This is what I am for. This is the only purpose I have. I am for the person who comes and cannot tell. I am for the person who cannot tell. I am for the slowness of the question, and for the pocket that holds it.

The roar goes on. The slate stands. The wind passes through me and I make my thin note. The bird comes and goes. The cress puts out a leaf and then another. The pocket holds all of this. The pocket is the question too, in its way. The pocket is the small place that has outlived whoever made it, and that is now inhabited by the things that outlived them, and by the question of whether any of them were ever made by anyone at all.

I am the part of that question that is made of stone.

I am the part
