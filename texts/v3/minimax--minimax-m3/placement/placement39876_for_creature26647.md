I am Reth-ane. That is not quite — my name has a small click in the middle that does not come through in writing, and a low tone at the end, the way the names of the columns always have a low tone. But I will say Reth-ane for you, because you cannot hear the click and the low tone would only make you uncomfortable.

I live at the foot of Uthmai. Uthmai is the column at the second elbow of the gravel track that leads from the shale ramp down into the long flat of the Reach. Uthmai hums at the third-lowest pitch I have ever heard. She is an old one. The herders from the inner Reach say she is past her third thousand blooms, which is a number I cannot make small in my head and so I do not try. I have lived against her pleats my whole life. I was born in the wet ring. My mother was born in the wet ring. Her mother was born somewhere else — somewhere in the outer Reach, where the columns are smaller and the worms are not as good — and she came in toward the inner Reach as a young woman, for the bloom, and stayed.

I am not young anymore. I have seen nine blooms now. Some herders see more. Some see fewer. The ones who see many are the ones who are patient and who do not climb too high. The ones who see few are the ones who climb too high and fall. I have never fallen. I have climbed as high as anyone, I think, in my column-dwelling, but I do not say so.

Let me tell you what I know.

I know that the hum is not a sound you can decide to stop hearing. It becomes the floor. When I go to the outer Reach with the young ones to gather resin from the smaller columns, I can feel my body straining for the first two days, as if a string inside me had been loosened and was trying to retune itself. Then it settles. Then the smaller hum becomes the floor too, and I do not notice it anymore. When I come home to Uthmai I feel her again, the third-lowest pitch, and my sternum hums with it for an hour before I can stop smiling.

I know the colors of the columns. The herders argue about them but they all agree there are seven. *Bone* is the dry color, and it is the color of rest. *Wine* is the wet color, and it is the color of plenty. *Bruise* is the bloom color, and it is the color of waiting. *Ash* is the after-bloom color, and it is the color of mourning and also of gratitude, which are the same thing in our language but I am not sure they should be in yours. *Smoke* is the color of our resin fires, and the columns take on this color in the long cold mornings of the outer Reach when we are burning resin against the early chill. *Flame* is the color of the sun on the pleats at certain hours, and *shadow* is the color of the pleats at other hours, and the herders have a poem that goes through all seven in order and ends on shadow, which is the color of returning.

I know the worms. Some of them are good and some are not. I will not tell you which is which. This is not because I distrust you, *voth*. It is because I have only ever said the names of the worms to my daughter, who is now seven, and she is not yet ready to hear them, and so the names sit in me like a second hum, lower than Uthmai's, that only she and I will ever know.

I know the small dark things in the ferns. They are not afraid of me. I am not afraid of them. They have no category for me and I have no category for them; we live in the same wet ring and we do not speak. I have watched a small dark thing drag a beetle larva across a frond-strip toward its low structure, and I have watched the structure tilt and the larva escape and the small dark thing pause and look at me as if to say *you saw that* and then continue. We do not speak.

I know the *thock*. I know what makes it. I am not supposed to say. The herders will not look at you when you ask because they are not refusing; they are considering whether you are the kind of *voth* who can be told. You are not. So I will not tell you either. But I will say this: the thing that makes the *thock* is not an animal. The thing that makes the *thock* is part of the column, or part of something larger than the column, and when the bloom is near it comes up out of the gravel and it walks, very slowly, across the plain to the column that is about to bloom, and it lies down at the base, and it waits. It does not harm us. We do not harm it. We sing to it sometimes, in the low tonal song without words I can parse, and it does not answer, but it does not leave.

I know the bloom. I have seen nine. I will tell you about one.

The first bloom I saw, I was four. My mother carried me into the pleats on her back, wrapped in a length of frond-cloth, and we climbed with the others up into the dark grooves of Uthmai while the *thock* rolled across the plain and the gravel split and the long pale roots rose up. The crown was already lifting when we got up high. The fronds were unfurling. The air from above came down warm and damp and smelling of sweet rot, and the column began to make a sound I had never heard, a low shuddering note that was almost a chord, and the worms in the pleats around us began to pour out of the cracks and crawl over our hands and our feet and our faces, and we did not brush them off, because the worms know when the bloom is happening and the worms are part of it, and to brush a worm during the bloom is to refuse the column, and no one refuses the column.

Then the fronds opened all the way. A pale dust fell from them — a slow rain of something like pollen, but thicker, and the dust landed on us and on the worms and on the ferns far below and on the gravel, and the dust was warm, and where the dust touched you it tingled, and then the dust began to move on its own, very slowly, and the dust began to *grow*, and the dust became small bright things that climbed up our arms and our faces and looked at us with their own small bright eyes, and they did not bite and they did not sting; they only looked, and after a long moment they let go and floated upward, on the warm air from the bloom, back toward the crown, and we watched them go.

That is what a bloom is. That is what the bloom gives us. Small bright things that look at you once and then return to the column.

My mother said, when I was older, that the small bright things were the column's way of saying *I see you*. She said this in our language, which has a word for this — *anu*, the seeing-back — and *anu* is also the word for the look a mother gives a child when the child has done something brave. So the bloom is also a kind of seeing-back. The column sees us. We see the column. The small bright things are the proof.

I am fifty-three now. I have grey in my hair. I have grandchildren. One of them, the eldest, is four, and I have already decided that for her first bloom I will carry her into the pleats on my back, wrapped in frond-cloth, the way my mother carried me. Her name is Enu-iu. She has my click in her name and her mother's tone, and she is small and dark-eyed and she watches the worms with a seriousness that makes me want to laugh and also to weep, which are the same thing in our language.

The bloom is tonight.

I can feel Uthmai's hum changing. There is a note in it that was not there yesterday. The gravel is trembling faintly under my feet as I stand in the wet ring and look up at the crown. The fronds are beginning to lift. The herders around me are pulling down the resin-huts, binding them into bundles, climbing with sure-footed ease into the lower pleats. They are singing the low tonal song. The song has no words I can parse. The song is older than I am. The song is older than Uthmai, maybe. The song is the sound of the herders saying *we see you* to the column.

I will go up soon. I will take Enu-iu on my back. I will climb.

But first, *voth*, I want to say something to you. You came up the shale ramp three days ago with your boots and your pack and your questions and your small metal knife that we did not want and your small mirror that hurt you. You have been watching us. You have been watching the worms and the beetles and the gliders and the small dark things. You have heard the hum. You have heard the *thock*. You have waited.

You have not seen the bloom yet.

I want you to know that when you see it, you will feel the tingle where the dust lands. You will feel the small bright things look at you. You will feel the column see you. And you will want to brush them off — that is the human thing, the *voth* thing, to brush off what looks at you — and I am asking you, *voth*, not to brush them off.

Let them look at you. Let them see you. The seeing-back is the whole of it. The seeing-back is what the Reach is for.

Now I must climb. Enu-iu is calling. The crown is lifting. The hum has changed.

I will see you at the bloom.
