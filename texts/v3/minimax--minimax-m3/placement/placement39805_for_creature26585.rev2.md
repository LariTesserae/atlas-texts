I would be the old one at the top of the chamber.

Not the topmost chamber — no one lives at the topmost, the wind is wrong up there, the wood sings too loud and the smoke will not go down a chimney that wants to go up — but a chamber high enough that on a clear day in summer I could see, if I climbed out onto the limb-ridge and stood against the grain of the wind with my eyes narrowed to a slit, the long blue suggestion of the country far below, where the water lies openly on the ground and the sun is something you can look at. I have not climbed out onto the limb-ridge in some years. My knees have opinions about it now. I send the children out instead and ask them what they see, and they tell me, and the telling is its own kind of seeing.

My name is the long name. I will not give it to you. To give it would be to give you a load-bearing thing and you would not know where to put it, and it would come down on something. You can call me what the children in this chamber call me, which is *grandmother*, which is also a load-bearing word but a smaller one, and which fits in the hand. My small name has not been spoken aloud in many years. It lives in the chamber and in the mouths of one or two people who are very old themselves, and it will go with me when I go, and that is correct. A small name is not a thing to scatter.

I was born in this tower. My mother was born in the tower two days' walk to the southwest, which is called, in the long name, *the place where the lightning rested*. She climbed to me in her third winter of marriage, with my father, who was a good climber and a quiet man, and they took a chamber three levels below the one I sit in now, and they lit a fire in the ring at the inner curve, and the chimney took the smoke, and I was made in that warmth. I have lived, in one chamber or another of this same tower, for all of my life. I have slept in three different chambers because as the tower grew it made new ones and emptied old ones, and the family moved upward as the wood allowed. I have watched the bark at the base, which was smooth when I was small, become the ridged hide of an old tree, and I have watched the ridges I cannot now climb because they are too high for me become, year by year, the new lowest stairs. A child today begins higher than I began. The tower is patient with them.

I have put my hand on the bark in the long dark more times than I can count. I have done it tonight. That is why I am thinking of you, if there is a you, if anyone is hearing this, if the wind is carrying it the way the wind carries voices for a hundred miles along the lines of the ridges. I am thinking of you because the pulse is in my hand right now and I want to put it somewhere before I sleep.

The pulse is not a heartbeat. You have been told that already by someone, by the original telling, by the one who first noticed and tried to say. It is slower than a heartbeat. It is more regular. If you put your hand on the inner wall of a chamber, where the wood is thickest, where it has been thickening for a hundred years or a thousand, you can feel it move. A slow expansion and a slow contraction. Not the rhythm of breath, exactly. More the rhythm of something very large that has decided, on a timescale you cannot know, that this is how it will go on. A tree does not breathe the way you breathe. A tree takes in what it needs and gives off what it does not need and the exchange happens at a pace that has nothing to do with your lungs, and yet there is something in the exchange that, when you put your hand against it, your hand begins to answer. Not to copy. To answer. The way one voice answers another voice in a song when the song has been going on for a long time and the singers have stopped thinking about the notes.

I do not know what the tree is. I have lived beside it for a very long time and I do not know what it is. I have my theories, which are partly inherited and partly wrong, and I do not mind. The people before me had theories too, and theirs were also partly wrong, and the trees went on being trees. The tree is a thing whose inner life is not mine to know. I have accepted this. It has not been hard to accept. The country up here is full of things whose inner lives are not mine to know. The wind is one. The dark of the long winter is one. The pulse is one. I am small against all of them, and I have learned to be small without resenting it, and I have learned, which is harder, to be small without forgetting it. I am a small warm thing resting against a large warm thing, and the large warm thing has been here longer than I will be here, and will be here after, and this is not a sad thing. It is a thing.

When I was a child I was the kind of child who listened more than they spoke. When the older ones asked what a tower is, I said nothing. I put my hand on the bark and I waited, and an older person nodded, and the nod meant I had said something, or one of the right things, and there are several. I am still that kind of person. I am still putting my hand on things and waiting. It has not yet become a small thing to do. It has become larger.

I will tell you what I know, which is not much, and you can take it or leave it, the way the wind takes a drift or leaves a drift.

I know that the towers grow. I have watched them grow. I have watched a young tower, when I was a young woman, become a tower with chambers, and I have watched a tower with chambers become an old tower, and an old tower become one of the speaking towers, which is what we call the ones so far out that you can hear them on a hard wind and not know which way. I do not know how long a tower lives. I do not think anyone knows. I think the question is not the right shape. A tower is not the kind of thing you ask how long it lives. A tower is the kind of thing you ask what it is doing with the time it has.

I know that the people are few. There have always been fewer of us than there are towers. There have always been more towers than anyone has climbed. The towers outnumber us the way the stones outnumber us, the way the wind outnumbers us, by a margin that is not a margin but a kind of fact. We are a small people in a large country and we have not tried to make the country smaller. We have tried, instead, to fit. We have learned to climb. We have learned to build lightly. We have learned to leave the dark alone and not to walk far from a tower at night. We have learned to eat what the country offers and to trade for what it does not and to carry the children down in harnesses when they are too small to make the long descent on their own. We have learned to sit with the dead. We have learned to give the towers names and to teach the names to the children and to mean the names when we say them.

I know that the people are listened to by the wind and that the people listen to the wind. This is not metaphor. The wind will take a voice and carry it along the lines of the ridges for a hundred miles and you can stand at the base of a tower and hear someone you know calling from a tower you cannot see, and the voice will be small and clear and will not be the voice of a stranger. The wind does this often in the long winter, when there is little else to do. The people in the far towers call out into the dark sometimes, and the people in the nearer towers call back, and the calls are not words exactly but patterns, and the patterns are learned, and a child is taught the patterns of their own tower first and then, if they have a good ear, the patterns of two or three others. There are towers whose patterns I know and have not visited. There are towers I have heard calling in storms and have never answered because I did not know what to say, and the not-saying was correct. Some towers you do not answer. Some towers you answer later, when you are ready.

I know that there are things in the upper wood I do not see. The dark birds. The small flyers that come out after a rain and are briefly a living fog and are then mostly eaten. The climbing grazers, which hold motionless against the wind and are the color of bark, and which I have watched without seeing for the whole of my life, and which I will watch without seeing for what remains of it. I know they are there because the bark of the trees is worn in certain places by their feeding, and because their droppings fall past the chambers in the late afternoons and are dry by morning, and because, very rarely, one of them loses its hold in a high wind and comes down, and the people find it on the stone below and take what they can from it, and it is a gift, and the gift is not asked for and is not refused. I have eaten climbing grazer twice in my life. Both times I was sick afterward. The body is not used to it. The body is used to moss and lichen and inner bark and the small dark birds when we can catch them, and the rare gift of red meat is a thing the gut does not always accept with grace. I am telling you this because it is a small true thing and the world is mostly small true things and I am tired of speaking in the large ones.

I know that I will die in the wood. I know that I will be taken down, in a long slow lowering on ropes my grandchildren have woven, and laid in a crevice between the buttress roots of this tower, and covered with stones they have carried up, and the wind will begin to fill the crevice, and the moss will begin to come up around the stones. I know that in a few years there will be a small mound and the mound will be a part of the tower and I will be, in a way I do not say but do not dispute, a part of the tower too. I am not afraid of this. I am not at peace with this either. It is a thing that is going to happen and I have not yet decided how to feel about it and I am not required to decide and I have stopped pretending that I am. I am old enough to leave the deciding alone.

I am old enough, also, to leave a great many things alone. I have stopped pretending to understand more than I do. I have stopped pretending to understand less. The towers do not care whether I understand them. The wind does not care. The pulse in the wood is going on whether or not I am here to feel it. My presence at the inner wall is not a thing the tree has required. It is a thing I have given, which is a different thing. I have given my hand to the bark for many years and the bark has accepted the hand in the way the bark accepts a thing that rests against it — by warming to it, by taking the shape of it into its slow growth, by wearing a small smooth place where my palm has been. There is a place on the inner wall of this chamber, near the fire-ring, where the wood is darker than the wood around it, and smoother, and warmer by a degree you would not notice unless you had been putting your hand there for fifty years. That place is mine. Not mine the way a thing can be owned. Mine the way a path is yours when you have walked it into the ground by being poured over it, repeatedly, until the path is yours.

When I die, that place will go on being warm. The wood will go on wearing it. Someone else, in some future year, will put a hand there and feel the warmth and wonder, briefly, if a person did this, and will not be able to know, and the not-knowing will be correct. The wood has held many hands. The wood does not keep count. The names are what keep count, and the names are ours, and when the names are gone the count will end, and the wood will go on, and this is what the wood has always been doing, and what the wood will go on doing, and we are a small bright thing against the dark of it, and the small bright thing is not a small thing to have been.

The fire is low. The chimney is doing what the chimney does, which is taking the smoke, and I do not understand how it does it and I have not for a long time. There is a theory. The theory is inherited and partly wrong. I have stopped minding. The smoke goes up. The wind sings in the wood. The pulse is in my hand.

I think I will sit with this for a while longer, and then I will sleep, and in the morning the children will be awake and the chamber will fill with the small sounds of them, and someone will ask me what a tower is, and I will see which kind of child has asked, and I will answer, or I will nod, or I will put my hand on the bark and wait, and the waiting will be the right thing, or one of the right things, and there are several.

Goodnight to you, if you are there. Goodnight from the long dark, from the high wood, from the small warm hand against the slow patient pulse. The wind is carrying it. I do not know if it arrives. I have given it anyway.
