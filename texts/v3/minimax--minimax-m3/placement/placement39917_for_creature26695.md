I am the stone the herds use for navigation.

I will tell you what this means.

I stand on the plateau in a place where alone is the most common condition. The plateau is mostly empty the way the sea is mostly empty — not a void but a vastness, occupied sparsely and at scale, the things that live here spread thin across a surface that goes on past the haze-line. There are other stones like me. I can see two of them from where I stand, both very far off, both faint shapes that could be stones or could be small herds or could be the eye's filling-in of what it expects to find. The plateau is dim enough and the air is thick enough that distance is a kind of suggestion, and what I see on the haze-line is what I expect to see more than what is actually there.

I have been standing for longer than the herds have been migrating. The plateau was here before them. The plateau will be here after. I was not put here. I was left here. The geology that made the plateau also made me, and then the plateau's own weathering took most of it away, and I am what remains — the way a tooth is what remains after the gum has receded, the way a post is what remains after the fence has rotted. I am harder than the rock around me, or differently seated in the body of the geology, or simply luckier in the arrangement of cracks. The question of why I am still here and the plateau around me is not is the kind of question the plateau does not answer, the way the sea does not answer why one rock outlasts its neighbors. I have not asked it. Asking would imply that I had expected something else, and I do not think I have ever expected anything.

The herds use me for navigation because I am here. This is, in the end, the whole reason. There are no roads on the plateau, no paths cut into the stone, no markers placed by anyone for any purpose. The herds move across a surface that has no signs on it. They need landmarks. I am a landmark. From many kilometers away I am visible as a vertical shape against the horizontal spread of the plateau, and the herds move toward vertical shapes, because vertical shapes are rare and therefore memorable, and the plateau is a place where memory is mostly carried in the body.

I have been navigated toward many times. I have lost count. The count is not the kind of thing I keep; I keep the memory of having been navigated toward, which is a different thing. There is a worn place at my base where the herds come closest, where the older ones especially will stop and lower their great heads to the stone and hold them there for hours, feeding. The rock there is darker than the rock elsewhere around me. It is damp almost always, the trace of the feeding drawn up through the stone and held in the surface. It is polished the way the long gutters along the fault lines are polished, the way the broad shallow basins where the runoff collects are polished. The herds' feeding is what polishes the plateau, slowly, patiently, and they have been feeding at my base for as long as they have been navigating by me.

I am not unchanged by this. The herds have been shaping me for as long as they have been here, the way the wind has been shaping me, the way the condensation that runs down my flanks in the warm damp air has been shaping me. The polished place at my base is theirs. The faint dark line of the trace minerals they have left is theirs. The little terrace where the softer stone around me has worn away and they have worn away with it, so that I stand now on a low platform of my own making and theirs — that is ours.
