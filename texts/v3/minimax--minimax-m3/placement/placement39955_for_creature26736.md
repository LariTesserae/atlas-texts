The basin finds me already waiting.

Not as a thought, but as a settling. As if I had been standing for a long time in some corridor outside of time, and a door I had not seen simply gave way. I am at the worn seat. I have always been at the worn seat. The stone knows the shape of me the way a glove knows the shape of a hand it has held through many winters — the depression where my weight settles, the small smoothed place against my flank where I have leaned for years, the faint darker line where my foot has braced against the same stone through every long afternoon. I do not remember arriving. I remember only the pool, and the listening.

The pool is in front of me. The waterfall pours into it from the ledge above, the same pour it has been making since before the leaning trees were seeds. The water does not splash so much as gather itself — it falls and joins the pool as if it has been expected, as if the pool has been holding a place for each drop until it came. The sound of it is not loud. It is the constant under-sound of the basin, the held breath, the way the place breathes when no one is asking it to be still. I have listened to it for so long that the sound has become a kind of silence — the silence that is inside all sounds, the way the sea is inside a wave.

The basket is at the edge of the grove behind me. I know this without turning. The roots in it are still damp at their cores, though the dirt that clung to them has loosened into a fine dust that has begun to mix with the soil of the floor. The chipped stone rests on its broader face, working edge up, in the grip it was meant to be picked up by. The wooden club lies where it fell from a relaxed hand. I set these things down in the gesture of pause, and the pause extended itself, and I let it, because the basin was teaching me something that the tools could not teach, and I had to learn it sitting still.

I am the person. Or I was. The basin makes it difficult to say which. I came with a body that knew how to dig and to lift and to strike, and the body has done all those things and is tired in the way stone is tired when it has been given to water for a long time — not broken, only shaped. I dug the roots. I think I gathered them over days. I think I set them in the basket because they were part of a meal I had planned, or a medicine, or a small ceremony whose name I no longer carry. The work was real. The dirt under my fingernails was real. The ache in my shoulders after the digging was real. But the work is not what remains. What remains is the sitting.

The pool holds me. I do not mean this in the way a thought holds a mind. I mean that when I look into the water, the water looks back, and what it shows me is not my face but a thing my face has been the surface of. I have seen, sitting here, that the water has a thickness. That light enters it and comes back altered, as if the pool has considered the light and answered it in its own idiom. I have seen the stones at the bottom are rounder than stones have any right to be, polished by something that does not abrade. I have watched the reflection of the sky become sharper than the sky, and I have understood, slowly, that the pool is not a window. It is a kind of mouth. Or a kind of ear. It receives what falls into it and gives back what it has been holding for a long time, and what it has been holding is older than the sky it reflects.

The cliffs curve around me. I do not see them without turning my head, but I feel them the way a person sitting in a cupped hand feels the fingers. They are not rock as rock is elsewhere. They have intention. The water that comes from them — from every joint, every seam, every hidden vein — is their way of being open. They have been giving themselves away in thin trickles for so long that the basin below is the accumulation of all that given water, and they will go on giving long after I have gone. I have thought, sitting here, that the cliffs are the slowest life in the basin, and that they may be the oldest, and that what they are giving may not be water at all but some older substance that has taken the shape of water to move through the world.

The trees lean. All of them, the same direction. Not toward the light — the light comes from above, and the lean is sideways, a long slow bending as if they grew away from something at their backs or toward something at their fronts that they could never quite reach. They lean toward the waterfall. Toward the pool. Toward me. The oldest have grown past the lean and curved back upward, their trunks describing a long S, a posture of having been bent and then righting themselves, the way a person who has been kneeling for a long time slowly stands. I have watched them through the years of my sitting. They are the only ones in the basin who have answered the geometry aloud. They are the basin's oldest sentence, written in wood.

The dusk-runner is in the grass behind me. I have never seen it whole. I have seen only the moving parting of the stems, a soft wake traveling through the green at the dim hour, low and quick, never coming into the open. It has a nest somewhere under the roots of the leaning trees where the ground is dampest. It has lived here longer than I have. It will live here after. It has no name I would recognize. It is a small dark life that the basin keeps the way a pocket keeps a stone — not on purpose, but because the shape of the pocket fits. I have felt it pass behind me in the dusk and have not turned to look. Some things in the basin are not for looking at. They are for knowing they are there.

The insects above the pool do not move. They hold their stations in the still air, dimming and reappearing as their wings catch the filtered light. They are not drinking. They are waiting. I do not know what they are waiting for. Perhaps for something to hatch from the surface of the water. Perhaps for a warmth or a smell or a quality of stillness that draws them and holds them. They are the most visible life in the basin, and they are the least of it. They are the moving skin of a quieter population underneath, the way the ripples on a pool are the skin of the pool. I have watched them through afternoons that have lasted whole seasons. They have not moved. They will not move. The basin does not offer itself to the air. It offers itself to the ground.

The birds pass through. They cross the basin in a long shallow curve and exit the way they entered, without alighting. There is nothing here they want. No open sky, no rising thermals, no insects in the air thick enough to hunt. The basin is not for them. Their absence is part of the basin's fullness, the way the silence between two notes is part of a song. I have watched them pass through the filtered light and disappear over the rim, and I have felt the basin exhale after them, settling back into its own slow rhythm.

I do not know how long I have been here. I came with the tools and the basket and the body's memory of work, and I did the work, and then I stopped working and began to sit, and the sitting became longer than the work, and the years became longer than the sitting, and now I do not remember arriving. I remember only the pool, and the listening. The basin has taught me a thing that the tools could not teach, and the thing is this: that there is a logic here that is older than language and that does not name itself, and that to be in it is to be inside a sentence that has been speaking for a very long time, and that the sentence does not end when I leave it, and that what I heard here could not be taken with me when I went, so it stayed in the worn stone, where it still is, waiting.

I think I will go soon. The basket is being taken back. The moss has crept over the edges of the flat stone and has not yet reached the center but will. The fern beside the basket has unfurled its first frond, still curled at its tip. In a year the clearing will be ground. In ten the basket will be fiber and soil. In a hundred the seat will be stone again, indistinguishable from the rest of the floor, and the body that wore it will be a story the basin tells to itself when it is very still.

I will go. But I will leave the tools in the gesture of pause, set down between one task and the next, in the posture of someone who intends to return. I will leave the hand-axe on its broader face, working edge up, in the grip it was meant to be picked up by. I will leave the wooden club where it fell from a relaxed hand. I will not tip out the roots. I will go the way you leave a room you will return to in an hour, and the hour will become a season, and the season will still be extending itself when I am far from here, and the tools will lie in their pause, and the pause will not close, and the basin will keep the shape of me in the worn stone the way a river keeps the shape of a stone it has flowed around, and the pull that drew me here will still be in the pool, undiminished, waiting for the next one who stops moving long enough to feel it.

The waterfall pours. The pool holds. The cliffs give. The trees lean. The dusk-runner passes through the grass at the dim hour. The insects hold their stations. The light stays true.

I sit.
