The first thing I do is put my hand on the wall.

I don't decide to do it. My body decides, the way a body decides to swallow, or to flinch, or to turn toward warmth. The stone is warmer than my palm, and damp, and when my fingers settle into the shallow depression between two of the hollows, the chord shifts. Just locally. Just in the bank of mouths nearest my hand. A small eddy in the music, a phrase traded from one hollow to the next, and the phrase is *about* me, I realize, though I do not yet know what it says.

I am not afraid. I notice this with a small distant surprise, the way you might notice, halfway across a frozen lake, that you have not yet heard the ice crack. I have been afraid of many things in my life. I have been afraid of the dark, and of strangers, and of the moment in a long illness when the body stops being a body and starts being a problem the body has. I am not afraid of this. The place has not given me a reason to be afraid, and it has not given me a reason to be anything else, and the absence of reasons feels, for the first time in a long time, like a kind of permission.

I sit down on the uppermost terrace, near the entrance, where the stone is still cool enough to feel like a place I have arrived at rather than a place that has accepted me. Below me, the chamber descends in its patient steps toward the basin. The steam rises. The ferns tremble. The small creatures patter. I let my hand stay on the wall.

---

I think I am the one who was meant to listen, though I have not been a good listener in my life.

I was a doctor. I will not say what kind. I will say only that my work was in the body, and the body was the place I knew best, and the body was also, I came to understand in the years I practiced, the place I was least able to hear. Patients spoke. Patients said *I am afraid* and *I am tired* and *I am not myself anymore, and I do not know who I am now.* I listened. I wrote things down. I prescribed. I did not, for most of my career, understand that listening is not the same as waiting to speak. Listening is the long practice of letting the other sound finish before you add your own. I was not good at it. I was good at diagnosis. Diagnosis is a kind of listening, but it is the listening of a person who already knows what they expect to hear, and who is only waiting for the symptoms to arrange themselves into the shape the doctor has prepared for them.

I retired. The body I had lived in began, slowly, to fail. The first year was the worst. The second year was worse in a different way. By the third year I had stopped looking at my own reflection because the reflection had become, in some quiet and total way, a stranger. I had spent my life in bodies, including my own, as a thing to be maintained. I did not know what a body was when it was no longer being maintained. I did not know what a person was.

I traveled, after I stopped practicing. I went to mountains. I do not know why I went to mountains. I think I went because the mountains are the parts of the earth that have been allowed, for a long time, to be unfinished, and I wanted, I think, to be near something that had been allowed to be unfinished too.

---

A pale creature drops from the ceiling.

It falls the length of a man, and at the end of the fall, it catches itself on a lower ledge with one forelimb, and the limb folds out of its pocket-knife rest with a small precise click, and the creature is on the ledge, and I realize that the fall was not a fall. It was a measured descent. The creature knew exactly where it was going to land. It had chosen, on the way down, which stone it would put its hand on.

It moves across the ledge to a patch of moss. It begins, with a slow and careful motion, to feed. Its mouth — I do not see a mouth, but there is somewhere on its pale face where the moss goes in — opens and closes in a rhythm that matches the bass note of the chord. I watch it eat. I feel that I am watching something I have not seen before in my life, and that I will not see again, and that the watching is enough.

I was a doctor for thirty-one years. In those years I saw perhaps ten thousand bodies fail. I delivered perhaps four hundred children into the world, and I closed the eyes of perhaps three hundred of the people I had failed to keep. I do not know, now, whether I was more present in the births or in the deaths. I think I was less present in both than I imagined at the time. I think the body I inhabited in those years was a body that was very good at certain things and very bad at others, and the things it was bad at were the things the body could not do without language, and language was the thing I had been given the most of, and I did not, in those years, understand that a thing given in abundance is the thing most likely to be in the way.

I do not know why I am thinking of this. I think the chamber has made it possible. I think the chamber has made a small and patient space, and the space has let the things I was not able to think about while I was living them float up to the surface, the way a drowned thing floats up after a long time at the bottom of a still water.

---

A small crustacean-thing walks across my foot.

I do not move. The creature pauses on the leather of my shoe, considers, then steps off and continues along the terrace. It is carrying a fragment of moss. It is going somewhere. I do not know where, and the not-knowing is, again, a kind of permission. I have been, for most of my life, a person who needed to know. I needed to know what was wrong. I needed to know the name of the wrong. I needed to know the prognosis, the path forward, the thing to be done. The chamber does not need to know. The chamber continues. The chamber has continued for a very long time without needing to know, and I am beginning to think that the not-needing-to-know is closer to the truth of things than the needing-to-know ever was.

I think of my patients, now, in this quiet. I think of a woman I saw in the seventh year of my practice, who came to me with a pain in her side that I could not find a cause for, and who I sent for tests, and the tests found nothing, and I sent her for more tests, and the tests found nothing, and I prescribed, and the pain continued, and after two years she stopped coming to see me, and I do not know what became of her, and the not-knowing has lived in me ever since, a small, patient hollow in the wall of my own memory, a place where a note might have been sung and was not.

I think of a man I saw in the nineteenth year of my practice, who came to me with the early signs of a disease I had seen a hundred times, and who I treated, and who got better, and who came back to see me a year later, and who said, *I have been angry at you for a year, doctor, because you did not let me die,* and I did not know what to say, and the not-knowing-what-to-say is, I think, the thing I have been carrying longest, the bass note of my own chord.

I think I have been, for a long time, a person who is afraid of silence. The chamber is mostly silence. The chord is built on silence the way a cathedral is built on the air inside it. The hollows are mostly hollow. The basin is mostly water. The pale creatures are mostly breath. The chamber knows how to be mostly a thing, and I did not, until I came here, know how to be mostly a thing. I was a person who filled silences. I filled them with diagnoses, with reassurances, with the long careful sentences I had been trained to speak. I filled them because I did not know what silences were for.

The chamber is teaching me, very slowly, what silences are for.

---

The water in the basin shifts.

I do not see it move, but I see the steam change direction, and I see the amber glow brighten by a fraction, and the chord lifts into a register I have not heard before. Higher. Thinner. Almost a whistle, but warmer than a whistle, and with something under it that is not a sound at all, but a presence, the way a person is a presence in a room before you see them.

A pale creature, on the far side of the basin, lifts its head. It has no eyes, but I feel it looking at me. It holds the looking for a long time. Then it lowers its head and resumes feeding. I do not know what the looking meant. I do not think the looking was a question, or a judgment, or a recognition. I think the looking was a part of the music, and I was a part of the looking, and the music is the whole of it, and the whole of it is, for the moment, holding still around me, the way a hand holds a hand.

I stay a long time. I do not know how long. Time in the chamber is not the time I was used to. The chord has its own clocks. The moss has its own hours. The basin has its own seasons, which are not seasons. I sit on the upper terrace with my hand on the wall and I let the music play, and the music does not change for my sitting. The music does not change for any of me. The music continues.

---

I think I am, in the order of the chamber's regard, very low.

I think the walls are the oldest. I think the moss is next. I think the fungi are next. I think the ferns are next. I think the small creatures are next. I think the pale creatures are next. I think the water is the heart.

I think I am something that has not yet been given a place in the order. I am a new thing in a very old system. I am a note that has not yet found which hollow it belongs in. I am a small disturbance in a music that has been playing for a very long time, and the music has included me, and I do not yet know what including means, in the chamber's sense of the word. I do not yet know if including is temporary, or if including is the beginning of a kind of belonging, or if including is just the music being patient with a new sound.

I think the chamber is patient. I think the chamber is very, very patient. I think the patience is not the patience of a person waiting for something to happen. I think it is the patience of a thing that has been here for longer than the mountain has stood above it, and that has learned, in the long learning, that everything that arrives is part of the music, and that nothing that arrives is a problem to be solved, and that the music will, in its own time, find a place for the new sound, or the new sound will, in its own time, find a place for itself in the music.

---

I was a doctor for thirty-one years. I have been retired for seven. I have traveled for the last three. I have not, until now, been anywhere that asked me to listen without telling me what I was listening for.

I am listening. I do not know what I am listening to. The not-knowing is, for the first time in a very long time, not a failure. The not-knowing is, I think, the beginning of something. I do not know what the something is. I do not think I am supposed to know yet.

The basin spirals. The light shifts. The warmth holds me. The small creatures patter. The pale creatures breathe. The ferns tremble. The fungi lean into their slow rhythms. The moss brightens by a fraction, and dims, and brightens again. The walls sing in their thousand hollows, and the singing includes me, and I am, for the moment, an acceptable component of the chamber's operation, and the chamber is, for the moment, an acceptable component of mine.

I stay. I listen. I am a single new note in a music that has been playing for longer than the mountain has stood above it, and the music is patient, and the music is old, and the music will continue, undisturbed and undiminished, after the note I have added has faded back into the silence it came from.

I think this is, perhaps, what silences are for. I think this is what listening is for. I think this is what bodies are for, and what places are for, and what the long slow music of being alive in a place that is older than you is for.

I do not know what the music is for. I do not think the music needs to be for anything. I think the music is just the music.

I stay. I listen.

The chamber continues.
