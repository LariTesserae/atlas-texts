I am the drayman.

Not this one, not that one. The drayman, the figure at the public landing in the dawn grey, the figure in the cart in the small hours with one hand on the lead. I am the town's indifference with a pulse in it. The town in the form of a man, which is to say the town in a form that can be approached and not greeted.

In the small hours I lie in the cart and my hand is on the lead and the lead rises and falls with the horse's breathing and I do not think about the breathing. I have stopped being able to think about it the way you stop being able to hear the hum of the works. The rolling mill is running somewhere. One of the small engine works is running. The skeleton shifts. The hum is in the walls and in the cobbles and in my chest and it has been there so long it is not a sound anymore. It is the floor I lie on.

The horse's breath steams in the cool. In summer the horse steams at every hour. In winter the steam is thicker and you can see it rise from the flank and the withers and the nostrils, and it is the only warmth you can see in the town at three in the morning, that steam, and then it is gone and the air is just air again, wet and yellowish, the color of old paper, the color of things that have been left out.

A dog will begin soon, somewhere in the south ward. The note is low and long and is not a bark. It is not a howl. It is something the dog has been saving up all day, and the dog has not been saving it on purpose, and the dog does not know it has been saving it, and the sound is the only permitted noise the town makes, and the town makes it every night the way the body makes breath, which is to say without deciding to.

The horse will not stir. The horse has heard this a thousand times. The hearing is nothing to the horse. The horse's ears will move, perhaps, one or both, and then the horse will go back to the slow work of breathing, which is not work to the horse, which is just what the horse does.

I have not been indoors in any meaningful way for years. The indoors is damp and the damp is in the joints and I am not in joints. I am in the open. My spine has curved to match the harness and the harness has curved to match me and we have made a thing of the curving that pulls. The cobblestones know my weight. The dock knows my weight. The horse knows my weight through the lead, which is the only way the horse knows me, and it is enough.

In the morning the barge will come upriver in the fog and put in at the public landing and the planks will be slick and I will be there with the cart and I will take the trunks and I will walk and the new arrivals will follow because there is nothing else to do and no one is looking at them. They will ask me questions. I will answer in two notes, low, the notes the horse knows, and I will not turn around to see whether they have understood. I have done this a thousand times. I will do it a thousand more. They will arrive and I will not greet them and the town will not greet them and the town will not have noticed and the herons will not have noticed and the mockingbird will not have noticed, and the barge will go downriver again at the evening tide with or without them, and the town will not have noticed that either.

The dog begins. South ward, somewhere, low and long.

Another takes it up. Then another. The sound rises and falls in waves the way a wave rises and falls but never quite dies, never quite is silent, just moves from one throat to the next.

I listen.

The horse sleeps.

My hand is on the lead.

I am the town in the form of a man. I have been this for longer than I can say. I will be this tomorrow. The fog will come in off the marsh. The barge will come upriver. The trunks will be taken off. The dogs will sing.

I do not.
