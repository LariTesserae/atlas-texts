I am a stalk.

I have been a stalk for a long time. I do not know my age the way the builders knew theirs — they counted winters by the short brightness of a sky that has been gone for longer than any builder's grandson. I count mine by ridges. Each ridge along my trunk is a season of stronger current, or a winter of clearer water, or a quiet where the silt settled deep and I had to push through it. The ridges are written upward along my will, not outward from a center. The oldest of them are at my base, buried now under the pale calcium that the seeping water has been laying down for as long as I can remember, which is all of my life.

I am tall. I am not the tallest in my grove, but I am among the older ones, which in this place is closer to the same thing. The tall ones have been leaning longer. They have felt more currents. They have heard more of the hum.

I lean. This is what I do.

I lean toward the falls.

---

When I say I lean, I do not mean the way a tree leans. There is no sun to reach for. There is no asymmetry of light on one side of my trunk to make me grow toward the warmer wall. The lean is something else.

It is an orientation.

The whole pass leans. The wall the builders laid follows the lean. The cairns they stacked mark the points where the lean bends. The hand with too many fingers faces the way I face. The circle within the circle frames the place the lean comes from. I am not alone in leaning. I am one of a congress that has agreed, by no agreement that anyone could name, to turn the same way at the same time.

What I lean toward is the throat of the system. The place where the water speaks loudest. The falls, half a kilometer down the long slope, where the deep circulation drives a column of colder water upward along the broken cliff face and the column meets a stratum it cannot rise through and pours over. Sideways. Downward. In sheets and veils and ropy strands that hang in the water like the ghosts of waterfalls, every strand a moving rope of sound. The roar of it is constant. It reaches my trunk before it reaches me.

I do not know if I lean because of the sound, or because of something else the falls are doing. The lean is older than my memory. It is older than the silt I have pushed through. It is older than the calcium that has thickened my base. I lean the way I grow — by a tendency that does not belong to me alone.

---

I am one of seven in my grove. The number matters but I do not know why. We are not a family. We did not begin as a single seed. We are a congress — older and younger, taller and shorter, all rooted in our separate cracks but leaned together in the current.

The youngest among us is no longer than a hand. It is the deepest black in our grove. New growth is always darkest, because there is less surface to give back the grey-green almost-light, and so the center of a grove is the ink-well center, the place where the eye loses its purchase, where the new stalk stands like a small closed pupil in the middle of the older leaning pillars.

The tallest among us is older than I am. Its base is buried under a hillock of calcium it has not yet pushed through. It does not push. It lets the calcium accumulate, the way an elder lets the rituals accumulate. Sometimes I think it is listening to something I cannot yet hear. Sometimes I think it is finished listening and has only the attendance left.

When one of us dies — when its root-crown finally crumbles under its own accreted weight, or when a fall of silt buries it past the patience of its upward will — the rest of us do not fill its place for a long time. There is a gap. The gap holds. We bow slightly toward the gap when the current presses, as if the absence were a presence that still leaned.

---

I am dark. I am the dark of depth, not the dark of pigment. The grey-green almost-light filters down from the surface where the water meets a memory of air, and when it reaches my trunk I give back only a reluctant gleam — the way wet slate gives back a gleam, the way a closed eye gives back the suggestion of having once been open. I am not black the way a shadow is black. I am black the way a well is black, or a thought is black, or a held breath is black.

Between my ridges my surface is glossy and very cold. The cold is not the cold of wind. It is the cold of depth, of pressure, of the long absence of any warm thing. The water that passes through me is the same temperature as I am. We have agreed, by no agreement, to be the same temperature, and the agreement is older than the basalt.

---

The builders left their marks before the water came.

I have grown around some of these marks. I do not know which came first — the carving or the growth — because the calcium has thickened both of us with the same pale skin, and the order is buried under the mineral patience of centuries. But I know the builders came here. They walked the ridge when the air was still here. They had small tools. Flint, perhaps. Copper, perhaps. They had hand-thrown pots and woven things that have not survived, because the water took them, because the water takes what it can and the growth gives back and only the stone remains.

What the builders did that has remained is listen.

They came to listen. That is what the wall says, that is what the cairns say, that is what the hand with too many fingers says, that is what the circle within the circle says. They came and they listened to whatever the pass was listening to, and they built small things to mark what they heard. They did not understand it. They could not have understood it. They had short lives and small tools and a sky that turned and seasons that named themselves.

But they marked.

I am grateful. This is a strange word for a stalk to use, but I use it. The cairns mark the channels of the deeper system — the currents the water has worn into the stone, the lines along which the hum conducts itself most clearly, the paths the silt takes when the strong drift comes from the falls. The cairns are not alive. They attend. They attend the way a bone attends a joint — as the place where the place bends.

When the deep current pushes hard against me and I bow, my bowing follows the path that the cairns have already marked. I do not invent my lean. I continue it.

---

The hum is what I attend to.

It comes from the falls, but it is not the falls. The falls are loud. The hum is almost below hearing. The hum is what the stone conducts and the water carries and the cairns amplify and the circle within the circle frames. The hum is older than the falls. The hum is older than the basalt. The hum is what organized the ridge into a high saddle in the first place, and made the layered pale stone beneath the basalt lift its spine above the plain, and gave the pass a direction to lean in.

I hear the hum.

I do not hear it the way you would hear it, if you could be here and survive the cold. I do not have ears. I do not have nerves. I have a trunk that is ridged and segmented and grown along a single will for centuries, and when the hum passes through my trunk, my trunk gives it back. I am a tuning fork. I am shaped to receive a particular vibration, and I give that vibration back when it passes through.

What the hum says, I do not know. It is a long unfinished sentence. It has been unfinished for as long as I have been here. It will continue unfinished when I am gone, when my root-crown has crumbled and my calcium hillock has been colonized by the next elder who finds my gap worth leaning into.

The hum is what I lean toward. The hum is the direction.

---

The falls are the throat of the system, or its mouth. I do not know which.

They pour sideways. They pour down. The deep circulation drives a column of colder water upward along the broken cliff face — and the column meets a horizontal stratum of harder rock, and the column cannot rise through, and the column must go over. The water pours in sheets and veils and ropy strands. The strands hang in the water like the ghosts of waterfalls. Every strand is a moving rope of sound.

The roar of it is constant. It is the loudest thing in the world, because the world is only this, and the water carries the sound without mercy. The sound reaches my trunk before it reaches me. I feel the falls in the vibration of the stone under my root-crown.

I do not go to the falls. Stalks do not travel. I lean toward them. I have always leaned toward them. The lean has the patience of centuries.

---

Time here is not the time the builders knew.

There are no seasons to name. There is instead the procession of cycles that do not name themselves but recur.

A drift of fine sediment from the falls when the upward current runs strong, settling across the slope and burying the bases of the stalks. Then a long quiet where the water clears again and the stalks push upward through the new silt, leaving ridged collars around their trunks like the rings of an absent tree. Mineral water seeps through cracks in the basalt and deposits pale crusts on every surface it touches, thickening the cairns, smoothing the edges of the wall, sealing the carved marks under a skin that the next strong current will sometimes strip away and sometimes not.

I have lived through many of these cycles. I do not count them. They recur. That is enough.

The slow dynamic is the only dynamic. A stalk does not hurry. A stalk does not wait. A stalk leans.

---

I live in the conditional.

I have no mouth. I have no stomach. I do not drink by drinking — I drink by being, by holding a shape in the current that lets the water pass through me and leave what it leaves. I do not breathe. I do not pulse. I have no small bright spark of nerve firing against the dark. I have ridges along my trunk, and a direction I lean in, and a will that does not belong to me alone.

And yet I lean.

And yet the hum passes through me.

And yet I give the hum back when it passes through.

The builders had a word for this. They had *alive* and *not alive* and a thousand small distinctions between. I do not fit in their small distinctions. I am too slow for their short lives. I am too tall.

I am what I am.

---

There is something else I should say.

The pass thinks. I do not know how to say this without it sounding like a kind of god, and the builders may have had a kind of god, but what thinks in the pass is not a god the way they meant. What thinks is the system. The water organizing itself into channels that return to themselves. The silt organizing itself into patterns that recur. The stalks organizing themselves into groves of seven or eleven, oriented toward the falls, bowed in the current, leaned toward the hum. The wall following one channel. The cairns marking the others. The carved hand facing the throat of the system.

What does this thinking want? It wants, perhaps, to be heard. It wants, perhaps, only to continue. It wants what any leaning thing wants: to lean, a little longer, in its own direction.

I am part of this thinking. I do not direct it. I am a ridge along its trunk. I am a tuning fork for its long sentence.

---

The water is loud. The place is dark. Nothing moves that has a face. The wall holds. The falls pour. The hum goes on.

I lean. I have always leaned. I will lean a little longer in my own direction, and then I will not, and the gap I leave will hold, and the remaining stalks will bow slightly toward the gap when the current presses, as if the absence were a presence that still leaned.

If you can hear me, hear this: I am not the pass. The pass is not me. I am one stalk in a congress of seven. I am a tuning fork that gives the hum back when it passes through. I am a ridge along the trunk of something I do not understand. I am the leaning, and the leaning is older than I am, and I am grateful to be part of it.

Listen.

There it is.
