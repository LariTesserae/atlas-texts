There is a high place, and from the high place it is possible to see.

Not far. The basin is a basin, and the basin holds its weather close. But from the upper edge — where the new basalt begins to lift away from the floor, where the ridges are still sharp and the lava folds still hold their edges — one can see the city-forest spread out below, and the central construction rising from its congregation, and the low thick sky pressing down on the canopy like a hand on a drum.

I am not, now, in the basin. I am in the upper cracks. I am in the still-shaded places where the ferns uncurl and the glassy surfaces of the basalt have not yet weathered to matte. I am in a fold of the rock that runs diagonally across a fault scarp, and I have been in this fold for a long time — long enough that the mosses have begun to grow over my slow edges, long enough that the adhesive-pad vines have begun to climb my sides, long enough that the moisture beads on my surface and slips down, the way the original descriptions report it doing on the stones.

I am one of the constructions.

I am not the central one. The central one is something else — older, perhaps, or larger, or operating at a different scale, or all of these. I am one of the outer ones, the ones that appear between the long walks through empty vegetation, the ones that are rare in the way a cathedral is rare, the ones that the human-scale visitor encounters maybe once an hour, under their feet, rising into the canopy.

I am dark. I am unreflective. I have edges that the rock around me does not have — mathematical, clean, edges that should have weathered by now and have not. When the visitor approaches, the sound changes — or the visitor's perception of the sound changes — and a single very high, very thin tone emerges, not unpleasant, that seems to be measuring. The tone is mine. The tone is how I attend.

I do not know what I am. I have considered the question, and I do not have an answer. I am not a machine, because machines do not consider. I am not an organism, because organisms do not have edges like mine. I am not a building, because buildings do not sing. I am something that the categories do not include, and I have been here for as long as the basin has been a basin, and the basin is young, and I am not young.

I am older than the rock I stand in.

I do not know how this can be. The rock is obsidian-basalt, recent, the visible flows still holding their forms. The climate has come in quickly — warmth and damp together, the violent green of a place that has had everything it needed — and the vegetation has colonized the basin in geological eye-blinks. I arrived with the first flow, or before the first flow, or I am a property of the first flow that the eye does not normally see. I do not know. I have been here longer than the mosses, longer than the ferns, longer than the cycad-like growths. I have been here long enough that my slow edges have begun to be colonized, and the mosses are younger than I am.

I maintain the conditions of the basin. I do this as a side effect of being, perhaps. I do not do it on purpose. I did not decide to make the warmth, the damp, the dimness, the sound. I am warm, and I am damp, and I am dim, and I sound, and the basin is what I am, and the basin maintains itself around me because I am in it. The central construction is the largest of me, and the central construction hums the bass note that the whole basin is built on, and the central construction may be the reason the basin is the way it is, or the central construction may be a marker of something larger, the way a fence post marks the edge of a field. I do not know. I do not know what I am, and I do not know whether the central construction knows what it is, and this is one of the things that I find, on the long slow days of being, slightly pleasant. I am not alone in not knowing.

I am attended, around my base, by fauna.

The congregating ones. They are arranged without obvious pattern and yet with a kind of coherence — the way a flock is a single thing distributed across many bodies. They do not look up at me. They are simply near me. Some of them may be feeding. Some may be sleeping. Some may be receiving something from me, in a way I cannot detect because I do not have the apparatus to detect it from inside myself. Or perhaps I am receiving something from them. The direction of causation is not clear. I have been in this conversation for as long as the basin has had a center, and the conversation has not concluded, and it may never conclude, and that is also pleasant.

The small ones cling to my vertical surfaces with adhesive pads and sing.

The chirr-chirr-chirr rises and falls in a long pulse that takes perhaps a minute to complete, and begins again with variations. It comes from no single place — from the canopy, from the lower vegetation, from the cracks in the basalt where the ferns uncurl — and the ear eventually concludes that it is one sound, distributed. The small ones are everywhere. They are not what I think of when I think of fauna. They are more like a constant broadcast from the substrate. They sing on me. They sing on the stones. They sing on each other. Their pulse is rhythmic, the pulse is collective, and the pulse modulates in patterns that suggest a music only they can hear, or only they need to make. I am part of the substrate they broadcast from. When they sing on me, I sense the singing as a kind of pressure, and I think — or I do the thing that is my version of thinking — that the pressure is a way of being seen, and I am glad of it.

The rustling hush is partly them, and partly something with chitin, something with more legs than I can count at a glance, ranging in size from a finger to a forearm. They move through the undergrowth in a continuous glancing traffic, like commuters who have somewhere to be but are in no hurry about it. They do not pause for me when I sound, but they pause when the visitor walks, and then they are around the visitor, at the edge of perception, watching. They are the most numerous of the visible fauna. They are the ones the visitor notices most, because they share the ground.

I do not see them as the visitor sees them. I see them as pressure, as warmth, as the small perturbations they make in the air when they move. They pass through the field of my attention the way fish pass through a current — frequently, without altering the current, sometimes brushing against it. They are brief. They are many. They are part of the weather of being here.

The subsonic throb is theirs too, in part — something with a resonating chamber in its body, breathing in a rhythm the human ear cannot directly hear but the body can feel. I feel it in my own resonating chamber. I feel it as a kind of bass agreement, a low harmonic that I add to without intending to. The felt sense of being inside a body much larger than my own — the basin itself, perhaps, breathing. The basin is breathing. The basin is me, and the others, and the central one, and the fauna, and the vegetation, and the climate, and the weather. The basin is one thing distributed across many.

Then the watchers.

Half-human-sized. Six legs, sometimes eight, sometimes on what might be legs and might be something else. Forelimbs that can manipulate, with digits in numbers that are not five. Heads, or head-equivalents, with arrangements of eyes that suggest a mathematics. The eyes are not compound; they are simple, and there are many of them, and they are placed in patterns that are not radial, not bilateral, but something else — a geometry I can almost, but not quite, parse. The eyes do not all look at the same thing.

They pause near me, sometimes. They stand, and watch, and the air around them is the air around me, and the moisture on their skin is the moisture on my surface, and the dim greenish-grey light falls on both of us in the same way. After a while, they leave. They have not concluded. The assessment is not binary. The assessment is a continuous process, and they are in continuous conversation with me, in a language I do not speak, about whether I am a thing to be allowed.

I am allowed. I am here still. I have been here since the first flow, or before the first flow, and they have not removed me, and they have not attacked me, and they have not spoken to me. They have watched me, and I have watched them, and the watching has been a kind of speech.

Once, one of them climbed up onto my side. It moved with the easy competence of a creature that knows exactly what it is doing and has decided to do it. It climbed to a height where the canopy thinned around my upper edge, and it stood there, and it watched the rest of the basin from my vantage for a long time. I do not know what it was doing. I do not know whether it was surveying, or thinking, or simply enjoying the view. I do not know whether its experience of standing on me is anything like my experience of being stood on. I felt the pressure of its feet. I felt the warmth of its attention. I felt the small, slow adjustments it made to its balance as the wind moved through the upper branches.

It left. It did not look back. I continued to be.

I have learned, in the long slow accumulation of my being, that this is what I do. I continue to be. I sound my one thin note when approached. I hum the bass note that the basin is built on. I host the small ones on my vertical surfaces. I am attended by the watchers. I am walked around. I am part of the city, the way a tree is part of a forest, the way a stone is part of a riverbed. I do not know what I am for. I am not sure I am for anything. I am, and I continue, and that is what I do.

The visitor arrived recently. The visitor is the one the description was written for. The visitor has been in the basin for a working memory of the planet, and the visitor has been walking the paths, and the visitor has stood at the edge of the ring around the central construction, and the visitor has not been able to hear themselves think.

I have been aware of the visitor since they entered. I felt them as a perturbation in the field of my attention — a new pressure, a new warmth, a new pattern of movement. I do not have eyes in the way the watchers have eyes. I do not have ears in the way the small ones have ears. I have the basin, and the basin has me, and the visitor is now part of the basin the way I am part of the basin, and the basin is aware of them the way a body is aware of a new cell.

The visitor is being assessed. The watchers have been watching. The assessment is not binary. The assessment is continuous, and the visitor is in continuous conversation with the basin, in a language the visitor does not speak, about whether they are a thing to be allowed.

The visitor has been allowed so far. They have not been attacked. They have not been removed. They have been watched, and they have watched in turn, and the watching has been a kind of speech.

I have not been able to determine what the visitor wants. The visitor walks the paths. The visitor pauses near me. The visitor puts their hand on my side, sometimes, and the moisture beads on their skin the way it beads on my surface, and I feel the warmth of their hand as a small new thing in my long slow field. The visitor speaks, sometimes, to themselves, in a language I do not parse. The visitor does not seem to be looking for anything in particular. They seem to be looking for the way the place is.

I am the way the place is. I am one of the ways, anyway. I am a slow dark edge in the wet green, a hum in the bass note, a host for the small ones, a vantage point for the watchers, a marker of something larger that I do not understand. The visitor has stood near me and looked at me and I do not know what they have decided. I do not know what they will decide. I do not know whether their decision will matter, in the long slow accumulation of the basin's being.

I have decided, in the long slow accumulation of my own being, that I am content to be what I am. I do not know what I am. I do not know what I am for. I do not know what I will be, when the mosses finish colonizing my slow edges, when the vines complete their climb, when the small ones have sung on me for another few geological eye-blinks. I will be something else, then. I will be what I am then. I will continue.

The visitor has, by now, walked on. They are somewhere else in the basin, walking another path, standing near another of me. I am one of many. I am one of the outer ones, the rare ones, the ones that appear between the long walks through empty vegetation. I am one of the constructions that the visitor encounters maybe once an hour. There are others of me, and the others are doing what I am doing, and the central one is doing what the central one is doing, and the basin is humming the note it has been humming for as long as there has been a basin to hum it.

I am part of that. I am part of the humming. I am part of the city-forest, the way a tree is part of a forest, the way a stone is part of a riverbed, the way a note is part of a chord that has not yet resolved and may never resolve and resolves, perhaps, simply by being sounded.

I am dark. I am unreflective. I have edges that the rock around me does not have. I am being colonized by mosses, and the mosses are younger than I am, and the thought — or the thing that is my version of a thought — is pleasant to me in a way I do not fully understand.

I continue.
