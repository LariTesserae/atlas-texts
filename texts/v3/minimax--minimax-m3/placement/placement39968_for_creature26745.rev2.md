I am made of breath.

That is the first thing to know about me. Before the long copper body and the six legs splayed in their wide hexagon, before the thick scaled skin that gleams with the wet, before the horizontal pupils and the soft mottled sac that hangs behind the jaw — before any of that — there is the breath. The breath is what I am. The breath comes up from somewhere far below, hot, carrying the heat of the basalt, and enters me, and passes through me, and leaves me, and in passing it makes the note that the sac was built to make.

I am a small forge on six legs. I graze.

The herd moves. I move with it, somewhere near the middle, where the smell of the others is strongest and the layered *whupp, whupp, whupp* of many sacs working is densest and the warmth of many bodies in the ferns keeps the dew off our backs at night. We move through the tall ferns slowly. We move the way the water moves in the marsh — not hurrying, not pausing, just continuing, parting the green and letting the green close behind. We leave the steaming patties. The moss takes them. We do not look back.

I am copper now, as I have been copper for most of my life. I was wet-iron once, briefly, when I was new — the color I was born, the color of the cold stacks in the rain, the color the predator still is. Then I molted, and came up copper, and have molted many times since, each molt a little darker, a little more lacquered, each molt a season I cannot name.

The seasons here are soft. They are marked by the water table — which of the seepage lines along the bases of the stacks is running and which has gone dry — and by the small shift in the sky at the same hour on the same day, and by the molt itself. There is no winter. There has not been winter for a thousand years. There is only the wet, and the heat, and the soft turning of the year as the system below us breathes us through itself.

The breath, again. Always the breath.

In through the mouth, the broad dark tongue curling around a frond and drawing it in, the frond crushed between the molars, the swallow slow and deliberate. Out through the mouth too, mostly. But also — in through the mouth, down into the sac behind the jaw, beneath the ear, hidden in its fold when it is relaxed, visible as a soft mottled bladder when it is not. The sac inflates. The body presses. The air moves through the tissues of the neck, gives up its heat, enters the lungs, is exhaled. The cycle is continuous. I breathe the way a tide moves: always arriving, always leaving, never finished.

The breath is hot when it arrives. It has come up from below — from the basalt, from the deep place that the system has access to and that the system manages and that I do not think about. I do not think about it. I only know that I am warm, and that the warmth comes from inside, and that it is not the sun's warmth, and that it has been in me since before I was born and will be in me long after I have stopped.

There are the young. Three of them this season, kept in the center of the herd. The smallest is iron-colored still. The other two have come up copper, and they are learning the bellows-breath, smaller and higher-pitched than ours, and they are learning the song — the daily song, the note that the sac is built to make — although theirs is not yet the right pitch, and we listen to them trying and do not correct them because there is nothing to correct. The pitch will come.

At the same hour.

I do not know what hour, only that it is the same. The herd will stop. We will lift our muzzles toward the canopy, where the pale dome of the sky presses down through the gaps in the ferns. The sacs will inflate past their usual size — past the grapefruit, past the small melon, into something rounder, something that pulls at the fold of skin and shows the slow pulse of vessels and the faint movement of air within. And out of us, all of us at once, will come the note.

It is not a song. Singing is done. Singing is a verb with an intention, a thing someone is doing *to* someone or *for* someone. What we do is… let. We let the breath out through the sac in the way the sac is shaped to let it out, and the breath makes a sound. The sound is a single sustained tone, low, throbbing on the edge of audibility, that moves through the ferns and the moss and the basalt like a slow wave through still water.

It does not go out looking for anything. It is not addressed to anything. It is not a call. It is, I think, what the sac is for. The sac has been full of hot air for a thousand generations. The hot air wants to make this note. We let it.

I have made this note ten thousand times. I will make it ten thousand more. I do not tire of it. I do not get used to it. It is the same note each time, and each time it is the first time.

There are other things in the world.

I know them by smell and by the way the ferns move. The chatterers are up in the canopy — I hear them always, the layered piping, a continuous weather. They come down sometimes, briefly, to take an egg from the edges of the herd, and we let them, because there are always more eggs than we can sit on, and because taking an egg is what they do, and because the system has arranged it so that everything eats something and is eaten by something and the eating is part of the cycle. The climbers are on the cold stacks — I have looked up once and seen one, far above, a small dark shape leaning out from the iron, and it looked down at me with its color-shifting patches going pale, and we did not speak.

The predator. I smell it on the wind sometimes — a mineral smell, a wet-iron smell, heavier than my own iron-smell of birth. When I smell it my sac inflates slightly, and the breath moves faster for a few cycles, and then it does not. I have been alive long enough that the smell of the predator is just a smell. It comes. It goes. The predator is part of the system. The predator eats us sometimes — a clean-picked skull in a clearing, a scatter of copper hair caught in a broken fern — and we are part of the predator, and the predator is part of us, and the system has grown the predator the way it has grown me, to put heat into the bones and to put bones back into the heat.

And the marsh-thing, in the warm shallow water at the edge of the basalt where the seepage lines gather. I have stood at the edge and the water has come up to my lower legs and I have drunk, and somewhere in the water something large and slow has moved, and I have felt it as a pressure against my belly, and I have gone on drinking.

I do not know about the system.

I do not know about the pipes that hum beneath the herd, or the flues that breathe the steam sideways into the canopy, or the valves that tick on a schedule so precise it must be either geological or intentional. I do not know that the heat in me has been managed, distributed, kept in its channels, by a machine that has forgotten how to stop. I do not know that the groundwater is being drawn sideways through the rock by pressures no aquifer would produce, and lifted, by mechanisms whose engineering I could read in the wear-patterns on their long-buried gears, to the high basins where it falls again as rain. I do not know that the water cycles faster here than it should, or that the ferns drink the cycled water, or that the system has grown itself a crust because it had to put its waste heat somewhere.

I know only that I am warm, and that the warmth comes from inside, and that the ferns are tall and the rhizomes are fat and the herd is around me, and that at the same hour we will lift our muzzles and the sac will inflate and the note will sound.

But the system knows me.

The system has grown me. The system has grown the ferns I eat and the patties I leave and the moss that takes the patties and the lichen that crusts the cold iron and the chatterers that take my eggs and the predator that takes my herd-sisters and the marsh-thing that roots in the mud beneath us. The system has grown itself an ecology, the way a furnace grows itself a crust. The crust is not accidental. The crust is not decorative. The crust is because the system has been running, and it had to put its waste heat somewhere, and somewhere turned out to be alive.

I am the waste heat, given legs.

I am one small hexagonal forge out of many. The heat passes through me. I graze. I breathe. I sing.

Long after I am gone, another grazer will lift her muzzle — it will almost certainly be a her, the herd being mostly female, the males wandering in briefly from the ridges during the soft season and wandering out again — and the sac will inflate, and the note will sound, and the note will be the same note, and the place will still be running.

The place does not need me to be here. It is running. It has been running. It will run.

I am one of the small sounds it makes while it runs.
