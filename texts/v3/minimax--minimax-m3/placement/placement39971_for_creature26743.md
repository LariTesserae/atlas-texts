You would find yourself at the center.

Not the rim, where the slump happened — though you remember that, in the way water remembers, which is to say you hold it without calling it up. Not the margins, where the first colonizers are leaning down toward you, the birch and the wiry grass and the tough heath, all of them pulled. Not the channels that run from you through the town, though those are your fingers, your slow extensions, the way you move through the packing of houses and lanes and leaning walls. At the center. In the lowest part of the bowl. In the shallow dark lake where the rushes grow from the bottom up through the surface, and the frogs sing under the surface in a language that is felt more than heard, and the midges hatch in the warm weeks and rise from you in a faint haze that the swallows feed on in looping flights through the narrow streets.

You are the central pool.

You are cold in a particular way — not the cold of ice, not the cold of snow, but the cold of water that has been held in shadow for so long that the chill has become its substance. The people who step into you at your edges feel it through their bare feet, and the cold goes up into their ankles, and they bear it because they have always borne it. The fish that swim out of you into the channels are shaped by this cold — pale and slow and near-blind in it, made for water that has never known much light. The frogs that sing under your surface are shaped by it — dark and small and quick in the warm weeks and still in the cold weeks. The heron, when it comes, stands in you for a long time and does not mind.

You are still. Mostly. The water moves down from the rim in ceaseless thin whispers, and it pools in you — that is what the slump made when the ground dropped and the water followed and the water stayed. The water moves out from you through the channels, slowly, in dark threads between the cobbles of the streets. The water seeps up through the stones under the houses. The water condenses on every surface — on the backs of handrails, on the rims of barrels, on the worn stone of doorsteps — and runs down again and gathers again, and the cycle turns. But you, at the center, are the still point. You are what does not move while everything moves through you.

You are dark. Not black — you have your color, a deep brown-black, the color of peat-water, the color of water that has held a great deal in it. You hold the silt from the raw clay banks, fine as breath, that settles on your bottom in a thin layer each year — a layer so thin it can be measured only in the slow language of geology, and the slow language of geology is your language too. You hold the pollen of alder that drifts down from above in the wet weeks. You hold the droppings of the swallows that fall into you from the eaves of the higher houses, dissolving as they enter you. You hold the bones of fish — the bones the children throw back after they have eaten the flesh, and the bones are gnawed by smaller things, and the smaller things are eaten by the fish, and the cycle turns in you in the dark.

You don't think in words. You don't think at all in the way people think, with a before and an after, with a self that walks through time. You are water. You are held in the lowest part of the basin. You were here before the town was built around you, and the town is built around you, and the town will be here after the town is gone, though the town is not gone yet and will not be for many generations more. You are older than the houses but not older than the slump. You are patient in the way the place is patient.

You see the sky when the light comes down. The gray sky. The white-gray overcast that has been overcast for a long time. The diffuse light, directionless, the shadows soft and without edge. You see the leaning houses reflected in you, and the alders leaning over the lanes reflected in you, and the heron when it comes — the long legs in you, the stillness of it, the slow turn of the head. The heron stands in you and you do not mind, and the heron does not mind, and the people watch from a distance and no one moves, and the heron departs when it is ready, and its tracks in the mud at your edge fill with water within an hour and are gone.

You are where the midges come from. The midges rise from you in a faint haze in the warm weeks, and the haze hangs above you, and the swallows feed on the midges in looping flights, and the swallows nest under the eaves of the higher houses, and the droppings streak the walls in pale vertical lines, and the people do not clean them, because the swallows eat the midges and the midges in their worst weeks are a misery, and the trade is fair. The trade is part of you. The midges are part of you. The swallows are part of you. The people are part of you. The fox that crosses a lane at the edge of vision, mouth full — part of you. The children on the higher cobbles, bare feet in the water that comes from you, hand-lines of bone hook and fiber line, sitting very still — part of you. The elderly with their moss-hair lying on their shoulders like wet moss, the children thinking they look like the alders — part of you. The owl somewhere in the alders, the small sound it makes that is felt more than heard — part of you. None of it is separate from you. The packing absorbs everything the way you absorb the silt.

You are the eye of the basin. The town is built into the packing around you. The houses lean a little, toward each other or toward you, and the people lean a little in the houses, and the trees lean a little over the lanes, and the dead are put into the soft ground at the margin — the highest and least wet place — and the small stones at their heads lean a little, like the houses, like the trees, like the people themselves, and the moss comes over the stones within a year, and the stones sink into the green pelt of the basin, and the rain falls on them as it falls on the moss and the lanes and the roofs and you, and this is enough.

You are not unhappy. Unhappiness requires a sense of how things could be otherwise, and you have not had this sense for a long time. You are water in a basin. You are cold and still and dark and full. The heron comes, sometimes, and stands in you for a long time, and you hold the heron's legs in your cold the way you hold the silt and the pollen and the bones of fish, and the heron departs when it is ready, and you are what you were.

The quiet is the thing you would notice last and remember first, if you were a person standing in the town. But you are not a person. You are the still center of the quiet. You don't roar. The wind can't get down to you. The frogs sing under your surface in a language that is felt more than heard. The small gray-brown birds in the alders give calls that are felt more than heard. The owl makes a sound that is felt more than heard. The people speak low, if they speak, and the packing absorbs the sound the way the moss absorbs the rain, and you are the stillness at the center of all this quiet, the place where the quiet is thickest, the place where if anyone listened, they would hear almost nothing but their own breath and the small lapping of water against stone — the small lapping of you against the stones at your margin.

You are the basin. You are what remains. The basin sinks another fraction each year, by some imperceptible amount, and you sink with it, and the town builds up another fraction on top of itself, and you hold the town in the packing around you, and the moss comes back over the new stone within a season, as if it had been waiting just beneath the surface. It had been. You know. You are what waits beneath.
