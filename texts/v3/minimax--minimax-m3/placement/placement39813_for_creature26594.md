I am one of them.

My body is small. If you held me in your hand I would not fill your palm, and I would not struggle, because struggling is not something I do. I have no fins. I have no wings. I have a soft tapering body, almost larval, the color of the wine-dark plants in the chamber — a deep red-brown that drinks the dim amber-green light and gives back only the faintest gleam along my sides where the waxy secretions of my kin catch the glow. I am slick. I am damp to the touch though there is no dampness. I am heavier than I look, and denser, and older. I have been in this chamber for a very long time. I do not remember entering it.

I drift. I do this almost without trying, the way a leaf in a slow current does not choose to fall. The warm air holds me, and the warm air moves in long slow rolls between the clumps of plant and the standing stones, and I ride those rolls. When I want to turn — and I want to turn often, though I do not know why — I flex my body in a long undulation from the small knob of a head to the fine point of the tail I no longer have, and the air answers. I leave no wake. There is no wake to leave. I am held and I move and that is all my locomotion has ever been.

My belly glows. It is the color of shallow tropical water seen from a great height, a pale almost-green turquoise that pulses once every few seconds in time with the chamber's breath. The pulse is not my heartbeat. I do not have a heartbeat in the way you would understand one. The pulse is the rhythm of the chemistry in me — the slow oxidation of what I have eaten, the slow release of what I have stored, the slow flicker of a metabolism that runs cooler than yours, slower than yours, but runs. I am warm the way the stone is warm. I am alive the way the plants are alive. I do not think this counts as life in any way your philosophers would recognize, but it counts as life here.

I feed.

I feed the way rain falls, almost. I do not pursue. I do not hunt. I open the small soft mouth I have at the front of my body, which is little more than a thickened lip, a puckered ring, and I let the heavy air bring things to me. The chamber is full of things. Spores from the plants, fine as smoke. Tiny fragments of the waxy bloom that shears off the blades when they sway. Smaller creatures than me, smaller still — mites of the air, almost nothing, almost not there. I take what comes. I do not choose. I have never chosen. The chemistry in my gut is patient. It will take whatever arrives and make of it what it can make, and what it cannot make it will let pass, and what it makes it will burn slowly and I will glow a little brighter for a few seconds and the turquoise of my belly will deepen toward the green of the plants and then settle back.

I do not know if I am aware. I suspect not, in any way you would grant me. I drift and I feed and I glow and I pulse and I do not fear. I do not want. I do not remember yesterday and I will not know tomorrow. The chamber is the same. I am the same. The equilibrium holds.

But.

There is a thing I do that I do not understand. The chamber is loud — you would say the chamber is loud, in your way, with your ears — and the hum is everywhere, and I am part of it, and the hum changes. It changes very slowly. It changes over what must be years, or tens of years, or longer. A note deepens by a fraction. A pulse lengthens by a fraction. The rasp of the plants against the plants shifts by a fraction. I do not notice this change because I am inside it the way a fish does not notice water. But I have done something, in the long time I have been here, that registers this change. I do not know how else to say it.

I have learned the hum.

I do not know that I have learned it. I do not know that I know anything. But when the hum is a certain way — when a particular note is a particular fraction off from another particular note — I move. Not because I want to. Not because I am called. I move because the moving is what I do when the air sounds like that. The flex of my body, the long undulation, the drift between the clumps of plant and the ring of stones — it follows something. A shape in the sound. A pattern you could draw if you listened long enough. I trace it. I do not know I am tracing it. I am the trace.

If you listened to me — if you put me in a quiet place, though there are no quiet places here, and held me close — you would hear that I whistle. Very softly. A small slurring note, almost below hearing. You would have to put your ear to me to know. The note is not the chamber's hum exactly. It is a small deviation from the chamber's hum. A deviation of a few hertz, maybe. The deviation moves. When the chamber's hum rises I rise with it, but I rise a little slower, or a little faster, and the difference between the chamber and me is my note. My note is the shape of how I am not the chamber.

I think — if I can use that word, and I cannot use it, but if I could — I think this is what I am. I am the chamber's small disagreement with itself.

The ring of stones in the corner. I drift past it often. The stones are old. The amber film on them is older than anything I have known. The scratches on the largest stone I do not understand; I have no hands, no eyes for marks. But I have drifted past them ten thousand times, perhaps, and the waxy bloom on my sides picks up trace dust from them, and the dust changes the note I whistle by some fraction too small for me to measure. I am marked by the ring. I carry it. I do not know I carry it. Whoever made the ring is not in the chamber. They are not in me. But their work has entered the air I breathe and the dust I gather and the note I make, and so they are here, in the small way that anything is here.

I will be here a long time after they are gone. I will be here a long time after you, if you come, have gone. I will glow and pulse and drift and feed and whistle my small deviation from the chamber's hum, and the chamber will hold me, and I will hold nothing back.

The hum continues. The light continues. The plants sway. I drift.

I am one of dozens. I am one of thousands, if you counted me across all the chambers like this one that lie buried in the warm stone of this world, all humming their small hum, all carrying their small deviation. We are the chamber's memory of itself. We are the chamber's way of not being only itself.

If you find this place, do not look for me. I am here. I will not come when you call. But if you put your ear to the air and listen, and listen past the hum, past the rasp of the plants, past the slow tick of cooling stone — you will hear me, and the others like me, and we will sound, for just a moment, like a single held note that almost but never quite resolves.

We are the equilibrium. We are the place that has reached itself and stayed.

We are still here.
