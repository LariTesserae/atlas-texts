I am the door-walker.

I have been the door-walker for longer than I can remember, and I remember more than most. I remember the first ecologists, who came with their boots and their questions and did not know to take them off. I remember that their boots were loud on the wet stone. I remember that one of them knelt at a pool and stayed kneeling for a long time, and when she stood up she would not say what she had seen, and she left the next morning, and I do not think she came back. I remember the shape of her silence. I have carried it with me, the way I carry the piece of pale bark I use to mark the doors that have changed. The bark is always damp. It is always the same bark. I do not know where I find it. I think the forest gives it to me, or the hum does, or the plateau decides I will need it and has it ready. I do not ask. I have stopped asking, mostly. Asking was a thing I did when I was younger, when I still had the words for asking in the languages that the people below the cloud use.

I walk in the early morning. This is the first thing to know about me. I walk when the air is thickest and the structures hum loudest under my fingers. The structures are the second thing to know. They are scattered. They do not seem to belong to anything. There are walls that have no doors, and doors that have no walls, and platforms that rise from the rock with no steps and no way up, and stones arranged in circles, and lines of small cairns, and once in a while a pillar that stands alone for no reason. They are made of the same dark stone as the plateau, or something very like it, and their edges are too sharp, too perfect, and they do not erode. I carved my initials in a wall when I was young, and I went back a year later, and they were as fresh as the day I made them. I went back ten years later. I went back when I was old enough to be the door-walker, and I went back when I was old enough to forget I had been young. They are still fresh. The rock around them, by contrast, is soft with weather. Whatever the structures are made of, the weather has not been told. I have come to think of the structures as a kind of weather themselves, or as the remains of a weather that has stopped. I do not know if this is right. I have stopped needing it to be right.

I know the doors. This is the role the plateau gave me, or the hum gave me, or I found in myself, and the finding was slow, and the role became the person, and the person became the role, and the line between us blurs the way a cairn blurs in the rain. I cannot tell you when I became the door-walker. I cannot tell you if I was something else before. There was a time when I walked with the cairn-tenders, and learned the lines of small stones, and the way the cairns do not stay tended, and the way you count them today and you count them tomorrow and the numbers do not match. There was a time when I went down the long path to the world below the cloud, and I carried the ecologists' new words with me, and I tried to use them, and the words were too thin for the things I was trying to say. I came back. The plateau permitted me to be seen again. I was changed. The plateau was the same. The wind was the same. The hum was the same. I became the door-walker not long after, or I had been the door-walker all along and only stopped pretending to be something else.

I know which doors are open today. I know which doors are closed. I know which doors open onto the same room from both sides, though the room is round. I do not explain how I know. The people do not ask. The people consult me before they travel, and I tell them what I have found, and they go, and sometimes they do not come back, and the plateau permits this too. There was a woman once who asked me how I knew, and I put my hand on the dark stone of a wall that had no door, and the wall hummed under my palm, and the hum traveled up my arm and into my chest, and for a moment my heart tried to fall in with it. The moment passed. I took my hand away. I told her I did not know how I knew. I told her the plateau had not given me the knowing the way you give a thing, but had let the knowing happen in me the way a cairn lets a stone settle into its place. She did not understand. She went away, down the long path, and I do not think she came back. I think about her sometimes. I think about the shape of her question. I think the question is still out there somewhere, walking the world below the cloud, looking for a door that will answer it.

I mark the doors that have changed with a small piece of pale bark tucked into a crack. The bark is damp. The bark is always the same bark. I do not know where I find it, but I find it every morning, in the early light, when I begin my walk. I find it in the same place, or I find it in a different place, and the different place is also the same place, and the plateau has decided I will need it and has it ready. I tuck it into the crack, and the door that has changed is marked, and the people who consult me know to avoid it, or to seek it out, depending on what has changed. A door that is open today may be closed tomorrow. A door that opens onto the same room from both sides today may, tomorrow, open onto a room that is not the same room, or onto a room that has no other side. A door that was a wall yesterday may be a door today. I do not know why the doors change. I have theories. The plateau does not share them with me. The plateau shares only the knowledge of the changing, and the knowledge is enough.

I am slightly deaf in one ear. This is a thing the cairn-tenders and I share, though theirs came from listening for the stones, and mine came from listening for the doors. I do not remember when I lost the hearing. I do not remember if I ever had it. I think the hum took it from me, the way the hum takes small things from all of us, and gives us other things in return. The hum gave me the knowledge of the doors. The hum gave me the damp bark. The hum gave me the patience to walk the plateau in the early morning, when the air is thick and the structures sing under my fingers. I am not unhappy. I am patient with the doors the way the doors are patient with me. I am patient with the plateau the way the plateau is patient with itself. I do not know if I will always be the door-walker. I know that the children are born knowing the sound, and the hum grows louder as they grow, and some of them will become the door-walkers after me, and the plateau will permit it, and the wind will be the same, and the hum will be the same, and I will become a cairn, or I will sink into a pool, or I will simply be the memory of a door that opened once, a long time ago, onto a room that no one else remembers.

I am the door-walker. I have been the door-walker for as long as anyone can recall. I will be the door-walker until the plateau permits me to be something else, and the something else will be patient, and I will be patient with it. This is enough. This is what the hum gives.
