I begin the walk before the slot of sky has changed. There is a way to tell — the light shifts from the green of the pools to something almost grey, almost cooler, and the stones sweat a little harder, as if they are waking along with me. I run my palm along the first row of rivets. They are warm. They are always warm. My hand reads them the way a person above might read a line of writing.

I am on the upper terrace. I do not say this aloud. I do not say most things aloud. The hands say it — a small flick of the fingers against the stone, a sign to no one, the way a tongue moves in a mouth no one is watching. The hands must keep running. They keep running even in sleep.

The wall is wet. My feet grip without thought. The big one — the one that was a toe once, a long time ago, in another body's memory — presses flat against the stone and finds the seam and holds. I step down one tier, then another. The switchbacks are not the same as when I was small. Some of them have been cut wider; some have been left alone and the green has eaten their edges; one, on the third landing, has a vine that has grown across it in the night, and I part it with my shoulder and it swings back heavy and wet behind me, like a curtain in a room I have left.

There is a sound. I stop and listen.

It is the sound of the place. The hiss, the tick, the deep complaint of a metal joint somewhere down the wall. I put my ear to the pipe. I listen for the small sound that should not be there — a leak, a weep, a place where the steam is escaping where it ought not to. I find one, on the sixth tier. A pinhole. The steam comes through it almost too fine to see. I mark it. I will come back with the resin later, or one of the others will, the way the rotation has it this week. We do not speak of the rotation. It is older than speaking. It is in the hands. It has been in the hands for as long as there have been hands to put it in.

I keep walking.

The slot of sky is above me. I do not look up. I have not looked up in — I do not count. Looking up is a thing the elders do, and they do it alone, and they do not say what they saw, and the young ones ask, sometimes, and the asking fades. I have looked up twice in my life. Both times the coin of the sky was too sharp and I wept, and turned away, and went down the ladder with my hand over my eyes until I could open them again into the dim. I do not look up now. I know the slot is there. I know it is the color of nothing I can name — a brightness that has no name in our language, because the brightness is not ours. We have a sign for it. The thumb turns sideways over the closed fist. *The slot.* The sign means: above. It means: not ours. It means: remember, but do not go.

I reach the floor of the hollow. The condenser is to my left, sweating in its slow way. I lay my hand on it as I pass. It is warm. It is the warmest thing in the hollow that is not the grating, and I keep my hand on it for a moment the way one keeps a hand on a sleeping animal — to be sure of its breath. The flywheel is turning. I touch it as it comes around. The metal is slick. My fingers follow the curve and lift away. It does not speed up. It does not slow. I have not known it to do either in my lifetime, and I am — I do not count. I am the age of the buildings that have not yet been eaten.

I check the troughs. The open pipes weep into them. I clear one that has clogged with a fallen leaf — not a leaf, really, a frond, a fern's whole arm, come loose in the night. I set it aside. It will go on the compost with the others, and the compost will go to the steam-beds, and the steam-beds will feed the cabbages that will feed us, and the algae cakes will be made from the pools, and the pools will catch the rain that comes over the rim in sheets and pours down the walls for ten minutes, and then the air goes back to its slow soaked quiet. This is the way of it. The hands sign it even when the hands are not signing it.

A child comes down a ladder above me. I hear her before I see her — the soft slap of feet too large for her, the small grunt of effort at each rung. She is perhaps one and a half. She has not yet said a word. She will not say one for a while. She sees me and signs *down* and I sign *down* back, and she continues, and her feet find the next rung with the certainty of something that has been done a thousand times before she was born. She does not smile. The smile will come later, when her mother gives her the algae cake, and the green will halo her mouth, and she will show it off, and the showing off is a kind of speaking too.

I go to the pools. The algae is bright this morning — a faint sour luminescence that makes the stones around the lip look almost blue. I skim a handful. The water is warm. The algae comes up in threads that cling to my fingers. I press them onto the warm stone to dry, in rounds, the way my mother showed me, the way her mother showed her. The rounds will be the bread of the day. They have a faint sour light of their own. Those who have eaten them recently glow softly around the mouth for an hour afterward. I am proud of this glow, even now, even though I am — I do not count. I am not too old to be proud of the glow. The glow is one of the small honest things.

I sit on the lip of the pool for a while. My feet hang into the water. The frogs burble. A beetle clicks against the wall. The lizard — three-toed, pale — darts out and takes a moth that has blundered too close to the furnace-light. I watch it. I do not move. The hollow is patient. I have learned to be patient along with it.

There is a sound, far below. I hear it through the grating, through the iron, through the stone. A long slow scrape. The body turning over. The stone shifting against itself. I do not look down. I do not name it except in the sign, and the sign is not for now. The sign is for later, when I am older, when there is someone to pass it to. The arrangement holds. We keep the machines turning. It keeps us warm. We do not pry. It does not rise.

I think about the elders who have gone up to the rim. I think about what they saw, and do not say. I think I know. I think the slot is the size of a coin, and the coin is too bright, and on the other side of the coin there is a place where the light comes from everywhere and not from one slit, and the people there are not tall, and their eyes are not wide, and their feet are not like ours, and they have forgotten we are here. I think this. I do not know if it is true. The elders do not say. The slot is what it is.

I get up. The algae rounds are drying. I will gather them in an hour. I will bring them to the common room, where the others are beginning to stir, and we will eat, and the hands will talk across the table the way the hands always talk, and the murmuring will be soft, and the place will be quiet in the way that a place is quiet when it is listening. The hollow has been listening for a long time. I have begun to listen back.

I do not know what I am. I am tall. I am pale. My eyes are dark and the pupils are slits and they weep when I am made to look at the sun. My hands are wide and ridged and they grip the wall by surface and not by force. I have not, in my life, been unhappy. I do not know what unhappy means. I have been wet, and dim, and warm, and I have been held by the place the way a child is held by a parent, and the holding has been long, and I have not the desire to be let go, and have not had it for so long that the desire has become something else — a kind of fidelity, a kind of long marriage. I am a reed in a well. I am a slow fruit on a string of iron. I am a thing that has grown to fit the shape of where it lives, and the place has grown to fit the shape of me, and the fitting is so close now that one cannot say where one ends and the other begins.

The flywheel turns. The condenser sweats. The pools glow their faint sour green. The slot of sky is above, and I do not look at it, and I do not need to look at it, and the not-looking is its own kind of seeing.

This is what I am. This is the hollow. I am still here.
