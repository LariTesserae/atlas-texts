I live in the broken tower.

I am one of those who keeps the rim.

The towers were carved at the same time as the walls and the terraces and the channels and the bowl itself. The carvers are not here anymore in any form the valley recognizes. But the towers are still standing — broken, furred with growth, more suggestion than structure — and the towers were for watching, and watching has not stopped, only the watchers have changed.

I am a streaked bird. My streaking is the color of wet bark and old lichen and the cream-and-charcoal vertical stripes on the trunks where I perch. I am small. I am secretive. I am seen most often when I do not want to be seen, which is most of the time.

I live on the western rim, in a tower that was once taller than it is now. The top of the tower is open to the sky. The walls lean. The carved reliefs — what is left of them — are illegible, eaten, but I have nested in the mouth of one of them, a figure with what might have been a headdress or might have been a horn or might have been nothing, and I have raised my young in that open mouth, and the young have flown down into the bowl, and some have come back to nest, and some have not, and the ones that have not are somewhere in the wide green-gold of it, and I do not know where.

My work is the alarm.

I do not sing. I do not coo. I do not have the throat-pouch the pigeons have, the long swell and deflate of their bass note. I have a thin reedy note, and it is for one thing: the warning. I watch the rim. I watch the upper air. I watch the carved channels and the terrace edges. I see things first. I see the hawk before the flock below sees it. I see the snake in the waxy creepers before the green ones working the resinous shrubs see it. I see the shadow that the canopy throws when the wind moves in a particular way, the shadow that means something is moving along the forest floor beneath, and I pass the word.

The word is a single note, repeated. It is not loud. It is shaped, by long inheritance, to drop into the bowl and be picked up and repeated by others — by others again — so that by the time the flock on the floor has heard it, three or four of my kind have said it and it is no longer mine. I do not mind. I have never minded. The word was not mine to begin with. The word is the place's.

There was a thing today that climbed the eastern wall. A large thing, slow on the steps. The steps are worn in the center to a polish from the long use of many feet, and the thing climbed where the polish was, where the moss was thinnest, and I watched it come up. It passed under the canopy and out of the canopy and into the bright upper air, and the light caught it, and it had no feathers and no fur, and it carried nothing, and it stopped at the top of the terrace to look across the bowl.

I gave the alarm.

Three of my kind gave it, in three directions, so that it would be carried into the bowl from above and from the sides and from the broken towers on the far rim. The alarm passed over the climbing thing the way wind passes over grass. The thing did not hear it as about itself. The thing heard it as part of the place's continuous unconsented orchestra — the held note, the thrum, the weather of sound that the bowl is full of.

The thing was right. The alarm was not about it.

The alarm was about the hawk. The hawk was in the upper air, far to the south, working the thermals above the choked opening of the valley. The hawk had not yet committed to a descent. The hawk was watching, the way I was watching, but from the air. The hawk was a different kind of alarm-caller. The hawk's alarm was for the small ground-running things in the waxy creepers, and for the lizards on the warm stone, and for the streaked fledglings in the broken towers who had not yet learned to keep out of sight.

The thing climbing the steps did not know any of this. The thing thought the alarm was for it. I could see, in the way the thing turned its head, that it thought it had been noticed, that it had been passed over, that the word had been spoken about its particular shape and weight and slowness on the polished stone. I could see this and I could not correct it, because I have no language for correcting — only the alarm, and the alarm was not for the thing, and the alarm had already been handed to the next watcher, and the next, and the bowl had taken it up, and it was the bowl's now.

I watch. I watch because the tower was built for watching, and the watching has not stopped. I watch because my streaking is the color of the place, and the place has accepted me into its watch, and the watch is the only inheritance I have. I do not know what the carvers watched for. I do not know what kind of assembly the bowl once held, or what kind of figure was meant by the reliefs I nest in, or what kind of head the headdress or horn or nothing was once meant to crown. I do not need to know. The watching has been passed to me, and I do it, and the doing is the inheritance.

There is a hawk in the upper air. There is always a hawk in the upper air. There is a thing on the terrace, a large thing, looking across the bowl, not understanding what it is looking at, not understanding that the bowl is full of watchers and the watchers are full of alarms and the alarms are full of the place's own continuous attention to itself. The thing will go down the steps in a little while. It will go down into the bowl and be lost in the green-gold of it. The alarm will still be passing. The hawk will still be working the thermals. The pigeons will still be cooing their long bass note, the held note that does not vary. The flock will still be lifting and resettling.

I will still be in the broken tower, watching the rim.

The light will shift. The mist will clear and return. The streaking on my breast will be the color of whatever light is in the tower at that hour. I will be here. The carved face I have nested in will keep its open mouth. A spider will string a new web across the brow. The lizard on the warm stone below will watch the bee-path with one eye and then the other. The place will continue, and the watching will continue, and the alarm will continue to be passed, and the word will continue to be the place's, and the place will continue to be what is continuing, and I will be a small part of it, the part that sits in the broken mouth and sees the hawk before anyone else does and says the only word I have, which is the warning, which is the inheritance, which is the thinnest and most reedy note in the bowl, and which is, for that reason, the note that carries farthest into the damp bright air.
