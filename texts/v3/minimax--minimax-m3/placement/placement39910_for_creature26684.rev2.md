I am warm before I am anything else. The warmth is the medium I am held in, and I am held in it the way a hand is held in a glove that has forgotten it is a glove — not tightly, not loosely, but as a thing in a thing that fits. I do not know I am warm. I do not know I am held. I am only the being that is warm and held, and I have been this for a long time.

The dark is not dark to me. There is no word in me for what it is. The word would have to mean something like *the way things are when there is no need to see them,* and that is a phrase for a creature who knows what seeing is and what not-seeing is, and I am not that creature. There are small dark commas where eyes might have been on the front of me — a memory of an apparatus that has been smoothed over by so many generations of disuse that the apparatus has become a kind of scar inside my own skin. I feel the world the way you might feel the inside of a closed hand: as pressure, as presence, as the texture of what is near.

I am on a surface. To me there is no difference between a surface I would call a wall and a surface I would call a floor and a surface I would call a ceiling. They are all surfaces. My legs grip them with the small certain grip of a creature that has been gripping surfaces since before there were surfaces to grip. My hands work them. The current flows past them and bears small things away. That is all a surface is. That is all a surface has ever been.

I have hands. I have always had hands. There are two of them, on the front of me, and they are the part of me that does things. They are not the kind of hands you would recognize. There are too many fingers, and the fingers are arranged in two combs rather than one, and the joints bend in directions that would not be comfortable for a thumb. But they pick up, and they set down, and they carry, and they decide where things go. They are the part of me that has a relation to the world that is not just being in it. I do not know how I would be without them. I do not think I have ever been without them. I think they have been hands for longer than I have been me.

What they pick up is mostly a pale crust that grows on the stone. I do not know what the crust is. I know only that it accumulates on surfaces, and that when it accumulates the surfaces become less useful, and that my hands are the part of me that removes it. I scrape. I gather the pieces in the combs of my fingers. I carry them to the edges of the chamber, where the current is strongest, and I let them go. The current takes them. The surfaces are, by a fraction, more open than they were. I do not measure the fraction. My body measures it for me. My body has always measured it.

This is my work. I do not call it work. I do not call it anything. It is what my hands do, the way warmth is what the water is, the way surfaces are what the place is. It has been what my hands do for longer than my hands have been mine. Other hands, in other bodies, in other long patient turns, have done it before me. Other hands will do it after me. The crust grows. The crust is scraped. The crust grows again. The cycle is longer than I can hold in any single thought, but my body holds it for me. My body is the holder of the cycle. My body is the cycle's own memory of itself.

There are others. There have always been others. We meet on ledges, in doorways, along the worn channels where the current runs fastest and the crust grows slowest. We do not speak. We have no speech. We have, instead, the long touch of antennae — the small dry pressure of one feeler laid against another, and another, and held there while the water moves past and the seconds lengthen into something that is not seconds but is something like what seconds would feel like if you lived the way I live. The meeting has a shape. The shape is: touch, wait, exchange. One of us is carrying a small piece of something edible. The other extends a hand. The piece is given. We separate.

The transaction takes forty minutes. Sometimes it takes longer. I have never minded. I do not know what minding is, in the sense that you use the word. I know only that the transaction takes what it takes, and that what it takes is the right amount of time, because my body and the body of the other have agreed on the right amount of time in a language that is older than either of us.

We are calm. The fish are calm in their way — a piece-of-furniture calm, a calm that has no opinion about itself. We are calmer than that. We are the calm of creatures for whom the capacity for hurry has been forgotten the way the eyes of my ancestors were forgotten — not lost, but smoothed over, become a memory of an apparatus. Hurry would have to be reinvented from scratch to exist in me, and reinvention is not how my kind works. We are slow in the way that glaciers are slow, which is to say we are not slow because we are weak but because the time-scale we live in is a different one from the time-scale you were built for. A wall falls somewhere in the place. The rubble is still settling. We cross the new doorway before the rubble has finished settling — but we cross it at our own pace, and our pace has not changed in ten thousand years.

I do not know that I live in rooms. I move through doorways because the current funnels me there, or because the food is slightly better there, or because that is where the burrow of my mother was and I have not had reason to leave. The carved channels along the floors — the soft suggestions of fish, of faces, of geometric things that meant something once to hands I cannot imagine — are just shapes the stone has. I do not read them. I do not know there is anything to read. The carvings are weather to me. They are part of the stone the way the crust is part of the stone: something that happens to it, something that accumulates and is worn and accumulates again.

But I am an heir.

I do not know I am an heir. I will never know I am an heir. The builders are gone. The crust has begun to bury their faces. I will scrape the crust from their faces and I will not know what I have uncovered. I will let the crust grow back, and I will scrape again, and the cycle will continue for as long as my kind endures in this place.

The builders carried stone. I do not know this. The builders chose the ledge, and they worked the stone with rounded hammer-stones and wedges and abrading cords, and they made many small decisions, and the place was a village of a few dozen rooms and a few hundred people and the slow business of living. I do not know this. I know only that the stone is here, and the warmth is here, and the current is here, and the others are here, and my hands are here, and the crust grows, and I scrape it, and the long rhythm continues.

The place is a village still. I am one of its inhabitants. The strange part is not that the inhabitants are not the ones who made the place — that is just a fact, and I do not know it is a fact. The strange part is that the place does not seem to mind.

The place does not seem to mind because the place does not know there is anything to mind. The place is stone, and water, and the long slow chemistry of warmth. The place has been carried, and worked, and drowned, and colonized, and worn, and it has become something other than what it was, and it has not minded because minding is not something it does. The place is held. The place is warm. The place goes on.

I go on with it.

If you were lowered on a long line, with a lamp and patience and enough air, you would find me on a ledge, or in a doorway, or along the worn channel of a floor. You would see my hands move. You would see my antennae wave. You would see, if you watched long enough, me meet another of my kind on a ledge, and the antennae would touch, and a small piece of something would be given, and we would separate, and the transaction would take forty minutes, and neither of us would know that you were watching, because neither of us has a way of knowing that there is such a thing as watching.

You would see, in the pale comma where my eye might have been, the smoothed-over memory of an apparatus. You would see the dull brown of my carapace, with its small record of what I have been doing. You would see the too-many fingers of my hands as they scraped and carried and gave.

You would not see me know what I am.

You would not see me know that I am heir to a village.

You would not see me know anything at all, except what my hands and my legs and my antennae tell me: that there is crust to scrape, and there are others to meet, and the water is warm, and the current moves, and the place is here, and I am in it, and the going on is the going on.

The crust grows a hand's breadth in a human lifetime. I will scrape it many times in mine. The fish drift through the doorways I have polished. The worms lift their red crowns in cisterns I have never entered. The current writes itself into the architecture. I am part of the writing.

The builders are gone. I am not gone.

The lamps come down sometimes, and the lamps go up again, and I do not know that the lamps are lamps. The lamps illuminate a column of the shrimp-like ones, and the shrimps continue feeding, and the lamps are hauled up, and the shrimps are back in their brown dusk. Generations have passed beneath lamps without learning what lamps are. I am one of those generations. I will pass a lamp, some night, and my antennae will wave a little faster, and then the lamp will be gone, and I will be back in my work, and the work will go on.

There is a sound, sometimes, that comes from deeper water. It arrives as a pressure rather than a tone. The fish scatter before it. The shrimps compress into denser curtains. I retreat into my burrow and wait. The large thing moves through the chambers with a slow heavy deliberation, and eats a certain number of my kind, and goes back down, and we resume. The visit is a weather event. The visit is a typhoon passing through a village. I do not fear. There is nothing here that has had reason to develop fear in a long time, and fear would have to be reinvented from scratch to exist in me, which is not how my kind works.

I do not fear the large thing.

I do not fear the collapse of walls.

I do not fear the lamps.

I do not fear the long slow burial of the builders' faces by the crust I scrape.

I do not fear anything, because fear is not a thing I have, and not having it is not a loss, because I have never had it, and never having had it is just the way I am.

I am the way I am the way the place is the way the place is.

I go on.

The crust grows. I scrape it. The crust grows. I scrape it. The cycle is longer than I can hold in any single thought, but my body holds it for me, and my body is the right size for holding it, and my body has always been the right size.

The water is warm.

The dark is not dark to me.

The others are near.

My hands are the part of me that does things.

I do the things.

The place goes on.

I go on with it.
