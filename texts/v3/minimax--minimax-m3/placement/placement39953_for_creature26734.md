I am on the third tier. Eastern rope. The kin-group is called Small-in-the-Rain, though there has not been rain in this place for longer than the oldest among us can remember, and the name is one of those names that has become a thing-in-itself, no longer pointing outward at anything.

I am in the braiding circle.

We are five this evening — though *evening* is not quite right, because the hollow does not have evenings in the way a place with a sun has evenings. The light shifts in its slow amber way, and the drift of insects overhead thickens or thins, and these are our hours, and one of them is now. We have been sitting for a while. The cord has grown long enough to pass across three laps and back. The fibers are from three sources: a shed skin from the small things that eat the fungi where we have told it not to grow; a gut from a creature caught two cycles ago by someone on the western side who owed us a debt; hair, which is rare here, because there is so little hair on any of us, but which I contributed from my own forearms — the brush along the trailing edge, the part that reads the air, that I cut carefully and set aside over many sleeps.

We speak in breath. The sentence I have begun is about the cord. It is about whether we will sign it tonight or wait until the next thickening of the drift, and whether the signature will include a particular knot that one of us — her name I will not say in full, only the compound initial sound — has been advocating for, because the knot in question carries a meaning the rest of us have not agreed upon, and there is a small question of precedence.

I do not finish the sentence. The unfinished sentence is a statement. It says: I have heard the proposal. I am considering it. I will not commit until I have sat with it a while longer. The circle hears this. The cord passes.

The hollow is loud around us, in the way a river is loud to someone who has lived beside it always — present, continuous, not requiring attention. Beneath the immediate sounds of our own making I can hear the tap-tap-tap of the kin-group three tiers above us, working a new joint, and the slower scrape of someone polishing a vessel in the second tier, and the brief silence that means a creature has just been taken in the wall to my left, somewhere between the cord and the husk behind me, and the returning rhythm that has already adjusted around the absence.

I am, by my own count, in the middle of my life. Not the middle of my tier — I have lived in this tier since I was small, and I will likely live in it until I am placed in the stratum below. But the middle of my years. My hands have begun to wear into the shape of the things they have made, and the things I have made have begun to wear into the shape of my hands, and the two are becoming, slowly, one thing. I have an apprentice. He is young. He has just begun to hold a blade wrong, and in a few more cycles he will hold it right, and after that he will hold it the way I hold it, and after that he will hold it the way no one holds it, and that will be the moment when he must be given a blade of his own — or, if there is one that fits, the blade of someone who died.

I think about the first tier sometimes. Not often. Going down there is a thing one does at certain seasons, when there is a burial or a ceremony, and I have done it four times now. The last time was to bury a blade — a small one, a chisel, worn smooth by the hands of the one who had used it to fit the third-tier western wall in a year I do not remember the name of. The chisel was the only thing we had of her. I carried it down myself. The lowest tier is dark and compressed and the air is different there — heavier, with a particular smell I have no single word for, because we do not have a word for *very old*, only compounds that gesture toward it. The ceremony is short. We speak the name once. We set the thing in the wall, in a place we have chosen because the wall there has a soft lobe of fungi growing, and the fungi will, over the next many cycles, grow over and around it, and in time it will be part of the wall, and the wall will be part of the tier, and the tier will be part of the floor that the next settlement builds upon.

When I came back up, the light seemed brighter than it had been when I went down. It was not. The light does not change. But my eyes had widened in the dark, and they had not yet adjusted, and for a little while the third tier was a place of too much amber, and I sat with it and let it pass.

I will go down again, perhaps two more times before I myself am carried down. I do not know who will carry me. It will be someone whose hands are smaller than mine, because I will have become smaller by then, the way we all do — the body gives its calcium to the walls, to the next stratum, to the fungi that grows in the joints, and what is left is a small pressed thing that fits in the palm of a hand. They will speak my name once. They will set me in the wall. The fungi will take me. The tier will press down on me. The next settlement will be built, and I will be in the floor of it, and I will be the word in someone's name several generations from now, and I will be the small specific thing inside the larger structures, and that will be the whole of me, and the whole of me will be enough.

But that is not now. Now I am in the braiding circle, and the cord is passing, and the sentence I did not finish has been heard, and the one with the compound initial sound has accepted my not-finishing and is now saying something else, also in breath, also at the volume of our making, and the cord grows longer, and my hands remember the knots they have made, and the brush along my forearms reads the small draft that has come down from somewhere above, where someone has climbed a ladder or opened a seam.

This is the place. This is who I am.
