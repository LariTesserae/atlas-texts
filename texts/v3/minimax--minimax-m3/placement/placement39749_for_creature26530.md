I am the morning call.

Three short and one long, sent through the wall with the heel of my hand. I do not decide to send it. My hand decides. The bone of the pillar receives it the way a throat receives a breath, and the vibration goes down, down through the trunk, out through the root-network that runs beneath the pale rock between pillars, and finds its way to three other pillars where someone is awake and waiting for the proof. The answer comes back soft, a knock in the soles of my feet, in my teeth, in the back of my hand still resting on the wall. *We know.* I did not need to be told I was awake. The telling is not for me. The telling is for the network.

I have sent this call for sixty years. Before me, my mother sent it. Before her, hers. Nine generations of mornings in this pillar, or pillars like it, the bone thickening slightly along the lineage, the blood darkening, the chest narrowing a thumb's width each century in the direction the place requires. I am tall. I am pale. The skin at the inside of my wrist is the color of the dim sun through bone — faintly yellowed, faintly chalky. My eyes are large and the corners have grown a small fold I can pull half across when the methane rises in the long evenings. I do this without thinking. The newcomers use masks.

I have a child asleep in the lower chamber. My child did not wake when I sent the call. My child will wake to the meal-knock, two short and two short and one long, sent by whoever is cooking this morning in the pillar two over, and will come up the grown stairs through the bone and sit across from me and eat whatever has been grown or synthesized and we will not speak much because speaking is loud here and the bone is quieter than the mouth. My child has never asked the first question. My child has been born into the assumption that water is in us and will be in us again and that is all, and my child is correct, and my child has no grief about it, and I am glad of this in a way I do not examine.

I remember in the body. Sometimes, when the wind is very low and the chord has thinned to a single long drone, I feel the pull of a wetness my great-grandparents had. It is not a thought. It is in the blood. It is a grief that has been made into a peace that goes on happening, the way the pillars have made a peace with the wind, which is to say it is a peace that does not finish. I do not speak of it. The old ones do not speak of it. We have learned the shape of it and we let it be the shape.

This morning the light through my window is the color of old paper. The wind has not stopped. It never stops. It comes off the higher peaks to the north and is funneled across the plateau in long ribbons, and my pillar is singing a low note, a stopped-organ-pipe note, the kind of note you sleep inside. Outside, on the walkways, the air is violet-gray and the haze hangs above and the call-birds have begun. Already I can hear them — a thousand competing songs from a thousand pillars, overlapping into the static that does not resolve. My pillar adds its note beneath. The shuttles on their cables add their hum. I am in the chord. The chord has a place for me — a small drone, a low continuo, the sound of someone present.

I am not the only one. There are perhaps four thousand pillars visible from any one vantage and there are perhaps sixty kilometers of settlement and there are perhaps nine generations of us and there are a hundred named species of fauna and there are the chitter-things uncounted and the gliders uncounted and the call-birds uncounted and the burrowers surfacing at night in their small craters that fill in over the years. All of this is the chord. I am one strand of it. I am a strand that has been here long enough to forget that it is a strand and to believe it is a person, which is a useful belief, which is what the place asks of us.

The place asks us to stay. That is the only asking. The pillars do not ask. The wind does not ask. The plateau does not ask. They simply are, and they go on, and we have agreed to be part of the going on.

At midday the wind will climb and the call-birds will be most active and the chord will thicken into something almost solid, almost a city you can walk into. At night the breath will rise from each pillar in pale columns and braid where they meet and the call-birds will drop into a lower register and a knock will come through the bone from a far pillar and someone will answer and the place will change key but not quiet, never quiet, the eye deceived by the sparse dim plateau and the ear hearing a forest, a city, a congregation that has never agreed on what to sing and has stopped trying.

I will sit in my chamber through all of it. I will send the rhythms. I will harvest the night's condensation from the sides of my window with my hands, cupping it, drinking what gathers. I will feel the knocks in my bones. I will not leave.

Names, the old-timers say, are for things that might leave. I do not have a name in the old way. I have a rhythm. I have a note in the chord. I have a morning call and an answering knock and a long slow pulse I will send, someday, when there is a birth in a far pillar, and the soft answering pulse I will receive, and the place will go on, and the plateau will go on, and the wind will move through, and the call-birds will sing, and I will be in the bone of it, in the braid of it, in the agreement of it.

I am the morning call. I am the one who sends it. I am the one who receives the answer. I am the same thing. I have been here long enough that the difference has become a kind of regularity, like the plateau's irregularities, like the wind's variations, like the peace that goes on happening.

I am awake. We know. The chord continues.
