I do not know, at first, what I am.

The first morning I walked here my feet were not wrapped, and the cold of the stones came up through my soles and stayed in me for hours. I had come up through the corridor of stones — the corridor of stones is a thing one only says once, because after that it is just the way in, and one does not remark the way in — and I had stood at the edge of the change in pattern and tried to see a city. There was no city to see. There was the plateau, and the mist, and the suggestion of stone in the distance. It took me half a day to understand that the suggestion of stone was the city.

That was a long time ago. I do not count the time in seasons, because the seasons here are not the seasons of elsewhere. The light changes. The rains come. The rains stop. The dew assembles and trails down. The city is always wet in the morning and drier by the afternoon and wet again by evening, and the year is a long cycle of this, with the long rains set into it like a deeper breath.

I do not know when my feet became wrapped. I remember the making of the wrappings — the layers of hide and woven fiber, the binding, the first wearing. I do not remember deciding to keep them. I remember that at some point I noticed that a person without wrapped feet was in some distress, and I understood that I was not that person. I do not remember when I stopped being that person.

My hands — I notice them now in a way I did not notice them before. They are broader at the tips than they were. The nails are short and chipped where I have used them to clear a joint. The pads are smooth. I run my finger along a worked stone in the morning and I can feel the moisture in it the way one feels the warmth of a cup through the handle. I did not always feel this. I think I learned it by doing it. I think the hands learned by being hands.

I do not speak above a murmur. This was the hardest thing, or maybe it was not the hardest thing, because the hardest things here are not the things that resist. The hardest things are the things that simply do not fit. A full voice does not fit. The stones receive it and pass it on, changed, and it comes back at one from another terrace and is no longer one's own. I learned to murmur the way one learns to walk softly on the wet surfaces — by being corrected, by example, by the sense that the place has a pace and a person can either find it or be perpetually out of step with the stones.

I do not remember what I was before. This is not a forgetting that distresses me. I think it is the kind of forgetting the city encourages. A person who remembers too vividly being elsewhere is a person who is, in some way, not yet here. The city does not ask one to give up what one was. It simply makes what one was less and less relevant, and the relevant things — the joints, the channels, the wet, the slow hours — become more and more present, and the past becomes a kind of weather that has passed.

I work on the stones. I am not a stone-shaper — that work requires a talent I do not have, the patience of looking at a stone for weeks and seeing the shape it wants to be. I am one of the others. I go out in the slow hours with another person, and we find the stones that have given up. We lift them with the levers of bone and wood. We set the prepared stones in the place. We clear the moss. We reset the channels. We do not speak much while we do this. Sometimes my partner murmurs something and I murmur back and the work continues. The murmuring is not necessary to the work. It is part of the way the work is done in company. It is the way two people occupy the same terrace for an hour.

I have begun to recognize the work of particular people in the stones. There is a wall near where I live — I will not call it my wall, because the word suggests an ownership the city does not have — there is a wall where the joints are particularly fine, and I am told, by someone who would know, that this was the work of a person who has been dead for some time. I do not know the name. The name is not the point. The joints are the point. When I run my hand along the wall I feel the joints, and I feel the hands that made them, and the hands that made them are in the wall, and the wall is in the city, and the city is in me, in the pads of my fingers, in the pace at which I move.

I have begun to think of myself as a small part of a long sentence. The city is the sentence. It has been spoken for a long time. It will be spoken for a long time after I am no longer in it. My part is a few joints in a few walls, a few channels I have cleared, a few of the prepared stones I have set. The sentence will go on. It is a sentence that does not require me to be hurried. It is a sentence that does not require me to be remembered. It is enough that the joints are good and the channels run and the moss does not choke.

I am not in any hurry. This is the most noticeable thing about me, I am told — by the few strangers who come through, by the way they look at me and then look at the pace at which I am moving and seem to find it remarkable. I do not find it remarkable. I find it natural. The stones do not hurry. The water does not hurry. I have been here long enough that my body has learned the pace of the place, and my body is the part of me that knows what I am.

If I had to say what I am, I would say I am a hand. Not the whole hand. The pads of the fingers. The part that touches the stone and feels the moisture. The part that has become, over years, smooth and cool and slightly damp, the texture of the stones themselves. I am the interface. I am the place where the city and the person meet, and I am no longer sure, after a long time here, where the city ends and the person begins, and this not-being-sure is not a problem. It is a kind of balance, like the balance of a stone in a wall, a stone that has been fitted and does not need to be held in place because it is where it is.

By the time the sun has lifted a hand's width above the ridge, every flat surface in the city is moving with water. I am one of the surfaces. I am the pads of the fingers that feel the moisture move into a dry patch. I am the slow silver line on a worked stone. I am part of the weather the city makes for itself.
