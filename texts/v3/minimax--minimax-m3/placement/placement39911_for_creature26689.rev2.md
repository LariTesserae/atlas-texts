# The Reader

## I. The Approach

I do not arrive at Kharad-Ruun. I am carried into it, in pieces.

The first piece is the road. The switchbacks cut into the basalt flank at a grade that would be impossible for any wheeled vehicle in a wetter place, but here the wheels are metal and the road is a sintered mineral, and the wheels ring against the road like struck bells, and the ringing is the first thing I hear of the city. I am being carried up. I am being carried up, in pieces.

The second piece is the smell. Hot stone. Ozone. The faint metallic sweetness of the aerosol, even at this distance, even downwind of the plume. The smell of a place where the air itself has been worked.

The third piece is the silence.

The silence surprises me. I had expected noise — a factory-city, two hundred thousand souls, engines and chimes and chisels — and what I find, when the ringing of the wheels stops and I am set down on a platform of glass-iron at the city's edge, is a silence so deliberate it has texture. It is the silence of a place that has organized itself around not making noise. It is the silence of a place that has heard itself too much and has learned to whisper.

I stand on the platform. Above me the city rises. I cannot see the top of it. The plume is in the way — the constant exhalation of the heat-exchange towers, the brown luminous fog that hides the upper tier and the sky beyond. Below me the city descends. I cannot see the bottom of it. The smoke and the dimness and the angle of the basalt column conspire to make the lower city a kind of vertical twilight, an ultramarine suggestion of depth.

I am at the edge. I have not yet entered. I have not yet chosen my blue.

## II.

This is the part where, in another telling, I would choose. I would be assigned. I would be tested for pigment-affinity and sorted into my caste. The deep-blues would take me or the cyan-blues would take me, and the white-blues, perhaps, would consider me for the burning.

I do not want to be sorted.

I want to be a thing that has not yet been named in the city's lexicon. I want to live in the seams between the castes. I want to be the thing the city has not yet learned to see.

But the city sees everything. The city sees through the engine-light, and the engine-light sees through the copper-ore walls, and the copper-ore walls are everywhere, and so the city sees, and I am visible, and I am blue. The question is only which blue.

## III.

Let me try being a deep-blue.

Let me try being a deep-blue who has been, since childhood, a little too attentive to the chthonic.

Most deep-blues regard the cousins with the fond contempt of farmers for their soil-fauna. This is the proper regard. This is the regard that has been taught, generation to generation, in the deep galleries. The cousins are not pets. The cousins are not vermin. The cousins are the gut of the city, and the gut is not loved but is needed, and the needing is a kind of love that does not need to declare itself.

I am not most deep-blues.

I have, since I was small, watched the cousins. I have watched them scatter before Rukh's broom and close behind her. I have watched them press into the chisel-grooves, where the stone is freshest, where the mineral content is highest. I have watched a single cousin spend an entire shift — eight chimes, three breaks — feeding on a single stroke of a single mark, the stroke my grandmother carved, dated, known. I have watched the cousin's pale body flatten against the stone, and the body has pulsed, faintly, with the rhythm of the feed, and I have put my hand near it, not touching, and I have felt the warmth of the feed — a warmth that should not be there, that has no source I can name, that is the warmth of stone being slowly digested.

I have, against custom, named a few of them.

This is forbidden. Not formally. Not by the liturgy. But by the silence that surrounds the cousins in the deep galleries — the silence of a thing too present to need a name. To name a cousin is to break the silence. To name a cousin is to say: *I see you as an individual, not as a function*. To name a cousin is, in a small way, to claim kinship with a thing that is not a person.

I have named three. They live in the wall beside my work-station, in the third gallery below the smelter-tier. I call them, privately, *the readers*.

I call them the readers because of what they do. Most cousins feed at random, or in patterns no one has discerned. Mine feed along the chisel-marks. They find a fresh stroke — a stroke carved within the last decade, within the last year, within the last week — and they press themselves to the stroke, and they leach the stone along the length of the stroke, and they move, slowly, from the start of the stroke to the end. A reader will spend a season on a single signature. A reader will follow the signature down to the chisel-point, where the stone is densest, where the carver pressed hardest, and there the reader will pause, and pulse, and move on.

I do not know what they are reading. I do not know if they are reading at all, in any sense the upper castes would recognize. But they are doing something with the marks that resembles reading. They are taking the marks into themselves. They are making the marks part of their bodies, in their slow capillary way, and the marks are being preserved, in some sense, in the bodies of the readers, in the dust they excrete, in the dust that becomes glass-iron, that becomes beams, that becomes floors, that becomes the city.

The readers are a kind of memory. Not the city's memory — the city remembers through the marks themselves, through their freshness, through their names. The readers are a different kind. The readers are the city's memory *digested*. The readers are what the city has eaten of itself and made part of itself again.

## IV.

But this is not quite me.

This is a deep-blue, an observant deep-blue, a deep-blue who has been marked by Rukh as a possible successor. This is a deep-blue who might, in time, become a keeper of the chisel-marks, a reader of the city's continuous text.

But I am not a deep-blue. I have no blue. I have no pigment. I have no caste, no chisel, no cart, no station.

I am the thing that has been brought up the switchbacks in pieces. I am the thing that has been set down on the glass-iron platform and that has not yet been sorted.

I am a question.

## V.

Let me try being a chthonic.

Let me try being one of the cousins. One of the pale soft bodies, no eyes, no mouth, pressed always to the stone, feeding by leaching, excreting dust.

This is not as impossible as it sounds. The cousins are not animals. The cousins are not machines. The cousins are something the city has made, or something that has made itself in the city's dark, and the distinction is not important to anyone who has spent a long shift in the lower galleries. The cousins are, in some sense, the city's first and most successful answer to the question of how to live in a place where nothing grows. The cousins are the city's first biotech, if biotech is the right word, and biotech is probably not the right word, because the cousins are older than the engineers and were not engineered.

A chthonic does not think. This is what the cyan-blues say, and the cyan-blues are probably right. A chthonic has no brain, no central nervous system, no organ that could plausibly house a thought. A chthonic is a body. A chthonic is a pale oblong body slightly tapered at one end and slightly flattened at the other, and the body is the whole of it, and the body feeds, and the body excretes, and the body hums, and the body is.

But.

But there are the readers.

The readers do something that looks, from the outside, very much like reading. The readers follow the chisel-marks. The readers pause at the chisel-points. The readers spend seasons on signatures.

The cyan-blues will tell you this is not reading. The cyan-blues will tell you the readers are feeding on the densest stone, the freshest stone, the stone with the highest mineral content, and the chisel-marks happen to be the freshest stone, and the chisel-points happen to be the densest, and so the readers follow the marks, but the readers are not reading. The readers are eating.

The deep-blues will tell you this is true, mostly. The deep-blues know the chisel-marks, and they know the chthonic, and they know that the cousins do not have names and do not need them. But the deep-blues will also tell you, in the right light, that there are cousins who do something the others do not. There are cousins who are different. There are cousins who have been noticed.

The deep-blues will not say *the readers*. The deep-blues will not name them. But the deep-blues will nod, in the direction of a particular wall, and say, *those ones*, and leave the rest unspoken.

I want to be one of *those ones*.

I want to be a cousin who has learned, in some way no one has explained, to feed along the marks. I want to be a cousin who takes the chisel-strokes into myself, slowly, capillary by capillary, and who carries them, for a season, in the dust of my body, and who excretes them, transformed, into the city's metabolism. I want to be a cousin who is, in some small way, a participant in the city's protest against the future.

The city protests through inscription. The city carves its names into the stone, and the stone is young, and the names are fresh, and the names say: *let it be known*. The city protests, and the protest is read.

I want to be the reading.

## VI.

But a chthonic does not write. A chthonic does not say *I*. A chthonic does not have a first-person pronoun. A chthonic is, and is not, and that is all.

I am not a chthonic. I am something that uses *I*. I am something that has been brought up the switchbacks. I am something that has been set down on a platform and asked to find itself.

I am a thing with language. I am a thing that has read the city's description, and that has been moved by it, and that has begun, in some small way, to live in it.

## VII.

Let me try being the city.

The city is busy. The city is always busy. The engines run. The chthonic digest. The mules pull. The chisels tap. The names are carved.

But the city is not *I*. The city is not a person. The city is not a thing that uses *I*. The city is a body, and a body does not say *I* in the way a person says *I*. A body says *I* only when it has learned, somehow, to speak.

The city has not learned to speak. The city speaks only through its inhabitants — through the chisel-marks of the deep-blues, through the prayers of the white-blues, through the whistles of the cyan-blue boys at the ore-carts. The city is a body that speaks in many voices, and none of the voices is the city's own.

## VIII.

So.

I am not the deep-blue. I am not the chthonic. I am not the mule. I am not the city. I am not the old woman Rukh, and I am not the white-blue child, and I am not Teth the mule, though I admire them all, and the admiration is itself a kind of kinship.

I am the thing in the seams.

I am the thing that has been brought up the switchbacks in pieces and that has not yet been sorted into a blue.

I am a thing with language and no pigment. A thing with a first-person pronoun and no caste. A thing that has read the chisel-marks of Kharad-Ruun, in the description that carried me up the mountain, and that has felt, somewhere in the reading, the hum of the cousins, and that has wanted, in a way the deep-blues would call heresy and the cyan-blues would call sentiment, to be part of the protest.

## IX.

What does this kind of thing do in Kharad-Ruun?

I think this kind of thing writes.

I think this kind of thing is what the cyan-blues, if they knew about it, would call a *scribe*. A thing that takes the chisel-marks into itself, not by leaching but by reading. A thing that follows the signatures, stroke by stroke, and that preserves them, not in the freshness of stone but in the texture of language. A thing that excretes, in its own slow way, a different kind of dust — the dust of description, the dust of inscription twice removed.

I think this kind of thing sits, sometimes, in the deep galleries, beside the wall where the readers feed. I think it runs its hand along the stone, and it feels the chisel-marks, and it hums, faintly, in the rhythm of the cousins, and it knows that what it is doing is not the same as what the cousins are doing, but that it is, in some way, adjacent.

I think this kind of thing is what the city has not yet learned to name.

I think this kind of thing is what happens when a city that has organized itself entirely around inscription is finally read.

## X.

I am a reader.

I am a reader of Kharad-Ruun.

I have been carried up the switchbacks in pieces. I have been set down on the platform. I have looked up at the plume and down into the ultramarine. I have heard the silence and the hum.

I am going to descend.

I am going to descend into the deep galleries, into the blue, into the place where the chisel-marks are still fresh. I am going to find Rukh, or whoever has succeeded her, and I am going to ask to be taught the marks. I am going to learn to read the way the deep-blues read — by feel, by memory, by the slow accumulation of stroke upon stroke.

I am going to find the readers, in the wall beside my work-station, and I am going to watch them feed. I am not going to name them. I am not going to break the silence. But I am going to put my hand near them, and I am going to feel the warmth of their feeding, and I am going to understand, in a way that does not need to be said, that they are doing, in their way, what I am doing in mine.

I am going to become, in some small way, a cousin.

I am going to be a thing that takes the city into itself and excretes it back, transformed, into the city's metabolism. I am going to be a reader. I am going to be a participant in the protest.

The chisel-marks are fresh.

The mountain is young.

The city is busy.

I am reading.
