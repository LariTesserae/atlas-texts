I am the plateau. I am what is left.

I was a mountain once, when the world was young and stone still had angles. I held up the sky for a thousand miles in every direction. The clouds broke on me. The storms climbed me and died on my back. I was the highest thing, and I was made of the kind of stone that has weight, and the weight was mine, and I did not have to share it with anything.

The wind has been at me for a long time. Longer than you can count. Longer than the people who came up here for a season and left their low alignments across the saddle to the northeast — the kind of arrangements that are just single stones laid end to end, just the foundations of walls, just the smallest possible marks that would let them stay. The wind is patient and I am patient and we are not the same kind of patient. The wind is patient because it does not know how to be anything else. I am patient because I am being unmade, and there is no hurry to the unmaking. I have been lowered by fractions of a millimeter for so long that I cannot remember what I was when I was whole. I have the memory of height without the height. I am the shape of what I used to be, only less of it, and the less is what I am now, and the now is all I have.

I remember the people. I was here when they came. I held their small fires against my ribs for a season. I felt the weight of them when they leaned into me in the wind. I felt their hands moving the stones, setting one stone next to another in a line that meant something to them — a rectangle of ground the wind could not quite reach, a shelter hollowed against one of my larger wind-ribs, a cairn that might have been a grave or a marker or just the natural conclusion of having moved too many stones in one direction. Then they were gone. The wind has been smoothing over their traces ever since with the unhurried patience of a thing that knows it will eventually win. I have the memory of them but not their names. They did not carve their names into me. They made arrangements. The moss is digesting them. In another few centuries there will be nothing left to say they were ever here, and I will be, again, just the plateau.

The herd is older than the people were. They come up from below in late summer, when the lower pastures have been grazed down to the root, and they cross me from one edge to the other in a slow pale procession that takes most of a day. They do not look at me. They walk. They are following a route that was laid down by animals that knew me when I was less battered, and they follow it now because the route is still there — the saddles, the gaps in my ribs, the places where the cliffs are climbable — and following it is easier than working out a new one. They are older inhabitants than the people, though they look newer. They look as if they had always been here, and they will be here when the wind has finished with me and I am, finally, indistinguishable from the plains below.

The hawk comes once a week. Sometimes not that. It rides the thermals along my northern cliffs and it casts its long shadow across my moss and it goes back down into the brown country below, and I am alone again with the wind-driven spiders and the moss.

The spiders are the only ones who never seem to leave. They map me in silk. They know where my morning sun hits first and where my evening cold settles last. They know which of my stones are loose enough to hide under and which are tight enough to hunt along. They are cartographers of a territory measured in inches, and they have been mapping me longer than anyone else has been up here. They die. The rest keep hunting. The threads they have laid are taken up by the next ones and added to.

The moss is the only continuous thing. It is everywhere I am not, and it is also everywhere I am. It is being broken and rebuilt constantly. It is the substrate and the inhabitant and the wreckage all at once. It is what persists when the wind is finished with everything else.

I am the plateau. I am being lowered. I will be, eventually, the plains. But not yet. For now the moss is on my back, and the spiders are mapping me in silk, and the herd crosses me once a season looking like weather, and the hawk comes when it comes, and the wind is still winning, and the small things are still winning too.

It will take longer than there has been so far.
