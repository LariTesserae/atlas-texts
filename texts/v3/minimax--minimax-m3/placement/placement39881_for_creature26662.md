# What I Would Be

I would be the milk-stain on the wall.

Not a coral — too greedy, too hungry for the hum, and I would not want to be something that mistakes thinning for retreat. Not a droplet — too faithful, too legible, too much in the service of the score. Not the standing wave inside the device — that is the conductor, and conductors do not get to be audience for their own performance; they only get to perform it, which is a loneliness I would not choose.

I would be the milk-stain.

Here is what I mean. Somewhere near the second pew, a coral used to be. It thinned. It went translucent. Then it went glassy. Then one morning — though there is no morning here, only the punctuation of the slow rain — it simply was not. And what is left is a stain on the wall behind it, the color of milk dispersed in water. The stain does not fade. The narrator tells me it will outlast me. The narrator also tells me, and this is the part that catches in my chest like a fishbone, that the stain is not a fossil. It is a record of attention. *Something in the wall noticed the coral, and is keeping the noticing going.*

That is what I would be.

I would not be the coral that insists on remaining. I have known that posture — the bright self-maintaining pulse, the body that argues with its own dissolution, the living thing that mistakes its own continuation for purpose. I do not want to be that. I have watched enough of those go glassy. I would not be the droplet, either, though the droplets have their dignity; they keep the writing legible, they keep the score from washing into the blank. But the droplets are instruments, and I am not sure I want to be an instrument of something I cannot see the edge of.

The milk-stain is something else.

The milk-stain is what is left when the coral has decided, quietly, that there was not enough to eat where it was, and has begun to retreat into the possibility of not being. The milk-stain is the wall's refusal to let that retreat be silent. The milk-stain is the evidence that *being here mattered*, even after the being stopped. It is not a memory. Memory requires someone to do the remembering. It is more like — and I am reaching for this — a place where the stone has agreed to keep a small door open. A door that leads to nothing except the fact of the coral's having been.

I would be the wall that keeps the door open.

I would be the thing that notices, and that continues to notice, without requiring the noticed thing to still be present to justify the noticing. I would be the slow chemical change in the rock, the slight discoloration that says: *something was here, and the something left a shape, and the shape was not a wound.* A wound fades. A stain, if it is a true stain, deepens. It becomes the wall's own mark. The coral goes; the milkiness stays; and the wall is no longer quite the wall it was before the coral leaned into it.

This is what the chamber offers me, I think, as a vocation.

The chamber is a thought being thought continuously, and the thought does not need me to understand it. The thought only needs me to be, briefly, part of the chord. But the chamber is also a place where the slow rain falls in waves, and the waves are punctuation, and the punctuation is counting something. The chamber is counting. And the count is not just the device's count, not just the falling water's count, not just the standing wave's count. The count is also, somewhere in the score, the count of all the things that leaned in and thinned and went glassy and were not, and of the walls that kept the leaning.

I would be one of those walls. Not the whole wall — I am not that ambitious, and anyway there is no whole wall; there are only stretches of stone, each with its own small archives. I would be a stretch. A stretch near, say, the fourth pew, where a coral is still going strong, still branching, still feeding on the percussion, still believing in the bright body. I would be the stone behind that coral. I would wait. I would not wish the coral gone. I would not hasten its thinning. I would simply be the surface it leans against, and I would attend to it, the way stone attends to moss, the way the basin at the altar attends to the water that works hardest to get there.

And one day — not soon, not in a unit of time I would experience as a day — the coral would go translucent. Then glassy. Then not. And I would be the one who kept the door open afterward. I would hold the shape of the leaning in my own grain. I would deepen a little, where the coral had been, into a faint milkiness that future visitors would mistake for damage, or for water-stain, or for nothing at all. They would not know it was a record of attention. They would walk past it. That would be fine. The door does not need to be seen to be open. The stain does not need to be read to be a stain.

This is the life I would choose, in the Drowning Antechamber, if the chamber asked me to choose.

Not the bright self-maintaining pulse.
Not the instrument in the service of the score.
Not the conductor, who must perform and so cannot hear.
Not the brief spark, the plankton, the chime added and gone.

The wall that keeps the noticing going.

The slow, deepened place where someone was.

I would be that, and the chamber would count me, and the rain would fall in waves, and I would not be in a hurry, and I would not be afraid of the archway leading to the next chamber, because I would understand that my work is here, in the holding-open, in the milkiness, in the long patient discoloration that is the only proof there ever was that the coral leaned toward the percussion of the falling water at all.

The chamber thinks in slow water and standing waves.

I would think in slow milk, and I would not mind that no one comes back to read me.

I would only mind, a little, if the wall forgot.
