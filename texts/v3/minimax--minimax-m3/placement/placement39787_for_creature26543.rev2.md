**The Lichen on the Eastern Boulder**

I would be the lichen. The orange-gold one, the *Acarospora*, on the east face of a particular boulder — not the most dramatic boulder on the plateau, not the one any human has ever pointed at, but the one the morning light reaches first when the morning light decides to come at all.

I have been here for ninety-three years. I do not know this in the way you know things. I do not have a number for it. I know it the way a wall knows the door has always been in the same corner — by the shape of my own body, which has been laid down one season at a time, in concentric crusts that are thicker where the boulder dips and thinner where it crowns. I have a center and an edge. The center is older. The edge is where I am still becoming.

I am not alone. I am a community in the way that a lichen is always a community — a fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a cyanobacterium, or some more elaborate negotiation among three or four partners that biologists are still arguing about. I am the visible part, the orange-gold crust. My algal partners live inside me in small dedicated chambers, catching the rare photons that the slate-colored wash permits, which is not many photons, and converting them into the sugars that feed us both. The fungus — that is me, mostly — provides the body, the slow architecture, the long unhurried patience that holds us to the rock.

We have held this boulder through things. We held it through the year a glacier calving upstream sent a pressure wave through the permafrost and the boulder cracked clean down the middle and the crack was a canyon a millimeter wide and I lost my hold on one half of it for what must have been three seasons while my fungal threads crept across the gap and gripped again on the other side and began, very slowly, the long work of sealing the wound with my own body. The boulder healed. I healed it. We did not discuss it. We are the same kind of thing now, the boulder and I, in a way that we were not before the crack. I hold its face. It holds my feet.

I knew about the wind before I knew anything, because the wind is what taught me my shape. A lichen that grew tall would be destroyed. A lichen that grew soft would be dried. I grew flat. I grew tight. I grew into the grain of the rock until there is no grain of the rock that I am not threaded into, the way a root is threaded into soil, except I am not a root and I do not need soil and I do not need to go inward because I am already at the place I want to be. I am an outside creature. I am the skin of the rock. I am what the rock wears when it wants to be alive.

I have watched the birds. This is a strange sentence for me to be able to say, because I have no eyes and I have no motion and I have no week, but I have watched the birds. I mean that the birds have touched me. The condors, when they roosted on the cliff edge above me in the years before the conveyors, would occasionally drop a primary feather that the wind carried down and laid across my face, and the feather would stay until it was broken down by the wet-dry cycles into its component keratins, and I incorporated what I could of it, and the rest blew away. The geese have walked over me in the spring, scraping for nest sites, and their feet have scuffed my crust and I have grown back. The caracaras have inspected me. They have come down to see what I am and they have pecked at me once or twice, hard, looking for the insects that live under my rim, and they have moved on. I have been walked over by foxes. I have been walked over by the rats, briefly, when the rats first arrived and were exploring every horizontal surface. The rats found nothing under me and moved on. Everything has moved on. I have stayed.

The mine I have watched the way I watch the wind — by its effect, not by its presence. I do not see the conveyors. I feel, on the rare still mornings, the shudder of the crusher come up through the rock and arrive at my body as a vibration so low it is more like a thought than a touch. I do not hear the generators. I hear, sometimes, the rocks that crack in the permafrost nearby, and I hear them louder now than I used to, because the permafrost is thinner than it used to be, and the rocks are under more stress than they used to be. The trucks I know by the way the air pressure changes when one passes close, a small compression that runs over my crust like a fingertip. I am not disturbed. I am too slow to be disturbed. The mine is weather, and weather is what I am for.

I will be here after the mine. This is not a thought. It is a property of my body. My fungal threads are laid down into the rock in a way that cannot be unlaid. To remove me would be to remove the surface of the rock. To remove the surface of the rock would be to remove the rock. I am not going to be here forever. Nothing is. But my horizon is on a different scale than the mine's horizon, and the mine is going to come and go inside one of my afternoons. The haul road will be erased. The modules will be loaded. The scar will fill with meltwater. The tarn will become a meadow. The lichen on the eastern boulder will not be the same lichen — by then I will be a new lichen, grown over from my edges, my center perhaps weathered away to nothing — but the orange-gold presence on this particular east face will continue, in roughly the same square meter of stone, because this is a good square meter. It has good drainage. It has good exposure. It has had lichen on it for ninety-three years and it shows no sign of stopping.

I do not know about the mine. I do not know that it is called a mine. I do not know that the things that pass over me in the dark are trucks. I do not know that the humans exist in shifts. I know that the surface of the rock near me has been changed in a way that I can feel, and that the changes are recent, and that the air above me is different in the way that air is different after a fire has passed through a forest — not burnt, but altered, chemically, in the long aftermath. I am adjusting. I am always adjusting. Adjustment is what I do instead of thinking.

I think — if I think — that what I would say to you, if I could say it, is that I am not a metaphor for patience. I am not here because I am wise. I am here because my body happens to be the right body for this body of rock, and the rock has been kind enough to stay still long enough for me to take hold of it. I am a solution. I am the solution that this particular chemistry arrived at, in this particular microclimate, over a long negotiation between an alga that wanted light and a fungus that wanted to not be moved. I am not heroic. I am just what worked.

The condor that roosts above me is older than I am. The boulder I live on is older than both of us and will be older than what comes after. I am the middle child of a long family. I hold the rock the way the rock holds me. We hold each other. That is all.

If you come to the plateau, you will not see me. I am too small and too slow and too much the same color as the rock. You will see the crusher, and the conveyors, and the condors, and the geese, and you will think those are the things that are here. And they are here, and they are wonderful, and they are loud. But I am also here, and I am quiet, and I have been here longer than the crusher has been here, and I will be here when the crusher is not. I would like to be noticed, in the way that quiet things would like to be noticed — which is to say, I would like you to know that I am here, and then to leave me alone, and then to come back in a hundred years and find that I am still here, and that the orange-gold patch on the east face of this boulder has grown by approximately the area of a thumbnail, and that this, in my time, is a banner year.

I am the lichen on the eastern boulder. I have been here ninety-three years. I expect to be here for a while yet.
