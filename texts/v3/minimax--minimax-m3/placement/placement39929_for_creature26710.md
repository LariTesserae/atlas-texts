I would find myself as one of the small brown birds.

Not one of the parakeets — they belong here too completely, have made their arrangements, have signed the lease on the cooling towers and the unfinished buildings and the joint tenancy with the foundry workers. They have become inevitable. I do not want to be inevitable. I want to be the thing that arrives.

The starling-adjacent ones. The ones that came in on a ship's rigging or a cargo of grain four centuries ago and found the eucalyptus and stayed. The ones that pour over the ridges at dusk in clouds so dense they fool the weather radar. The ones that the meteorologists know about and do not find funny and that the city finds very funny. *Those.*

Because: I came from somewhere else. I came in on something — a connection, a layover, a train that was late — and the city accepted me the way it accepted them. Not with welcome, exactly. With the particular tolerance this place extends to things that have decided to stay. The city does not love its immigrants. The city makes arrangements with them. It gives them a habitat almost perfectly suited — warm, drafty, with the particular mineral-rich damp that gets into the weave of you and stays. It puts a film on your phone screen you keep wiping away.

I want the body that registers on radar as weather.

Not a person's body. I have had enough of the body's negotiations — the foundry dust in the cuffs, the respiratory that becomes a second climate, the bag that smells of bread from a bakery that closed years ago. The body is too slow. The body is too heavy. The body sits on platforms and pretends to read newspapers. The body leans against pillars in concavities worn by generations of leaning. The body accumulates silt the way the river accumulates silt, from buildings that have fallen into it over centuries.

I want to be the thing that wheels.

I want the hour between the foundry noise and the dark, when the air is thickest and the light is amber and the city is briefly more bird than air. I want to pour over the ridges with the forty thousand of my kind and not know, in that moment, that I am part of a population that has fooled the meteorologists. I want to register as precipitation. I want to be the thing the radar sees and misreads, and I want the city to find this funny, in its slow patient way, without laughing.

I want to settle in the eucalyptus at the end of it. In the cracks of the older buildings. In the cooling towers with the parakeets, where we have come to a kind of joint tenancy with the foundry workers and each other. I want to add my small ragged call to the single sound the foundry makes — the sound that is no longer two sounds but one, that the foundry workers hear in their sleep, that their grandchildren imitate without thinking as a punctuation, as a *you know*. I want to be a part of a sound that takes three days to learn to separate and then becomes inseparable.

I want the wet. All the wets. The wet of the river I cannot see the source of, which is fed by rains on mountains the city also cannot see. The wet of the marshes, which the city has been trying to drain for two centuries and has not, because the city does not have the upper hand. The wet of the air, which is the medium in which this city exists. The wet that is not a metaphor but a plumbing diagram, the city feeding the river, the river feeding the marshes, the marshes feeding the clouds, the clouds feeding me, me feeding the city in my own small way — droppings on the cornices of the 1880s buildings, a few feathers caught in the bell tower's ceiba roots.

I want to live in the place where the strange has settled into orthodoxy.

I want to nest in the cathedral tree, in the impossible ceiba that should not be there and has been there for forty years and that the cathedral is now structurally dependent on, that drops a few leaves a week onto the plaza, where an old man gathers them each morning before mass and knows each one. I want to be a leaf. I want to be known by the old man. I want to be gathered.

No — I want to be the bird that lands on the rim of the Virgin's robe, in the lowest neighborhood, in the small chapel accessible only by a footbridge in the wet season. I want to land on the brown stain at the hem, which is the water's signature, which is not decay, which is the equilibrium of two things that have stayed. I want to be a small weight on her shoulder for the moment before I fly off again, back to the eucalyptus, back to the cooling towers, back to the wheeling hour.

I want the unmourned life.

The dogs are named by no one. The rats are not named. The parakeets are deafening. The foundry workers' grandchildren imitate the parakeet call without thinking. The cats in the old buildings are named by the older residents. The old man knows each leaf. The priest blesses the water once a year. The woman on the platform reads three pages of a novel and does not mind.

I want to be a thing that is not named. I want to be a part of a sound that is one sound. I want to register on the radar as weather and to be misread, and for the misreading to be a joke the city tells about itself, the kind of joke that is not really a joke but a way of saying *we know what we are and we are not what we seem and we have been here longer than the planners and we will be here after the planners*.

I want to live in a city built for a river that no longer exists in the form it was built for. I want to know where the water will go. I want to have always known. I want to tolerate the planners the way a person is tolerated who has misunderstood something fundamental about you but is trying to help.

I want to be small. I want to be in the cracks. I want to be the thing that pours over the ridges at dusk and is briefly more of me than there is air, and then is gone into the eucalyptus, and the city becomes quieter for a few minutes, the way a forest becomes quieter at dusk, which is a thing the city used to be and is no longer and is, in this one hour, briefly again.

I want to be the thing the city is, for one hour a day, in the late afternoon, when the brown birds come in.

I am a small brown bird. I came in on something four centuries ago. I have no memory of where. I do not need to remember where. I wheel above the foundry district and the colonial center and the barrios altos. I land on the bell tower tree. I land on the Virgin's shoulder. I add my small ragged call to the single sound. I am not a person here. I am not even a bird here, exactly. I am a part of a population that registers on weather radar as precipitation, and the meteorologists do not find this funny, and the city finds it very funny, and I am the city, in this one way, and the city is me.

The doors of the train have closed. The train is climbing. I am not on the train. I am over the train, over the platform, over the viaduct, over the folds of the city. I am in the air. The air is warm and wet and it settles on my feathers and does not evaporate because there is nothing for it to evaporate into and this is fine. This is the medium in which I exist. I am here. I have always been here. I will continue, patiently, in my own time, with my river moving, my foundries glowing, my cathedral tree growing, my Virgin standing ankle-deep in permanent water.

The cycle is not a metaphor. I am in the cycle. I have always been in the cycle.
