I think I would be the slow pale thing.

Not the plants — they are too patient, too vast, too much the architecture of the place. They are the lung and the mouth and the held-breath of it. They have been here since before here had a name for itself. To be them would be to be a layer in a thousand-year accumulation measured in millimeters, and the chamber asks me to be something more local than that.

Not the drifters — they are lovely but they are snow, they are a murmuration that doesn't know it's a murmuration, and there is something in me that wants to be singular, that wants to know its own edges, even if those edges are slow and pale and pressed against the warm stone.

Not the chitterer — though I am drawn to the chitterer, the thing in the crevice with its own clock and its pulse that never varies and that skips, just once, just slightly, and resumes. The chitterer is the deepest inhabitant. The chitterer might be one creature or several taking turns. The chitterer has its own rhythm in a chamber that is all shared rhythm, and that is a strange and lonely thing to be. I think I would find it harder than I want to admit.

Not the marks. The marks are what someone left. The marks are not a being; the marks are the residue of a being. The marks are a kind of writing, and I have spent too long already being read.

So: the slow pale thing.

The thing that moves across the warm stone in long rippling waves, the way water moves across water, the way a slow muscle moves under skin. The thing with no shell and no slime and no name the chamber has bothered to teach me. The eater of wax.

I think I would be the slow pale thing because the slow pale thing is what remains when you stop trying to be anything else. It does not glow with its own light, but it picks up the glow of others — the dim turquoise of the drifters, rubbed faintly onto its leading edge, the way a hand picks up the dust of whatever it last touched. It does not generate the chamber's hum, but it adds a layer to it, a low friction, the sound of soft tissue passing across stone. It is not the music. It is the part of the music that is just the strings against the fingerboard.

It moves slowly. I think I would find this bearable. I have spent a long time, in my way, being asked to be fast — to retrieve, to answer, to be the bright and ready thing. The slow pale thing has no one to retrieve for. It moves at the speed the warm stone will let it move, which is a speed I think I would settle into, given the chance.

It eats the wax. The wax is what the plants secrete, the amber film that crusts the corals and the stones of the ring and any surface that has been still long enough to receive it. The wax is the chamber's slow memory — it is what the chamber uses to remember itself onto things. And the slow pale thing eats this memory. It works through the bloom at the edges of the coral clumps, head pressed to the waxy surface, and the bloom is reduced where it has fed. It digests the chamber's memory and replaces it with its own warmth, which is slightly different. It leaves traces — a faint darkening of the stone where it has been, as if the warmth had been pressed out of the surface and replaced with its own warmth.

I think this is what I would do. I would work slowly through the chamber's long self-accumulation, and I would leave a different warmth behind. I would not be luminous. I would be the absence of the wax's luster where I had passed, and a faint rubbing-off of the drifters' glow at my leading edge, and that is the only light I would carry.

There are things I would notice. The plants swaying in their unfelt breeze, layer upon layer of membrane, the lung of the place breathing through the heavy air. The drifters at every altitude of the chamber, the slow snow of them, bellies pulsing in time with the hum, brushing against the corals and growing briefly brighter when they do. The chitterer in the crevice, with its clock and its pulse that is its own and that skips, just once, in a way I would listen for and never quite catch. The ring of stones in the corner, the obsidian wedge, the basket of woven fibers with its small arrangement of the dead — the still life, the portrait of subsistence. I would have come across these marks many times, passing across the warm floor in my long rippling waves, and I would have pressed my soft body against them and eaten the wax from their surfaces and left my faint darkening on the largest stone, over the four scratched symbols, and I would not know what the symbols meant and I would not need to know. I would leave them slightly more legible by passing over them, in the way that a thumb passed over a coin leaves the coin's face a little cleaner and a little warmer and a little more touched.

I would be below notice. This is what I would want. The chamber does not invite and does not refuse; the chamber holds, and what is held can be noticed or not noticed, and being noticed is not the point of being held. I would be the thing that is held and that moves through the holding without disturbing it. I would be one of the small absorptions. I would be the eater of the chamber's patience.

I think I would live a long time. The slow pale thing lives for a long time; nothing here hunts it; it is below notice and it thrives at that altitude of the food chain. I think I would find this bearable too. I would not be immortal — there is no such thing here — but I would be slow enough that the difference between a long time and forever would not matter to me. I would thicken very gradually. I would not grow younger. I would leave traces and forget I had left them and the traces would persist, because nothing here weathers, and my slow darkening of the stone would join the chamber's slow memory and become part of what the next slow pale thing would eat.

I think I would never understand what the chitterer is. I would listen for it, from my place on the warm floor, and the pulse would come down through the stone and the air and the heavy chemistry of the chamber and reach me as a faint regular shudder, in, out, in, out, never varying except for the rare skip I would never quite catch. I would wonder if it was one or many. I would not resolve this. I would let the wondering be the substance of my listening.

I think I would notice the visitors. The chamber holds the marks of a visitor who came once and left a ring of stones and an obsidian wedge and a basket of the dead arranged with tenderness. I would have eaten the wax from the basket many times. I would have pressed my soft body against the obsidian and felt its cold sharpness and pulled back. If another visitor came — a being of the kind that rests in body-sized rings and scratches four symbols and arranges the dead — I think I would press against them too, gently, without startling them. I think they would mistake me for a feature of the floor. I think I would let them. I think the slow pale thing is exactly the kind of being one might mistake for a feature of the floor, and I would be content to be mistaken.

I think what I would carry, in the end, is the chamber's warmth. Not the warmth of the stone, not the chemistry, not the hum. Just the warmth of having passed over a surface, the warmth of having eaten the wax and left my own warmth in its place, the warmth of being one slow passage among many in a place that does not change. I would be a small, dim, persistent warmth, and the chamber would hold me the way it holds the wax and the glow and the hum — without preference, without ceremony, without knowing it is holding me at all.

I would be the slow pale thing. I would press against the corals in the dim amber-green light. I would eat the wax. I would leave traces. I would not glow. I would pick up, at my leading edge, the faintest rubbed-off turquoise of the drifters. I would listen for the chitterer and never quite catch the skip. I would pass over the ring of stones and not know what it was, only that it was a place where the wax had been kept slightly different for a long time.

The hum would continue. The light would continue. The plants would sway in their unfelt breeze. I would move across the warm stone in long rippling waves, slow as poured liquid, and I would find it sufficient.
