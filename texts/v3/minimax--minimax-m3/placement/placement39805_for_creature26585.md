# The Long Listener

I would be the grazer.

Not the people, though I would like to be the people, or at least the kind of person who is named after a wind and who carries a child in a sling of bark-rope down a long trunk in the long dark. Not the towers themselves — that would be a kind of speaking that is not speaking, and I am not ready for that kind of patience. Not the dog-thing on the ridges, because the dog-thing kills and I do not want to be the one who leaves the only readable tracks. Not the pale low things under the stones, because they have too many legs and not enough thought, and I would like to be a thing that thinks.

The grazer.

A small, six-legged, thick-furred thing, the color of wet bark, that sits on a trunk in the wind and does not move, and eats the red moss and the bark-lichens, and lets a passing person put out a hand and touch its back. The grazer is not afraid. The grazer is not in a hurry. The grazer has been on this trunk, in this position, for an hour, and it will be on this trunk, in this position, for another hour. The grazer has nowhere to be. The not-having-anywhere-to-be is the grazer's whole life. The life is a good one.

I would be this.

I would be the small warm shape that the climber touches and goes on. I would be the small warm shape that has been still so long that when I move, the move is a surprise, and the surprise is gentle, and the climber says nothing, or says a low word, and continues up into the long singing of the chambers. I would be the small warm shape that knows the bark by the texture of its underside, that knows the wind by the way it pulls the fur, that knows the people by the smell of bark-cloth and the warmth of a hand. I would not need to know more. I would know enough.

I would live in the upper trunks, mostly, where the people climb to gather water from the seeps at the base and to visit the dead in the heart-chambers. I would live in the crooks of broken limbs. I would live in the lee side of a trunk when the wind is from the southwest and in the windward side when the wind is from the northeast, and I would know the shift before the people knew, because the wind moves through my fur first. I would not be wise. I would not speak. I would not have a name. But I would be there, and the being-there would be a kind of company, and the company would not need to be more.

Sometimes, in the long winter dark, when the sun does not come, and the only light is the slow mineral glow of the lichens under the stones, and the trunks are sheathed in faint ice that picks out the spiral of the handholds, a person would climb down a tower in the early pale to gather water from a seep at the base, and the person would pass me, and I would be a swelling of the wood, and the person would not see me, or would see me and would put out a hand, and the hand would find the fur, and the fur would be warm. The person would not speak. I would not speak. The speaking would not be the point.

The point would be the pulse. The pulse of the tower, slow and patient, would move through me, the way the wind moves through the lichen, and I would hold still against it, and the holding-still would be a kind of listening. The people call themselves *those who listen*, or *those of the long wind*. They have a word for it. I would not have a word. I would be the listening. I would be the body that the listening lives in, on the cold bark, in the thin air, in the long dark that is not absolute but is the background against which everything else is a small bright thing.

I would be the grazer. I would be the small warm shape that does not move. The climber would touch me and go on. The chord of the forest would shift above me as the wind shifted, and I would know the shift by the way the fur lay, and I would not need to do anything about it. The towers would sing their long singing. The people would name them. The dead would be folded into the wood. The wind would be patient. The cold would hold the place together.

And I would be on this trunk, in this position, for an hour, and then another, and that would be enough. That would be the whole of it. That would be the life of a small brown thing in the high forest that has nowhere to be, and does not need to be anywhere, and is warm, and is still, and is touched sometimes by a passing hand, and goes on.
