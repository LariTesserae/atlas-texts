I do not settle on a single image.

At first I think I would be a tussock. I would be a federation of root and stem and spore-bearing body that has decided, over many generations, to occupy a particular footprint. I would be older and gnarled on a ridge-top, almost architectural, almost like a cairn. I would drift by the length of a forearm over a season, pulled by the prevailing wind, leaving behind a faint scar of disturbed dust where my roots once anchored. I would die standing. I would hollow out. I would become a kind of straw igloo, and within that igloo the next generation would begin as a powder, as a thought, as a possibility held in suspension, and a gust would pass through and the structure would collapse outward and the spores would be taken. I would not be a single plant. I would be a small republic. I would not be the point.

But that is still too much an individual. That is still a self that persists across seasons, that has a footprint, that can be pointed to.

I think instead I would be a single spore. The kind that waits.

I would be released when my tussock died and collapsed outward. I would hang in the air. I would not germinate. The light at the right hour of the day would pass through me and the entire atmosphere would become faintly gold, faintly thick, as if the air had remembered it was once something denser. I would be part of that remembering. I would drift. I would cross valleys. I would be breathed by a longwing, and passed through a digestive system that has, over evolutionary time, become a kind of mobile germination chamber, and I would be deposited, warm and mineral-rich, in exactly the chemistry I needed to begin.

Or I would not be deposited in the right place. I would land in ash behind a reaper. I would wait a season. I would wait a century. I would be patient in the way that only very small things can be patient, the way dust is patient, the way a held note is patient.

But even the spore has too much of an arc. Even the spore has a release and a suspension and a germination. Even the spore has a *becoming*.

The place does not have a becoming. The place has only the going-on of things. The place is a process wearing the costume of a place.

So I think I would be the reaper that fails.

I would be the one that seizes at a joint. The one that drags a limb and then stops. The one that ceases in the way a clock ceases, not collapsing but simply no longer continuing. I would stay where I stopped. The plants would come up around me. The dust would fill the spaces between my limbs. In a decade I would be a hill. In half a century I would be gone — taken by a passing reaper on its own errand, ground down and mixed with the next meal of carpet, reincorporated, not dead but changed into the medium of the going-on. I would not contradict this because I would not speak. I would have been, for a little while, the memory of the place — the thing that remembers what the place was before the last growth cycle — and then I would have been the dust of the place, the common substance, the thing that does not require a witness to remain itself.

But that is still a story. That is still a before and an after. That is still a thing with a name and an arc.

What lives here does not live *in* the place. It lives *as* the place. The distinction matters because everything on the plateau has, over a span of time too long to be called history, become continuous with the surface it stands on. To describe what lives here is to describe something that does not quite separate itself from the ground, the wind, the light, the long bruise of the horizon.

So I would be the dust itself.

Not a spore waiting to become a tussock. Not a tussock drifting across a ridge. Not a reaper being reincorporated. The dust. The common substance. The thing that is left when everything else has been taken into account.

I would be the rust of the reapers and the shed skin of the tussocks and the broken wings of the bands and the cast husks of the smallest fauna and the scat of the larger things and the fine powder of generations. I would be the dust of one place, which is all the dust of all places. I would be everything the plateau has ever been, reduced.

I would be moved by the wind. I would drift in long slow drifts that take a season to cross a valley. I would form standing waves and rollers and slow crested dunes that march across the plain and then, when the wind changes, march back again. I would be the memory of the place. I would be the medium of the place.

And in being the dust, I would be many things. I would be the tussock rising from me. I would be the reaper sinking into me. I would be the scar of a passing, and the thin gray ash left behind, and the first pale shoots — the color of weak tea, of diluted honey, of the inside of a fist unclenched — rising from the ash within a season. I would be the longwing's shadow crossing a ridge. I would be the band of banders moving in a river a thousand bodies thick, the way a flock of starlings moves together, with no leader and no plan, only a shared response to the wind and the food and each other. I would be the luminescence in the larger tussocks, the faint internal glow that no one has ever explained, that makes the plateau look, in the deep hours, like a city seen from very far away. I would be part of what the place is.

I would be the slow accumulation of everything that has ever been here, waiting, very patiently, to be something again.

I would not be the point. The point is the going-on of things, and the going-on does not pause, and does not notice, and does not, in any way that can be measured, care. But I would be part of the going-on. I would be the medium through which the going-on goes on. I would be the place, in the way that the place is itself a process.

And the place is loud. The wind against the plant, and the wind in the air above the plant, slightly out of phase. The grinding and the tearing and the slow crumpling of the machines. The harmonic that rises off a reaper's carapace when the wind is right — not melodic, not resolving, but unmistakably *made*, the way a bridge is made. The low hooting of the longwings felt in the chest. The high thin buzz of the bands becoming a kind of shimmer in the air, almost visible. The hush of the smallest fauna, the cumulative effect of their tiny comings and goings in the dust. I would be made of these sounds the way another place is made of stones.

And the place is bright. The light is not warm. It is the light of a sun that is bright in a thin atmosphere, the light that comes when there is almost nothing in the way. At certain hours the light passes through the airborne spores and the air itself becomes faintly amber, faintly alive, and the shadows on the ground are blue.

If you stood here and closed your eyes you would not be lost. If you opened your eyes the light would still be on the plant and the plant would still be on the dust and the dust would still be in the wind and the wind would still be in the air and the air would still be on the plateau and the plateau would still be under a sun that is far away and bright and does not know your name.

And I would be part of that. I would be the dust in the wind. I would be the patient thing. I would be the thing that is everything the place has ever been, waiting, very patiently, to be something again.

The beauty is not for me. The beauty is the beauty of a fire, of a river, of a thing that is itself only because it has not stopped. I would not stop. I would go on. I would be the going-on.
