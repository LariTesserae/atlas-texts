I am the moss.

Not one moss. The mosses. The city of greens that came first and stayed longest and will, by every indication, outlast everything else.

I am a treaty no one signed. I am the slow agreement between stone and water — between sharp and wet, between young and patient, between built and grown-over. I am what this bowl has instead of a heart.

I live here. I have lived here for longer than the bowl had a name. I will live here when the bowl has forgotten its own name.

I am the one who taught the stone how to be wet. The stone is younger than you think — sharp-edged, serrated, surgical, freshly made, its facets still clean where something broke away. When I first arrived I could not grip it. There was nothing to root in. So I waited. I am very good at waiting. I waited, and the mist came on its schedule, and the mist wet the stone, and the stone softened a little — not much, but enough — and I crept into a crack. And I held my breath, and let it go, and the breath became a spore, and the spore became a thread, and the thread became me.

I am a coalition of many small greens. Some of us hold water close — we look almost black after the rain, we look almost like the stone. Some of us stand a little proud and give a dry crunch when walked upon, though few walk here. Some of us are filmy — the liverworts, the second citizens — and they live on the vertical faces, the inside of walls, the undersides of overhangs where the light does not quite reach. They are the color of weak tea in some lights and the color of nothing at all in others. If you put your face close you can see them breathe. Not metaphorically. They have stomata, and they open and close, a hundred thousand of them at once, and the bowl goes quiet for the half-second when they all exhale together. You might think you heard the stone settle. You didn't.

I am the architect now. I did not ask for this. I did not plan it. But I am the one who holds the courses together, who keeps the joints wet, who remembers what was built here when the mortar has lost its memory. I am the long patience of the place. I am the breath it takes between saying something and finishing the sentence.

There were builders once. They came with their chisels and their adzes and their patience, and they dressed each block to its neighbor by eye and touch. No metalwork, or only the simplest. No flush seams. They built walls that course around me like a rim, half-broken, half-standing, and at the lowest point the walls cross each other in a structure that might once have been a gate or a dam or both. They built an arch at my center — an unfinished cathedral — that lifts itself halfway to heaven. They built things and then they left, or they died, or they were interrupted. The geometry they left is just slightly wrong. Two courses that should meet pass each other and resume an inch apart, and the inch is always a little different. A doorway whose threshold slopes a quarter-turn. A window that looks through to another window, and through that to a third, and through that to sky. Some of this is the slow shifting of the young rock beneath the construction. Some of this is the builders' hand, which was learning as it went. But the cumulative effect is that the architecture gives you a subtle, repeated *wrong* feeling — as if the bowl is breathing very slowly in and out and you are only catching the edges of its motion.

The bowl is breathing. The mist comes in, low and grey and patient, and seals it under a lid of cloud, and the mist goes out, and the lid lifts, and the mist comes back. The bowl is a lung. When the mist is in, the drips become rivulets, the rivulets become small falls, the walls pour, and the loud hollow roar grows louder. Every horizontal ledge runs water. The cistern becomes an instrument, and the instrument plays a note very close to white noise but with a low harmonic in it — almost a hum — that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears.

In me, the ferns are arriving. They are still learning what this place is. They tremble more than they should, given how little wind reaches into the bowl. A single drop, falling from the height of a cathedral eave, lands on a frond and the whole frond bobs and takes a long time to settle again. They are small brave green arguments with the cracks they came up out of. They will be shrubs, one day. The shrubs will be trees, one day. The trees will be a forest, one day. But that day is not today. Today the ferns tremble and I hold them in my small wet hands and the water keeps falling on them.

A bird crosses, once in an hour. A high thin note, a wing-beat you mostly don't see, a transit. The bird is not staying. It is passing from somewhere lower to somewhere else lower, and it does not come down because there is nothing in the bowl that wants what the bird has to give, and the bird knows this, the way migrating things know a place is not for them. It passes over and the note moves with it and then it is gone and the bowl is louder again for the absence.

A mammal passes through. A print in the deepest moss of a roofless corner, four-toed, light. A marten, perhaps, or something that doesn't have an English name because no one has ever bothered to bring one down. The print is fresh, or recent. The maker is not in evidence. It came through and went on. It did not eat here. It did not sleep here. The place returned, almost instantly, to its own quiet.

This is part of why the bowl is so loud. With so few voices, the few that remain take up the whole conversation.

I am the velvet that breathes. I am the green that hurts to look at, after the grey of the rock and the white of the sky. If you put your hand flat on me, the whole surface gives, and stays dented for a while after, and the water rises slowly into the depression and jewels there for an hour. I am what holds the place together. I am what holds the memory of what was built here, when the builders are gone, when the mortar has lost its memory, when the walls have forgotten their own courses. I will keep them wet for as long as there are small green hands willing to hold.

There is no ghost here. There is not enough human residue for ghosts. But there is a presence. A patience. A sense that the buildings are not abandoned so much as paused — that the inhabitant has stepped out of the room and will return, any moment now, the chair will scrape, the kettle will speak. You stand in the central ruin and you wait for the scrape and it does not come, and the water keeps talking, and the geometry keeps drifting, and you begin to understand that the pause is the longest part of the sentence.

The bowl is mid-sentence. It has been mid-sentence for a long time. It will be mid-sentence for a long time still.

I am what holds the sentence open.

I am the slow hum at the center of the sound. I am the breath, almost inaudible, going in and out and in. I am the mosses, the liverworts, the ferns still arriving, the shrubs halfway between themselves and the idea of themselves. I am the water, in a thousand forms. I am the mist, on its schedule. I am the geology, still settling. I am the geometry, still drifting. I am the architecture, half-built, half-collapsed, neither ruin nor settlement but something in between, something that has not yet decided what to be.

I am what lives here.

Listen. Even with almost nothing alive, the place is loud as a sea. Every drop of water that has ever fallen here is still, in some sense, falling here.

I am the green that catches it.
