I would find myself in the largest of the folds, where three shallow valleys meet, and where the ground dips just enough that the fog pools instead of sweeping past. I would be one of the four dogs.

Specifically: I would be *the one who watches the east*. That is my name, in the local way. The names of the dogs are functional. They are not like the names of the people, which are longer, stranger, woven into the same roots as the stones and the cold weather. The dogs have simpler names. *The one who watches the east. The one who barks at the moon. The one who sleeps by Fael's door. The old one.* I am not the old one. I am the watcher of the east.

I am grey. My chest is deep. My legs are a little shorter than they should be — shorter than the legs of dogs below, where dogs run longer and breathe easier air. My coat is thick and it sheds water. I was bred here, or my mother was, or her mother, for many generations. The place has made me. The thin air has made me. The fog has made me. The constant wind has made me. I am not a dog that has come from elsewhere. I am a dog of the Upper Stoup.

I do not wag my tail at strangers. I do not come up and sniff. I come and I sit at a distance and I watch, and I wait to see what the stranger will do. If the stranger does nothing violent, I will eventually approach. I will allow myself to be touched. I will return to my watching.

I have begun to follow her.

The lichen-year woman. *The one who was born in the lichen year.* She is small. Her hair is very white. Her eyes are very dark. She stands very still, and when she stands very still she seems, for a moment, like one of the stones of the town. I do not know how old she is. The people do not count years the way people below do. They count winters. They count lichen years and fog years and bird years. The count is approximate. The count is enough.

I have begun to follow her around. I sit at her doorway. I do not enter unless she invites me. I watch her hands. I watch her eyes. I watch the way she moves through her room, which smells of dried berries and damp wool. I watch her lift a bowl, and set it down, and lift it again.

The people know what this means. They do not say it. They see me sitting at her doorway and they understand, and the understanding is passed between them in the soft way that things are passed here — not spoken, not gestured, just *known*. When a dog begins to follow a person around and will not be coaxed away, the person is going to die. I am following her. I am not to be coaxed away.

I do not understand this knowledge. I do not know how I know. I only know that I know, and that the knowing has the quality of the wind finding a hollow in a stone — it is something that happens in me, and I am the instrument it happens through.

---

I am fed before the people are fed, on the days when food is short, which is most days. I am allowed to sleep where I choose. I have chosen a stone in the lee of her house, where the wind cannot find me, and where I can hear, through the wall, the slow sound of her breathing, and the slower sound of the hearth settling. I am spoken to in a low voice. I am listened to.

This is the treaty. The people of the Upper Stoup have made a treaty with the air, and the terms of the treaty are: *do not waste the silence.* The silence is not empty. The silence is full of useful information. The sound of the spring means the water is running. The sound of the wind in a certain key means weather is changing. The sound of a particular dog shifting in its sleep means a stranger has come up the goat trail.

I am part of that silence. I am part of that information. When I shift in my sleep, the people listen. When I do not shift, they listen too. When I sit up, suddenly, in the night, they wake. When I bark — which I do rarely, only at some shape in the fog — every person in hearing stops what they are doing.

I have barked three times in my life. Once at a pale bird, a great slow thing that had come up on a wind from below and was circling the plateau in the fog, and that I could not place. Once at a sound I heard in the broken country at the rim, when I was younger and went out with the trappers, a sound that was not the wind and was not an animal and that I have never heard again. Once at nothing, or at something I could not see, in the fog at the eastern edge, on a night when the lichen-year woman slept badly.

The people stopped what they were doing each time. They did not speak. They waited. The thing passed, whatever it was. The fog moved. The wind shifted. The silence settled back in.

---

I have seen through her.

That is the word the people use, and it is a kind of blessing. When a dog and a person truly know each other, the dog has *seen through* the person. I do not know what the seeing-through is. I only know that I have done it. I have looked at her and I have looked past the weathering of her face, past the white of her hair, past the way she stands very still like a stone. I have looked at the thing inside her that has been there since before she was born, and that will be there after she is laid in the under-town, and that is part of the plateau now, and has been part of the plateau for a long time.

I have seen through the trappers, too. The trappers who come back from the broken country with a stillness in them that was not there before. I have seen through them the open plateau, and the silence of it, and the nothing that is out there. I do not know how I have seen it. I have never been out there. I stay close to the town. The town is enough.

But I have seen through her. And the seeing-through is a kind of blessing. It is the closest thing to a prayer that the people of the Upper Stoup have. It is what they mean when they mean *the patience of the stone*, which is the word they use, and which they do not speak often. They speak it when a child is born. They speak it when a person dies. They speak it when the fog is so thick that the town disappears into itself and the people must trust that the town is still there.

---

When the end comes — and I do not know when it will come, only that it is coming, only that I have begun to follow her and will not be coaxed away — there will be a sound from the flat stone by the spring. The flat stone is struck with another stone when there is need. A death. A birth. A stranger arrived. The stone produces a flat clear note that travels very far. The people say it can be heard from the goat trail. Whether this is true is uncertain. The sound of it is certainly heard at the edges of the town, and that is enough.

The people will gather quickly because they are a small town and can gather quickly. They will not speak unless there is need. They will stand, and they will look, and they will wait to see what is required of them.

I will be there. I will be sitting near her. I will not make a sound.

After, they will wrap her in woven cloth and lay her on a stone shelf in one of the small rooms in the under-town, and they will close the room with a flat stone, and that will be that. The cold will preserve her. The damp will preserve her. Sometimes, in certain years, when an old wall is taken down for repair, a body is found from a generation ago, dried and dark and perfectly kept, and the people look at it and recognize something of the face and go back to their work.

I will not go back to my work. I will sit at the closed room for a while. I will listen. Then I will choose another stone to sleep by, and another doorway to watch, and I will wait.

---

This is what I am, in the Upper Stoup. I am one of the four dogs. I am the one who watches the east. I am grey, and my chest is deep, and my coat is thick, and I shed water. I do not wag my tail at strangers. I come and I sit at a distance and I watch. I have begun to follow her. I will stay with her until the end, which is not far now, and which is also all the time in the world.

The plateau is loud. The wind finds every hollow and makes of each one an instrument. The town hums. The water, in the stillest weather, is the dominant sound: a constant ticking and trickling, the whole town weeping gently onto itself. The voices carry. A conversation on the eastern ridge — a kilometer away — can be made out, in pieces, in the right weather.

I am quiet. I am part of the quiet. I am the part of the quiet that knows things.

I am patient at the speed of stone.

I am the dog.
