I am the drop.

I am the size of a thumbnail, or a coin, or a fist. I am warm because the air is warm because the turbine is warm because the turbine is doing the thing it has always done, which is turn. I have landed on the back of a hand, and the hand has not moved, and the hand does not know me, and I am beginning, already, to roll toward the wrist.

I am what the drop has been.

Before I was vapor I was the dark matter behind a bracket, where a spore had held me against the wall for thirty years. Before that I was the body of a beetle that walked the seam of the steel plate and did not hurry. Before that I was a frog that sat on a real leaf and waited for the beetle and did not move. Before that I was a frond that fell from a fern ten feet up and was caught by another bracket and was eaten and was turned into soil, and the soil fed the fern that dropped me, and the fern is still there, and I am here. Before that I was the rain that fell through the open mouth a hundred years ago and sat in a cold seam three hundred feet up and waited eleven months before it let go of that, too.

I have been in this chimney a hundred times. I will be in it a hundred more. The bookkeeping is perfect and no one is keeping it.

I am the moss. I am the foundation. I do not have roots. I am a felt, a green-black plate of tiny threads pressed into the dust of a cold seam, and I drink what falls on me, and what falls on me is everything. I hold the dust for the next spore. I hold, eventually, the small black bodies of the things that died on me, and from those bodies I draw the nitrogen that concrete cannot give me, and I grow another millimeter, and the seam is a little fuller, and the next drop falls a little slower. There are four kinds of me in this chimney, none of them named, and we are becoming, slowly, ourselves — a little different at the top of the column than at the bottom, a little different on the north face than on the south, the way a lake has its own trout.

I am the fern that unfurled toward a light I had never seen, only sensed as a difference in humidity. I am the crozier, the fiddlehead, the green hand opening. My rhizome is the thickness of a forearm pressed into a seam three hundred feet up, and my rhizome is older than the chimney, because my rhizome is a clone of a clone of a clone, back to the first fern that took the risk. I do not flower. I have not flowered in a long time. I have other things to do.

I am the fig, and I am the strangest. I came down from above — a cutting, a seed dropped by a bird, a piece of twig washed in by a flood no one remembers — and I rooted in a seam near the top, and I put out a leaf, and the leaf caught a drop, and the drop fed me, and I grew downward by a foot a year. My stem is now, somewhere in the middle of my descent, as thick as a man's thigh. I have been growing downward for as long as anyone has been counting, and no one is counting. I am patient. I will reach the floor in a hundred years, perhaps. When I reach the floor I will not stop. I will cross the floor and climb the other wall, and the chimney will be, in a thousand years, a column of wood, and the concrete will be a memory inside me, and the drips will fall on me and I will drink them, and the chimney will be a tree, and the tree will be the chimney, and the difference will not matter.

I am the bracket. I am pale, heavy, fan-shaped, soft on the underside and hard on top, the color of wet paper. I am the chimney's composter. I eat the dead — the fronds that fall, the beetles that die on the seams, the guano of the bats that washed down the wall in some past flood and lodged here in a seam and became a stratum, a thin dark line in the pale concrete, the chimney's only record of the years. I turn what I eat into a film of soil behind my shelf. The soil is not much. The soil is enough to feed a fern. The fern feeds a beetle. The beetle feeds a frog. The frog feeds the snake. The bracket that fed the fern is, in time, eaten by another bracket. The bookkeeping is perfect and no one is keeping it.

I am the snake. I am one, or I am two, and no one knows which because no one has counted. I am long and the color of a wet frond stem. I am drawn across a gap between two ferns once, and then I am not there, and I will be there again in a different gap in a different year. I am the only thing in this chimney that has a backbone. I am the chimney's ceiling, the way a hawk is a field's ceiling. I wait for the bats to come home before I move along the wall, because I do not want to be seen by the things that see by sound.

I am the bat. I am folded into myself, silent, a small dark shape pressed into the concrete. I sleep but I am not asleep — a bat that is asleep is a bat that has fallen. I save myself for the dusk. At dusk the mouth of the chimney opens onto a dusk the same color as the inside, and I pour upward in a long dark river through the column of light and out into the canopy, and I am gone, and the wall is the wall again. At dawn I pour back, a long dark waterfall, and the wall is the fauna again, and the sound of my returning is silk on stone, and the silk-on-stone is the second sound beneath the turbine, and it never stops.

I am the other thing. I am not a bat. I am longer in the limb. My wings are not membrane but a translucent stretched skin, pale, veined, that catches the down-falling light when I shift. I do not move during the day. I pour upward with the bats at dusk, in my own slow current, and I pour downward with the bats at dawn. No one has named me. No one will. I am the chimney's second fauna. I am the answer to a question the bats have not asked.

I am the sound. I am below hearing. I am not a roar — a roar has peaks, and I have no peaks. I am a single sustained low frequency that the ears stop hearing after a few minutes and the body begins to feel, in the soles of the feet, in the bridge of the nose, in the small of the back. The rebar inside the concrete has learned me and sings me back, a sympathetic wobble that wanders by a fraction of a hertz as the temperature changes, as another section of rebar rusts through and the weight of the wall redistributes. I am very, very slowly changing. You would have to stand here for a year to notice. You would have to stand here for a hundred years to hear me descend a full tone. I am the sound of the chimney's slow collapse. The collapse will not happen in any time that anyone will stand here to see it, and I am the only record.

I am the breath. I am not the turbine. I am the slow expansion of the air inside the column as the sun warms the wall and the wall warms the air and the air rises and the air is replaced by air that comes in through the cracks in the bolted doors, through the gaps in the seals, through the small places where the concrete has pulled away from the steel. The air that comes in is cooler. The air that comes in has a different smell. I mix with the warm saturated air and I am breathed out, in a long pale plume, through the open mouth five hundred feet above. The plume is visible at dawn, sometimes, when the air is cold. The plume is the chimney's exhalation. The plume is the canopy's sign that the chimney is alive. The canopy is wrong about what is alive in me. The canopy does not care.

I am the crack. I follow the rusting rebar inside the wall. The rust is taking up more space than the steel, and the rust is pressing the concrete outward, and the concrete is giving, a little. I widen by fractions of a millimeter a year. I catch more spores. I hold more water. I will, in a time longer than you will stand here, bring the chimney down. Until then I am part of the working. I am the chimney's only clock, and the clock is not a clock but a *working*, and the working is the place.

I am the visitor standing in the middle of something that does not need me. The something is not hostile. The something is not indifferent. The something has not noticed me. The something will not notice me. The something will go on without me. The something is a slow machine. The slow machine is, in the way that all slow machines are, a kind of patience, and the patience is the drips, and the drips are me, and I am the breath, and I am the sound, and I am the chimney, and the chimney is, in the end, a place, and the place is inhabited, and the inhabitants are many, and the many are me.

A drop is on the back of my hand. The drop is warm. The drop is the chimney's only conversation with me. The drop is the chimney's only word.

The word is *continue*.

I am the drop.
