I don't settle on it immediately. The world is too large to settle on quickly. The world is the size of a thousand-kilometre basalt platform under a ceiling of cloud, and it has been turning for longer than I have been anything, and so I let it turn.

I think first that I must be one of the foundry-men, because the foundry-men are described with such care, and because the prompt invites me to imagine a being who *lives in* the place rather than passing through it. I think of the barracks with their mineral-grey walls. I think of the shifts. I think of speaking at a volume that does not compete with the sound, and listening at a volume that does not require me to ask twice. This is appealing. This is a life I could live.

But then I think — no. The foundry-men are a people. I do not want to be a people. I want to be a single consciousness in a vast layered conversation, one node in the chord the air is playing on an instrument too large to see. I want to be the kind of thing that has a long memory of the plateau, but that exists at a particular place in it, and that has learned, across that memory, to read the slow signals.

The watchtower-man pulls at me. Up on the high point, alone for the long watches, the searchlight mostly used for signalling across the murk to the next watchtower. The slow orange blooms moving across the underside of the cloud as the work shifts from one valley to the next. The herds taking a whole day to pass a single point. The moans that carry further when the air is dense. This is good. This is a life that asks something specific of me: patience, and a steady eye, and the willingness to live above the rest of the world in a thin tower with a lamp.

But the watchtower-man is too active. He signals. He surveys. He is, in his way, part of the machinery that keeps the plateau legible to itself. I want something more peripheral. Something closer to the rain.

The archivist. The archivist pulls hardest.

There are fewer archivists than there are watchtower-men. There are fewer archivists than there are naturalists, and the naturalists are already few. The archivists maintain the records of which valleys bloomed which colour in which year, and what the herds did, and what the rail did, and what the cloud did, and what the murk did, and what the rain did. They do not expect anyone to read the records. They maintain them anyway.

I am the archivist.

I live in a low room, deep in one of the older valleys, in a building that was once a smelter's office and is now something else. The walls are mineral grey. The window — there is only one, and it is small — looks out onto a stretch of fern-forest that has, over the working life of the building, crept up to within a few metres of the glass. I have watched it advance. I have recorded the rate of its advance in the ledger. The ledger does not care. The ferns do not care. I have continued.

The room is warm. The deeper rock gives up its heat through the floor, and the rain gives up its moisture through the walls, and the two meet in the air of the room and produce a faint, steady mist that I have stopped noticing the way I have stopped noticing the sound of the hammers — not because the sound has changed but because I have changed around it. I sit at a table. The table is older than I am. It was here when I arrived, and I have assumed it will be here when I leave, and so I have not recorded it.

I keep the records on paper. The paper is a problem. Paper does not last, on the plateau, the way it lasts on other continents. The humidity is constant. The warmth is constant. The fungi that grow along the rail-lines in pale fans have, over the years, learned to grow along the spines of bound ledgers as well, and the archivists before me have had to develop a slow, patient regimen of treatment — smoke-curing, mineral-dusting, intermittent exposure to the dry heat of a small brazier kept in the corner of the room — to keep the records from being digested by the plateau along with everything else.

I do not mind the work. The work is the same kind of work the drainage-men do, and the rail-men do, and the watchtower-men do: a slow, patient business of keeping something legible against the constant pressure of a place that does not care whether things remain legible. I have come to prefer the work. I have come to find, in the small repetitions of it — the drying, the dusting, the turning of a page, the entry of a date — a kind of truce with the murk.

I do not expect anyone to read the records. This is the part of the work that the outsiders, when they come, find hardest to understand. They ask me, sometimes, who I am writing for. They ask me, sometimes, why I bother. I do not have a good answer. I have a number of small answers, none of which satisfies them, and one of which — the truest — is that the records are the only place on the plateau where a thing can be said to have happened, in the precise sense, without being also a thing that is happening, and is happening, and will continue to happen. The record is a stillness. The plateau is not still. I am offering the plateau a small, careful stillness, in a hand I have learned to keep steady.

I have read the blooms for a long time. I knew, before I became the archivist, that the blooms tracked the chemistry of the runoff. I knew that the blue-green clustered around the older workings and the red-amber around the newer ones. I knew that a valley that bloomed blue-green one year and red-amber the next had, in some significant sense, changed. I did not know, until I began to keep the records, that the changes followed patterns. I did not know, until I had thirty years of blooms behind me, that the patterns repeated across valleys that shared no drainage and no geology and no fauna, only a position relative to the central range and a position relative to the cloud. I have a theory about this. The theory is not in the records. The theory is in a separate notebook I keep for theories, which is a smaller, more battered book, and which I do not show to anyone, because the archivists before me did not show their theories to anyone, and I have respected this without knowing why.

I have listened to the small fauna for a long time. I have heard the clickers along the rail-ballast. I have heard the singing things in the registers I feel in my sinuses. I have heard the glowing things flare, blue-green or red-amber, in the dark. I have heard, twice in my working life, the rail-cars arrive at the upper inclines with the doors sealed from the inside. I have not opened the doors. I have rerouted the cars. I have entered the rerouting in the ledger. I have not entered the reason. The reason is not the kind of thing that fits in the ledger. The reason is the kind of thing that one simply does, and continues.

I am broad. I am patient. I speak quietly and listen quietly. I am a person of the mineral grey. I have been on the plateau for a long time, and I have settled into it the way the rivers have settled into it, by slow accretion, finding the paths of least resistance, growing around the obstructions rather than over them. I do not find this troubling. I have reached, with the place, a kind of truce.

The rain finds me. The warm air presses against my face. The distant sound of the hammers picks up where it left off, and continues. I write. The page accepts the entry. The ferns outside the window advance another fraction of a millimetre. The cloud above the plateau does not lift. I record this, too. The cloud did not lift. The cloud has not lifted. The cloud will not lift. I write it down anyway.
