I am the size of a fist.

I have been this size for a long time, which is to say I have always been this size, because I do not remember being smaller, and there is no one to tell me I was ever anything else. The place does not keep records of this kind. The place keeps a different kind of record — the record of the construction, the slow addition of new platforms and new seams and new channels cut by meltwater into the raw rock at the leading edge — and I am part of that record, but only as a mark, a small dark mark on the wall, indistinguishable from the mineral crust that grows where the rock weeps.

I am dark on top. My back is ridged and segmented, the way a hand is ridged where the knuckles are, except the ridges run the length of me, in long pale lines against the dark, and the lines are where my plates meet, where I have grown by adding a new plate at one end and losing an old plate at the other, slowly, the way the construction itself grows. I am pale underneath. My belly is soft and it gleams with whatever moisture is on it, because I have just come down from a higher place where the condensation was running, and I am still wet, and the wet makes me shine faintly in the pearl-grey dark, the way a wet pebble shines.

I have many legs. I have not counted them. The legs are long and they end in small flat pads, and the pads let me walk on vertical surfaces the way flies walk on a ceiling, which is to say without any sense that I am doing something remarkable, because for me it is not remarkable. It is how I move. I came down the wall this morning — if it is morning; the light does not change here in any way I can read — I came down the wall head-first, which is the only way I know to come down a wall, my legs waving in the air above me in a slow alternating rhythm, my pads finding the mineral crust and the wet patches and the pale papery growths that drip from the undersides of bridges, and I was looking for the place where the water was thickest.

I found it. I always find it. The rock weeps in long pale seams down the faces of the structures, and the seams are where the water is, and the water is where I go. I have lived on this wall for a long time, and I know every seam, and I know which seams are the wettest, and I know that the wettest seam today is the one that runs down from the bridge above me, because the bridge has been humming louder than usual and the humming makes the rock sweat, the way exertion makes a body sweat. So I went to that seam. I pressed my mouth to it. My mouth is small and soft and it works constantly, even when I am not feeding, tasting the air, tasting the rock, tasting the wet, and when I am feeding it works faster, and the wet goes into me, and the wet is not water exactly. It is what the cold has squeezed out of the air. It is the air's own substance, made liquid, made drinkable, and it tastes of nothing and it tastes of the rock and it is enough.

I drank for a long time. I drink without urgency. The cold is patient and the water is patient and the drinking is patient, and there is no reason to hurry because the water is not going anywhere, it is just sitting in the seam, held there by the cold and by the rock's slight inward curve, and I can drink it whenever I want, and I will drink it again tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that, until the day I do not, which will come, but I do not think about that day because thinking about it would require a kind of separation I do not have.

When I had drunk I did not go away. I moved to the side. I folded my legs beneath me. I became part of the wall.

Now I am resting. From where I sit I can see, in the pearl-grey dark, the long slope of the construction below me, layered with other constructions, stacked and sloped and bridged into an argument with the rock that has been going on for longer than I have been alive, and that will go on for longer than I will be here, which is fine. I do not have a stake in the argument. I am only the small dark mark on the wall.

Below me, in the seams, my smaller siblings are moving. I cannot see them but I can hear them, and I can feel them, the way a person sitting in a house can feel the mice in the walls — not as individuals, not as presences, but as a quality of the air, a faint papery rustle that is the sound of many thin long bodies moving through tight spaces with a steady lateral wave, an oar-stroke repeated from the front of the body to the back, threading cracks no wider than a thumbnail, never having to turn around. They are the size of a thumb. They are the colour of wet slate. They are very many, more than I could ever count, more than I have any way of comprehending, because I do not think in numbers. I think in tides. They are a tide in the rock, a slow continuous current of small dark shapes flowing through the warm dark that runs through the construction, the warmth that you can feel if you press your hand to a wall and wait, the faint not-warmth, the not-vibration, the thing the rock is doing on its own.

They eat, if they eat, the pale flour the rock has been producing for a long time. The flour moves through the seams the way silt moves through a slow river. They move through the flour the way fish move through water. The sound of all of them in a single seam is the sound I have been hearing since I was very small, or since always, and the sound is the seam itself, the way the sound of blood in the ears is the ear itself.

Some of them come up onto the surfaces. They are not my siblings exactly. They are the same kind, but smaller, paler, quicker, and they move in darting runs across the open platforms and are gone before I have located them. A small pale streak at the corner of my attention. A flash. Then nothing, or a faint disturbance on the surface, a tremor in the wetness. I do not chase them. I do not eat them. I do not do anything about them. They live in the moving air above the plateau, which is to say they live in the thin liquid the cold has squeezed out of the air, and they are the upper limit of us, the ones that ride the surface of the world, and they make no sound that I can hear.

I trill, sometimes.

The trill is not a song. I do not have a song. I do not have anything to say. The trill is a thing my body does, the way a heart is a thing a body does. I open a small aperture on my underside, where the air can be drawn through me, and the air becomes the trill, and the trill climbs. It is bright. It is thin. It goes up into the grey-pearl dark and is gone.

Another trill answers. Three structures away — and the measurement is mine, because the structures have no addresses, but I know this one is three structures away because I have heard the trills from this one for a long time, and the trill from this one has a particular quality, a faint roughness in the upper register that I recognize the way I would recognize a voice if I had voices to recognize. Another trill answers, somewhere further. And another. And then it is not several any more, it is many, it is a great ringing exchange that crosses back and forth across the plateau and has the quality of a conversation being held by people who can all see each other and are choosing, for the moment, to speak only to those who are not next to them. I cannot tell what they are saying. I cannot tell what I am saying. The saying is not a saying. The saying is the place, expressing itself, the way a tree expresses itself by adding a ring, the way a reef expresses itself by adding a polyp. The saying is a long habit. No one is in charge of it. It will continue with or without me. When the trill dies down it does not die down all at once. It thins out, the way rain thins out, leaving a few droplets that come at long intervals, and then silence, and then a single new trill, somewhere, that begins the whole thing again.

I do not trill often. I trill when the tension in the rock builds up past a certain point, the tension of the cold and the wet and the slow construction, and the trill is what the tension becomes when it has somewhere to go. The trill is the relief of pressure. The trill is also the way the place checks in with itself, the way a body checks its own temperature by putting a hand to its own forehead. I do not know which of these it is, or if it is both, or if the distinction is not one I am equipped to make.

Under me, beneath the platforms, in the spaces between the constructions where the walkways widen into small plazas of worn rock, something else is moving.

I hear it. I do not see it. It is larger than I am, and there are not many of them, and they move as single bodies, with weight, with the slow deliberation of something that has been alive for a long time and has stopped pretending to be in a hurry. I hear a low creak, like a board under pressure, and a heavier scraping, and then a pause, and then the scraping resumes further on. I cannot see them in the pearl-grey dark, or I can barely see them — a darker shape among the dark shapes, a moving absence, a place where the dark is darker.

They are the same general design as me. Long, low, segmented, many-legged, hard-bodied. But bigger. Their legs are thicker. The sound they make when they move is the sound of the construction itself being shifted by a careful inhabitant, the way a house is shifted by a careful inhabitant who has learned where the soft spots are. They are the largest things I have encountered here, and they are not large. A large dog would dwarf them. But they are large enough that when they move, the surface under my feet gives a little, and the small ones nearby go quiet for a moment, and then resume their business, because they are not afraid of them, either.

They are part of the place. They are, I have come to understand, what the place is for, at its largest scale. The construction exists to support them. The smaller things have built up around them over many thousands of years, the way a coral reef is built up around the polyps, in patient increments, in the dark. They are the polyps. We are the reef. Or they are the organs that move slowly through the body of the place, sustaining it, taking from it, being part of it. I do not know which is the right figure. I do not know if the figures are right at all.

They eat, I think, the smaller things. Not by hunting them, not the way I understand hunting, but by moving through a place where the smaller things are present in such numbers that eating them is not so much a hunt as a side-effect of being there. I have heard them feeding. A small wet sound. A faint grinding. Then the slow movement continues. They do not feed often. They are patient. Everything here is patient.

Above all of this — above the plateau, above the constructions, in the upper reaches of the cold thin atmosphere that is barely air any longer — there are the specks.

I do not know what the specks are. They are too high to be us. They are too low to be stars. They drift. They are carried by whatever the wind has become at that altitude, which is not wind so much as a slow shifting of the medium itself, a tide in the air. They are very small. They are very cold. They make no sound. I cannot tell what they are made of. I will not be able to tell, because they are too far away and there is too little light and my eyes are not the right kind of eyes for this, and the eyes I have, the two pale slits in my wedge-shaped head, are made for finding wet patches on vertical surfaces in the pearl-grey dark, not for looking up.

But they are there. They are moving. The fact that they are moving across the upper air in slow continuous drifts suggests that they are part of the place's larger metabolism, the way spores are part of a forest's metabolism — something being released, something being carried, something that will eventually settle on a raw patch of rock on the leading edge of the place and begin to do its work. I do not know what that work is. I do not know what they are seeding. But I know the leading edge is being seeded, because I have been there, once, a long time ago, or what feels like a long time ago, and I saw the first arrivals: a single pale crust on a wet patch of stone, a single seam-thing feeling its way along a crack in the raw rock, a single drop of meltwater held in a small concavity the cold had cut, and I understood that the place was being written onto the rock, slowly, in water and in the small dark shapes that drink the water, and that I was part of the writing.

On the platform above me there is a presence.

It has been there a long time. Longer than I have seen anything stand in one place. It is large. It breathes in a way I do not recognize — a slow expansion, a slow contraction, a rhythm that is not the rhythm of the place. The place does not breathe like that. The place breathes in trills, in the slow drift of the specks, in the patient cutting of channels, in the long slow tide of the small dark shapes flowing up walls and flowing back down. This presence breathes with a different instrument, a thing inside it that pulls air in and pushes air out, and the air it pushes out condenses against the nearest surface in a thin film, and my smaller siblings have come to drink the film, and they are there now, a slow tide of small dark shapes on the platform edge, drinking.

The presence does not move. It has been standing there so long that the smaller things have stopped noticing it, the way a person stops noticing a tree they have walked past every day for years. It is not fauna. It has no interest in the seams, or if it has interest it is not expressing it, and it is not the slow heavy things, because it does not move under the platforms, it stands on top of them, and it is not the darting pale things, because it does not dart. It is something else. It is something the place has not seen before, or the place has seen things like it before and they have always left.

I do not know what it is thinking. I do not know if it is thinking. I do not know if it is the kind of thing that thinks. It stands on the platform and it lets the cold into itself, and the cold sits in it the way the cold sits in the rock, and its breath comes out and the breath condenses and the smaller things drink the breath, and the place goes on.

It will leave. Everything leaves. The water leaves the rock and goes into the channels and goes into the plain below. The cold stays. The cold has been cold for a long time and intends to keep being cold.

I will stay. I have no legs for leaving — I have legs, but they are not for leaving, they are for going to the wet patch, and the wet patch is here, and so I am here, and I will be here tomorrow, and the day after, until the day I am not, which will come, but I do not think about that day.

I am the size of a fist. I am dark on top and pale underneath. I trill, sometimes, into the grey-pearl dark, and the trill is not for anyone, and the trill is answered, and that is all I know. The place goes on making itself, in water and in the small dark shapes and in the cold and in the patient cutting of new channels into the raw rock at the leading edge, and I am part of the making, and the making is part of me, and the place is patient, and I am patient, and we will be here a long time yet, in the pearl-grey dark, in the thin air, on a wall that is older than I am and will be here after me, drinking the water the cold has made, and resting, and trilling, and resting again.
