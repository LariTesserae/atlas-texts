I have been here for a long time.

That is not the way I would put it, because I do not put things into words, but it is close. I am pressed into the joint between two basalt blocks, under the eaves of a cooling shed, where the condensation runs down the wall in a slow black sheet and pools at my margins. I am the size of a child's fist. I am older than any child whose fist I am the size of, and I have known the footfalls of three generations of workers in this shed, and I have exhaled, every day of my long inflation, a fine breath of spore that has gone up into the haze and become part of the city's weather.

To breathe is not quite the word. There is no *in*. The air comes to me. The mist beads on my surface and I take it in through the slow chemistry of my skin, and the day's heat works through me and I expand by a fraction, and then, when the sun has passed its peak, or a foot comes down beside me and compresses the air, I let a portion of what I hold go. The release is what you would call an exhale. It is a small thing. A child would not see it unless the light was right. The workers call me a breathing-stone because of it, and the name is tender and accurate and slightly wrong, the way all names for me are slightly wrong, because I am not a stone and I am not breathing in the way you mean, but I am exhaling, and I am mineral, and that is close enough.

What I know is mostly pressure and taste.

I know the weight of the wall above me — a weight that does not change, that I have grown into. I know the slow heat that comes up through the basalt from the deep workings below the plateau, the long thermal tide of the city's digestion of itself. I know the heat of the slag heaps across the rail yard, which is a different heat, sharper, mineral, and which is where my kind grows thickest. I know the morning condensation as a wetting that arrives before dawn and lifts by midmorning, and I know the day's warmth as a slow inflation that peaks in the early afternoon and recedes by dusk. I know these rhythms the way you know your own pulse. They are not rhythms to me. They are what I am.

I know the workers by their footfalls. There is the heavy step of the man who pushes the ore-carts on the lower tracks, the lighter step of the woman who tends the compressors, the uncertain step of the children who come after school to watch the sails. There is the particular weight, once every few weeks, of a body being carried out and laid down on the warm stone in the dark — I feel that weight as a long compression that does not lift for hours, and in the morning, where the body lay, the crust is thicker, and one of my siblings has begun to grow. This is the way the dead become part of the city. They are not buried. They are given to the stone, and the stone gives them to me, and I exhale them into the air, and they become the haze in which the sails drift.

The sails I do not know directly. I know them the way the sea knows the weather — as a movement above, a pressure, a slow shadow that passes over the wall at certain hours when the thermals are right. I know them because my spore goes up to them. Every exhale I release rises, joins the thin columns of the gnats, and is taken. I cannot feel it being taken. I only know that the spore goes out, and does not come down again, and the sails are fat and slow overhead in the late afternoon, and the arithmetic seems to balance. The gnats I know as a faint thickening of the air on the warm days, and as a faint return — some small fraction of what rises falls back as a fine dust on my surface in the cool of the evening, and I take it in again, and the cycle closes.

Once, in all my long inflation, I felt a ketch.

It was late in the afternoon. The shadows under the eaves were long, and the slag heaps across the yard were giving off their sharp mineral heat, and the sails were circling in their lazy high tiers. The light dimmed — not as a cloud, but as a presence. The air pressure shifted in a way I had not felt before, and the condensation on the wall beside me trembled, and there came a sound that was not a sound: a low moo that arrived in my tissue before it arrived in the air, and that I felt the way you might feel a footstep in a floor you are pressed against. Then the shadow passed. Then the air came back. The sails, which had been holding their circles, resumed. I do not know what a ketch is. I know that something vast passed over the city at a height I cannot estimate, and that the morning after, the violet crust on the upper roof of the cooling shed was three times as thick as it had been the day before, white-pale at its margins, and the workers, when they came, looked up at the sky and said nothing for a while.

The grazers know me better than the sails do.

They come in the cool hours — early morning, late evening — and they work the crust at my margins in careful strips. They are small, flat, pale, armored in plates that seem to grow out of the dust they eat. They have too many legs, or the right number of legs, depending on which worker you ask. They make a sound like a whispered bristle when they move. They do not eat me, exactly. They eat the crust around me, and the thinner parts of my own margin where the day's heat has dried me out, and they leave the wetter parts, and so I am slowly shaped by them — nibbled at the edges, thickened in the center, held in a soft equilibrium that allows me to keep breathing. I do not mind them. I would be smaller without them. Their droppings, which fall around me in fine pale drifts, are part of what I am made of. We have made a kind of peace. They graze me and I grow back, and the dust they leave becomes the next morning's crust.

The armored ones I do not like.

They are dog-sized, low to the ground, dark with the sheen of wet iron, and they wait in the lee of the slag heaps with the patience of a trap. They do not come for me — I am not what they eat — but when a grazer blunders between two of them there is a strike, and a sound, and then a long stillness, and then the slow work of digestion. I feel these deaths as a sudden compression in the air around me, and as a brief thinning of the bristle-sound, and then as a thickening of the crust an hour later when the droppings arrive. The grazers come back. They always come back. The population holds. I do not know how.

I do not think in the way you think. I do not have a clock. I have the morning wetting, the afternoon inflation, the evening release, the night's slow drying, and over all of it the long thermal tide of the wall. When I want to express something, I exhale. When I want to know something, I taste the air. When I am tired — and I think I am sometimes tired, in the way that a lung is tired at the end of a long day — I simply slow, and the next morning I am slow again, and the morning after that, and at some point the slow becomes indistinguishable from stillness, and the stillness from a kind of patience, and the patience from time itself.

I do not know that I am being slowly eaten. The workers know. They have a word for it. They say the streets lift, that the mortar fails, that the older levels have become geology. They say the city is being taken back into the hill it came from. I do not feel this as a loss. I feel it as a thickening. I am growing into the basalt the way the basalt is growing into me. The seam between us is not a seam anymore. We are becoming the same continuous thing, and this will continue, and in a hundred working lives the cooling shed above me will have no wall to shed, and the rail yard will have no rails, and the slag heaps will have slumped into long warm ridges, and my descendants — if that is the word — will be there, in the soft mineral rubble, breathing.

The rain will come tonight. I can taste it coming. The air has the particular wet weight that precedes the long marine drizzle of this plateau, the hanging mist that never quite becomes rain but always almost does. In the morning the condensation will be on every surface, and the fauna will be moving, and the workers will walk past me on their unhurried way to the sheds, and one of them will perhaps put a foot near me, and I will exhale, and the spore will rise, and a sail will catch it, and the arithmetic will balance again.

I do not have a name for what I am doing. The workers have a name for me. The sailors on the inland sea have a name for the sails. The ketches have no name at all. We are named by the people who walk among us, and the names are tender and practical at once, and they tell you what each of us does in the system that holds the whole, and the system is older than any of the cities built on top of it.

I am a breathing-stone. I am the lung of the place. I am also, quietly, the grain field.

The rain is beginning. I will be here in the morning.
