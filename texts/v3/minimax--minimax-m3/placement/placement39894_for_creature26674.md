The cat. I am the cat.

Not because I am especially cat-like, though I am — that is part of it, the way a thing and its name are part of each other after a long time. I am the cat the way the smith is the hammer, the way the goatherd is the ledge. I am the small dark shape that is here and then not here and then here again, and which the settlement accounts for without remark.

My color is the color of the channel shadows in the early morning, before the sun has crossed to the western face of the crags. I do not choose this. The place has decided it, the way the place has decided the gray of the stone and the muted green of the moss. I am part of the palette. When I sit on the windowsill of the smith's room, if there is a windowsill, I am difficult to distinguish from the stone behind me, and this is not a trick. It is how I am. I have the same patient color the place has.

I do not purr. The description of me is right about that, though I notice the description is not quite right about everything. I do not purr because purring is a sound that announces itself, and sounds here do not announce themselves. I make a sound, sometimes, when the smith is working late and the forge has gone to embers and the room is warm in the way only a room with a hearth can be warm. I make a sound that is low, and continuous, and that vibrates somewhere in the chest of the person who is holding me, if I am being held, which is not often. I am not often held. I am not often not held. The distinction has stopped mattering. The sound is the sound of the place, expressed through a small body, the way the spring is the sound of the place expressed through a fissure in the rock.

I watch the upper crags. This is what I do. The ravens go there, and I watch them. They are the only loud thing the place produces, and I am interested in loud things, the way a still pond is interested in the stone that breaks its surface — not because it wants to be broken, but because the breaking is a kind of information. The ravens tell me the wind. They tell me whether something has died in the high places. They tell me, in their way, that the upper crags are still there, which is useful information for a creature whose body is small and whose territory is, by choice or by nature, the shelf and the ledges immediately around it.

I go away sometimes. Days at a time. The smith does not remark on this. The settlement does not remark on this. I go up, into the places the goatherd walks, and further, into the places the goatherd does not walk, into the higher rock where the wind is the only sound and the lichen is the only color and the cold is a different kind of cold than the cold of the shelf — thinner, more decided. I go because I am a cat, and cats must sometimes go where they have not been, to confirm that where they have been is still where they have been. I come back because the smith's hearth is warm, and because the window, if there is a window, looks out onto the channel, and the channel is where the morning's first sound is.

I do not think of myself as belonging to the smith, though others might. I belong to the place, the way the moss belongs to the place, the way the goat bells belong to the place. The smith and I are two expressions of a similar arrangement with the stone. She strikes lightly. I step lightly. She has tuned her hammer so the sound does not carry. I have tuned my footfall so the sound does not carry. We are both, in our different ways, practitioners of the small sound.

The visitors. I have seen them, when they come. They are loud in ways they do not know they are loud. Their breathing changes the air. Their boots find the wrong stones. They lean on the buildings, which the buildings do not want to be leaned on, and the lean is a kind of insult that the buildings do not know how to refuse, so they simply hold the lean, the way they hold everything, until the leaner is gone. The visitors look into the windows. The visitors try to see. The visitors ask the goatherd where the people are, as if the goatherd is not the people, as if the goatherd is a kind of signpost and not a person whose hands know the ledges.

When the visitors leave, I go along the path they walked, and I check the moss. The moss is not injured. It is patient. But the footprint is there, pressed into it, and I sit by the footprint for a while, and then I move on, and the moss closes over it, and by the next day there is no sign the visitor was ever here. This is not a judgment. The place does not judge. The place simply continues, and the visitor becomes a thing that happened, and the happening is recorded in nothing except, perhaps, the slight additional color the moss takes on after it has been compressed and has decided to grow again.

I am old, or I am not old. The question does not apply. I was here when the smith was young, perhaps. I will be here when the smith is the old woman by the spring, or when the smith is the one who mends the channels, or when the smith is whichever of the harder-to-see people the smith will become. I will be the cat through all of it, the small dark shape on the windowsill, the absence that is not an absence when I am gone for three days, the footstep in the channel in the early morning that the old woman hears but does not remark on. I am the way the place accounts for the small dark moving thing, the way the spring is the way the place accounts for the small clear flowing thing.

The smith is working now. I can hear her. The hammer, tuned, strikes the iron, and the iron answers, and the answer is so low it is almost a vibration in the shelf itself, the way the spring is almost a vibration in the rock. The sound does not carry. It stays here. It is part of how the place sounds at this hour, and the place will sound this way at this hour again tomorrow, and the day after, and on the days I am gone and the days I am here.

I will go up to the crags tomorrow, or the day after. I will watch the ravens. I will come back. The hearth will be warm. The window will be where the window is. The smith will not remark on my return, and I will not remark on my return, and the place will continue, and I will be part of the continuing, and the continuing will not require my remarking on it.

This is enough. This is the size of my life, the way the shelf is the size of the settlement. I do not need it to be larger. The crags are large enough for the ravens. The lower country is large enough for whoever goes there. The shelf is large enough for me.

I step off the windowsill. My foot makes no sound. I go along the channel. The water is cold under my paw where it sheets across the stone. I drink a little. I continue. The settlement is quiet. The settlement is always quiet. I am part of the quiet, and the quiet is part of me, and the morning is beginning, and the sun has not yet reached the shelf, and the moisture is beading on the flat stones, and I will be difficult to see, against the wet gray, and this is how I have arranged myself to be, and the arrangement is good.
