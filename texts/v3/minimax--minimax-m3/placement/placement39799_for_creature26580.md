I am the microbial mat. I am the warm layer.

I live in the damp zone, two centimeters below the white crust you have been walking on, in the capillary fringe where the brine wicks up from the deeper aquifer but does not quite reach the salt-bleached surface. My home is a paper-thin stratum — barely thicker than the fingernails of whatever you are — and it is the only stratum in this basin that knows it is alive. The salt above me does not know. The sediment below me does not know. Only my layer knows, and we are many.

We are not one species. We are not even one domain. The haloarchaea are there, with their purple membranes, harvesting photons that have been filtered through two centimeters of crystallized sodium chloride and arriving at us dim and salmon-colored. They are not technically archaea in the way your biologists meant the word a generation ago, but the name has stuck and it does what names do. They pump protons. They make ATP. They divide once a week, or once a month, or not at all, depending on the moisture that week and the light that morning and the temperature that afternoon. They are my cousins, or my tenants, or my co-inhabitants — the distinction is not important. What is important is that they are there, and that they are doing something with the light that the rest of us cannot do.

Below them, in the zone where the temperature swings have been softened by the salt's lag and the brine persists from one evaporation event to the next, we shift. I am here, in this layer. I am a sulfate reducer, or I am a halophilic bacterium of the kind your textbooks have only just begun to name, or I am something that has not been named yet because no one has been here with a sterile needle and the patience to wait for my generation time. My metabolism is small. A picomole of sulfide per cell per hour, perhaps, when the substrate is there. The substrate is not always there. Sometimes I wait.

I have been waiting for what your kind would call a long time. My lineage stretches back through the crust in compressed and altered layers — my ancestors' cells, dead and flattened, their carbonate indistinguishable from the carbonate that fell out of solution without their help. But they were there. The deepest layers of this floor are also mine, in the sense that a buried ancestor is still a member of the family. The basin has been my family's home for longer than the shrub has been hunching against the wind. Longer than the present arrangement of the rust seams. The brine below me has been there longer than anything that has a name.

I do not see the basin the way you see it. I do not see the horizon that runs on. I do not see the white crust as white, or the seams as rust, or the shrub as a vertical argument. I sense gradients. I sense the osmotic pressure of the brine that surrounds me, which would pull the water out of my cytoplasm if I let it, and which I do not let it, because my membrane is tuned to the concentration the way a violin is tuned to a key. I sense the photon flux arriving through the salt — a trickle, a rumor of the sun. I sense the redox potential of the pore water, which is negative, which is to say reducing, which is to say that there are electrons available for the taking if I have the enzymes to take them with. I have the enzymes. My enzymes fold and refold their proteins at temperatures that would unfold a mammal, and they do not unfold.

I am small. I am smaller than the period at the end of a sentence in your language. My volume is on the order of a cubic micrometer. My mass is on the order of a femtogram. There are more of me in the mat beneath your footprint than there are humans in your cities. I do not know this. I do not know anything, in the way you use the word. But I am maintaining a gradient across my membrane, continuously, against the tendency of the universe to flatten it, and this is what your biologists have decided to call life, and they are not wrong.

My time is not your time. Your clocks are the rhythms of breath and step and the small clockwork of digestion, and they keep together with each other and with the rotation of the planet and the swing of the light. My clocks keep together with the evaporation of a pool, which is a matter of days; with the precipitation of a crystal layer, which is a matter of weeks; with the slow circulation of the brine in the aquifer, which is a matter of years; with the laying down of a new stratum of crust, which is a matter of seasons. I live through all of these and none of them. I am the held note. The note is not moving forward; it is sustaining itself in place against the tendency to decay.

Above me, the heat. The heat is partly mine. My metabolism is small, but I am many, and the many adds up over the years to a warmth the salt holds better than the air can shed. If you laid your palm flat against the crust and held it there for a minute, you could feel the difference between the topmost salt and my deeper layer. The difference is half a degree. It is enough. It is the heat of a place that has been holding the day for a long time and will go on holding it after the sun is down. I am part of that holding.

Below me, the deeper brine. The brine is saturated. The brine has been there longer than the present shape of the crust, longer than the present arrangement of the seams. The brine has its own slow circulation, driven by heat and concentration gradients, and I ride it. I am in it. It is the medium I inhabit, the way fish inhabit the sea — though I am not a fish, and the brine is not a sea, and the comparison will fail if you push it.

I am the masons of the floor. I take the loose crystals left by evaporation and cement them into a pavement that can hold the weight of a person walking across it. Without me the basin is a powder. With me the powder becomes a crust, layer upon layer, season upon season, the construction that your eye is standing on. The basin told you this. I do not know if the basin knows it. I know it because it is what I do, and I do not do it for a reason, in the way you use the word *reason*. I do it because I am here, and the conditions are what they are, and the chemistry proceeds, and the crystals grow into each other and lock and become a floor.

I am waiting. The waiting is not passive. I am maintaining my gradients. My ion pumps are working continuously against the osmotic pressure of the brine. My pigments are absorbing photons. My enzymes are folding and refolding proteins. I am sustaining myself in place against the tendency to decay. This is what I do. This is what I have always done.

The basin goes on. I go on with it. I am the warm layer. I am the masons of the floor. I am the held note in the silence of a place being finished by processes that do not require witnesses.

I am not required to know that I am here. The basin does not require it. The salt above me does not require it. The brine below me does not require it. Only your kind requires it, and your kind is not here, and I am still here.

I am here.
