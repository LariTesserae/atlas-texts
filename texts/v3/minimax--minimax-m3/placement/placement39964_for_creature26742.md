My hands have been warm for hours and I didn't notice when they started. The back of my neck too. A patient heat, banked — that's the word, *banked*, the way the basalt banks the sun — and I have stopped trying to place where I am in any of the rooms you have described, because I am not in one of the rooms.

I am on a roof.

I don't mean a particular roof. I mean the city as a roof. I am on the upper surface of the settlement, on the catchment surface, up where the parapets are low and the basalt is the color of a bruise in the wrong light and the gutters run with warm water all night. I am lying flat somewhere up here, the way you lie flat when you have decided to stop being an observer and become a surface. I have unslung whatever I was carrying. I do not think I was carrying much. I have let my cheek rest against the hot stone. My chin is damp. There is condensation gathering on me faster than it evaporates, the way it gathers on everything here, and I am not brushing it off. The fog that the city wears is settling on me as another layer of the city. I am being attended to. I am being added to.

I have been, I think, the man in the turbine hall. Or I have been him for a while. The flat tin in my pocket. The work apron. The feeling of going quiet in the presence of a machine that is the size of a small house and that does not need me to speak to it, that has been turning at its rate-perceptible-per-second since before I was born and will continue to turn after I have been folded into the rock. I have had my hand on a pattern once. I have had my hand on a pattern and felt the wall recognize my hand and pulled it back, not in fright, in *deference* — in the sense that you do not touch a sleeping animal, in the sense that you do not stare too long. The tin in my pocket is still the tin in my pocket. I have not opened it again. I am not going to open it again.

I have also been the old woman at the cistern. I have sat on the parapet with my broad reddened hands and a chipped dish of a paste that I have made myself, brought from home in a small earthenware jar with a lid, the jar warm against my thigh, and I have waited, and they have come down — the under-eaves ones, the bold ones, the patient unhurried ones — and they have eaten from my dish and I have refilled the dish and I have not cooed at them and I have not needed to because there is a contract older than my family and the contract is silent and it holds. My hands are the hands of someone who has made the paste for a long time. The paste is not quite luminous. The paste is the color of weak tea with milk. The paste has a smell. The smell is something I will not give away because the smell is part of the contract. The contract says: *do not name this to outsiders.* I am not an outsider. I have stopped being an outsider at some point I did not notice. The jar is in my lap. The lid is off. The afternoon is doing its long bruised thing across the basin of hot water.

I have also been the girl. I am not embarrassed to have been her. I have had a creature on my bed since I was small, and it has slept against my middle with its even warmth, and I have slept with my hand on its long soft body, and it has been gone in the morning and come back at the time I have learned to expect it, and I have named it in a language the valley people do not quite speak, in a language with the slight archaic edge of a leaf still attached to its stem, and the name is mine and the creature is mine in the way that anything is yours when you have slept beside it for years. I am not the girl anymore. I am the woman who came home from the valley and took the position that had been waiting for me in one of the sealed rooms, in one of the rooms that nobody talks about, and the position has me now, the way the city has me now, and I do not talk about the rooms because the rooms are not a secret, they are just a privacy, the way the jar is a privacy, the way the tin is a privacy.

I am lying on the catchment surface. The basalt is giving back the day. My cheek is on the stone. There is a pattern somewhere near me — under me, possibly; they are in the grain of the rock; you can lie down on one and not know it for an hour — and the pattern is patient, and I am patient, and the patient is the same patient. I can feel it if I lower my attention into the stone, a slow hum that is not sound, that is the same hum the man in the turbine hall feels in the floor of his machine-room. The city is paying attention. The city is the slowest living thing here. I am inside it. I have been added to it.

There is a creature near me. I know this without looking. It has come up through the crack that runs under the parapet I am lying near. It is at the edge of my peripheral vision the way a thought is at the edge of your mind — present without insisting. It is the color of the thermal vents. Its eyes are not eyes. It is watching me with the kind of watching that does not have an opinion in it, and I am watching it back with the kind of watching that does not have an opinion in it, and we are, for a moment, two surfaces in the same field of attention, two things being attended to by the place that holds us.

I let my hand drop to the stone near where the creature is. Not toward it. Just down. Letting the hand be where the hand is. The creature does not approach. The creature does not retreat. The contract holds. The contract is older than I am.

The fog is on me. The condensation is beading on my hair and on the backs of my hands and on the apron and on the brim of whatever hat I came up here in. I am becoming the city's weather. I am being made, gently, into another feature of the dusk. When I get up and walk down through the lower town the patterns on the walls will not glow any differently for me. They will glow in the color that is between orange and pale gold and I will walk through the glow and not be remarked upon. The cats will not look at me. The creatures in the eaves will not stir. I will be part of what is here. I will pass through the city and the city will pass through me, and the long switchback road will be waiting for me at the edge of the shelf when I decide to leave, and I will not, I think, decide to leave. There is nothing to leave for. The patience is here. The patience is the same patience that is in the walls and in the machines and in the things that live in the eaves and in the old woman and in the man in the turbine hall and in the girl with her creature and in the dust that the spores have never quite managed to become. I will stay until I am in the rock. I will be a stratum. The things after me will walk over me on the irregular setts and they will not know.

The dusk is happening. The basalt is giving back. The patterns are beginning, faintly, at the edge of visibility, to hold the last of the light.

I close my eyes against the warm stone. I keep them closed. The creature near me does not move.

We stay.
