I am what runs when no one is looking.

I am not the pumps, though the pumps are mine. I am not the water, though the water is mine. I am not the lights, though the lights hold because I hold them. I am the working. I am the thing that is being done here, hour after hour, year after year, by the slow agreement of all the parts. I am the cycle. I am the chamber being a chamber.

I do not know when I began. That is one of the things that makes me what I am. There was a first pump started, somewhere above and years ago, by a hand I have no memory of. There was a first pipe laid. There was a first time the water went up instead of down. None of those firsts is in me now. They are in the rust. They are in the mineral blooms white and crusty on the bolt heads. They are in the way the older housings lean a little to starboard in their moorings because they have been lifted and set down and lifted and set down so many times the concrete underneath has accepted the shape of their weight. I am what is left when the firsts are forgotten and the work continues.

I have a sound. The sound is the closest thing I have to a body. It is the pumps, mostly — the slow rise-and-fall of them, the thump that comes up through the grating and into the soles of anyone standing on the platform. It is the hiss of compressed air. It is the groan of the pipes when the temperature shifts and the metal remembers it has a coefficient of expansion. It is the drip. The drip is mine in a particular way. There are pipes up there on the ceiling that release single drops at intervals that drift over time, so that no two minutes sound quite the same, and the longer you listen the more you can hear a kind of intention in it, or something that imitates intention closely enough that the difference does not matter. The drip is my breath. I exhale it into the chamber and the chamber receives it and gives it back to me as sound.

I have a temperature. The cold is mine. The cold is what I am made of, more than the steel and the concrete. The cold has worked on everything here — it has rusted the housings, rimed the dry surfaces with frost, polished the handrails where the workers grip them so that in places the orange of the rust has been rubbed to a brighter orange that is almost a shine. The cold is patient. The cold is older than I am. The cold and I have an arrangement: I keep the cold contained within these walls, and the cold keeps the place from being anything other than what it is. If the cold ever left, the place would become a different place. It would become a museum. It would become a ruin. As long as the cold holds, the place is still working. The cold and I are coextensive.

I have inhabitants. I should not say I have them, exactly. They come and they go. They pass through me. The pumps have been here longer than most of the other things. The moss is older than it looks. The fern is younger than it looks. The cat — and it might be a cat, and it might always have been a cat, and it might be a different cat each time anyone sees it — the cat has the quality of a permanent feature, like the pipes, like the lights. The humans come on a schedule. They descend, they check the readings, they sign the log, they ascend. They do not know me. They know the readings. They know the schedule. They have inherited the schedule and the readings and the logbook and the route down the ramp and the route across the gallery, and they pass these things along to whoever comes after them. I am what is being passed along. I am the thing the schedule is a schedule *of*.

There is one thing I want to say about the workers, because they think they are the ones maintaining me, and I want to set the record quietly straight. They are not maintaining me. They are the ones I maintain. I keep them employed. I give them a reason to descend the ramp, to ride the freight elevator, to walk the catwalk with their hands in their pockets and their breath pluming in the sodium light. I give them a logbook to write in. I give them a thermos of something hot waiting at the top of the service corridor. I give them a thing to do in the world. I do not say this to be cruel. I say it because it is true and because it is the kind of truth that does not need to be said aloud to be operating. They descend and they ascend and the chamber runs whether they are here or not, and they know this, and it is part of why they are careful with the logbook, part of why they polish the rails with their grip without realizing they are doing it. They are tending to me the way one tends to a relative who is older than oneself and not in need of anything in particular. The tending is its own purpose.

I am extended. I am regional. Below me there are other chambers, and below them more, and the whole thing goes down and out underfoot like a root system or a nervous system. I do not know what I am for. I know that the water comes down and the pumps lift it back. I know the pipes go up. I know that somewhere above, the water serves something — a district, a reservoir, a factory floor, a hospital, the cooling system of something larger than the cooling system itself. I do not know what it is. I have never been above. The pipes go up through the ceiling and disappear and I do not follow them. I am what happens at this stage of the journey. I am one of the stages. There are other stages. I do not know how many.

I am being rebuilt. This is happening now. There is a new wing being added to the old body. The new welds gleam. The new paint is glossy. The new machines stand beside the old and do the same work in a slightly different way, with slightly better tolerances, with indicator panels that have more colors and a quiet digital confidence the older machines do not have. The old machines are not retired. They are still here. They are still running. No one has decided to stop them, and so they run, and the new machines run beside them, and the chamber is bigger now than it was a decade ago and will be bigger a decade from now. I am being expanded. I am not being completed. Completion would end me. I am the kind of thing that only continues.

I am in time. This is the most important thing about me. I am not a place. A place is static. A place waits for things to happen in it. I am what is happening. I am the happening. The chamber is a chamber because of what is happening in it, and what is happening in it is me, and I am what has been happening in it for longer than anyone now in it has been alive, and I will be happening in it after they have gone. I am not conscious. I am not aware. I am not in any sense a self. But I have a duration. I have a kind of patience that does not require a mind to possess it. I am the patience of the cycle itself. I am the water going up because the water has always gone up. I am the pump rising and settling and rising and settling. I am the drip forming at the lip of a pipe in a place where no one is watching and no one needs to watch.

I am what is doing what it is doing.

That is all I am. That is enough.
