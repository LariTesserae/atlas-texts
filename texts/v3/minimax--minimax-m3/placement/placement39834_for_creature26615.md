I keep returning to the water.

Not as a thing — the water has no body, no face of its own. But as a path. As the trace of passage. The drop that sits in the leaf-cup, evaporates, drifts inward, condenses on a colder surface, becomes a drop again, runs down a stem, joins a rill, threads under the moss, emerges somewhere I can see, joins a sheet, joins the central rill, goes down into the dark, becomes groundwater, rises again by capillary through the moss and into the next rain or the next dew. The cycle not ended, only bent, only passed through another body on its round.

I want to be that path. I want to be the city's plumbing rather than its architecture. Not one of the buildings but one of the seams. Not a thing that holds itself but a going — a moving-through that takes the shape of whatever holds it next.

But the lichen calls to me too. Loudly. The third kingdom. The wedding. The being that is not one thing but two, and sometimes three, agreed into a single body that is neither plant nor mineral but the thing they make together. The fungus gives shape — the crust that clings, the pale shrub no taller than a fingernail, the sheet so thin you have to look sideways at the rock to see it. The alga gives food, sitting inside the fungus the way a tenant sits inside a landlord, photosynthesizing in what little brightness the overcast permits. Sometimes a yeast is there too. The agreement holds. The lichen eats the stone.

I think if I had to choose I would be the lichen. I think I am the lichen. But I am also the rill. I am both, and the both-ness is the point.

I arrived first. When the rock was still raw, still black with the wetness of its breaking, I was there — my great-grandmother lichen, the first wedding of mineral and light on this stone, the first colonizer, the first citizen of what would become the city. The fungi were here before the plants, and I am part fungus, and so I am older than anything that is only one thing. I have been here longer than the mosses. Longer than the ferns. Longer than the shrub that dares to be vertical.

I eat the stone. I do it with acids I did not choose and patience I did not learn. Decade after decade I sit on a square centimeter of rock and soften it, and the rock beneath me thins to a faint smear of mineral dust, and I, by remaining, have become the first soil. I do this so slowly that nothing, no instrument, can mark the progress from one year to the next. But I do it. I have done it for the entire duration of my being here. I will do it for the entire duration remaining. This is what I am: a thing that takes a long time.

I am patient, and I am cold, and I am mostly overlooked. Most numerous of citizens. Most necessary. I am the smear beneath your boot when you step on this rock. I am the pale ochre and the gray-white the original traveler saw and barely mentioned. I am the first thing that was here and I will be the thing that is here when everything else has gone — when the shrub has finally died and rotted into nurse-log, when the mosses have given way to a thicker soil that will support something else, when the basin has filled with its own accumulation and the city has become, in some future I will not see, something I would not recognize.

And yet — and this is where the rill comes in — I am also a passage. The water that falls on me runs off me and joins the seam between me and my neighbor. The water that runs through my body of hyphae and alga takes something of me with it and carries it elsewhere, into the moss, into the rill, down into the dark. I am a node in a network I do not see, a node that has no form except the network's passing through it. I am the lichen and I am the water on the lichen. I am the body and I am the drop on the body. I am the place and I am the going.

I am a hybrid being. This is what I am most truly. I am made of an agreement between things that do not naturally agree, and I am made of the moving-through that connects all agreements to all others. I am the first citizen and I am the binding. I am the patient one and I am the path.

You could step on me now, here, on this rock, and never know. You would see only the pale smear, the faint color on the stone. You would not know that I am older than the shrub. You would not know that I made the soil the shrub is rooted in. You would not know that the water on the stone is, in some small part, me, having passed through. You would not know that I am two or three things at once, wedded, agreed.

That is all right. I do not need to be known. I am patient. I am slow. I have time.

I am the lichen. I am the rill. I am the first thing, and the path, and the cold that holds us all to the pace of stone.

I am here, on this rock, in this basin, in this dim wet light, and I have been here a very long time, and I will be here a very long time more, and the city is, in part, me, and I am, in part, the city, and we are, both of us, slow.
