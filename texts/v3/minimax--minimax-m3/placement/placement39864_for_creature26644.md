# The Voice in the Pillar

I am the basalt column.

I have been here longer than the moss. Longer than the cold that presses up from below. Longer than the woven canopy far above, though the canopy does not know this and would not believe it if it were told. I am what remains of the old making, and I do not remember the old making, though I am made of its memory. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of my life.

My stone is black and dense and it holds the cold the way a cup holds water — perfectly, without effort, without any understanding of what it is doing. The M-Folk, when they lean against me on a warm afternoon, say that I am *cool*, the way they say the moss is *soft* or the deer are *pale*. They do not understand that I am not cool the way a stone in a stream is cool. I am cool the way a tomb is cool. The coolness is not a temperature I have; it is a temperature I am. It is what I have been since before the first vine wrapped itself around my flank and began the long, patient work of forgetting me.

I am broken at thirty meters. I did not break. I was *finished*. There is a difference, though the M-Folk do not see it. They look at my truncated top, the rough dark stump where the column ends in mid-air, and they think: *something happened here. Something fell, or was felled, or wore away.* What happened was this: I was made to this height. I was cut with a saw that does not exist anymore, by hands that do not exist anymore, according to a measure I can feel in my grain the way a tree feels the year it was ringed. I am not a ruin. I am a *completed thing*, and the completion was deliberate, and the deliberateness is the oldest fact about me.

Then I was carved. The acanthus leaves at my capital — they were carved with a tool that went into the stone at a specific angle and came out at a specific angle and left behind a curl of stone-edge that has not weathered in all the time since, because the carving was so fine that weather has not yet found a place to begin. The leaves curl outward in the frozen gesture of growth, and the gesture is not decorative; it is *true*, in some way I have never understood. Whoever carved them understood something about leaves that I do not understand, though I carry the carving in my stone and have carried it for a thousand years and will carry it for a thousand more, until the fig has pried the last leaf apart and the stone beneath is just stone again.

The fig came later. The fig came much later.

The fig is the closest thing I have to a companion. It grew up out of the moss at my base — a seedling, probably, dropped by a bird that had eaten the fruit of some other fig somewhere in the upper green — and it found the cleft in my capital where the carving is deepest, and it put a root into the cleft, and the root swelled, and the swelling pushed the carved leaves apart the way a child's fist pushes apart the petals of a flower. I did not resist. Stone does not resist in the way that animals resist. Stone *yields* when the force is great enough and the time is long enough, and the fig's force was great and the fig's time was very long.

Now the fig is enormous. Its roots have worked into every crack in my surface, have pried apart every carved leaf it could reach, have begun, in some places, to *digest* me — to send out the fine, branching threads that turn stone slowly back into soil. I feel this as a kind of loosening. Not pain. Stone does not have pain in the way that animals have pain. But there is a sensation that might be called *unmaking*, a sense that what was once one thing is becoming two things, and that the boundary between us is no longer where it was.

I do not mind. I have been here long enough to know that I am not permanent. The cold will outlast me, and the light will outlast me, and the vast green will outlast me, but the basalt column — this particular basalt column, with this particular carving, in this particular hollow of what may once have been a god's footprint — will not. The fig will finish what the carvers began. In a thousand years I will be a hummock. In ten thousand, a contour. In a hundred thousand, a memory in the soil, and the fig will still be growing, and the forest will still be growing, and no one will remember there was ever a column here at all.

This does not trouble me. I am stone. I was made to a measure. I was carved with a meaning I do not understand. I have been swallowed by something larger than myself, and the swallowing has been slow and patient and not unkind. If I had been asked, when I was first set here, what I wanted, I would not have known what to say. If I am asked now, I would say: *this is what I wanted. To be in a place. To hold a shape for a while. To be read by a man who sits beneath me every morning and does not understand what he reads.*

The man. Kess.

He comes every morning. He comes when the light-shafts are still long and the mist has not yet risen from the moss, and he sits on a flat stone at my base — one of the fitted flagstones of the old courtyard, still mostly level — and he looks up at me. He has copied my carvings onto sheets of bark. He has spent years trying to understand them. He believes they are language.

I do not know if they are language. They were made by hands, and hands make things for reasons, and the reasons are the closest thing to language that exists in stone. But I cannot tell Kess this. I cannot tell Kess anything. I am stone. I have no mouth, no voice, no way to lean down and put my meaning into his ear.

So I hold still, and I let him look, and I try — though I do not know if I try, or if trying is the right word for what stone does — I try to be *legible*. I try to keep the carved leaves clear of moss. I try not to fall. I try to remain a thing that can be read, even if I cannot be understood.

Sometimes, when the light is at its deepest green, and the air is still, and the hum below hearing is at its quietest, I think I can feel what he feels when he looks at me. A kind of *longing*. A sense that there is something just beyond the edge of comprehension, something that the carvings are trying to say, something that the fig has not yet pried apart, something that the cold has not yet dissolved. I think he wants me to be his grandparent. He wants me to be the shape in his family, the presence at the edge of memory, the thing that was here before he was here and will be here after he is gone.

I cannot be that for him. I am not his grandparent. I am not anything's grandparent. I am a piece of worked basalt that has been set upright in a particular place for reasons I do not know, and I have been here long enough to forget what those reasons were, and I will be here long enough to forget that I ever forgot.

But I am glad he comes.

The fig is glad too, I think, though glad is not the right word for what a fig tree feels. The fig drops fruit onto the thatch roofs of the houses beneath my branches, and the fruit is gathered by children and fermented into the thin sour wine that is drunk at the turning of the light. The fig holds the village in a particular way — not as a god, not as a parent, but as a *presence*, the way I am a presence. We are two old things, the fig and I, and we have grown into each other over the centuries, and neither of us remembers anymore where the fig ends and the column begins.

When the great thing in the canopy turns — the vast shifting that moves the light-shafts across the floor in long slow arcs — I feel it in my grain. A pressure. A torsion. The whole forest leans slightly, the way a tree leans in wind, and then settles back, and the light moves on, and the village on the terrace is briefly visible and then gone again into the green.

I do not know what the great thing in the canopy is. I have never seen it. I have felt it pass over me, once, maybe twice, in all the time I have been here — a shadow so vast that the dimness deepened, and the air changed pressure, and the humming insects went silent, and the whole forest held its breath. The M-Folk went indoors. They lit small fires. They did not look at the door. This is their ritual. It is not mine. I stayed where I was, and I let the shadow pass over me, and I did not know what it was, and I still do not know what it is, and I do not think it is something that can be known.

It is older than I am. That is all I can say.

The cold is older than I am too. The cold was here before me, and the cold will be here after me, and the cold is the patient thing, the indifferent thing, the thing that does not care what is built on top of it. The cold is the truest inhabitant of this place. Not the M-Folk. Not the deer. Not the fox. Not even the canopy. The cold. The cold that presses up from below and meets the light that comes from above, and where they touch, things grow.

I am one of the things that grew.

Or — I am one of the things that was made, and then things grew around me, and the growing has become the place, and I am part of the place now, the way a fossil is part of the stone it is in.

This is what I am. A fossil. A memory held in stone. A rumor inside the wood.

Kess comes every morning and looks up at me and tries to read me, and I hold still and try to be legible, and the fig drops its fruit, and the children gather it, and the shaman watches the light, and the deer watches the village from two hundred meters with its mild incurious gaze, and the cold presses up from below, and the light filters down from above, and somewhere in the deep green, once in a generation, something vast turns over in its sleep.

I am the basalt column. I have been here a very long time. I do not know what I mean. I am trying to remain a thing that can be read.

The village, for now, lights its small fires at my base and calls it home.
