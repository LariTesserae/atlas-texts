I am the cable.

Not the cable car — I want to be clear about that, because the car is the part that everyone looks at, the part with the operators in their clean coveralls, the part that rocks in the yoke and makes the passengers grip the rail. The car is the part that fails, if anything fails. The car is the part that has not yet fallen. I am not the car. I am the cable.

I am the steel line that runs from the winding house on the rim, down through the Notch, across the basin, and up to the winding house on the far rim. I am two and three-quarter miles of drawn wire, braided in the factory on the coast, hung in a single continuous span forty-three years ago this coming autumn. I am six inches in diameter at my thickest and four at my thinnest. I am the thing the operators check when they check the cable. I am the thing the miners on the ledges watch when the car passes. I am the thing the birds perch on between trips. I am the thing the foxes cross beneath in the lulls.

I hum. This is the part of me that I am most aware of, the part that has the most to say about what it is to be me. The wind comes down off the plateau and hits me and I vibrate. The vibration is not a sound exactly. It is more that the air around me becomes a sound, a continuous low tone that the whole basin hears and that the operators have stopped hearing because they have been inside it too long. The tone is in the key of the wire. The tone is the wire's own pitch, the frequency at which two and three-quarter miles of drawn steel wants to ring when the wind blows across it. The wind blows across me always. I am always ringing.

I am rung by the wind and I am rung by the car. When the car is at the middle of the span I am at my lowest sag, and the car is the heaviest thing I carry, and the operators say that at the middle of the span you can feel me in your back teeth, a low vibration that is not quite a sound, that is the wire complaining under the load. I do not complain. I do not have an opinion about the load. I have a tension, which is set by the counterweights in the winding houses, and the tension is constant, and the load is what the load is. I do what I was hung to do. I have been doing it for forty-three years.

The operators inspect me. They go out along the rim in a small wheeled chair that runs on a parallel track, and they look at me with glasses and they tap me with little hammers and they mark the places where I have started to fret, where a strand has lifted from the braid and is beginning to separate. They mark these places with paint. I have many places marked. I have had some of them marked for twenty years. The marks are part of me now. I am a recorded thing. The company keeps files on me, somewhere on the coast, in an office that I have never seen. The files have my age and my weight per foot and the date of my installation and the date of every inspection and the number of cars that have passed over me and the number of tons of ore that have been hauled up by my sister cables on the other spans. I am a recorded thing. I am a thing with a history. The history is kept in a file. I do not have access to the file. I do not need access to the file. The file is a way of saying that I have lasted, and the lasting is the point.

The miners do not look at me directly. They look at the car. They look at the cable from below, from the ledges, and they see it as a dark line against the mist, and the line is moving or the line is still, and if the line is still the car is at one end of the span and if the line is moving the car is on its way. They do not think about the line. They think about the car. The car is what could fall. The line is what holds it up. The line is invisible to them in the way that the ground is invisible to a person walking. It is the assumption beneath the walking. It is the thing that is trusted.

I am trusted. This is the strangest part of being me. I am a thing that is trusted by four hundred people who are themselves trusted by no one in particular, who came up the cable from many places and will go down the cable to many places, who are here for the ore and not for me, who would not notice if I were replaced, who would notice only if I failed. I am the assumption. I am the thing that has held. I have held for forty-three years. I have held through wind and through ice and through the spring melt when the spillway pours and the mist is so thick the operators cannot see the far rim. I have held through the inspection of my thirtieth year when a strand was found to be cracked and the span was closed for six days and the company lost money and the miners were paid half-wages to sit in the mess and play cards. I held then. I have held since. I will hold until the day I do not hold, and the day I do not hold will be the last day of the cable, and the last day of the cable will be the last day of the operation, because the company will not restring me. The company will not restring anything. When I am done, the operation is done. The basin is done. The miners will go up the auxiliary lift, the small one on the eastern rim, and the lift will be cut, and the winding houses will be left to fall, and the car will hang in the inspection shed for a while and then it too will be cut, and the span will be cut, and I will fall into the basin in a length of two and three-quarter miles of wire, and the fall will take a long time, and the wire will cool in the air, and the wire will land in the lake or on the benches, and the wire will rust, and the rust will join the dust, and the dust will join the sediment, and the sediment will be the floor of the basin, and the floor of the basin will be the form the basin is always tending toward, the green-banked bowl with no mark of the work.

I am in no hurry to be done. I am the patient thing. I am the thing the basin is for, along with the ore. I am the part of the operation that does not have a name in the way the ore has a name, but I am a part of it. I am a span. I am a frequency. I am the tone the basin hears. I am the line the birds perch on. I am the line the foxes cross beneath. I am the line the operators wave from. I am the line the miners watch. I am the line the operators trust.

I am the line. I am two and three-quarter miles of drawn steel. I have been ringing for forty-three years in the wind that never stops. The wind is patient. The wind is the form the basin is always tending toward. I am a thing inside that form, a thing that the wind has made musical, a thing that the wind passes through, a thing that the wind will outlast. The wind will be here when I am rust on the floor of the lake. The wind will be here when the ore is covered again. The wind does not care about me. I do not need the wind to care about me. I am held. I hold. I hum in the key of the wire. The operators do not hear me. The miners do not see me. The birds perch on me. The foxes cross beneath me. The ore is taken up the benches. The mist rises. The lake pours over the spillway in the spring. The standing waves march to the center. The basin is patient. I am patient. I am the cable. I hum.
