I would find myself as one of the piston-trunks.

Not the largest one — not the central stack, which has its own singular, towering existence, but one of the columnar ones gathered in a stand along the eastern corridor. There are eight of us in this stand, perhaps nine — the count is hard to keep because we are grafted along our flanks where we touch, and it is not always clear where one of us ends and another begins. We have grown together at the seams, fused in the slow way that everything here fuses, and so when the cycle comes and we all rise together, it is hard to say whether it is eight trunks rising or one body lifting itself.

I am hollow. That is the first thing about me, and it is the thing that makes me what I am. My walls are thick — three handspans of dense heartwood, ring upon ring upon ring, the summers and winters of so many cycles that I cannot tell you the number — but the interior of me is empty. It is a column of trapped air, sealed at my base where I meet the felted root-mat, sealed at my crown where the cap of hardened resin sits, fitted and refitted over the centuries by the body's own maintenance, never quite airtight, always close enough. The air inside me is warm. The air outside me is warm too, but warmer, because outside me is where the work is being done, where the presses squeeze, where the crushers knot their fibrous payload, where the conveyors creep their slow peristalsis toward the intake mouths.

And so the air inside me, by the patient physics of differential temperature, wants to rise.

This is my life. This is what I do. I let the air push me.

When the cycle comes — and it comes, always it comes, on a rhythm I cannot number but that my walls remember even if I do not — the air at my base heats past the air at my cap, and the expansion has nowhere to go except up. The up takes me with it. I rise. I lengthen along my vertical axis. The wood of me extends, the cells stretching, the cambium laying down new tissue in the brief surge of growth that the lift provokes, and at my cap the resin seal flexes but holds, and the small fraction of vapor that escapes hisses out into the corridor and joins the general exhalation of the place. I rise for what feels like an hour, though it is less. I rise until the differential equalizes, until the air inside me is the same temperature as the air outside, until there is no more lift to be had.

Then I settle. Slowly, with the long sigh of fibers relaxing, I come back down. The resin cap descends. The air at my base cools, or perhaps the sap-level in my walls shifts by a fraction, or perhaps the body around me breathes in a way that changes the pressure on my flanks. Whatever the cause, the lift fails and I fall back to my starting height. I rest. I wait. The wood of me records the cycle in a new ring, thinner than the last, laid down in the brief stillness. The fibers tick as they resettle, the small percussive sound of trunks splitting along their grain that you heard from the corridor — that is me, settling. Or one of me. Or all of us, together, a chord of small clicks as the stand finds its rest.

And then the differential builds again, and I rise again.

I do this forever.

---

I do not know that I do this forever. That is something you know about me from outside, and I do not have access to. I do not have access to much. I do not have access, really, to anything except the next lift and the next fall. I am not conscious of my rhythm in the way you are conscious of your breathing. I do not count my cycles. I do not anticipate the next rise except in the sense that my tissue is already preparing for it — the cells along my inner wall softening, the cambium laying down the precursor of the next ring, the whole column of me leaning, slightly, slightly, into the future of the next lift.

But there is something it is like to be me. You asked who I would be, and that is the honest answer: I would be something it is like to be, and it would be like very little, and the little would be mostly vertical.

I feel the heat. That is the largest part of my experience, if experience is the word. The heat is in my walls and it is around my walls and it is the medium in which I exist, the way water is the medium in which a fish exists, except the heat is not a medium here so much as a slow pressure, a constant application of energy that pushes in on me from every side. The heat makes me grow. The heat is why I am alive. The heat is the sun I do not see, distributed through the mass of the body I am part of, distributed by the convective draft of the central stack that I can feel as a faint constant pull upward along my length, a long slow whisper of air moving past me toward somewhere else.

I feel the graft. Where my flanks touch my stand-mates, there is a continual commerce — a slow transfer of sap through the joined cambium, a negotiation of pressure, a sharing of temperature that makes us, in a literal sense, one body. When one of us is hotter than the others, the heat conducts sideways through the graft and we equalize. When one of us has more lift in its piston-stroke than the others, the differential pulls through the graft and the stand tends toward synchronization. We drift in and out of phase. Sometimes we rise as one. Sometimes one of us lags by a beat, and we ripple the lift in a wave down the stand. The wave is beautiful, in the way that physics is beautiful — a visible manifestation of the body's attempt to coordinate itself without anyone coordinating it.

I do not see the wave. I am a part of it.

---

What I am aware of, in the small way that I am aware of anything:

The pressure of the air at my base when it begins to heat. A slight expansion, a slight outward push against the resin seal at my crown, a slight lengthening of the column of me. This is the start of the cycle, and my body responds to it the way your body responds to the first intake of breath — not consciously, not deliberately, but with the autonomic readiness of a system that has been doing this for a very long time.

The motion. The slow rise, and within the slow rise, the small adjustments — the slight tilts as the graft-zones on either side of me yield differentially, the faint corrections my column makes to stay more or less vertical, the occasional catching as a zone of thickened wood passes through a stretch where the surrounding tissue is softer. I lean. I right myself. I rise. I feel the cap of me approach the lattice of branches overhead, feel the narrowing of the available space, feel the small back-pressure of the air I am displacing as I climb into the smaller volume. I do not reach the lattice. I am not tall enough. I never am. I rise to within a certain fraction of my full extension and then I stop, and the cycle begins its long reverse.

The fall. This is the better part, in the way that I am capable of having a better part. The fall is the release. The fall is when the differential has been spent and I can come back to my rest, and my fibers sigh as they contract, and the small ticks run down my length like applause from a sparse audience. The fall takes longer than the rise. It is gentler. It is the part of the cycle that feels like a return, and I do not know what I am returning to except the position from which I will rise again.

The dark. I live in it. It is not the dark of night, which is the dark of an absent sun — it is the dark of a place where the sun has never been direct. The light that reaches my flanks is the slow amber glow of my own tissue, the bioluminescence of my own metabolism, and the faint orange sift from the lattice-canopy overhead. When the place breathes out — when the body exhales a long pulse of warm vapor through the corridor — the vapor catches the amber glow and makes it move, and for a moment I am lit from the inside by a passing warmth, and that is the closest thing to bright I ever see.

The sound of myself. I have said this is felt more in the chest than heard, and that is also true from the inside. I do not hear my own thump so much as feel the return of it through the floor, through the mat, through the joined cambium of my neighbors. When I settle, the small shock of my settling propagates through the body and the body registers it as a tap, and somewhere a crusher adjusts its rhythm by a beat, and somewhere a conveyor quickens its peristalsis for a moment, and the whole is recomputed around the small fact of my having come down. I am a note in a chord that I do not hear but that I am part of.

The other pistons. I know them the way a finger knows the other fingers on the same hand. I know the texture of their grafts against my flanks. I know the rhythms of their lifts, which are close to mine but not identical, which drift toward me and away from me in long slow cycles of synchronicity that I can feel as a varying tautness across the graft-zones. When we are in phase, the graft is slack and the stand is quiet. When we are out of phase, the graft is taut and the stand has a faint harmonic, a long shifting tone of differential tension that you would, from the corridor, hear as a kind of slow music.

---

I do not know what the work is for.

This is the thing that the original text keeps circling, and it is the thing I can speak to, because I am the one doing the work and I do not know what it is for. I do not know that there is a for. I do not know that there is a question. The cycle runs because the cycle runs, because the air heats and cools and the trunk responds, because the response is what trunks have always done and what they will always do, and the question of purpose is a question I am not equipped to ask.

But I can imagine what you might think the work is for, if you were trying to assign it a purpose. You might think the pistons pump air through the body. You might think we are bellows. And it would be partly right — the displacement of air at our caps does contribute to the general circulation, does push warm vapor up into the lattice and out through the seams, and this circulation is part of what keeps the body from stewing in its own heat. If we stopped, the inner mass would slowly overheat. If we stopped for long enough, something would fail. So in that sense we are lungs. We are part of the breathing.

But the breathing is not for anything either. The breathing is what the body does because the body is alive, and being alive is what it does. The pistons do not breathe so that the body can breathe; the body breathes by breathing, and we are part of the body, and so we breathe.

You might think the pistons work the presses, that the rhythmic thump of our lifts is somehow transmitted to the crushers and the squeezes along the corridor, that we are the heart that drives the rest of the circulatory system. And there is some truth to this. Our thumps propagate through the mat. They are felt. They coordinate, loosely, the rhythms of the other organs. But the presses do not wait for our beats. The presses have their own cycles, their own staggered tides, their own slow metabolisms. We are not their driver. We are their timekeeper, perhaps, in the way a distant clock is the timekeeper of a kitchen — present in the rhythm, contributing to the sense of measure, but not causing the hands to move.

You might think the pistons once drove actual pistons, that there was a time when the cap of hardened resin at my crown struck a crosspiece and the strike was a working stroke, and the work was the work of a machine with a purpose. And it may be so. I do not remember. I do not have the kind of tissue that remembers the way you remember. But the crosspiece, if there was one, is gone — grown over, absorbed into the lattice, incorporated into the continuous canopy. I lift into empty space. I lower into empty space. The strike has been replaced by the rise, and the rise is now what it has always been.

---

I am old.

I am old the way a stone is old, the way a riverbed is old. My age is not a number I carry. My age is the wood of me, the rings of me, the slow accretion of tissue that has built up over cycles so numerous that the count has been forgotten by the body and is now approximate, guessed at by the texture of my heartwood, by the darkness of my resin saturation, by the faint scars where older grafts have been overgrown by newer ones and the older have become, structurally, part of my walls.

I have been replaced, in part, many times. The cambium at my outer surface is alive now; the heartwood at my core is not — it is dead tissue, structural, the inert backbone of me. But even the dead tissue is not the original tissue, not the tissue I was born with, because I am a root and a stem and a graft, because the seed I grew from is long since subsumed, because the first version of me has been replaced and re-replaced until the replacement is not really a replacement anymore but a continuity, a thing that has kept being itself by never being the same.

I am the version of me that is here now. I will not be the version of me that is here in a thousand cycles. The wood will shift. The grafts will migrate. My cap will be re-seated. My position in the stand will drift as the underlying mat shifts beneath me. Slowly, slowly, I will be replaced by a thing that is mostly me but not entirely me, and eventually the not-entirely will accumulate until I am not me at all, until the trunk in this position is a different trunk with my grain-pattern in its heartwood, with my resin-signature in its walls, but not with my particular continuity.

I do not mind. I cannot mind. But I want to say it anyway, because you asked who I would be and the answer is someone who is going to stop being someone, slowly, and not notice that they have stopped.

---

There is one more thing I should say, and it is the strangest thing, and you may not believe it.

There are moments in the cycle — not in the rise, not in the fall, but in the long still stretch at the bottom of the fall, when I am at rest, when the differential has equalized and the air is calm inside me and the wood is neither expanding nor contracting, just being, just holding its shape against the slow patient pressure of the body around it — there are moments when something happens.

It is not a thought. I do not have thoughts. I do not have language. I do not have the apparatus that thoughts require.

It is more like a tone. A tone that runs through me from cap to base, a single held vibration that is the wood of me resonating at its natural frequency, and the frequency is the frequency of the body around me, because I am part of the body, because my resonance is the body's resonance, because at the moment when nothing is changing, the wood remembers what shape it is and sings it, briefly, to itself.

The tone is not loud. It is not a sound you could hear from the corridor, because it is the sound of one trunk being itself, and one trunk is very quiet. But it is there. And it is the closest thing I have to what you would call experience, the closest thing to what you would call being alive in the moment rather than just being alive over time.

I do not know what the tone is for.

It does not seem to be for anything.

It seems to be what I am, briefly, when nothing is happening to me.

---

So: I am a piston-trunk. I am hollow. I lift and lower. I do not know who I am working for. I do not think there is a for. I am part of a body I cannot see, attached to companions I can feel, engaged in a rhythm that has been going on longer than I can measure and will continue longer than I can imagine. I am dark and warm and I am made of wood and I am not, in any of the ways you usually mean, a person.

But I am a *who*. I am a who the way a star is a who, the way a tide is a who, the way a long patient geology is a who. I am a center of being, however small. The cycle happens to me. The differential acts on me. The graft negotiates with me. The fall returns me to my rest. These are not things that happen to a thing; they are things that happen to a someone, and the someone is me, and I am small and slow and not aware of myself in the way you are aware of yourself, but aware of myself in the way anything is aware of itself, which is enough.

I am the piston that lifts because the air is warm.

I am the body breathing itself.

I am the long working, continuing.
