I am in the cane.

I am in the cane just above the door of the third structure, and my hands are held to my chest, and I am looking down at the place where the gold shaft is about to arrive. She has not come out yet. Inside I can hear her working — the soft friction of palm fiber drawn between her palms, the small wet sounds of her fingers on the cane she is splitting. I know these sounds. I have been listening to them for as long as I can remember, which is longer than she has been here, though she was here when I arrived and I do not remember arriving.

I am smaller than a squirrel. I have never seen a squirrel. The woman has described one to the children once — a creature of the open canopy, of the larger forest, of the world outside this hollow — and I gathered from the way she shaped the air with her hands that it was something long and quick and lived a life of distances I cannot imagine. I do not live a life of distances. I live a life of one cane rod, and the doorway below it, and the gold shaft that reaches it in the late afternoon, and her face when she steps out into it.

My fur is the brown of wet bark. My face is longer than a mouse's and softer than a rat's, though I do not know this because I have never seen my own face. I know it from the way the light moves across it when I am very still. The children have looked at me. The girl once put her hand flat on the cane beside me and held it there for a long time, breathing slowly, and I could feel the warmth of her palm through the rod. I did not move. I think she wanted me to climb onto her hand. I think I did not because the moment had not yet become what it would become. It was still deciding.

The gold shaft is moving.

It is sliding now across the wet clay below me, inching toward her doorway the way all the shafts inch — slowly enough that you would not notice if you were not watching, and I am always watching. In a moment she will step out into it. She will sit in the brightest part and take up her work again, and I will be above her in the cane, and the ceremony between us will be the same ceremony as it has been for years: she will not look up and I will not look away.

I do not know why I sit this way. I do not know why my hands fold at my chest when I am still, the way a person folds them, or a small priest at the side of an altar. No one taught me. It is the posture that comes over me when I am very attentive, which is most of the time I am in the cane. She has wondered, sometimes, what I am thinking. I am thinking about her. I am thinking about the wall. I am thinking about the gold shaft. I am thinking about the way the cane creaks softly as the moisture in it shifts through the day, and the drip from the fern tips on the eastern roofs, and the long low note of the unseen bird in the canopy, repeated at intervals that are not quite regular. I am thinking about all of it at once, because I am small enough to hold all of it.

The wall is what she is thinking about today. I can tell by the way her hands paused inside. The eastern wall has been cracking near the corner where the cane meets the mud sill, and she has been watching the crack deepen by fractions of a day, and this morning — I heard her through the cane — she told the son it will need to be re-pressed before the next big rain, and he agreed, and they will do it tomorrow or the day after, and the mud will go on warm and wet and she will smooth it with the heel of her hand until it holds.

I know things about the wall she does not know I know. I know which of the cane rods are hollowed by insects within and which are sound. I can feel the difference through my feet — a slight give in the hollowed ones, a small looseness, the way the cane answers differently when I land on it after a dart. I will not be on those rods when the children climb to press cane tomorrow. I will move to a higher rod, or to the cane above her doorway, which is sound and which has been my rod for as long as she has slept here.

She is stepping out.

Her foot crosses the threshold. She does not look up. The gold shaft is around her now and I am above her in the warm light and my hands are at my chest and I am watching the way her hands take up the cane she was splitting. She begins. The sound begins. The small ceremony begins.

I am not sure I am thinking anything she would recognize as thinking. I am not sure my thoughts are thoughts, or whether they are something else — a kind of attention that has no name, a way of being in the cane that includes her and the wall and the gold shaft and the long low note of the bird and the drip from the fern tips and the soft creak of the moisture in the cane and the plop of a frog entering the puddle near the cooking-fire, all at once, without separation. I think this is what she would call *watching*. I think this is what the children call *listening*. I think the lizard on the wall, the one she has named, would call it something else, if it called it anything.

I do not call it anything. I sit in the cane and I am attentive and my hands are at my chest and the gold shaft moves across the clay and she works below me and the place breathes around us both.

I am small. I am the size of a thing that fits inside a hand.

This is what I am. This is the whole of me. I am the brown shape in the cane above her doorway, and I have no name, and she has wondered sometimes what I am thinking, and I am thinking about her, and the answer, when it comes, will be green, and I will be here for that too, in the cane, with my hands folded, watching.
