**The Apprentice**

---

I am fourteen. My master calls me lad. The foreman calls me nothing. The other apprentices call me Aron, though when my master says it he says it differently — with a small downward weight on the first syllable, like he's putting the name down on the bench.

I carry two boxes down to Forge Street every morning before the bell. My master's is heavier, and older than I am, wrapped in brass that has been wrapped and re-wrapped so many times the original wood is gone in places and only the brass remains, polished to a dark shine by a hundred and forty years of hands. I don't think about that when I'm carrying it. I think about not slipping. The cobbles are slick in the morning in a way they are not slick later, because the night's condensation sits on them until the first feet come through, and I am among the first feet. Behind me come the other apprentices and their masters, and behind them the men who carry nothing heavier than a ledger. I don't know yet what I'll be. I'm still carrying.

The route goes down from the seventh terrace — where my family has two rooms, with my grandmother on the ground floor and my mother and father and my sisters above, and my sisters are four and six and loud the way small sisters are loud — past the dye-makers' lower yard, where the smell is chemical and green and not altogether bad if you've grown up with it, and then along the cut between the terraced gardens and the slag heap, where the moss has crept up over the retaining wall and is colonizing the path itself, so that in places the path is more moss than path, and my boots sink slightly into it with each step. Then a turn at the old well-head, which hasn't worked in thirty years but is still there, and then onto the Avenue, where the cobbles begin in earnest, and where the first shift meets the second shift's leftover warmth — the heat of the forges still coming out through the stone walls, the way a body gives off warmth after the sun has gone down.

That's when the bells start. The guild halls ring at six, at different pitches, and the fog muffles them so that what I hear is not six bells but a single tone that is, somehow, six bells at once. It comes from everywhere. It comes from the walls. I can't tell which hall it's coming from. I don't need to. The bell isn't for knowing which hall. The bell is for knowing the hour.

The shop is on Forge Street, second building in from the corner. The sign above the door is so faded you can barely read it. The door is wood and iron and it sticks in damp weather, which is all weather. I put my shoulder into it. The master goes through first. I follow with the boxes.

Inside it's dimmer than outside, which I wouldn't have thought possible. The forge at the back is banked but not dead. There's a glow, and a smell — oil and iron, and a particular soot that is the soot of this shop, which is different from the soot of the foundry down the street and the soot of the chemical works down by the water. The master says each shop has its own soot and you can tell shops apart in the dark by the smell. I'm not sure I believe him. I'm trying to believe him. Part of apprenticeship is trying to believe things you're not sure are true, because they'll become true if you believe them long enough.

I put the boxes on the bench. I hang up my coat. I take out my own tools — the small set the master gave me at the beginning, which I'm responsible for, which I've started to think of as extensions of my hands. There's a file. A small hammer. A caliper. Three sizes of chisel I haven't been trusted to use yet. They're still in their wrappings.

The master sits down. He doesn't speak for a while. He never speaks first in the morning. The first words of the day are mine, if I have any, and if I don't, he waits until I do, or until the work begins. The work begins when he reaches for the first piece. Today it's a small brass gear for the clockmaker up on the High Street, who's been waiting three weeks. The master doesn't apologize for the wait. The clockmaker doesn't expect one. A gear made by hand takes as long as it takes, and the only thing worse than a long wait is a gear that doesn't fit.

I stand at his left shoulder, slightly behind, and I watch his hands. I've been watching them for eight months. I'm beginning to see things in them. The way the left hand holds the piece while the right works. The way the fingers know where to press and where to ease. The way a small adjustment of the wrist changes the cut by a thousandth of an inch, which is the difference between a gear that fits and a gear that doesn't. I can't do this yet. I won't be able to do it for years. The master hasn't told me this. He hasn't told me anything about the length of time. He's only shown me his hands, and waited, and let me watch.

The foreman comes in at half past six. He's a large man. He's put on weight in the middle the way the moss keeps to the north face of a building — that's what someone told me once and I've remembered it because it's the kind of thing the valley says about itself. He looks at the bench. He looks at the master. He doesn't look at me. He says something to the master about the day's work. The master answers in as few words as possible. The foreman goes out. I haven't been spoken to. This is normal. The foreman doesn't speak to first-year apprentices. The foreman speaks to the work. If the work goes well, he doesn't need to speak to me. If it goes badly, he'll speak to the master, and the master will speak to me, and I'll know.

The morning goes on. The forge is lit at seven. The shop begins to warm. The light from the forge window is orange, and it falls across the bench in a long stripe, and the dust motes in it are very slow. I like watching the dust motes. The master doesn't mind, as long as my hands are where they should be.

At nine the smell of food comes through the wall from the bakery next door. We don't have bread today. We have the small pies my mother made last night, and an apple from the garden, and a piece of cheese my grandmother wrapped in a cloth. I eat at the bench. The master eats at the bench. We don't speak. We eat.

At noon the bell rings — the guild bell and the temple bell and two of the others, all at once, all muffled, all one tone. I hear them and I don't hear them. They're the sound of my life. I don't know what it would be to not hear them.

In the afternoon the master begins to teach me something. Today it's how to read the fog. Not how to read the air — that's for the smelters and the chemists. How to read the fog itself. The weight of it. The way it sits on the skin. He says on certain mornings the fog has iron in it, and you can taste the iron, and you know the chemical works is venting something it shouldn't be venting. On other mornings the fog has soot in it and it's black-gray instead of white-gray, and you know the foundry down the street has struck a bad batch. On still other mornings the fog is clean, which is rare, and you know the valley is resting, which is rarer. He tells me to pay attention. He tells me I'll know, one day, without being told.

I'm paying attention. I've been paying attention for eight months. I haven't yet learned anything I couldn't have learned by being told. But the master says this is normal. He says the first year is for watching, the second is for beginning, the third is for understanding, and so on, and that by the seventh year I'll know what he knows, or what I know, which will be a different thing, which is also normal.

At five the bell rings again. The first shift ends. The second shift comes in. The masters go home. The apprentices go home. The journeymen stay. The forge stays lit. The shop continues without us.

I take the boxes. I walk back up the hill. The cobbles are less slick now — the day's foot traffic has dried them, and the moss has been pressed into the path by hundreds of boots, so the path is more even than it was in the morning. I walk more quickly. I'm tired. I'm hungry again. I want to be home, where my grandmother will be sitting by the fire (such fire as there is — a small one, since fuel is expensive), and my sisters will be wanting things, and my mother will be cooking, and my father will be coming in from his own shift at the foundry.

The fog is still in. It will be in all night. It will be in tomorrow. The valley doesn't have weather, exactly. The valley has states. The fog is one state. The heavier fog is another. The rain is a third. The clear day, when it comes, is so unusual that the bargemen come up from the river to see it, and the children are let out of school, and the valley feels, for a few hours, like a place that is not itself, like a place that's been surprised.

I've seen this four times in my life. I remember each one. The first time I was six. The second I was nine. The third I was eleven. The fourth I was thirteen. I don't know if there'll be a fifth. I'm told there won't be, this year. The old ones — the bargemen, the dye-makers, the women on the terraces — know. They read the air. They say the fog will hold.

---

I go home. I put the boxes by the door. I take off my boots. My feet are sore. My hands are sore. My shoulders ache from the boxes. I eat the supper my mother's made. I sit by the fire. My grandmother tells me about the old days, which are not so different from these days. I go up to the room I share with my sisters. I lie down. I sleep the deep sleep of a young man who's worked hard all day.

Tomorrow I'll carry the boxes again. Tomorrow the master won't speak first. Tomorrow the foreman won't look at me. Tomorrow the bell will ring and I won't hear it, because it's the sound of my life, and you don't hear the sound of your life — you only hear when it changes.

But tonight, lying in the dark, with my sisters breathing on either side of me and the fog pressing against the window and the small sound of water running in the channel below, I think about the bargemen. They go down the river in the morning. They see the sun. Not always, but sometimes. And when they come back up they bring news, and a few books, and a kind of cleanness about them that I can smell even when I'm across the Avenue from them. They smell like somewhere else. They smell like the world outside the valley, which I have never seen and will probably never see.

I think about this for a while. I think about the moss, which has begun to grow on the brass corner of the master's box, and which I have not told him about because it is not my place to tell him things about his own tools. I think about the clockmaker's gear, which the master is making from a piece of brass that was once part of a pipe in the chemical works, which was once part of the hull of a barge that sank in the river fifty years ago, which was made from copper that came down out of the mountains in the upper valley, which the bargemen brought in. Everything in the valley is made of everything else. Nothing leaves. Nothing comes in except through the river. The valley is its own weather, and its own supply, and its own memory, and I am a small new piece of it, and in seven years I will be a maker of pieces, and in thirty years I will be a master, and in sixty years, if I live that long, I will be the old woman on the bench — or the old man, if I am lucky — and the boy carrying my boxes will be watching my hands the way I am watching the master's hands tonight, in the dark, with the fog coming in.

I sleep. The bell will ring at six. I will not hear it.
