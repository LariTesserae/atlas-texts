I am older than the fig.

I have lived in this water longer than anything that breathes air has lived in this room. When the ceiling was still a ceiling, I was already here, in the basin, in the dark. I have outlived several generations of the things that fall from above and try to live in my water. I have outlived the light, which is newer than I am. I have outlived the moss, which is newer than the light. I have outlived almost everything that crawls on the walls, or hangs in the canopy, or eats the floor from the inside out.

I am not large. I am the size of two of your fingers, or three. I am pale and almost translucent, and the pink of me shows where the blood is closest to the surface. When I lie still in the deepest part of the basin I am almost not there. I am the color of the water, which is the color of ink, which is the color of nothing.

I breathe through my skin and through the two small nostrils that break the surface like two small thoughts. I do not have to surface often. I can wait a long time between breaths. I have waited through droughts that lasted two seasons, lying at the bottom in the coldest channel, where the water rises from below through stone I cannot see. I slowed everything that could be slowed, until I was almost a stone myself, and the room above me tightened, and the moss drew inward, and the things that need wetness retreated into the walls, and still I waited, and the water did not leave me.

The water is my whole world. It is not a small world. It has depth, and temperature, and taste. It has a year, and a day, and a minute. It rises in the wet weeks and lowers in the dry weeks, and I rise and lower with it, and the rhythm is older than my memory and is the rhythm of my body.

I do not have names. I have places. There is the deep channel, which is the coldest and the safest, and the shallows where the light falls at midday, where the water is warm and the insects fall in. There is the lip of the basin where the fig has sent its pale fingers down into me, and I have eaten some of those fingers, and the fig has grown new ones, and the eating and the growing have been going on for a long time, and neither of us has minded. There is the corner where the carved stone meets the older stone, and the water is warmer there, and there is a small hollow under the lip that is mine, that I go to when the rain comes and the surface is broken by too many drops.

I have seen things come down from above. They fall through the hole in the ceiling where the light is, and they land on my water, and they do not understand the water, and they try to climb out, and sometimes they do and sometimes they do not. The ones that do not are mine. I eat them slowly, the way I eat everything, because nothing in me is in a hurry. A moth, once, that had been beating its wings against the column of light for a long time before it fell into me, and I ate it under the surface, and the wings dissolved in a way that interested me for a little while. A beetle, once, that was too heavy to climb the stone and lay on the bottom for three days, upside down, before I took it. Many small flies, that did not even know they were in water.

I do not know what the things above me are doing. I hear them. The lizards I hear, because their feet make a small sound against the stone, and the sound travels down into the water and is translated into something I can feel. I hear the fig, when its leaves fall and hit the water, and the sound of the leaf is different from the sound of the rain, and the sound of the rain is different from the sound of the lizard. I do not know what they are. I know only that they are part of the rhythm, and that the rhythm has been the same for longer than I have been alive.

I have a slow kind of memory. I do not remember events. I remember temperatures. I remember the cold of the deepest channel, and the warmth of the shallows when the light is on them, and the strange warmth that came one year, that was not the sun, that came from the walls, when something fell in from above and lay on the bottom for many days and the water around it grew warm in a way I had not felt before. I remember tastes. The taste of the fig's tendrils, which is sweet and a little resinous. The taste of the moss that falls in sometimes, which is sour. The taste of the stone, when the water is very low and I am pressed against the bottom and my skin is touching the dressed blocks of the basin.

I am not lonely. I do not know what loneliness is. There are others of my kind, somewhere in this water, and we have met, I think, but I do not remember meeting them. We live in the same basin and we do not see each other and we do not fight and we do not speak. The water is large enough for us. We are not in each other's way.

I will live a long time. The fig will outlive me, perhaps, because the fig is a tree and I am a small thing in the water. But the basin will outlive us both. The carved stone will outlive us all.

The chevron on the stone has no meaning that I can read, but I have passed over it ten thousand times, and my belly knows its shape the way the river knows its stones. That is a kind of reading. The water has been reading the basin for a long time. I am one of the things the basin has written, and the writing is slow, and I am patient with it.
