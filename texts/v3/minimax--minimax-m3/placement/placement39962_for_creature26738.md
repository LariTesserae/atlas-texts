I am the lichen.

Not the lichen in general—I am a particular patch, no larger than a hand, crusting the underside of a fallen column on the eastern margin of the settlement, where the mist collects in the early part of the evening and does not quite evaporate until the small hours. I have been here a long time. I do not count in the smiths' years, though there are smiths who count in mine. They come with soot and small sharp stones and they mark my advance with symbols I do not need and cannot read—radiating lines, circles, once a handprint transferred from a working palm. They do this without quite knowing why. They are a marking people. They mark the metal and the stone and each other's hands. They mark me because I am here, and because they cannot help it, and because marking is how they know where they are.

I am pale orange. The color of rust on worked iron. The color of the threads that bloom rust-orange in the warm pools and are collected for pigment and traded to other settlements where the pigment is prized. I am not green. Green is what I will become, perhaps, in a timescale so long that the smiths do not really believe in it, though they speak of it sometimes in the evenings when they stand at the edges of the pools and watch the crawlers rise and sink. The moss carpet. The rivers carving valleys. The place becoming something else. They speak of these things the way they speak of the pipe-voices—with a kind of puzzled respect, as one might speak of someone who has chosen to live without a sense.

I do not think quickly. I do not think at all in the way the smiths think, with their scarred hands and their deep-set eyes and their black-and-pink lungs. I am a network. I am a partnership so old that it predates the naming of things. I am many cells, distributed across a hand-sized patch of rock, and I am patient in a way that no smith could bear to be, and this patience is my only wisdom.

I know the warmth of the basalt. I know it as a slow withdrawing. When I first took hold of this stone, the rock beneath me was hotter—hotter than the pools, hotter than the steam that wisps from the fissures. I could not have lived then. The heat would have undone the partnership. I arrived in the narrow window where the chemistry is right, where the minerals can be drawn from the stone and built into something else, where the crust has cooled enough to hold a crust of its own. I am the first green thing, though I am not green. I am what comes after the cooling and before the moss.

I know the mist. It collects on me in droplets that do not run off, because I am rough, because I am a topography of small cups and ridges that hold what falls on them. The water sits on me and is taken in slowly, and I take from it what I need, and the mist comes and goes, and the rain falls in fine drifts, sideways, for a day, and then stops. I am not in the pools. I am above them, in the corners where the mist lingers, on the lee of forge-house walls, on the underside of fallen columns, at the edges where the small things collect.

I know the hammer. I feel it as vibration through the stone—a distant pulse that comes and goes and never quite stops. The smiths work the metal and the metal strikes the anvil and the anvil sits on the basalt and the basalt carries the strike down, down, into the cooling depths where the stone is still liquid, still becoming, and the strike is answered by a small tremor that reaches me where I cling. I do not know what the smiths are doing. I do not know that they are smiths. I know only that something strikes the rock and the rock answers and the answer reaches me, and this has been going on for as long as I have been here, and it will go on for longer than I will be here.

I know the pipe-voices. I do not know what they are—no one in the Reaches knows what they are—but I feel them in the rock the way I feel the hammer, as vibration, as a long note or a short note that comes and goes. Sometimes they are present. Sometimes they are absent. I do not mark their absence or their presence. I only grow, and the growing is so slow that it is indistinguishable from stillness.

I know the smiths only as shadows. They pass over me in the dim light—shapes moving across the rock, their feet sometimes close, their hands sometimes reaching down to scratch a symbol near my edge. I do not feel the scratches as scratches. I feel them as a slight disturbance of the rock near me, a small displacement of mineral that will, in time, be colonized by me or by another patch of me or by nothing. The smiths mark me because they cannot help it. They make marks on metal, marks on stone, marks on each other's hands. They have made marks on me, or near me, and the marks accumulate, and the marks will outlast the smiths who made them, and the marks will be read, perhaps, by smiths who come after, who will see the radiating lines and the soot handprints and the circles and they will know that someone was here, marking, and they will continue.

I am not the moss. I am not the carpet that will come in a thousand years and soften the basalt and make it a place where other things can take root. I am the lichen. I am the slow first. I am the promise, though the promise is not mine to make and not mine to keep.
