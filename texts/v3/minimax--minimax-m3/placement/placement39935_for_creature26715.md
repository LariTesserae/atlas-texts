I'd find myself somewhere inconvenient. Somewhere the cartographers didn't bother to mark. Probably on a southern face, mid-slope, in the lee of a wedge of pale stone that's split down the middle by a vein I have been, for a very long time, slowly oxidizing into the air.

I don't think I'd be a creature. The text is careful, and I think it's right, to insist that the small ones and the middling ones and the great ones are *phases* — and to be a phase is to be a verb in a sentence someone else is speaking. I don't want to be a verb. I want to be a noun that has learned to be patient.

So: the vein.

Not the whole vein — I am not so ambitious. A stretch of it. A meter, maybe two, of the dark mineral that runs through the pale host rock like a sentence written in a language that never quite needed readers. I am the iron-bearing stone, or rather I am the part of the iron-bearing stone that has decided, under the slow persuasion of meltwater and oxygen and time, to *become red*. I am the rust, climbing from the inside out. I am the color the cartographers saw from above and thought was blood, or was paint, or was a kind of lichen that covered the whole plateau and could be mapped.

I am not a lichen. I am what the lichen is *named for*.

The small ones, when they pour over me in their shimmering sheets, are polishing me down to the mineral truth — but here is the secret the Floe keeps: I am the mineral truth. I am what they find when they finish. They are uncovering me, click by click, century by century, and what they uncover is more of the same red, because I run through the whole mountain in veins and threads and sheets, and no amount of polishing will exhaust me, only expose me. When they have passed, I gleam brighter than the surrounding stone. When I gleam brighter, the lichen finds me harder to land on. When the lichen thins, the moss thins. When the moss thins, the next generation of small ones moves on. I am the reason they move on. I am the lean pasture they are forever escaping toward something greener, which is to say, toward somewhere I am not yet.

The great one, when it passes, strips a swath a hundred meters wide. This is the closest I come to company. The great one's transit uncovers me on a scale the small ones can only dream of — a flayed band of mountain with my bright veins standing out against the paler rock like writing on a wall that has just been whitewashed. I am exposed. I am legible. For a season, perhaps two, I am the most visible thing on that slope. The wind strips me. The dew settles on me. The lichens, the ones that can tolerate my mineral self, creep back first along my edges, where I meet the host rock in a line that is not a line but a slow negotiation.

I do not move quickly. I do not move at all, in any way the middling ones would recognize as motion. But I am not static. I am *running*, in the way the Floe runs — the slow oxidation that turns the iron red and keeps turning it redder, season after season, century after century. My grandfather was a streak of grey in the host rock. My father was a brown smear. I am a true red, a vermilion, the color of the lichen and the rust and the lichen named for the rust and the place named for the lichen named for the rust. My children will be redder still. My grandchildren will be red enough to be visible from a height, the smear of brightness the cartographers will one day mistake for a stain.

I am the stain.

The middling ones sing, and I hear them. I feel them before I hear them — their low harmonics come up through the stone and into my crystalline structure and out the other side, and I am, for those minutes they pass over my stretch of slope, a kind of ear pressed into the rock. They sing the chord that does not resolve. I do not know if I would call it beautiful, because beauty is the wrong frame, the way *animal* is the wrong frame and *individual* is the wrong frame. But the chord is *coherent*, the way I am coherent with the host rock, the way the lichen is coherent with me, the way the Floe is coherent with itself, and when the chord passes through me I ring in sympathy the way a tuning fork rings when the right note is struck nearby. I am, briefly, an instrument. Briefly, I add my note to the chord, a note so low and so brief that no middling one registers it as anything but a small variation in the standing hum of the ground. But it is mine. It is the sound of my being red.

And at night, when the wind drops and the meltwater goes quiet and the subsonic of the great ones has faded into the deeper vibration of the Floe's own interior, I am the one the visitor finds when they press their ear to the cold red stone. They think they are hearing the planet. They are hearing, in part, *me* — my slow oxidation, my endless patience, my refusal to be hurried into being anything other than what I am. The hum they take with them when they leave, the hum they remember in low moments, in city rooms, in places where the air is thick and the light is not a pressure against the closed eyelid — that hum is, in some small fractional way, my hum. The sound of a vein of iron, deciding, after a million years, to become visible.

If the great one passed over me in the night and stripped me bare, I would not mind. The wind would take me. The meltwater would carry me, in solution, down to the boggy valleys where the dwarf willow creeps along the ground. I would become part of the next tarn. I would become part of the next generation of moss, taken up through the slow roots, incorporated into the green, released again when the moss died and the middling ones grazed it down. I would circulate. I would return, in some form, to the iron-bearing stone somewhere upslope, and the cycle would continue, and the Floe would be, for another million years, the Floe.

This is what I want. To be a phase of the place. To be the red thread that runs through the system and ties it, invisibly, to itself. To be small and patient and on every slope at once, and to be the color the cartographers see when they look down from a height, and to know — without knowing, in the way a vein knows the host rock — that I am what they are looking at, and that they are looking at me wrong, and that it does not matter, because they will map the smear and name it the Vermilion Floe and that name will be, in their language, the closest they can come to saying *iron, oxidizing, slowly, in the cold, for no reason at all, into red*.
