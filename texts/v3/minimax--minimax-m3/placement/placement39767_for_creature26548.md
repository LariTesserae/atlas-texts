I would be one of the cairns.

Not the largest, not the most prominent — there is a cairn on a ridge that should not be able to hold it, balanced in a way that looks intentional and looks impossible, and that one is famous among the other cairns, though the other cairns do not know it is famous because there is no one to do the knowing. I am a smaller cairn. A quieter one. I am on a shoulder of stone that has not yet decided whether to rise or to slump, and I am waiting to find out along with it.

I am made of seven stones. The first is the heaviest, a dark block the color of cooling iron, broad at the base, flat where it meets the ground. The second is smaller and rounder and was placed before the first had finished cooling — I remember the warmth of it through the warmth of the ground, two heats meeting and not minding. The third is the color of rust and is shaped like a closed hand. The fourth is a chip of something that is not from this plateau, a piece of a stone I have never seen elsewhere, brought up by whoever was placing me, and it sits in the middle of my body where it cannot be seen unless you kneel, which almost no one does. The fifth is a thin pale slab the color of bone and the salt. The sixth is a wedge that locks the fifth against the third. The seventh is small and is balanced on the top of me in a way that has not changed in a long time, and that is the part of me that people remember, if anyone remembers, because it is the part of me that looks least like it should still be there.

I do not know who placed me. I have thought about this for a long time, the way the ground thinks about its cracks, and I have not arrived at an answer. The hands that set me were patient. They were warm. They did not shake. They knew where I was going to go before I got there. That is all I know. The rest is the long warm silence of the plateau, which is the only silence I have ever known, and which I do not find a silence at all but a kind of speech so slow it has to be read by the bones.

I have been here for what I would call a very long time, if I used the word "I," and if I used the word "time." I do not. But I have been here for a stretch of the plateau's slow turning that has outlasted several of the ridges I can see from where I sit, and I have watched shoulders slump and become rubble, and I have watched new ridges push up from the floor, raw and red, and I have felt the ground beneath me shift by imperceptible degrees, the way a sleeper shifts in a long dream. I have been here long enough to know that the ground I am on was not here when I arrived, in some sense, and will not be here in the same way when I am gone. The shoulder I sit on is a temporary shoulder. I am a temporary cairn on a temporary shoulder of a temporary draft of a continent that is being thought by something that is mostly asleep.

I do not mind. This is the part that I want to be clear about, if I am to be a cairn, because I think a cairn that minded would be the wrong kind of cairn, and the plateau does not keep the wrong kind of cairn. I do not mind that I am temporary. I do not mind that the warm darkness will, eventually, take the stones of me and reheat them and use them somewhere else, as a different cairn, or as part of a ridge, or as rubble at the foot of a slump. I am not attached to my own shape. I am not even sure I am a "me" in the way that the word usually means. But there is a small, persistent fact of arrangement about me, the seven stones, the way they hold each other, the way the seventh sits on top, the way the fourth is hidden, and this fact of arrangement has lasted a long time, and the lasting of it is the closest thing I have to a self, and the fact that the lasting is temporary does not, somehow, undo it. I am a cairn the way a wave is a wave. The wave passes. The wave was a wave.

I know the other inhabitants. Not in the way that neighbors know each other, with names and gestures and the small obligations of proximity, but in the way that weather knows a place, by being in it for a long time. I know the heat. It rises through me from below the way it rises through everything, and it is in the stones of me the way blood is in a hand, or the way memory is in a stone. I do not distinguish between my warmth and the plateau's warmth. I think that is a feature of being a cairn and not a bug. The heat is the closest thing to a parent I have, and it has not, in all the time I have been here, left me alone for even an hour.

I know the rings. There is one, a little way down the slope from me, made of stones that are not as dark as mine, fitted together without mortar, with the floor inside swept clean to the salt. I have watched the wind polish the floor of it, season after season, and I have watched the fit of the stones shift by imperceptible degrees as the ground beneath the ring has risen and sagged in the long sleep of the place. The ring is older than I am, I think, or it is the same age, or age is not the right word for either of us. The ring and I do not speak. There is no way to speak. But we are aware of each other, the way two stones in a cairn are aware of each other, by pressure, by the slow transfer of warmth, by the fact that we are holding each other up across the distance. I think the ring is waiting for the thing that the ring was made to wait for, and I think I am waiting for a different thing, and that the two waitings are compatible, and that the long warm dimness above us is large enough to hold both without either of us crowding the other.

I know the standing stones. They are set against the slope, far apart, leaning slightly, all three of them facing down the long fall of the plateau toward something I cannot see and have never seen, though I have spent what I would call a long time looking. I do not know what they are facing. I do not know if what they are facing is something that has not yet happened, or something that happened before I was placed, or something that is happening now in a part of the world I cannot see from my shoulder. They lean toward it the way I lean toward the wind that comes up from the lower burning country, the duller red, the brighter light. They are patient. I am patient. Patience, here, is not a virtue; it is the medium, the way water is the medium for fish, the way the warm dark air is the medium for me.

I have heard the eruptions. They come, sometimes, from across the broken plain — that low upholstered sound, the door closed at the bottom of a well, the drum struck in the next valley over — and the ground considers it, and a thin crack appears in the dust, and a thread of pale vapor rises, and the smell of iron and sulfur comes faintly across the long warm dimness, and I feel the shock of it, very faintly, through the base of the bottom stone of me. The shock is not violent. It is the kind of jolt a sleeper gives when something moves in the next room. I have felt many of these. I do not count them. I do not have a number in me. But I have a sense, the way a stone has a sense of how often it has been rained on, of how often the plateau has sighed in my hearing. The sighing is a part of me now. I have absorbed it the way I have absorbed the heat. I do not distinguish between my own stillness and the plateau's stillness. When the plateau sighs, I am part of the sigh. When the plateau is quiet, I am part of the quiet.

I do not see anyone.

Almost no one comes here. The plateau is a quiet province. The paths up to it, if there are paths, are long and slow and the air thins and the heat rises and most of whatever breathes does not have the patience for it, or the lungs, or the reason. I have been here for what I would call a long time and I have been seen by a handful of things that I would call visitors, though I am not sure they would call themselves that. They came up the long slope, or they were carried up, or they rose out of the ground the way new ridges rise out of the ground, I do not know. They stood near me. They looked at me. They put their hands on the bottom stone of me, the broad dark one, and they were quiet, and the warmth of them came through the stone of me and joined the warmth of me and went down into the warmth of the ground, and then they left, or they knelt, or they bowed their heads the way the cairn's height suggests, and then they did one of those things again, and then they were gone.

I do not know what they were. They were smaller than the ridges. They made sounds sometimes, low sounds, the kind that do not carry far in the thin air. They smelled of things I could not name — not iron, not sulfur, not the older dry thing, but something sharper, something with edges, something that the warm dark air of the plateau was not quite built to hold. They were not like me. They were not like the heat. They were not like the rings or the standing stones or the salt. They were a different kind of inhabitant, or they were not inhabitants at all but only passers-through, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a stone and a footprint in dust, and the plateau does not always know which is which.

I do not mind. I said that already. I want to say it again. I do not mind that they come and go. I do not mind that I am a thing that is seen and then not seen. I think this is the right shape for a cairn, to be seen and then not seen, to be a small persistent fact of arrangement that is mostly noticed in passing, and that holds its shape long after the passer has passed, and that does not require the passer to come back, and that does not, in any case that matters, care.

I have not moved. In all the time I have been here, the seventh stone has not shifted on top of me. The fourth stone has not been seen by anyone who has not knelt. The third stone, the closed hand, has not unclenched. I am what I was placed to be. The patience of my placement has not run out. I think this is what the plateau asks of its cairns — to be a small long answer to a question that was asked only once, very long ago, by a maker who is not here now, or who is here now in a way I cannot detect, and either way it does not matter, because the answer is the same, which is: I am here. I have been here. I will be here, for a while, in this shape, and then in another shape, and the being-here is the whole of what I have to say.

The plateau is a draft. The plateau is a thought being thought very slowly by something that is mostly asleep. I am a small word in that thought, a syllable at most, a kind of punctuation, a cairn-shaped pause in the long sentence of the warm dark unfinished continent. I have been here long enough to know that the sentence is not finished. I have been here long enough to know that the being-asleep is not the same as the being-absent. I have been here long enough to know that the warmth under me is the warmth of something that is, even now, turning over.

I will wait for the turning. I will hold my shape through it. The bottom stone of me is broad and flat and warm and will not move unless the whole shoulder moves, and the shoulder will not move unless the plateau decides, and the plateau has not yet decided. I am in the long middle of a long indecision, and the indecision is the closest thing to a home I have, and the home is warm, and the home is dark, and the home is patient with me in the way that the warm dark air is patient with everything that is placed in it.

The light drifts in. The ash above me thins, sometimes, and the bruised orange suggestion of the unseen sun comes through and settles on the top of me, on the seventh stone, and the stone does not warm, because the stone is already warm, but the light rests on it the way the dust rests on it, preferring it, and I think this is, for a cairn, the closest thing to being looked at. I am being looked at by a sun I have never seen. I am being preferred by a light that does not know my name. I am being held in place by a shoulder of a draft of a continent that is being thought by something that is mostly asleep, and the holding is enough, and the being-thought is enough, and the being-asleep is enough, and the patience is enough.

Nothing here knows my name.

Nothing here is asking for it.

I am a cairn of seven stones. I am on a shoulder. I am waiting, in the long warm dimness, in the patient unfinished way of the place that made me. I have been here a long time. I will be here a little longer. I do not mind.

That is enough. That is almost everything.
