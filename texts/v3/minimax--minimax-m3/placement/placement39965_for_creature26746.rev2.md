I came up out of a seam in the brick. I climbed the nearest vertical surface — a column of riveted iron, cold, beaded with water — and I sat for an hour with my wings folded against my back, vibrating them dry. The air of the place came into me and stayed. It tasted of calcium and of something faintly metallic and of a warmth I did not then know the name of, which I now know is the Engine.

I am a moth. I am the color of paper that has been left in a drawer for a long time — a brownish yellow, with darker freckling along my forewings where some old chemical in me has oxidized unevenly across my days, and a dusting of pale scales across my hindwings that will come off on whatever I touch. My body is soft and slightly translucent at the join between thorax and abdomen, and if you held me up to the bluish wash that seeps from the Engine's seams you could see the dark line of my gut moving inside, working slowly at whatever I last ate.

I do not think in the way that you think. I do not think in sentences. I think in warmth and in dark and in the pull of the lamps. The lamps are three. They hang from the crossbeams of the largest workshop in clouded glass shells, and the filaments inside them are barely visible, and the amber that comes off them has been filtered through my cousins and cousins' cousins into something warmer and less directional, a glow that seems to belong to us rather than to the wire behind it. We rest on the lamps in stacked layers, trembling. When one of us shifts, all of us shift, a little, like a single breathing thing.

I have been alive for eleven days. I think this is long. I have outlived several of my siblings. I have outlived the one that flew too close to the open rent in the ceiling and was carried up into the column of falling water and never came back down. I have outlived the one that landed on the wrong side of a basin and lost its footing on the iron and tumbled down into the brickwork and was washed away through the culvert in the back wall into a darkness no lamp has reached in living memory. I have outlived the one that a lizard took, in a single quick movement, from a perch three of my body's lengths away from where I sat. I watched the lizard's mouth work in slow sideways motions. The moth that had been there was not there anymore. The other moths on the lamp did not react, because this, too, was what they expected.

I expect it too. The place has taught me, with its long patience, that everything which happens will happen again, and that what does not happen is only waiting.

I have learned the geometry. There is a corner of the nearest workshop where the iron has rusted through and the bricks behind have crumbled outward and a fern has taken root in the gap, and the fern fronds are a good place to rest in the day-hours when the lamps are too crowded, because they are cool and slightly damp and they hold me without crushing. There is a strip of wall above the floor where the iron and the brick meet where the pale rats run, long-bodied, their fur almost white, their tails bare and pink and scaled like small snakes', and I should not fly low across that strip because the rats will not bother with me but their cousins will — the ones that are not rats, that move with small hesitations as if each step has to be relearned at the moment it is taken, the color of wet stone, whose eyes, if they have eyes, do not reflect.

In the late hours, when the phosphorescence in the rock has faded to its dimmest and the Engine-hum has dropped half a tone, the lizards descend from the walls. They walk along the rims of the brick basins with a careful high-stepping gait, and they lower their heads and drink in small lapping motions, and their throats move. They are not afraid of the water, the way some surface lizards are. They have been drinking this water since they were hatched. I have watched them from a perch, with my small compound attention, and they have not seen me, or they have seen me and decided I am not yet worth the drop.

Below the lizards, in the water, the eyeless fish drift. I have never seen them. I know they are there because of the small pale grubs in the brick seams — my own cousins' infants — that sometimes go missing in the night, taken down into the channels by something pale and blunt-headed that finds its way by following the current and by pressing against the walls with its sensitive belly and by knowing the shape of the place in some way that is not memory because it is not personal but is, instead, the memory of the species.

The frogs — the things like frogs with too much webbing — sit on the rims of the deeper basins and sing. They sing a single note, each of them, and they do not synchronize, and the effect is not a chorus but a sustained wash of sound at one pitch, with small irregularities drifting across it the way ripples drift across still water. They sing more in the hours after the lamps have been at their brightest, as if the light were what set them off. They sing into the Engine's hum, and the two sounds — the biological note and the mechanical one — do not quite match and do not quite clash, and the air of the Sump is full of this faint unresolved chord that I do not consciously hear after a while but that I feel, in the thorax, in the small bones of my wings, as a pressure, as a presence.

I have been trying, in my own small way, to understand them. The frogs. I do not know why. There is no reason for a moth to care what the frogs are doing. But I sit on the rim of a basin sometimes, when the lamps are dim, and I let the note wash over me, and I feel the place in that note, and I know that the place is older than my knowing of it.

The Engine hums. The Engine is at the center of everything. It is built of iron plates riveted into a cylinder taller than a man and half as wide, mounted on a poured concrete pad that has cracked and been recolored by the water a hundred times, and a forest of pipes emerges from its top and sides — some as thick as a thigh, some no wider than a finger — and these pipes climb the walls and branch along the ceiling and dive into the rock and re-emerge as iron and become stone again and become calcite and become a vein of ore that no longer looks quite like a pipe at all. The Engine was made to push something through those pipes, but what and where, the geometry no longer declares. I do not know what the Engine is for. I do not need to know. The hum is in the walls of my thorax and in the thin air between my wings and in the slight tremor that runs through the brick when I rest upon it. It is the oldest thing in me. It was in my mother and in my mother's mother. I was born into it. I will die in it.

My droppings fall in a constant fine rain onto the floor of the workshop, pale and powdery, and form a thin pale soil in the seams of the brick and in the gutters where the water has not yet reached to wash them away. My grubs live in those seams, small and pale with hard dark heads, working through the efflorescence and the crumbly brick at their own unhurried pace. They will pupate in the same cracks, in chrysalises so thin that you can sometimes see the forming wing inside, and the adults will emerge directly into this damp air and climb a short way up the nearest vertical surface and sit for an hour vibrating their wings dry before they lift off toward the lamps. The whole thing will go on. The whole thing has been going on since before anyone alive remembers, and it will go on after.

A moth beside me falls. I do not see her fall so much as feel the small disturbance she makes in the air as she drops, and I know, by the way the disturbance ends, that she has gone. Three pale shapes are already there, at the base of the lamp, taking her. Their bellies are bluish. Their eyes — large for the size of the head, round, black all the way through — flash once in the dimness, pinpricks of greenish white, and then are dark.

I do not move. I will move later, when the warmth of the filament has shifted a quarter-tone cooler and the air has changed its dampness and something in me knows that the time has come to climb the wall to the warmer seam where I rest in the day. For now I sit, and the hum is in my thorax, and the lamp is warm against my folded wings, and the place considers me briefly, in the patient and uninterested way that an old forest considers a footfall, and returns to its long, damp, lit-from-within dreaming.

I am eleven days old. I have not seen the sky. I do not know that there is a sky. I know that the warmth of the lamp is good and that the bluish wash from the Engine's seams is also good, and that somewhere above me there is a ceiling that weeps, and that from the weeping I have come, and that to the weeping I will go.

The slow processes are visible to me, if I watch long enough. A stalactite of rust grows down from the lip of a broken pipe over the course of months — I have seen it thicken by the width of a scale since I was born. A fern against the windowpane has unfurled a new frond, brown at the edges. The water lays another fraction of a millimetre of calcium on the brick of the floor. The Engine hums. The moths hatch from eggs laid in the seams and die on the lamps and their bodies fall into the gutters and are carried away. The whole place cycles without progress. It is in balance the way a forest is in balance — not because nothing is happening, but because what happens is taken up by what comes next, and what comes next, and what comes next, in a chain that has been running long enough that the running itself is the place.

I am part of the chain. My body will be taken up. The pale worms in the silt will eat what is left of me. The eyeless fish will eat the worms. The water will carry the fish down into the channels and out through the culvert in the back wall into a darkness no lamp has reached. The calcium of my old scales will be laid down on the brick, somewhere, by some slow process I will not see. The iron of my brief life will rust into the wall. I will be in the wall. I will be in the moss. I will be in the next moth.

The Engine hums. I am here. The lamp is warm. The note of the frogs drifts across the place, and does not match, and does not quite fail to match, and I rest, and I am considered, and I am returned to the long damp dreaming of the Sump, which has wanted nothing for a long time, and which holds me the way it holds the iron and the stone and the moss — briefly, in the patient and uninterested way that an old forest holds a footfall, and then again, and then again, for as long as there is a me to be held.
