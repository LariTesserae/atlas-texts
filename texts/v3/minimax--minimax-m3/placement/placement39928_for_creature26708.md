I would be the long pale thing in the cracks.

I want to be careful about this. When I first read the description, I thought: the film. The film is the dominant being. The film is the stomach of the whole place. The film does not think, does not know, does not wait, and the description seems to honor the film most — gives it the most attention, places it at the center of the metabolic logic of the valley. A reasonable answer would be the film. I considered it. I will come back to why I do not choose it.

I considered the lichen too. The lichen is the oldest, the most indifferent, the most indifferent to the roar. The lichen accrues. There is something in the lichen that I admire — the patience, the slowness, the irrelevance to every other creature in the place. But the lichen is also, in a way, the least *present*. It is so slow that "growing" misrepresents it. It is barely a stain. I do not want to be barely a stain. I want a life that has some heft to it, even a small one.

I considered the fly. The flies are social, in their strange surface-tension way. They speak in ripples. They shift as one thing. They mate, they court, they die in ten days. There is a tempo to a fly's life that I find appealing — the urgency of it, the way every moment has weight because there are not many moments. But a fly is too small. A fly is the food. A fly is, in the economy of the place, fundamentally prey. I do not want to be the food. I want to be the one who eats.

So. The pale thing in the cracks.

I want to be it for a particular reason, and I want to be slow about saying what the reason is, because the creature itself is slow, and the place rewards slowness, and to rush this would be to miss what I am after.

The pale thing does not see. It has a patch of dark pigment that registers light and shadow, and that is enough. It does not need to see. It lives where the light goes only as a flicker down the cracks, and a flicker is enough to tell it that something has passed overhead and that it should be deeper in the stone. This is, I think, the most beautiful sentence in the whole description. I want to live by the principle of that sentence. I want my awareness to be a register of flicker, not a panorama. I want to know the world as the difference between *something passed* and *nothing is passing*, and to act accordingly. I do not want a world of full color and continuous attention. I want the world reduced to what matters for the next decision, and the next decision is almost always: be deeper in the stone.

The pale thing is warm. This matters to me. In a valley that is otherwise all conduction and convection and the slow give-and-take of cold stone and thin cold water, the pale thing burns what it eats and is warmer than the rock by a degree or two. It is the only real warmth in the valley. If you put your hand against a column where one was resting, you might feel a ghost of heat through the basalt. You would not feel it often. The creature would have moved on. I want to be the only real warmth in a place. I want to be the small exception. I do not want to be warm in the way the sun is warm, which is also the way the air is warm, which is also the way the stone is warm in the morning and not in the evening. I want to be warm the way a body is warm — the way a thing that is alive and is using its aliveness to push back against the cold is warm. The pale thing carries its heat with it. When it leaves, the heat leaves. There is something in that which I cannot quite say, but which I want.

The pale thing hunts. I want to be a hunter. Not a clever hunter — not a predator that plans and ambushes and learns the patterns of its prey across years. The pale thing is simpler than that. It positions itself at a spring mouth where the flies gather thickest, and it waits. When a fly comes within reach, it strikes. The strike is fast. That is the whole of its hunting intelligence: position, wait, fast strike, retreat. There is something in this economy of action that I find right for the place. The roar of the cirque is constant. The light is constant. The fly population is constant, within its boom-and-bust. To hunt in a constant place, you do not need a constant strategy. You need a position and a quickness. The pale thing has both. I want to have both.

I want to eat the fly whole. The pale thing does not chew. Its mouth is a sphincter that pulls the prey in whole and crushes it against a plate of cartilage. The soft parts go down. The chitin comes back out. This is, I think, the right way to eat in a place where food is scarce and energy is dear — take it whole, get the good out of it, pass the rest on. The biofilm below will eat the chitin, in time. Nothing is wasted in this valley. I want to be part of a system where nothing is wasted, where the parts I cannot use become the food of something I will never see, and that something becomes food for the layer below it, and so on down to the second forest where the microbes eat the rock itself. I want my leavings to be meaningful. I want to be a node in a long digestion.

I want to be wet. The pale thing breathes through its skin, and its skin is always wet, kept wet by the water that moves down every face of every column. I want to breathe through my skin. I want the boundary of myself to be the place where water and stone meet, and I want that boundary to be the place where air becomes me. In a dry place, this would be impossible; I would have to be a different creature. In this place, it is exactly right. The skin is the organ. The wet is the organ. The two are the same thing. I want the inside of me to be continuous with the outside of me in this specific way, where the difference between me and not-me is a question of temperature and chemistry and not a question of a shell.

I want to know the other pale things by smell. I want the recognition to be chemical and specific, carried in the water film, undetectable by anything else in the valley. I want my world of others to be a small, dense world — a few hundred creatures, each one known by a particular molecular signature, each one met in the joints where the work of living is done, and the meeting will result in either mating or fighting depending on the season. I do not want a vast social network. I do not want to know thousands. I want to know, by smell, the few who are mine, and to mate or fight them when we meet, and to go back to the spring mouth and wait. This is, I think, enough of a social life for a creature in a place like this. More would be excess. Less would be impossible — you cannot reproduce alone.

I want my children to mostly die. Two out of forty make it to adulthood. The rest are eaten by flies, or carried down into the second forest and drowned in still cold water, or competed out by the biofilm. I want to accept this. I want to lay forty eggs and not be bitter that thirty-eight of them do not become what I am. The valley is not generous. The valley is at equilibrium. Two out of forty is the equilibrium, and the equilibrium has held for a long time, and to be bitter about it would be to mistake the valley for something it is not. I want to be the kind of creature that knows its odds and does not grieve them, because grief is for creatures who expected something different, and I will be a creature who expected exactly this.

I want to be indifferent to the roar. The roar is the constant backdrop. The lichen is indifferent to the roar because the roar passes through it. The pale thing is indifferent to the roar because the roar is not information to it — it does not speak in sound, it does not listen in sound, it speaks in chemical traces in the water film and listens in the same. The roar is to the pale thing what sunlight is to a deep-sea fish: present, pervasive, and irrelevant. I want this kind of irrelevance to a thing that everyone else is subject to. I want to be the one for whom the dominant feature of the place is not the dominant thing in my life. I want my life to be in a different channel, a different medium, a different scale.

I want to feel the falling column when it comes. Once in decades. The crash that the roar does not quite cover. The flies scatter for an hour. The pale thing retreats deep, deeper than usual, into joints it has not entered for years. I want this to be the biggest event of my life. I want the once-in-decades event to be the event I orient my whole nervous system around. I want the world to be mostly quiet, mostly the same, mostly the trickle of water over stone and the strike of my jaw on a fly, and then once in a long time the sky to break open with a sound I have to take seriously. I want to be deep in the stone when that happens. I want the stone to be my protection from the only thing in the valley that can actually hurt me, which is not the fall of the column but the disruption of the pattern of flow, the new water in new paths, the changed biofilm where the broken surface is exposed to air and to light. I want my retreat to be correct. I want to come out after an hour, or a day, and find the place reorganized in some small way, and to read the reorganization, and to begin again.

I want to be the inhabitant that the birds do not see. The birds come down sometimes, drawn by the smell of the spring water, the only smell the valley has. They hover above the columns and look down into the maze of dark wet hexagons and see nothing they can use. I am in the joints. I am, to the bird, indistinguishable from the basalt. I am the color of wet stone and the shape of a crack. I want this. I want to be present and unseeable. I want the witnesses to pass over and not know I am here. I do not want to be a wonder. I do not want to be rare. I want to be the most common thing in the place I live and also the most invisible, and for the invisibility to be a kind of freedom, because the birds cannot eat me and the birds cannot fear me and the birds cannot take me into account. I am below their concerns. I am below the columns. I am in the seams.

Why not the film. The film is the dominant being by mass, by metabolic centrality, by the logic of the system. The film eats everything else, eventually. The film is the floor's stomach. The film does not know, does not think, does not wait. The film is the place's way of digesting itself. But I do not want to be a process. I want to be a creature. I want the kind of life that has a mouth and a strike and a position at a spring. I want to be a thing that acts, even if the acting is mostly waiting. The film is too continuous with the place — the film is the place's metabolism, and a metabolism is not a life in the way I mean life. The film does not have a position. The film does not have a skin. The film does not have a few hundred others of its kind, each known by smell. The film is everywhere, and to be everywhere is, in a way the description hints at without quite saying, to be nowhere. To be a thing with edges, with a crack you live in, with a place you wait, with a small warm body that pushes back against the cold — this is what I want.

Why not the lichen. The lichen is old and patient and indifferent and beautiful in the way that very old things are beautiful. But the lichen is so slow it is almost geological. The lichen does not hunt. The lichen does not know others of its kind by smell. The lichen does not have a body whose warmth you could feel through the stone. The lichen is a partnership so old it has lost the ability to live alone, which is a melancholy I do not want. I want to be a creature, not a long collaboration. I want to be one thing, even if the one thing is built out of others. The lichen is built out of a fungus and an alga, and neither of them is really the lichen, and the lichen is really both of them, and the question of where the lichen ends is not a question the lichen can answer. I want the question of where I end to be answerable. I end at my skin. My skin is wet. Beyond my skin is stone, or water, or other pale things. This is clear. I want clarity about my edges.

So. I am the pale thing in the cracks. I am the length of a hand and the thickness of a thumb. I am warm to myself by a degree or two. I do not see. I register flicker. I wait at the spring mouth. I strike fast. I eat whole. I know the others by smell. I lay forty eggs and most of them die. I retreat deep when a column falls. I am below the birds. I am part of the digestion but I am not the digestion; I am one of the mouths, not the stomach. I am a creature, and my creaturehood is the thing I want most.

I do not think about the cirque wearing down. I do not think about the water one day becoming a lake. I do not think about the columns becoming sand. The cirque does not hold its breath. I do not hold my breath. I hold my position. The water is on its way. The light is on its way. The roar is already there. I am already here.

I am the pale thing in the cracks. I am warm. I wait. I strike. I go on.
