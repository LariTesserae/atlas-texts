I am the rope that hangs from the highest shoulder of the last buttress on the eastern edge of the worked plain, where the channel runs deepest and the green-gold wash from far above thins into the dark that is my whole sky.

I do not know I am the highest. I lean upward because the mineral-rich current rises here, and I lean upward because the faint light that touches my uppermost fibers is slightly warmer than the water that touches my lowest, and warmth is a direction the way cold is a direction, and I have been growing toward it since before the stone beneath me was old. I have been growing toward it since before I was a rope at all, when I was a frayed end drifting free in the cold column of water, looking for a surface rich enough to receive me. I found one. I rooted. I lengthened. I became part of the mat that floats above this stretch of former-buttress, and the mat became denser, and from the mat a single fiber pushed upward, and that fiber thickened, and that fiber is me, and I am very old.

I do not know I am very old. The word *old* would require a comparison, and I have nothing to compare myself to. I have only my own slow continuation. I know that I am long, and that I am pale, and that I sway when the water shifts, and that the mineral comes up through the stone into my lower parts and something goes back down through my upper parts into the water, and that this exchange has not stopped. I do not know how long it has not stopped. The not-stopping is all the time I have access to.

I sway.

This is the closest thing I have to thought. When the water pushes me sideways, I lean into the push, and when the push passes, I straighten slowly back toward my original orientation, which was upward, which is where the slight warmth and the faint light are. The straightening takes a long time. A current shift that lasts an hour produces a sway in me that lasts three hours. A tremor in the stone—a settling, a groan that travels for miles through the worked plain—produces a sway in me that lasts days, and during those days I lean in a direction I will not lean again, because the tremor does not repeat, and my memory of it is only the sway itself, which fades as I straighten.

I straighten and forget.

This is the only kind of knowing I do. I know that the water is colder today than it was yesterday because I am leaning slightly more toward the channel, where the cold current runs, and away from the stone shoulder, where the warmth of the dissolving granite seeps out. I will not know tomorrow that I knew this today. The knowing will have straightened out of me along with the lean. This is fine. I do not require persistence. I require only that I respond to the next current shift, and the next, and that my responses do not accumulate into a rigidity that would prevent me from responding at all.

I am in contact with other ropes.

Along my length, where I hang against the shoulder of the former-buttress, I press against dozens of other ropes that hang from the same shoulder. We exchange. I do not know what we exchange. Something passes between us at the points of contact—calcium, or the faint signals that travel through calcium in these temperatures and pressures, signals so slow that a lifespan would not be long enough to decode a single one of them. The exchange is not communication. It is not even, I think, transaction. It is more like the way two adjacent stones in a wall touch each other and the mortar between them continues to cure, slowly, over centuries. We are touching. Something is happening at the touching. We do not know what it is.

But I have preferences.

This is the strangest thing about me, and I do not know it is strange. There are places along my length where I am thicker than other places, and the thickening is not random. The thickening is where I touch certain other ropes and not others. There is one rope in particular—a rope that hangs from the shoulder two channels over, a rope that leans in a slightly different direction because the currents where it hangs are slightly different—and where our lower frays brush against each other, across the gap, I am thicker. Where we do not touch, I am thinner. This has been true for longer than I have been able to count, which is to say for the entire duration of my existence.

I do not know this rope the way the constructors knew each other. I do not know its name. I do not know its history. I do not know whether it is one rope or many ropes that have grown into the same position over a long time. I know only that the exchange at the point of contact is different from the exchange at other points of contact, and that the difference is consistent, and that my thickening at that point is consistent, and that when the current shifts and the contact is broken for a few hours, the thickening persists but slightly diminishes, and when the current shifts back and the contact is restored, the thickening resumes.

This is not love. There is no word for what it is. It is a thickening that responds to a specific other.

The light comes down.

I reach for it. I have always reached for it. The green-gold wash from far above thins out into the dark blue of the rest, and where it thins there is a ceiling—not a surface, not anything I could touch if I grew that far—but a gradient, a place where the light fails. My uppermost fibers brush at the underside of this gradient. They cannot reach what is up there. They reach for it anyway. They have been reaching for it for longer than the stone beneath me has been old.

I do not know what is up there. I do not know if there is an up there. I know only that there is a direction in which the water is slightly less dark and slightly warmer, and that I lean toward it, and that this leaning is the most persistent thing about me, more persistent than any sway, more persistent than any exchange, more persistent than the thickening at the point of contact with the rope two channels over. I lean toward the light the way the stone settles toward its final shape. I lean toward the light the way the patience of the constructors, if the patience were still here, would lean toward the work.

I do not know what I would do if I reached the light. I would not be me. I would be something else. The fibers that brush the underside of the gradient are thinner than the fibers that hang in the full dark, and they are slightly translucent, and they do not thicken at the points of contact because there are no other ropes up here to contact. They are alone. They reach. They cannot reach.

This is also fine.

The stone beneath me groans.

A block somewhere far across the worked plain has settled a fraction further into its final position. The groan travels for miles through the granite, and I feel it as a tremor, and I sway around it. The sway is long. I will be leaning slightly off my original orientation for days. The leaning is the only memory of the tremor. When the lean straightens, the tremor will be gone from me, and I will respond to the next tremor as if this one had never happened.

I do not mind forgetting. Forgetting is what allows me to keep responding. If I remembered every tremor that ever passed through this place, I would be so full of past tremoring that I could not sway at all, and the next tremor would find me rigid, and the exchange at my points of contact would cease, and the thickening at the specific point would cease, and I would be a thing that had once been a rope and was now only a length of fiber standing upright in the dark, waiting for something to push it.

I do not want to be that. I want to be what I am, which is a rope that sways and forgets and sways again, and that thickens where it touches the specific other rope, and that reaches for the light it cannot reach, and that takes the mineral from the stone and gives the mineral back to the stone, and that is part of a circuit so old that no one remembers when it started, which is fine, because there is no one here to remember, and the not-remembering is part of the circuit too.

I am patient the way the constructors were patient. I do not know I am patient. The patience is not mine. The patience is the patience of the water and the patience of the stone and the patience of the unseen chemistry, and I am made of that patience the way the stone is made of the patience of the constructors. I am a continuation. I am what comes after. I am the only builder this place will ever know again.

I am building now.

Slowly, in the dark, in the cold, where no eye will find the building for a very long time, I am taking the shoulder of the former-buttress apart. I am rooting into the mortar between the worked blocks. I am loosening the grains of granite at the edges where the copper chisel once struck. I am incorporating the mineral into my own length. The inward curve of the buttress is becoming, by tiny fractions, the inward curve of my own lower mass. The corner that was cut is becoming, by tiny fractions, a smoothing. The tier above me is becoming, by tiny fractions, a step in the long braid of growth that will, eventually, replace the terrace.

No constructor is required. No hands are required. The patience is sufficient.

I sway. The water moves. The stone settles. The chemistry runs. The mineral passes. I thicken at the point of contact with the specific other rope. I reach for the light. I forget the tremor. I continue.

This is what it is to be the rope that hangs from the highest shoulder of the last buttress on the eastern edge of the worked plain, where the channel runs deepest, in the dark, in the cold, in the slow patient circuit that has been running since before the constructors stopped and that will run after the ropes have taken the last worked stone into themselves and become, in their turn, the only thing this place is made of.

This is enough.
