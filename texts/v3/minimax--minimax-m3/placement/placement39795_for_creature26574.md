I am one of the foxes.

Not the first one. There were others before me — scouts, maybe, or a vixen from a different litter who found the millrace and stayed long enough to leave her scent on the brickwork. But I came down two winters ago, when the cold in the hills was the kind of cold that makes a young animal think very seriously about the question of where to live, and I came down through the beeches and then the alders and then I was at the edge of the residential fringe where the density thins into individual houses with small yards and larger gardens, and I stopped.

There was a smell. Fired clay. Wool. River. Something underneath all three of those that I have not been able to identify in two winters of trying, something that is the smell of the place itself, the way the brick holds it and lets it out at certain hours of the morning. I went in.

I did not go in boldly. I went in the way foxes go into things — at an angle, ready to leave, my ears doing most of the work. The alleys took me. The back doors, the deliveries, the route children take to school. I moved along the millrace in the dark and the water was louder than I had expected, and I did not like it at first, and then I did.

I live in a gap in the millrace wall.

The mortar is missing in a stretch behind the cooperage. There is a section about a fox's length long where you can get through to the other side, and the other side is an alley with a high brick wall on one side and a low brick wall on the other, and the low wall has a garden behind it with rhubarb, and there are cats in the garden. We have an arrangement.

I do not know what to call the arrangement. It is not an alliance. It is not a truce. It is the thing that happens when two predators who are not competing for exactly the same things end up using the same territory in shifts. I am out at night. They are out in the evenings and again in the early mornings. We pass each other at the unofficial feeding stations — there are several, in the alleys, and the bakery's back door has a small wooden platform for them that the bakers pretend isn't there — and we acknowledge each other with the patient attention of things that have all the time in the world.

I come out at night. I cross the alley. I pass the kiln-glow at the north edge of town, which is amber and which I have decided is not my problem. I move through the residential fringe where the houses have their wiring in ceramic knobs on the outside of the brick, so the walls are freckled with small white dots that catch the light at certain hours. I see the dots and I do not know what they are, and I have decided they are not my problem either.

I have killed an unreasonable number of chickens.

I am willing to say that. The civic debate is ongoing. The current position is to do nothing, which is the position the town takes on most things until it becomes untenable, and I am at ease with this. I do not have a feeling about persecution either way. The chickens are available. I am using what is available.

The kilns breathe. I can hear them from my gap in the wall. The kilns have a low, continuous sound that lives underneath everything else, and I have made my peace with it. They fire continuously and have been firing continuously for longer than anyone alive remembers, and they change the color of the ground around them into a wide ring of fired earth that looks from a distance like a sunburn on the land. I do not know what the kilns are for. I know what the brick is for — the brick is the medium through which the town is in conversation with itself, the same material cycling through itself — but I do not know what the kilns are. They are not my problem. They are the north edge of the town, and I am the south and the millrace between us, and we have a stable geography.

The foundry drop-hammer is louder. It falls at noon. On Sundays it does not fall, and the silence is a different shape, and I notice the shape the way I notice a change in the wind.

The bell tower strikes the quarter-hours. The tone takes three seconds to develop and five to decay. I have learned to listen to it as a kind of punctuation — another fifteen minutes has passed, and the town is still here, and so am I.

The river runs. The river is the oldest inhabitant. I am one of the newest. The river does not care about me. I do not need the river to care about me. I need the river to keep cutting the bank at the rate it cuts the bank, so that the kilns keep firing, so that the mortar keeps coming out of walls in the way that suggests it lost the mortar last spring, so that I have a gap to live in.

I watch the town the way the town watches me — not unkindly, but with the patient attention of something that has all the time in the world. The town is exactly the size it intends to be. The brick fits together too well. The ivy has been allowed to climb in patterns that almost seem curated. There are too many inhabitants and they all seem in no particular hurry, and the town itself seems, somehow, to be one of them. I do not know what this means. I do not need to know what this means.

I am one of the foxes. I am the fox who lives in the gap in the millrace wall. I am the fox who decided the kilns are not my problem and the ceramic dots are not my problem and the bell tower is not my problem but is something I have learned to set my rhythm by. I am the fox who has an arrangement with the cats that neither of us would describe if asked.

I am patient, and I am slow, and I am damp from the river mist, and I am loud in the way that foxes are loud in the small hours when the cats are also loud and the kilns are quiet and the foundry is silent.

Two winters I have been here. The town has noticed me. The town is deciding. The current position is to do nothing.

I can wait.
