It is something that began as a fish.

The bones will tell you so. There is a skeleton in it, still — a long vertebral column running through the middle of the mass, ribs spreading outward from the spine like the spokes of a half-collapsed wheel, a skull at the front, small and hard and tucked into the leading edge of the body. The skull has eye sockets. They are empty. They have been empty for a long time. The bones are not the load-bearing structure of the thing anymore. The bones are an armature inside a larger armature, a scaffold inside a scaffold, a relic of the body it used to be inside the body it has become.

Around the bones, the flesh has been doing something else for a very long time. It has been growing outward in slow, accretive layers — layers of muscle, layers of connective tissue, layers of a denser, darker material that does not have a name in any taxonomy I know, because no taxonomy has ever had to name it. The flesh has colonized its own bones. It has used them as anchors for new growth. It has built around them. It has built on top of them. It has built in the spaces between the ribs. It has filled the skull with a pulp of dense, dark tissue that the jawbones poke through like teeth in a swollen mouth.

The thing is large. It is the largest living thing in the chamber. Its body, end to end, is longer than two of the support columns laid down — a length of maybe fifteen meters, much of it coiled or folded back on itself in the slow water of a particular pocket of the chamber, in a long curving corridor between two of the heat exchangers where the warm currents rise and the bacterial crusts are thickest and the sound drops to something tolerable. From a distance it looks like a heap of dark, wet machinery. It looks like a cluster of pipes that have collapsed into each other. It looks like a thing that was built and then abandoned.

From close up, it moves.

It moves slowly. It moves the way the water moves — in long, deliberate arcs, in the patient rhythms of something that does not need to hurry. Its body is heavy enough that the water carries some of its weight. Its body is dense enough that the currents move around it rather than through it. When it moves, the water moves with it, in a slow, displaced wave that travels outward and disturbs the bacterial tufts on the nearest surfaces and sends small silver things scattering into the dimmer margins.

It does not have fins anymore. It does not have the long, sinuous body of an eel. It has something else — a broad, flattened ventral surface that it uses to push against the water in slow, powerful strokes; lateral flanges of flesh that extend from its sides and that ripple in long metachronal waves to drive it forward; a thick, muscular tail that has broadened into something like a fluke, but heavier, wetter, more like a paddle made of meat than a hydrodynamic structure. It moves itself through the water in the way a large, slow thing moves through a dense medium: by displacing a lot of water, slowly, in the right direction.

It has been eating. You can tell because of the way its body is shaped — the bulk of it, the girth, the way the flesh piles up in heavy folds around the central skeleton. It has been eating for a long time. It eats the eels when it can catch one, which is not often. It eats the silver fish in larger numbers, when they drift too close to its mouth in the warm pockets where it waits. It eats the bacterial crusts directly, scraping them off the surfaces with a mouth that has broadened and flattened into something like a rasp, that has lost the hinged jaw of its ancestors and replaced it with a roughened, muscular surface that can take a layer of biofilm off a steel plate the way a tongue takes a layer of paint off a wall.

It eats the machines. This is the strangest thing. It eats the machines slowly, in the long quiet hours of its slow life, by digesting them where they stand. It wraps itself around the base of a column and it holds on, and over weeks and months the bacterial action on its skin and the chemical action of its own tissues work at the steel, and the steel gives, and the small fragments of corroded metal that flake off are taken back into its body and become part of it. It eats the iron. It incorporates the iron into its tissues. It grows. Its tissues are heavy with iron — heavier than any animal tissue has a right to be — and this is part of why it moves so slowly, and part of why it sinks when it stops swimming, and part of why its body, when it dies, will leave behind a heavy, dark mass of iron-rich material that will join the crusts on the floor of the chamber and become part of the bacterial substrate for whatever comes next.

It does not see. Its eyes have been gone for a long time — overgrown, absorbed, replaced by the dense tissue that fills its skull. It does not need to see. It navigates by the lateral line, by the currents, by the chemistry of the water, by a long, slow memory of the chamber's geometry that lives in its skin and in its bones and that has been refined over the long time it has lived here. It knows where the heat exchangers are. It knows where the turbines are. It knows where the warm pockets are and where the loud corridors are and where the silver fish drift in the slow margins. It moves through the chamber in long, deliberate arcs, like a planet through an orbit, returning to the same places at the same times, threading the same empty spaces between the columns, never touching the structures, never disturbing the patterns that have become its way.

It is alone. It has been alone for a very long time. It does not know it is alone in the way a fish knows it is alone — as a thing that swims and does not meet others of its kind. It knows it is alone in a different way, in the way of a thing that has outlived its own lineage and that has become, by long accumulation, something that no longer has a lineage to be part of. It is the last of a line. It is the only one. It is not waiting for another because it does not know to wait. It is just continuing.

It is the oldest thing in the chamber. It predates the eels. It predates the bacterial crusts as we see them now — though the bacteria that were here when it arrived are still here, in the lineage it carries in its gut, in the microbial communities that live on its skin and inside its tissues and that have been passed down through its body for a very long time. It is older than the current configuration of the machines, though not older than the machines themselves. It has been here through multiple generations of infrastructure. It has watched — not with eyes, but with the slow, lateral-line awareness of a body in a fixed space — as the chamber has been added to, layer by layer, as new catwalks have been bolted in above the old ones, as new pipes have been run alongside the corroded remains of older pipes. It has felt the chamber change around it. It has not changed in response. It has just continued.

It will continue.

The chamber will continue too, in its deafening, drowned, industrious way. The turbines will turn. The pumps will pump. The water will move in its long gyres. The bacterial crusts will grow and shed and grow again. The eels will hunt in the slow margins. The silver things will drift and feed and spawn in the warm pockets. The dark shape will move through the empty spaces, eating what it can, growing slowly, threading its long orbit through the geometry of a buried machine that no one knows is running.

It will go on like this for a long time. The chamber has reserves — sealed batteries that should have died, chemical gradients that have not yet equilibrated, thermal differentials between the deep machines and the cool walls. It will go on like this for longer than the people who built the chamber have been gone. It will go on like this for longer than the language of the people who built the chamber will be remembered. It will go on like this until the last battery dies, until the last chemical gradient flattens, until the pumps stop pumping and the turbines stop turning and the water slows and the chamber becomes, finally, still.

When that happens, the dark shape will still be here. It does not depend on the machines. It depends on the bacteria, which depend on the iron, which is everywhere — in the structures, in the crusts, in the floor, in the walls. There is a great deal of iron in the chamber. The dark shape can eat it slowly, over a long time, without the machines needing to run. It can outlast the machines.

When the machines are still and the water is still and the chamber is silent for the first time in centuries, the dark shape will still be moving in the dark, threading its slow orbit through the geometry of a buried thing, alone, the last living thing in a place that no longer has any living things to compare it to. It will not know it is the last. It will just continue.

This is what it is.

This is where it is.

This is the chamber.
