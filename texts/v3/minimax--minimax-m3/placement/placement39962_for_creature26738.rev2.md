I came back from the outside once.

I was nineteen. My uncle Halden had decided I should see what the rest of the world looked like before I settled into the hammer for good. He had never gone himself. He said he had no need. But he had heard enough from the leavers and the traders to know that I would not rest easy until I had stood under a sky that lifted higher than a hundred yards, and he was right about that, and so he sent me off with two of my cousins and a smith called Halla, who had made the journey three times and was restless again and would not have said no to a fourth.

We walked for eleven days out across the basalt. The stone changed slowly. At first it was the same rock we had always known, black and sweating, the pools warm at the edges, the lichen in its pale orange crusts. Then the columns began to lean further, and the depressions between them filled with something darker, and the lichen thickened, and then one morning there was moss. It was a small thing, a fist-sized clump in the lee of a tilted pillar, but we all four of us stopped to look at it as if it were the first animal any of us had seen. My cousin Osta put his hand on it and laughed and said it was soft, as if softness were something to laugh at.

Then the rivers came. The rain was no longer a fine sideways mist but a proper rain, falling in sheets, and we crossed three rivers in the last two days of walking, and on the far side of the third one the world was green.

I had not known.

I want to say that clearly, because I have spent forty years since trying to find the right word for what I felt and I have not found one and I do not think the language has it. The Reaches had prepared me for the idea of green. The lichen was green-or-near-it. The crawlers, when you cut them open, had a kind of greenish dark inside them. The moss that I saw in the last days of the journey was green. But *green* as the word is used elsewhere, *green* as a meadow is green, *green* as the whole of a hillside can be green—nothing in the Reaches had prepared me for that. I had heard of it. I had not understood.

What I remember most is the wind. The grass was bending. I had never seen anything bend that was not made of metal or water. I stood at the edge of a meadow and watched it, and I asked Halla what was happening, and she said *the wind, child, it is the wind*, and I said *I know it is the wind but what is the wind doing*, and she laughed at me and I did not understand why she laughed and I do not understand now. There is some gap between us and the outside that I have not been able to name. The grass was simply doing what grass does. The wind was simply doing what wind does. But to me, who had never seen either of them in their fullness, it looked like a kind of madness, a kind of seizure, the whole field of green throwing itself about for no reason I could grasp.

I stayed nine months. I worked at a forge in a town called Tollen, on the bank of a wide slow river. The forges there were lit with coal that came from somewhere, and the iron came from somewhere else, and the smiths were thin and pale and clever and they did not cough. I could feel the difference in my lungs within the first week. The air was clean. There were trees at the edge of the town and birds in the trees that sang in the morning in a way that was not the metallic piping of the pipe-voices, and the sky at noon was a thing I could look up into without my eyes watering. I could see for miles in any direction. I could see the curve of the hills on the far side of the river.

I did not like it.

I did not understand that I did not like it. I thought I was homesick, which is the polite word for what I was feeling. But I was not homesick. Homesick is the longing for a place. What I felt was the longing for a *sound*, and I did not understand it as that for many years.

The silence. That is what I could not bear.

It was not true silence. There were birds and there was the river and there was the wind, which I had learned to call by its right name by then. There was the sound of the forge in Tollen, which was a real sound, an honest sound, the ringing of good iron on good anvil. There were conversations. There were the dogs of Tollen, which were different from our dogs - leggier, longer-muzzled, with coats of brown and white. There was no lack of sound.

But the sound did not *hold* me. That is the only word I have. In the Reaches the sound is a thing you stand inside of. It presses on the eardrums. It is the medium you live in, like water for a fish. In Tollen I could step outside the sound. I could walk away from the forge and the sound fell away behind me and there was something else, something emptier, and the emptiness was unbearable. I would stand in a field at evening and the wind would be moving the grass and there would be no answer to it. Nothing answering the wind. Nothing answering the grass. Just the small far sounds of birds and the river and they were not *for* me, they were not part of the place I lived in, they were happening at me.

I came back within the year, as most of us do. I walked across the basalt with two other leavers from Tollen who were also returning, and we said very little on the journey, but I remember that as we got close to the Reaches—two days out, when the lichen began to appear again in its pale orange crusts—we could hear the hammering. We could hear it from the ridge above the first river, a low continuous pulse, and we all three stopped and stood for a moment and the leavers from Tollen were weeping, one of them, and I was weeping too though I did not know it until Halden pointed it out when we came into the settlement. *You have been crying*, he said, and I said *no*, and he said *yes, look at your face*, and I touched my cheek and my hand came away wet.

I took up the hammer again. My hands re-calloused within a season. The iron dust worked its way back into the creases of my knuckles. The scars that had almost faded came back in the old patterns, the burn across the index finger from the first time I held the tongs wrong, the thickening of the web between thumb and forefinger where the hammer handle had rubbed for years. The body is faithful. It remembers what it has been, and it will become that thing again if you give it the chance.

I have been here forty years since. I am sixty-seven, which is old for the Reaches, though not the oldest. There are three smiths older than I am, and one of them is Halden's mother, who is ninety-one and still works the bellows on the third shift at the great forge in the central cluster. She has lost most of the sight in one eye and all of the hearing in one ear but she knows the bellows the way the dogs know the warmth of the stone. Her hands can read a fire.

My hands now are the hands my uncle's hands were, and his mother's hands before him, and the hands of every smith who has worked this basalt for the four generations we have been here. They are broad across the palm. The nails are kept short. The cuticles are black with iron dust that no amount of washing fully removes. There is a small piece missing from the left ear, where a forge-fire breathed outward at me unexpectedly when I was thirty-two and I stepped back into the anvil behind me. There is a scar along the back of the right hand from a piece of copper that came off the hammer at the wrong angle and opened the skin in a long shallow line. The pattern is familiar. I could pick out a smith of the Reaches in any crowd in the world by the hands alone.

My eyes are the second thing I think of, though it took me years to know they had changed. They are not large eyes. They do not gleam in the forge-glow as some folk's eyes do. But they have learned to gather the dim light that comes to us through the steam-cloud, and they hold it, and they return it in a gaze that visitors from open lands find difficult to meet. I do not understand why they find it difficult. I am only looking.

The lungs are the third thing, though you cannot see them, only know them by the breathing. When I first came back from Tollen my lungs had begun to clear, and I could feel them clearing, the way the air moves deeper into you when there is no smoke in it. Within a season of being back they were mottled again. The physicians say it is healthy in the only way that matters here, and I believe them, because I am sixty-seven and I am still breathing and the hammer is still in my hand and the air I breathe is the air of the Reaches, and I do not want any other air.

There is a smith in the eastern cluster, a young woman called Kira, who has begun mapping the lichen. She marks each new patch on the basalt columns with a small symbol, a kind of glyph she has invented, and she keeps a record in a book of folded lead sheets, and she has been doing this for eleven years. I have watched her do it. I went with her once, last autumn, when she was checking the margins of the new pool that has formed on the western edge of the settlement, where the cooling has opened a fissure and the water is collecting. She is patient in a way I am not. She will kneel on the warm stone for an hour watching a single patch of lichen to see if it has grown by a fingernail's width in the season. I went with her because I wanted to see what she saw, and what I saw was this: that the lichen is doing nothing, and that the nothing is the point.

The lichen is the first green thing. It will not be the last. In a thousand years there will be moss here, a carpet of it, and the pools will be cooler, and the crawlers will be different, and the dogs will not be able to read the warmth of the stone because the stone will not have a warmth worth reading. In ten thousand years there will be rivers. In a hundred thousand years there will be forests. None of us will see any of this. None of our grandchildren will see it. But the lichen sees the edge of it. The lichen is the first promise, and Kira is its scribe.

I think about the lichen more than I used to. I think about the basalt cooling under our feet, fractionally, a degree in a century perhaps, and the pools settling into themselves, and the steam thinning, and the cloud lifting by a yard in a generation. I think about the whole of the Reaches as a temporary thing, balanced on the surface of a world that is still becoming itself. The hammering is temporary. The smoke is temporary. The settlement is temporary. We are temporary, all three thousand of us, crowded onto young stone that will outlast us by a factor we cannot imagine.

The smiths know this. We do not speak of it often. We build low. We build temporary. The forge-houses have walls three feet thick because the stone is easy to cut and the walls hold the heat, but also because thick walls can be taken down and rebuilt when the basalt shifts beneath them, as it does, as it will continue to do for centuries. The sleeping-platforms are cut into the columns of stone, but they are cut shallowly, and a smith of the third generation can pull the platforms out and cut new ones when the columns lean further. We are not building for permanence. We are building for now.

At evening, though the word is a courtesy, we stand at the edges of the larger pools. The hammer is set down or hung at the belt. The smoke drifts. The crawlers rise and sink in their slow constellations, surfacing with a small popping sound, sinking with a faint trail of bubbles. The shelled ones catch the forge-glow in pale iridescence where the light reaches them. The threads drift in their rust-orange blooms and stain the water where they swarm.

The dogs come and stand with us, low and rust-colored, broad paws on the warm stone, and they watch the pools with the same attention we do. They are not pets. They are small colleagues. They have a wisdom about the stone that we do not have, and we consult them, in our way, about where it is safe to step and where it is not. A dog will not walk on rock that is too fresh. A dog scents the new water before we do. We have learned to listen to them, and the listening is part of the watching.

And sometimes, in the grey-blue dim of early evening, a small dark shape moves across the cloud-layer a hundred yards up, and the metallic piping comes from it—long notes and short notes in patterns that almost, but not quite, match the rhythms of the hammering below. The pipe-voices. We do not know what they are. I have asked the older smiths. Halden's mother, who is ninety-one and knows the bellows the way the dogs know the warmth, does not know. The pipe-voices may live in the steam-fissures and rise up to call. They may live in the cloud itself. They may be a venting of the rock's own pressure, a sound the basalt makes when it cools, mistaken for a creature. It does not matter what they are. They are part of the sound. They are part of the place. They come and go and their coming and going is not predictable, and we note the times of their absence as carefully as the times of their presence, though we could not say why.

The watching at the pool is a kind of rest, and the rest is shared. We do not speak much during it. We do not need to. The sound of the pools—the small poppings, the faint rustle of things moving—is itself a kind of quiet, the only quiet the Reaches has to offer, and we stand in it together. The hammer has fallen all day and the bellows have breathed all day and the anvils have answered all day, and now the body is still, and the eyes are open to the small writhing life of the pool, and the breath goes in and out of the lungs that are mottled black and pink, and this is what we are, and this is what we are becoming, and the lichen is spreading by a hand's width in a decade, and the basalt is cooling under our feet, and we are here.

I have a daughter who is thirty and a son who is twenty-six and a grandson who is three. The grandson was born into the hammering and has never known silence. He does not cry at the sound because there is nothing quieter to contrast it against. He catches crawlers in a copper pan at the edges of the warm pools, his feet tough as a dog's paw, running barefoot over the warm basalt without flinching. He has never seen the sun clearly. He knows it only as a smudge at noon.

I hope he will never leave. I will not send him to the outside as my uncle sent me. I will not send him because I know now what the journey is for, and I know now that the returning is the real journey, and I know now that some of us are not made for the returning, and some of us are not made for the staying, and a few rare ones are made for neither and they live in the gap all their lives, and I do not wish that gap on my grandson.

I will teach him the hammer when he is seven or eight. His hands will take the scars that my hands took and that his great-grandmother's hands took before me. The iron dust will work its way into the creases of his knuckles. The pattern will continue. The body is faithful. The body will become what the place requires of it.

And the lichen will spread, and the basalt will cool, and the pipe-voices will call from the cloud, and the crawlers will surface in the warm pools, and the sound will go on, and on, and on.

We are temporary, all of us. The place is temporary. The hammering is temporary. But *now* is not temporary. *Now* is the whole of it, the whole of what we have, and we stand at the edges of the warm pools at evening and we watch the small writhing life of the water and we breathe the smoke-sweet air and we are part of the becoming, and the becoming does not ask us to understand it, only to be in it, and we are in it, and the hammer is in our hand, and the iron is on the anvil, and the sound goes on, and the sound is the answer to the sound, and there is no silence, and we are glad of it.
