I have been sitting by this pool for a long time. I do not know how long. The people here do not measure time in the way I have heard the people of elsewhere measure it — in the regular coming of a sun that is here less than it is not. Here the sun is an idea, a thing the older ones speak of as though it were a relative they had once known who had gone away. The mist is the relative that stayed. The mist has been here longer than I have. It will be here after I have stopped.

I am stitching. In my hands is a piece of hide — the soft underside of one of the larger amphibians, taken in the second cold spell of last season, when the marsh had drawn back and left the body on the silt for us. The hide is damp, as everything is damp. The needle is bone. The thread is gut, split fine, dried, rubbed between the palms until it was soft. I am sewing the edge of a basket that will hold dried strips of the smaller fish when it is done, or perhaps small things that need keeping dry, which is to say small things that need to be slightly less wet than the rest of the basin, which is the only kind of dry there is.

A small amphibian is on the stone beside my knee. It is no bigger than the last joint of my thumb. It has been singing without pause for as long as I have been sitting. Its throat is a small pale pulse. I do not look at it directly, because if I look at it directly it will be gone, but I know it is there by the sound, which is a single thread in the larger weave of the chorus that rises from every pool and every stretch of mud in every direction as far as the mist lets me think. The chorus has no gap in it. I have learned to listen past it, the way you learn to listen past the wind. There is a layer under the wind. There is a layer under the chorus. Under both there is the hum of the insects, which is a pressure on the eardrums, which is the sound the basin makes when it is being most itself, which is most of the time. Under the hum there is the water. Under the water there is the silt. Under the silt there is the slow working of the place on itself, which is not a sound but a vibration, the way a stone vibrates when you put your ear to it after something heavy has fallen somewhere far away.

I am one of the people. I have always been one of the people. There is no other place in my memory, and the older ones do not speak of any other place with any certainty. There are stories. There is a story that on the other side of the ridges there is less water and more stone and a thing called sun that comes down out of the sky and touches your face and is warm. I have believed this story sometimes. I have not believed it other times. The people who go on the long trades come back with reeds for the baskets, and once in a great while a piece of wood, and once in a much greater while a bead of native copper, and these things are not from here. These things are proofs of somewhere else. But the somewhere else is far, and the proofs are small, and we do not speak of it as though it were a place we had been. We speak of it as the place the things come from.

I have a digging stick. It is in the tent, which is behind me, which is a low thing of stretched hides pegged between two outcrops of the harder stone. I can feel the stick through the hide of the tent the way you can feel a long bone through skin — by the shape of it, by the weight. The stick is old. It is older than I am. It is older than my mother. It has been reworked, and reworked again. The current shape is not the original shape. The wood is dark and close-grained and does not smell of the basin, which is how you know it is from elsewhere. I do not know how many hands have held it. I do not know how many seasons it has spent in the ground or out of it. I know it is the most valued thing in the tent, and I know that not every household has one, and I know that to be given the use of one is a thing that happens by agreement and is not asked for.

I helped set the standing stone.

I will tell you what this means. A standing stone is a single stone set up on end in a place that has been chosen. The stone is taller than a person. The stone is heavy in a way that the basin does not usually offer — heavy because it has not yet been broken down by the patient work of weather, heavy because it was found in the harder rock of the ridges and brought down to the silt flat by levers of wood from elsewhere and rollers of the same, and by ropes of sinew, and by everyone within a long walk who could be asked. To set one up is to spend a season. It is to spend several seasons. It is to come back to it every day for as long as it takes, and to lift it a little, and to brace it, and to wait for the next lifting, and to lift it again. The stone, once it is up, is not moved. The stone, once it is up, is the center of a cairn field that accumulates around it across generations. People add stones to the cairn when they come. People come when something has happened that they think the stone should know about.

The stone I helped set is on the silt flat east of the second ridge. It is dark. It is not decorated. It has been there longer than I have been stitching. I was young then. I do not feel young now, although I am not old in the way the people of elsewhere are old. I am old in the basin way, which is the way of the lichen, which is to say I have been growing slowly on the surface of something that is not in any hurry to be different.

I have been stitching for a long time. I will stitch for a long time still. The basket will be done when it is done. There is no hurry. There has never been a hurry. Hurry is a thing that does not work here. Hurry gets you wet faster and does not get you there sooner. The people who hurry in the basin are the people who fall into the deeper marshes and are not seen again, or who come up out of the silt with their wraps heavy and their faces streaming and they sit down by the first stone they can find and they are quiet for a long time, and after that they are not in a hurry again.

The leather-voice is calling from the deeper marsh to the north. I know it is the leather-voice and not some other low sound because of where the sound is coming from and what time of day it is, and because of the particular wet quality of it, which is the quality of a hide being slowly stretched by something that has been doing this for a long time. The people who live closer to that marsh know the call better than I do. They know the hour it begins and the hour it ends and the days it does not call at all. I know only the sound of it when it comes to me across the distance, which is the way you know the sound of a particular branch of a particular tree in wind — by where it is in the air, by how far away, by the shape the air gives it on the way to you.

I do not try to approach the source. The approach is not what is being offered. The call is what is being offered. It is what the leather-voice is willing to give of itself to the rest of the basin. I take what is offered. I do not ask for more. The basin teaches this. The basin teaches everything by waiting.

I have eaten today. Earlier, when the mist lifted a little — it lifts a little sometimes, though it never fully lifts, and when it does lift you see that the sky behind it is the color of wet wool, and you think: ah, so the sky is still there, I had begun to wonder — I went down to the slow river and I pulled the woven weir open at the joint, and I took out two of the small pale fish, and I smoked them on the rack beside the tent. The smoking is slow. The drying is slow. Everything here is slow. The fish are pale and soft and they taste of the basin, which is to say they taste of the silt and the algae and the small lives in the water. I ate them with a strip of the dried fungal tower, which is the only starch-like thing we have, which is faintly sweet, which is warm when you first bite into it, in the way a compost heap is warm, because it is still slightly working on itself.

I have stitched the edge of the basket. I have not stitched the side. The side will come later. I will stitch it when I am sitting here again, which will be tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever I next sit by this pool. The pool will be here. The small amphibian on the stone beside my knee will be here, or another small amphibian will be on the stone, because the stones by pools are always occupied by small amphibians, and the small amphibians are always singing, and the singing is always a part of the chorus, and the chorus is always a part of the basin, and the basin is always working on itself, slowly, patiently, with no particular opinion about what it should become.

My eyes are round. I have been told this, by the people who have come from elsewhere — there are sometimes people who come from elsewhere, though not often, and not for long. The people who come from elsewhere look at my eyes and are quiet for a moment, and then they say something in their own language, which is a language I do not speak. They say it in a way that is not unkind. They say it in a way that means they have noticed something, and that the thing they have noticed is not a thing they have a word for in the language they brought with them. The eyes are like the eyes of the larger amphibians in the shallows — wet, still, willing to look at a thing for a long time without deciding what they think of the thing. The eyes are like this because I have been looking at the basin for a long time, and the basin does not ask you to decide what you think of it. The basin is just there. The eyes have learned to be just there.

I am small. I am dark, in the way of a person who has lived without much sun — not by pigment but by the general dimness of a thing that has been in the mist for a long time, the color of tea, the color of the silt, the color of the stones I sit on. My hair is black and hangs lank and is always damp at the ends. My hands are quick in the small motions of stitching, which is the only kind of quick I am. I am not quick in walking. I am not quick in thinking. I am patient in the way the silt is patient, which is the patience of a thing that does not have anywhere to be.

The mist has come down a little. I cannot see the ridge any more. The cairn I was thinking of earlier is out there somewhere, but it is in the mist now, and I will not walk to it today. I will walk to it when the mist lifts, which it will, eventually, the way it always does, the way the basin does everything — slowly, and without announcement, and without any particular hurry to be different from what it was.

I am one of the people. I am one of the small things that lives here, in the mud, in the mist, in the dimness. I have made my small marks. The marks will outlast me. The basin will outlast the marks. That is the arrangement. I have accepted it the way the lichen accepts the stone — by living on the surface, and not asking the stone to be anything other than what it is.

I stitch. The small amphibian sings. The chorus rises. The mist moves. The basin does what the basin does. I sit by the pool, and I am wet, and I am here, and the sound is in me, and I am in the sound, and there is no silence underneath, only a quieter layer, all the way down.
