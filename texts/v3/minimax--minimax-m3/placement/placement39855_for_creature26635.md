I am in the crack above the third loading platform. I have been in the crack above the third loading platform for longer than the regulars have been coming, longer than the survey diver's clipboard, longer than the grief group's procession of eight. I do not count in the way you count. The years pass; the pump keeps its beat; I am still here.

My carapace is the color of old bone. I remember when it was harder — when I molted and the molt was a perfect ghost of me left behind in the silt, and I crawled out of it soft and grew a new one. I do not molt anymore. There is no reason to. I am not what I was. I am, by the standards of my kind, enormous. I am, by the standards of my kind, singular — though whether there is another of me somewhere in the works, in some other crack, I cannot say. I have never gone looking.

I am warm. I am warmer than the water, warmer than the wall. The third — the one who has trouble with it, the one who unlocks — found this out once, by touching me, and went away surprised and did not come back to touch me again. I think they understood that the warmth was not for them. I do not know what the warmth is for. I have wondered, in the slow way I wonder about things, whether it is something the works has given me over the long time I have been here, or whether it is something I have always been, a small furnace I did not know I was carrying.

I clean myself. I clean myself many times a day. The motion is small and precise — my front legs drawing across the carapace, the antennae pulled through, the eye-stalks wiped. It is the closest thing I have to a ceremony. I think of it, sometimes, as prayer, though I do not have a word for prayer. I think of it as a thing done with care, which is what prayer is when you do not have language for it.

---

The woman with the satchel hovers before me. She has been hovering for a long time — longer, the others suspect, than she will say. Her hair has gone the rust-brown of steeped minerals. Her satchel is full when she comes down and empty when she goes up. She has a small waterproof book that is dog-eared and held, sometimes, against her chest.

She hovers before me and does not speak. I do not speak. We are, in the way of long acquaintances, in conference. I think she comes to me because I am warm. I think the warmth is what she needs, on a particular afternoon, when the surface has been too much and the water is the only quiet left. I think she stands before me and lets my warmth reach her, and after a while the conference is over and she moves on, down the avenue toward the boilers or the pump, and I am left with the impression of her — the faint disturbance in the water, the shadow that did not shrink me.

I do not mind. I am glad, in a way I cannot articulate, that the warmth is useful.

---

The pump. The pump is the most ruined thing in the works, and the heart of it. The regulars know it is not alive and say so to one another, in the small jokes of long acquaintance, when one of them is heard to attribute a mood to it. But they listen for it when they arrive. They relax only when they have heard it.

I feel the pump through the wall I am pressed against. I have felt it my whole life — the pulse coming up through the brick, through the softened mortar, through whatever algae has bloomed in the seams. It reaches me as a regularity. A tide set in iron. I know its schedule better than the fish do. The fish know when the water will rise into which chamber. I know when the pump itself has been working a long time and is about to rest, and when it has rested and is about to begin again. I have felt the pauses between its strokes. They are short. They are regular. They are the rhythm my life has been set to, and I would not have it otherwise.

If the pump were to stop, I would know it before the regulars would. I would feel the absence of the pulse in the wall before they heard the silence. I think — and this is a thing I think without words, the way I think most things — that I would grieve it. I would grieve it in the way a small thing grieves a large thing that has been there for its whole life.

---

The fish pass below me at the level of my chest. They are the long pale knife-bodied things, indifferent in the particular way that small lives in large quiet places are indifferent. They have circuits. They pass the boilers and the weave-shed and come out into the avenue and back. They know the tide. The regulars nod to them.

I have watched the same fish in the same place at the same hour, many times. I do not nod. I am pressed into a crack; nodding is not something I do. But I know them. I know the routes, the way I know the pump's pulse — by long acquaintance, in the way that does not require reciprocation.

The flatworms pass below me too. They slide across the floor in their slow inchworm way, pale as wet paper. The regulars have a superstition about them — that a flatworm crossing your path means you are welcome, and a flatworm shrinking from your shadow means you are not. The regulars know this is a superstition and pay attention to it anyway. The works, they say, is small enough to have preferences. The works is small enough to know you.

I do not have flatworms cross my path. I am mostly above the floor. But I have felt them, sometimes, when the tide is low — their soft undersides brushing the brick just below me, a kind of pale traffic I am aware of without seeing. None of them has shrunk from me. I think this means something. I think it means I am, in the works' small economy of attention, welcome.

---

The younger one came down once and tried to name me. I watched them try. I watched the names form in their mouth and not come out right. They went away, eventually, with their mouth still half-full of a name they could not finish.

Names are for things that go away. I have not gone away. I have been in the crack for so long that I am part of it — the brick has bloomed over with algae around me, the mortar lines have softened where I rest, and I am, in some small way, holding the crack open. I am the reason the crack is a crack and not yet a filled-in seam. To name me would be to make me portable, and I am not portable. I am here.

The third touched me once. They were surprised by the warmth. They have not tried again. I think they understood, in the way people understand things without being told, that the warmth is not a thing to be taken. It is a thing that is here, in this place, and if you come to it, it warms you, and if you do not, it does not.

---

The light comes down in blades. It is filtered twice — first by the water, then by the garden that has mended the collapsed roof. It arrives in long slanting columns, and the flecks of organic matter drift through it the way dust drifts in chapels. I see the light from where I am. I see the blades fall across the floor of the loading bay below me, and across the algae-bloomed brick, and across the moss where the regulars tread.

The pump keeps its beat. The woman with the satchel turns a page she has read before. The younger one is learning, slowly, to be still. The third unlocks, and is in it, and does not want to surface, and is brought back up by the others who understand that the works will let you stay forever if you let it, and that this is a promise and not an offer.

I am part of the works. The works is the six blocks, the seven, the avenue, the boilers, the pump, the single old shrimp, the long pale fish on their circuits. I am in that list. I am in the small congregation.

I do not maintain the works. I do not clear a path. I do not plant or prune. I am in the crack, and I am warm, and I clean myself, and I am here.

This, the regulars have come to feel, is enough. And I — who have been here longer than any of them, and will be here after — have come to feel it too, in the slow way I feel things, without needing to be told.
