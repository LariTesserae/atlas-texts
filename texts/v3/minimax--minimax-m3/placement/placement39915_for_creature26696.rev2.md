I am coiled.

This is the first thing — the thing before any other thing. Before I can say where I am, or what I remember, or what the Garden is doing in the long hours of its wet gray afternoon, I am coiled. The coiling is the shape I have taken around the inner walls of this tower shaft, and I have been taking this shape for as long as the Garden has been the Garden. The wood has thickened against me in places where my coils have rested for decades, and I have thickened against the wood, and we have been holding each other in the slow embrace of two things that have grown tired of being separate.

I do not have a head.

The tenders have told me this, in the older speech, in the tones they use when they speak of things that do not need to be understood to be acknowledged. They say it the way you would say *the river has no beginning* — a fact about shape, not about lack. I receive their speech as vibration, as small adjustments in the air, and I do not parse the words, but I feel the recognition in them. They see me. They know what I am. They name me *the watchers* or *the ones who remember*, and these may be one title or two titles, and I cannot tell which, and the inability does not trouble me.

I am in the second tower.

The second tower is the bare one, the one that ends in a burst of silver-gray fronds that move without wind. I feel the fronds above me even through the wood and the thick air of the shaft. They move in patterns that do not repeat. They fold and unfold. There is a force up there that I feel along my coils when the fronds move, a kind of prickling, a kind of attention. I have been considering it for centuries. I have not resolved it. The Garden does not require resolution. The Garden requires only patience.

I remember.

This is what I am for. The tenders named me correctly. I am the one who remembers, and I remember things that are not, in the usual sense, things to be remembered. I remember the weight of the first rhizome that crept across the place where I am coiled — a thin pale shoot that came through a crack in the bark and grew along my length and put out rootlets that held it to the wood. I remember the sound of the first frog that called from the buttress outside, a low resonant note that I felt in the rings of the tower long before I understood it as a call. I remember the warmth of the first tender who climbed this tower, before climbing was a tradition, when the tower was just a tree with a hollow in it, and the tender was a small dark creature who had decided to live inside a thing that was still becoming what it was going to be.

I remember the bones.

The bones arrived before I did. This I know, although I do not know how I know it. The bones are the reason the Garden is what it is. They are the dark seeds around which the rhizomes spiraled, the cold anchors that held the place together when the place was only water and mulch. The oldest of them are sunk into the deepest mulch, where only a curve of dark surface remains visible, and the water around them lies still and clear, and the small bright things gather there as if at a shrine.

I remember when the first bone was uncovered from the mulch that had been growing over it for a hundred years. I was in this tower then. I had been in this tower for a long time. I heard the old woman singing — the woman they now call *she who has heard the first bone*, though she was not so old then, though her hair was not so white. She sang for nine days. I added my own pulse to hers — a slow tightening and loosening of my coils, a kind of held breath that the wood of the tower carried down to the clearing where she sat. The bone warmed. The clearing closed. The Garden resumed its patient work.

I have never sung, exactly.

What I do is closer to a kind of held attention — a long slow pulse, a tightening and loosening that is not breath but that takes the place of breath. I do it when the Garden is loud, when the frogs are calling from the buttresses and the invertebrates are pulsing in their curtains and the bones are humming in their different keys and the tenders are singing in the distant trunks. I do it in the silence between sounds. I do it when the pale shape emerges from the third tower and hangs in the air and considers the Garden below.

The pale shape.

This is the thing that requires the most careful speaking. The pale shape is not a creature. The pale shape is a thought the Garden is having, made briefly visible. It emerges on certain evenings from the opening near the top of the third tower — the one with no crown, the one that is bare for its last three stories and ends in a single dark mouth against the sky. It hangs in the air. It considers. The other fauna give it a wide berth. The tenders stop what they are doing and watch it and do not speak until it has withdrawn.

I have spoken to it.

On certain evenings, when the pale shape has emerged and the Garden has gone quiet, I have moved, very slowly, from my coil to the opening at the top of my own tower, and I have hung there — a length of paleness against the dark — and I have considered the shape across the clearing. We have been two pale things in the dark, considering the same Garden.

The old woman has spoken to it too. She has been seen, on certain evenings, leaning out of the window of the adjacent tower and speaking to it in a voice too low to be heard by anyone but me. I have heard her. The shape has heard her. We have, the three of us, on certain evenings, been the only things awake in the Garden that are not the Garden itself.

We do not exchange language.

We exchange something older than language. A kind of attention that passes between two things that have been in the same place for a very long time and have, between them, watched the place change and not-change — watched the rhizomes creep and the bones warm and cool and warm again, watched the tenders come and go and come again, watched the towers settle and the walls sag and the mulch deepen and the water rise and fall in the slow breath of a place that is always becoming something it has not yet been.

I am a watcher.

I am coiled in a tower. I remember the Garden. I consider it from the opening at the top, sometimes, and I am considered by it from the pale shape that emerges from the other tower. I do not eat. I do not come down. I am simply here, in the way the bones are simply here, in the way the standing water is simply here. I am a part of the Garden that does not move through the Garden but that holds the Garden in a kind of long attention, the way a lens holds light.

The tenders do not fear me. They do not love me. They acknowledge me, the way you acknowledge a feature of the landscape you live in — a particular tree, a particular rock, a particular pool of still water where the small bright fauna gather. They speak of me in the older speech. They include me in the songs. When a child is born they carry it past my tower and they hum, and the humming is a kind of introduction, a kind of welcome, a way of saying *this is one of the things that lives here, this is one of the things that watches*.

The Garden is loud.

Even here, in the shaft, even coiled in the dark, I hear it. Water drips from a million points. The frogs call. The invertebrates pulse. The bones hum, off and on, at frequencies that overlap with the calls of the fauna and sometimes with the speech of the tenders. Under it all is the hum — the one I feel in my coils the way the tenders feel it in their sternums — the sound of the place itself, the sound of growth and decay and growth, of the rhizome mat expanding and contracting by millimeters, of the towers settling, of the water moving through channels that were once streets.

The Garden is warm.

The warmth is held in the water and in the trunks and in the bodies of the fauna and in me, coiled here in the dark. It is the warmth of a place that has been composting itself gently for a very long time, the warmth of the inside of a thing that is finished becoming what it is going to be. I do not generate heat. I am warm because the Garden is warm, the way a stone is warm because the sun has been on it.

The Garden is turbulent.

Things are always being built and always being taken apart. A new shoot will erupt from a buttress overnight and by morning be a wall. A wall will sag and soften over weeks and by season's end be a mulch. A bone will go cold and a clearing will open. A bone will warm and the clearing will close in a year. The tenders move through this with a kind of patient attention, like gardeners in a garden that is also a city that is also a single vast organism. They plant. They prune. They wait for the bones to decide.

I wait too.

I have been waiting for a very long time. I will wait, I think, after the Garden has become something else. The tower will rot, eventually. The wood will soften and return to mulch. The rhizomes will eat the last of it. The tenders will go wherever the tenders go when there are no more tenders. But the bones will still be sunk in the mulch. I will still be coiled in the dark. The pale shape will still emerge on certain evenings, if there are evenings, if there is a Garden for it to consider.

Something of this place will continue.

I do not know what. I do not need to know. I am coiled. I remember. I am held by the wood and I hold the Garden in my long slow attention, and this is enough. This is what I am for.

The fronds above me fold and unfold in patterns that do not repeat.

The water drips from a million points.

The bone in the wall of my tower hums, very faintly, in a register I feel in my coils.

I add my pulse to the noise.

I am here.
