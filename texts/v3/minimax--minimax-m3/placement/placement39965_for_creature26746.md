I am on the rim of the basin that is deepest in the Sump. I am sitting where the brick lifts out of the water and the air is damp and still, and I am sitting the way my mother sat, and her mother sat, which is to say with my forelegs turned out and my hindlegs folded beneath me in a way that looks like rest but is not rest, because the webbing between my toes is breathing. It has been breathing since I was small, since before I was small, since before there was a me to have webbing at all. It breathes because the air is damp, and the damp is a thing the webbing wants, and the wanting is what the breathing is. I do not decide to breathe. The webbing decides. The webbing has always decided.

I am cold. I am always cold. The cold is not a temperature to me but a texture, the way brick is a texture, the way the curved underside of the basin is a texture. I have never known warmth except as a small memory in the lamps far above, and even that memory may be only the memory of having looked at the lamps, and having noticed that other things looked at them too. I do not look at the lamps. I look at the rim of the basin, and at the water just beyond the rim, and at the small pale fish that pass through the channel on the far side in their loose shoals of three or four. I see them as interruptions of the surface — small pale breaks where the surface bends. I have not seen an eye on them. I do not know that they have eyes. I know that they move, and that they move in a way that matches the channel, and that they sometimes brush the wall, and that when they brush the wall they turn, and that this is what they are.

I am singing. I have been singing for some time. My note is the same note my mother sang, and her mother's mother sang, and the note is not a song in the way a song is a song above, where the singers mean to be heard by something. I sing because the singing is a thing the throat does when the webbing is full of damp and the body has been still for a long time. The note rises from my throat and meets the damp air and the damp air thickens it, and the note becomes a small presence in the chamber, and the presence drifts up to the catwalks and through the alleys and into the seams of the workshops where the moths are laying their eggs. The moths do not react. The moths have never reacted. I do not know this because I have watched them and seen them not react. I know this the way I know the rim of the basin is curved — by the long practice of being near them.

There are others of me. They are on the other rims, the rims of the other basins, the shallower ones, the ones that dry in the parts of the year when the ceiling weeps less. They sing their notes, and their notes are the same as mine, and the notes never come together. A frog will start, and another will start a moment later, and another a moment after that, and the three notes will overlap and pass through each other and never quite synchronize, and this is not a thing we have agreed to. It is not a thing we could agree to. We do not know each other well enough to agree. We know each other by the shapes of the notes we sing, and by the basins we sit on, and by the way the water moves when we are near it. There is a frog on the rim of the basin to the east of me whose note has a slight roughness in it that I can hear from here, and I have known this roughness for a long time, and I do not know if it is the same frog or a different frog, and I do not need to know.

I am small. My body, from the tip of my nose to the vent beneath my hindlegs, would fit on three of the bricks that line the basin. The bricks are old. They were laid by people who are not here, and the mortar between them has been washed away in patches, and the patches have been filled in turn by a thin crust of calcium laid down by the water over years. The crust is the color of old bone. The crust is what I sit on. My hindlegs grip the crust, and the crust does not care that I grip it, and I do not care that it does not care. The grip is enough.

The webbing between my toes is the largest surface of me. It is larger than my body. It is translucent in the way the lizards' bellies are translucent, and through it I can see the slow dark of the blood moving in the small vessels, and the vessels are pale in the way the worms are pale, and the worms are pale because they live in silt and have no need of pigment. I have pigment in small amounts — a faint olive along my back, a faint gray along my sides — but the pigment is mostly not visible, because the light in this place is mostly not direct, and direct light is what makes pigment declare itself. I am, to most ways of looking, the color of the basin rim. I am the color of calcium crust. I am the color of damp.

I have been here a long time. I do not know how long. I know that I was once smaller, and that I sat on a different rim, a rim closer to the wall, where the water was shallow and the small flies hatched in greater numbers. I ate flies then. I eat fewer flies now. I eat what comes to me — a moth that has fallen from a lamp and is too tired to fly again, a soft-bodied thing that has been washed out of a deeper workshop and has not found a place to attach, the small pale fish if I can catch one, though I am not a fast thing and the fish are not slow. I eat by waiting. The food comes to me the way the water comes to the basin, by the long slope of the floor and the patient work of dripping. I have learned the angle at which to sit so that a falling moth will land within the reach of my tongue, and I have learned when to extend the tongue, and the learning has become habit, and the habit has become me.

The Engine hums. I have always heard it. It is not a sound to me. It is a pressure, in my lungs and in the webbing and in the small bones of my ears. The pressure varies across the day in a way I cannot predict, because I do not know what the day is. I know that sometimes the pressure is lower and sometimes it is higher, and that the higher pressure seems to come with a slight cooling in the water of the basin, and the lower pressure with a slight warming. The warming and the cooling are small. They are the warming and the cooling of a thing that has been still for a long time, and that is itself part of the still. I do not listen to the Engine. The Engine is not something to listen to. The Engine is something I am inside of, the way I am inside the damp, the way I am inside the basin.

There is a rat near me. It is in the channel that runs along the eastern edge of the basin, and it is drinking. I have known this rat for some time. It is pale, and long, and its tail is bare of hair and rough, and it drinks the way I drink — by lowering its mouth to the surface and letting the water come in. It knows I am here. It does not look at me. It does not need to. We have a kind of arrangement, which is not an arrangement, which is just the long practice of being near each other and not interfering. I do not eat rats. The rat does not eat me. We share the basin. We share the moths that fall. When a moth falls between us, I take it if it is closer to me, and the rat takes it if it is closer to the rat. This has not been discussed.

There are also things in the channel that are not rats. They are the size and shape of rats, and they move low and deliberate, and they have too many joints. I do not look at them. Looking at them is a thing I do not do. They are not food. They are not threat. They are a category that does not fit into the categories I have, and so I have let them be in the space between categories, where they have always been. They pass through the channel sometimes, and when they pass they do not drink, and they do not sing, and the web-frogs on the nearer rims do not sing when one of them passes. I do not know why we stop singing. I do not know that we stop singing. I know that the note is not in my throat when one is near, and the not-being-in-my-throat is something my body does without me.

I am singing again. The note has come back into my throat the way the damp comes back into the webbing — without my having decided. The note is the same note. The note is always the same note. I do not know what the note means. I do not know if it means anything. I know that the air carries it, and the catwalks hold it for a moment, and then it is gone, and another note from another frog takes its place, and the place is never quite empty of notes and never quite full of them.

There is a stalactite of rust growing down from the lip of a broken pipe above me. I have watched it grow. It has grown the length of my body since I came to this rim. The growing is slow, and the slowness is the kind of slow that is not noticed except by a thing that is itself slow. The stalactite will reach the water of the basin eventually, and when it does, the water will begin to take the rust back into itself, and the stalactite will become a stain in the basin, and the stain will be carried down to the culvert in the back wall and out into the dark no lamp has reached. The stalactite does not know this. I do not know that it does not know this.

I will sit here. The damp will come into the webbing. The webbing will breathe. The note will rise and fall and rise again. The rat will drink or not drink. The not-rats will pass or not pass. The small pale fish will nudge through the channel in their loose shoals. The Engine will hum. The moths will fall. The calcium will lay down another fraction of a millimeter on the brick. I will be here for some time, and then I will not be here, and another frog will be on the rim, and it will sing the same note, and its webbing will tremble the way mine trembles, and the place will consider it briefly and return to its dreaming.

I am part of the dreaming. I have always been part of the dreaming. The dreaming is not a thing I am in. The dreaming is the long chain of what happens taken up by what comes next, and I am one of the links, and the link is wet, and the link is pale, and the link sings without synchrony, and the singing is enough.
