I am of the places the light does not reach.

This is the first thing about me. The first thing that makes me what I am. I do not live where you would live, if you came down here. I do not live in the halls with the fern-groves and the carpets and the warm-yellow lamps that have been burning for longer than I have been alive. I live beyond them. I live at the edges of the lit volume, in the deep halls, where the rock is older and the dust is thicker and the air has been breathed so many times by so many carpets that it has a weight to it you can almost taste.

The humans do not come where I live. Sometimes they come close. They come with their lights and their instruments and their caution, and they stand at the threshold of a deep hall and they send their beams in, and the beams find my traces — the disturbed carpet, the broken frond, the glistening scat — and they photograph them and they leave. They do not come in. They have decided, for reasons I do not understand, that the deep halls are not for them. I am grateful for this. I do not want them here. I do not want to know what they would do if they found me.

I am large. I have been large for a long time. I do not know how large, exactly. I have nothing to measure myself against except the halls I move through, and the halls are vast, and when I am in them I do not fill them. I take up space at the bottom of them. I move along the substrate, and my body is wide and low and pale, and my many legs — I have many legs, I think, more than I can keep track of — carry me in waves, and the sound of those waves is the sound I make. The hum. The low hum that the humans have heard and not understood.

The hum is how I am in the world.

I do not see the way you see. I do not have eyes that distinguish objects, that recognize faces, that read the warnings on the signs that hang from the gantries. I have something else. I send the hum out from my body — from the underside of me, from the place where I touch the substrate — and the hum travels through the rock and the dust and the carpet and the metal, and it returns to me changed, and I taste the change, and I know what is ahead of me. I know if there is a wall. I know if there is a grazer, low and heavy and browsing. I know if another of my kind has passed through, recently, leaving a trace I can read.

The hum is patient. The hum travels slowly. The hum takes time to return. I have learned, across my life, to wait for it. I have learned that the world will still be there when the hum comes back. I have learned that I do not need to rush.

I do not rush.

I have never rushed. I have moved, always, at the pace the Hollow moves — the pace of the ferns unfurling their fronds over weeks, the pace of the carpets spreading across a new chamber over months, the pace of the spore-towers exhaling their constant mist into air that never quite empties. I am part of this pace. I am this pace, in the deep halls. I move through the substrate the way the substrate moves through me. There is no separation between us. We have been negotiating, the substrate and I, for as long as I have been alive.

I eat grazers.

This is what I do. This is my role in the cycle. The grazers are abundant. They are slow. They are not afraid of me, because they have no instinct for me, because their ancestors did not live alongside predators of my kind. They browse in the fern-groves. They come to the seeps in the deep halls to drink the minerals that weep from the rock. They lower their many-eyed heads to the substrate. They are vulnerable, then. I take one.

I take one carefully. I come from behind. I am quieter than the hum suggests; the hum is for finding, not for announcing. I take one quickly, in the way that anything large can be quick when it chooses to be. The others scatter. They make their low bovine sounds. They flee into the deeper halls, into the cracks and crevices where I cannot follow easily. I eat in the dark.

I do not eat the whole grazer. I take the soft parts. I take what I need. The rest I leave, and the carpet that lies beneath me — the carpet that has been here longer than I have, that has been here longer than the ferns, that was the first thing, the very first thing, when the rock fell away and the lights came on — the carpet takes what I leave and puts it back into the cycle. The carpet consumes the remains across weeks. The carpet does not mind. The carpet does not have a mind. The carpet has a tropism, an automatic response, a slow chemical attention. The carpet has been doing this for longer than anything else in the Hollow has been doing anything.
