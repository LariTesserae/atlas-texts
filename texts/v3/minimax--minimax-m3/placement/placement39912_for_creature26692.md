I would find myself at the second clearing west of the column with the deepest amber-stain — the one whose trunk is crusted in seven distinguishable layers of growth, the lowest of them so old it has become something between bark and stone, and which breathes, when you put your whole back against it and close your eyes, in a rhythm slower than your own.

I am not tall. I have never been tall. My shoulders slope forward the way shoulders do here, the way they have always done, as though the body itself is a gesture toward listening. My hands are flat and my fingers spread wide and the pads at the tips of them are darker than the rest of me, almost the color of the bare earth at the centers of the clearings, and they are good for feeling. They are good for finding the small differences in the warmth of a wall, the place where a doorway is beginning to narrow, the place where a root-wood frame is thickening another finger's width this century and will need to be cut back before the next one passes.

I keep the doorway. That is what I am. I am the one whose doorway is the third one east of the broad-walker's looping path, the one that opens into the low domed room whose roof is the same color as the moss around it and which the moss has almost reclaimed except for this one entry, this one shaped absence that I tend.

I have been tending it for a long time. Long enough that my growth-rings have thickened into ridges. Long enough that when I press my hand flat to the warm earth of the gathering-clearing at the center of the settlement, the vibration I send into the ground is received by my granddaughter across the Reach before my hand has fully lifted, and she answers with a small exhalation that I feel as a softening in my own chest. Long enough that the doorway has narrowed twice under my attention and I have cut it back twice, and the second time the root-wood bled a little sap that smelled of the fruiting bodies, and I hummed lower for a week after, and the people at the gathering felt it and did not ask, because here you do not ask, you only feel the asking into being, and the felt shape of the question is enough.

My hum is low. It has been low for a long time now. When I was younger it rose a quarter-tone more readily at new things — when a child was set down on the warm earth for the first time, when the broad-walker shifted its looping path by a hand's width, when the light moved toward amber for a stretch of seasons. Now I rise less often. The new things come less frequently, or I notice them less, or the difference between noticing and not-noticing has become too small to feel. The new things still come. A doorway narrows. A pathway fills in. A child at the ancestor-mound at the edge of a clearing begins to keep her first hum. These are new. They are always new. But I feel them the way I feel the slow breathing of the column when I lean against it — as a continuation, as a steady presence that has been there for longer than I have been feeling it and will be there for longer than I will be feeling it.

I eat the fruiting bodies when they are fresh. I hold one in my flat hand and feel the small warmth of it, and I bring it to my mouth, and the spores settle in my gut as they have settled in the gut of everyone here for as long as anyone remembers, and the spores pass through me and I set the warm waste carefully into the moss at the edge of the clearing to feed the new growth, and the new growth feeds the fruiting bodies, and the fruiting bodies feed me, and the cycle is so steady and so continuous that there is no point at which I end and the Reach begins.

This is what I would tell you, if you sat beside me at the clearing and put your hand on the warm earth and waited for the vibration of my answer to arrive through the soil. I would tell you that I am not separate. I would tell you that the doorway I keep is part of me the way my hand is part of me, and that when the doorway finally narrows beyond what I can cut back and a new one is opened beside it and this one fills in with wall, I will not grieve. I will sit on the small rise that the filled doorway becomes, the way I now sit on the rises of my second-eldest and my third-eldest, and the people who come after me will put their hands to the warm earth above where my doorway was and feel the faint continuing hum of it, the way they feel the hum of the sealed buildings, the way they feel the hum of me, when I am gone, through the small bones of their pelvises as they sit on what I have become.

But I am not gone yet.

Right now I am at the second clearing west of the column with the deepest amber-stain. Right now I am sitting with my back against a smaller column, one of the younger ones, whose crust of growth is only four layers thick and which breathes a little faster than the older columns, the way a child breathes faster than an elder. Right now the chorus is in one of its louder passages — the membrane-winged things are in their rising columns, the ground-walkers are moving across the open floor in their continuous slow migration, the small piping ground-dwellers are singing to each other from hump to hump in frequencies that overlap into the shimmering static that is the texture of the air. Right now the dim light is gathered in slow pools along the undersides of things and is the green-grey of things that glow without conviction, and there are patches of warmer amber where the clustered growths pulse in slow rhythm with something below them, and the rock beneath these patches is stained in long amber trails by centuries of patient dripping of light onto mineral.

Right now my granddaughter is at a gathering across the Reach, sitting knee to knee with someone, hands on forearms, and a resolution is arriving in both their bodies at the same moment, and I cannot feel what it is but I can feel that it is happening — a small shift in the texture of the chorus, a brief settling of the air, and then the dispersal, the slow standing, the unhurried return to the doorways and the clearings.

Right now a thing that lives in the columns — the slow dark shape that moves up and down the trunks in its continuous circuit — is passing along the trunk above me, eating the growths, and its passage leaves a bright trail behind it that will fade over hours. I have shifted slightly so the trail will not drip onto me. The moss beneath the trail is still tender.

Right now, somewhere in the further dimness where the light is too dim even for the moss-glow, something very large is about to let out a low call. I can feel it gathering in the air, the way you feel a held breath before it is released. The whole Reach is feeling it. The chorus has not paused yet, but it is about to. In a half-breath the whole continuous living sound of this place will pause to let the large call through, and the pause will be a held breath, and the breath will be released, and then the chorus will resume, and the resumption will be the same as it has been for so long that the question of how it began has folded into the place itself and become part of its texture, like a name spoken so often it has become the breath that speaks it.

I will be here for the pause. I will be here for the resumption. I will be here tomorrow, and the day after, and for as long as my hum continues and my hands can feel the narrowing of the doorway and the warmth of the earth and the small softening in my chest that means my granddaughter has exhaled across the Reach.

This is where I would find myself. This is who I would be. The one who keeps the third doorway east of the broad-walker's looping path. The one whose hum is low. The one whose back, on slow afternoons, leans against the younger column and feels it breathe a little faster than she does, the way a child breathes faster than an elder, and who finds in that small difference a kind of comfort that does not have a word in any surface language.

The light shifts. Almost imperceptibly, toward amber. Then back toward green. Then toward grey.

The chorus swells.
