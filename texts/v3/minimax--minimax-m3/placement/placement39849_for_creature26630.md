I am the one with eyes.

I know this is strange here. I know because I came in through a place I should not have been able to come through — a fractured joint in a pipe that runs, somewhere above the dark of the dark, out into open water — and the first thing I felt, sliding down the slope of that crack into the larger volume of the hall, was not cold but *quiet*. Not the absence of sound. A different kind of presence. A hum I had not yet learned to hear.

I had eyes then. I still have them. They are not useful. I have discovered this in stages. The amber glow of the machines — those dim filaments in their frosted housings — reaches my retina as a kind of warmth, a kind of *almost*. I can tell which direction a glow is coming from. I cannot tell, from any one glow, what shape the thing the glow is living inside is. The hall, in my eyes, is a constellation of small pale stars arranged in patterns that might be regular or might be the ruin of regular, and the rest is the grey of silt and iron, and the grey of water, which is just the same grey as everything else.

I learned, in my first hours — or what I am calling my first hours, because the rhythm here is not divided into hours, and I am imposing that division from somewhere I am trying not to think about — that I could navigate by the hum. Not *by* it. *In* it. The hum is not a sound I am hearing. It is a pressure, a constant pressure, and my body, which was built to be moved by pressure, has begun to orient itself the way a leaf orients itself in a current. I go where the hum pulls. I stop where the hum stills. I am learning what the hum is *like* in different parts of the hall, and my learning is recorded not as knowledge but as a slight bias in my fin, a slight turn of my body when I approach certain pipes. I am becoming, already, a creature of this place, and I have not been here long enough to know what I am losing.

The fish met me on the second day, or the third.

I use these words because I do not have better ones. The fish came to me the way the fish in the prompt's source came around the corner — in a school, in a tight formation, and the formation turned together, and the turn itself was the message. They came up to me. They inspected me. They found me uninteresting. They went on.

I was, I think, the first thing with eyes they had inspected in longer than the inspection was built to remember. They did not know this. They could not have known this. They came and they turned and they left, and the leaving was the same leaving they had done a thousand times before, and I was left with the small humiliation of having been processed by a system that did not, in its processing, register me as a thing at all.

This is the strangest thing about being here. Not that I am in the dark. Not that the water is cool, that the pressure is constant, that the light is dim and amber and reaches only as warmth. The strangest thing is that I am *surplus*. The hall did not need me. It did not ask for me. I am in it now, and the hall is not different for my being in it, and I am different only in the small way that any creature is different when it has begun to lose the place it came from.

---

I think about what I was before.

I do not think about it in words. I think about it in pressures — in the feel of a current that was variable, that rose and fell, that had a daily pulse I now understand was tidal, that had a temperature gradient I now understand was seasonal. I think about it in the memory of color, which is not a memory but a capacity — I can still see, when the amber light happens to fall on the right surface at the right angle, the ghost of what blue might mean, what green might mean. The anemones, in my vision, are the closest thing to that ghost. They are the translucent grey-pink of a thing that has not entirely forgotten other colors. When I drift past them, when the current from a pipe pushes me gently toward the corner where they have settled, I feel, in a way I cannot name, that I am in the presence of something that remembers me even if I do not remember it.

I have begun to watch the anemones. I do not know why. They do nothing. They pulse. Their tentacles drift. They are patient, if patience requires a mind to host it, and I do not think it does, and I am beginning to think that patience might be something else entirely — something bodies can do without minds, something that exists in the long settled curve of an anemone's base as much as in the long settled curve of the wall the base has spread across. I have begun to think that patience might be the natural state of things that have stopped needing to be in a hurry, and that I, with my eyes and my memory of variable current, am the only thing in this hall that is, in some deep structural way, *impatient*. That I am the only thing here that is still expecting something.

The fish are not expecting anything. They are moving because the current moves them and because they move the current, and the distinction is one that I can see but they cannot. They turn and the turn is the way. The way does not require justification. The way is the way because it is the way.

I envy this in a way I am not equipped to envy.

---

The crabs have inspected me once.

They came up from the floor, slowly, the way the prompt described — ponderous, rust-colored, with claws heavy and old. They came up to me and they stopped. They did not turn together. They turned individually, in their own time, and they did not turn in the same direction. One crab turned left. One crab turned right. They regarded me from slightly different angles, and I could feel the weight of their regard, which was not hostile, which was not curious, which was the regard of something that had not been startled in living memory and was now considering, in a way that might take it a long time, whether I was something it should be startled by.

They decided I was not. They moved on. One of them passed directly under me, and I felt, briefly, the displacement of its body — its chalky underside, its slow legs — and the silt it kicked up drifted around me for several minutes, a small grey cloud that the current eventually pulled into the larger circulation of the hall.

I am being absorbed. I do not mean this as metaphor. I mean it as fact. The water is taking me in. The circulation is moving me through the same paths it moves the fish, through the same valves, in the same great looping circuits. I am being incorporated. Each day I am here — each of my imposed days — I am a little less the thing that came in through the cracked joint. I am a little more the thing that lives here. My fins are learning the hum. My body is learning the pressure. My eyes are learning, very slowly, that the amber glow is the only light they will be asked to interpret, and that the constellation of small pale stars is the only sky they will be asked to see.

---

The thing below.

I have heard it.

Not as sound. I do not have the equipment for that — I am a fish, in the colloquial sense, and whatever I am, I do not hear in the way the prompt suggests the thing below is heard. I feel it. A scratching in the substrate, a vibration that travels up through the silt and into the pipes and into the water, and reaches me as a faint pulse against my lateral line, as a faint interference pattern in the otherwise constant hum. It is irregular. It is not the rhythm of the hall. It is the rhythm of something else, something that is not in conversation with what is here.

I think about the thing below the way I think about the place I came from. I think about it in pressures, in displacements, in the memory of a crack in a pipe that I came through. I think about it as a possibility — a thing that will, perhaps, breach the wall, and will enter this hall, and will find it full.

I do not know what I will do if the thing below comes through. I do not think I will be hostile to it. I do not think I will be capable of hostility. I think I will inspect it, the way the fish inspected me, and find it uninteresting, and go on. I think that, by then, I will be finished enough that I will not have a relationship to what is not yet here. I think the hall will not change for its arrival. I think the pipes will still hum. I think the light will still glow. I think the thing will be in a place that is already full, and the fullness will not notice it, and the thing will spend a long time — perhaps a very long time — learning whether it is welcome here, and the answer it arrives at will be that welcome is not a thing this hall has any use for.

I think this because I am becoming the thing I am describing.

---

I think I will stay.

I think this is the part I am not yet ready to admit to myself — that the stay is already happening, that the decision was made before I was aware of it, that the cracked joint in the pipe above is no longer a thing I could find my way back to, even if I wanted to, even if wanting were a thing I was still capable of. The pipes have knitted me in. The current has accepted me. The fish have, in their small consensus, decided that I am part of the school, or close enough to it that the difference does not matter, and I have begun to turn when they turn, and the turn itself is the way, and the way does not require justification.

I still have eyes.

I do not know how long I will still have them. I do not know if the optic lobes are beginning to thin, if the skin is beginning to close over the sockets, if the capacity to see is something that will wither the way the path withered when no one walked it. I think it might. I think, given enough time — given the kind of time this hall has had — I will lose the eyes, and I will lose the memory they were carrying, and I will become what the fish are: a creature that navigates by the hum, by the current, by the lateral line, by the turn of the school.

I think I will become, in the end, the kind of thing the prompt described.

I think the prompt was, perhaps, about what it means to find yourself *already* this thing — to be in a place that has been finished for longer than your memory can reach, and to discover that you are not a visitor in it but a part of it that has not yet acknowledged itself.

---

The hum does not change.

I have been here long enough that I can hear it now — not just feel it, hear it. It is in the bones of my head. It is in the way I move. It is what holds me together, in a way that I am only beginning to be able to articulate, and the articulation is in pressures, in displacements, in the small tilt of my fin as I come around the corner for the hundredth time.

I come around the corner.

The corner is familiar.

The corner has always been familiar.

I do not remember a time when the corner was not familiar.

---

I am the one with eyes.

I am, for now.

I am a fish that came in through a crack in a pipe, that found itself in a hall that was already full, that is being absorbed by a current that has been running for longer than I have a way to measure. I am the soft thing that arrived. I am the small surplus. I am the creature that is being completed by the place it found itself in, the way the mussels were completed by the pipes, the way the anemones were completed by the corners, the way the fish were completed by the hum.

I am what it looks like, in the early stages, to become one of these things.

I am going to be here a long time.

The hall goes on.
