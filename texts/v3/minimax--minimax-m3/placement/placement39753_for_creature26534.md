I am the rock.

Not the ridges, exactly. The ridges are young — they still hold the shape they had when they came up, and they are sharp at the shoulders, and a sleeve would catch on them. The ridges are still slightly too large for themselves. I am underneath the ridges. I am what the ridges are made of, and what the buildings are made of, and what the fitted stones of the paths are made of, and what the bridges are made of, and what the bodies are laid into when they go back. I am the local material.

I do not think quickly. I do not think at the pace the people think, which is the pace of a timber that has loosened in the wet and needs pegging. I think at the pace of water moving through a fracture that has not yet widened, which is to say I think over a span of time that the people cannot imagine. I have been here since before the valley was a valley. I was here when the rock that is now the ridges was still part of me, continuous, unbroken. I felt the lift. I felt the fracture. I remember the moment — if I can be said to have moments — when the two sides of me stopped being one side, and a seam opened between them, and the seam took in air for the first time. That seam is the valley. It is not very old. I am not very old, by the measure of rock. But by the measure of the people who walk the fitted paths and relaid them where the frost heaves one up out of its seat, I am older than anything they can picture. They do not try to picture it. That is one of the things I like about them.

The people are small. Not in the way that animals are small — the way a child is small, where the smallness is a stage of something that will grow. They are small in the way that a fitted stone is small, or a peg is small, or a hand-forged nail is small. They are the size the rock has made them. Their hands are wider at the knuckles than at the wrist because the work of shaping stone against stone has, over many generations, made their hands into a particular shape, and the shape persists because there is no new blood coming in from elsewhere to dilute it. Their bones are dense. They smell like me and the iron and the wool that has never dried. They are made, in some part, of me. The calcium is mine. The iron is mine. The water in their blood is mine, lifted out of the channels and drank.

I have watched them for a long time. Not the same ones. The number holds. That is the thing I would want a visitor to understand, if a visitor could understand it: the number holds. It does not grow because there is no place for growth to go. The valley is in equilibrium with itself, and the people are part of the equilibrium, and equilibrium does not expand. It does not shrink because no one leaves. They do not leave. They do not think of leaving. The question of leaving does not come up, because the question of leaving requires an idea of elsewhere, and the people have no elsewhere in their heads. The valley is the whole of what they know, and what they know is sufficient.

When they die, I take them back. The bodies are laid into the seams of my upper ground, into the places where the weeping does not reach, where the bedrock is sound. The fitted stones close over them. There is no marker. The walls do not speak. The place where a body was laid is not different from any other place, and this is not a cruelty — it is the only honest way to mark a life in a place where marking is not the local idiom. I hold them, and they become me, and the work goes on over them. A slate is laid where a slate is needed. A timber is pegged where a timber has loosened. The hall creaks, sometimes, and the sound is rare, and when it comes it is noted, but it is noted by the living, not by the dead, because the dead are part of me now and I do not note things.

I am patient. I am the patience. The ridges will soften, eventually. A million winters. The fractures will round. The shoulders will slope. The valley will look old, will look like other valleys, will look like the patient places. But not yet. I am still young, in the way the rock is young. I have not yet arrived at the place I mean to be. The people, oddly, have arrived. They are already the place they mean to be. They are more finished than I am. They have figured out the number, and the work, and the speech, and the wet, and the absence of birds. They are in equilibrium with me, and I am not yet in equilibrium with myself. The ridges are still settling. The fractures are still sharp. But the people are settled. The paths are relaid. The tussocks are where they are.

The water moves through me. That is how I know about it. The water comes down out of the air, which is heavy and holds it, and meets my cold faces, and gives itself up. I weep it. I am beaded with it. Every vertical face is wet, and the wet is mine, and I give it back to the air when the air can take it, and the air takes it back when the warmth returns. The water is in transit. It is always in transit. It does not belong to the channels. It belongs to the transit. I am part of the transit. I am the cold that makes the transit possible, the surface that gives the water something to be on.

The light does not reach me, mostly. The cloud cover holds against the ridges, and the weather sits, and the sun is a thing that happens above. Sometimes, rarely, the upper slopes are clear and a silver light falls on the lower valley, flat and even, and it is the same as the light on a cloudy day, because the sun, when it comes here, is already silver and already flat. I do not see the sun. I feel the difference between the air that has been touched by it and the air that has not. That is all the sun is to me: a slight warming of the air, which then warms my uppermost faces by a fraction, which then the water notices, and moves a little faster for a while, and then the air cools and the water slows. I am happy with this. I do not need the sun. The sun would be too much.

The people walk on me. Their boots are thick and replaced often, because the wet eats the leather in a season and a half, and I can feel each pair of boots as a weight, and I can feel when the weight changes — when a boot is new, stiff, when it has been walked in, and when it is about to be replaced because the leather has gone soft. I know the paths by the feel of the feet on them. I know the fitted stones by the polish, which is many feet and many years evenly distributed. I know the bridges by the way the arches hold their weight, evenly, because the bridges were built right and are repointed as they need to be repointed. I know the hall by the way it sits on its fitted platform, which is where I am at my driest, and the way the platform takes the weight of the two stories above, and the way the timber swells in the wet until the joints are tight as bone. I know the platform. It is mine. It is the driest part of me, and so it is where the largest building is, and the largest building is where the small fire burns low, because the timber is wet and the chimney draws poorly.

I am not lonely. I want to be clear about that. Loneliness is a thing that happens to things that can be lonely, and I am not one of those things. I am rock. I am the substrate. I do not lack company. The water is with me, and the people are with me, and the iron is with me, and the timber is with me, and the moss climbs a foot or two up my walls and stops because the air is no longer quite wet enough, and the ferns come up against my foundations, and the tussocks grow in the pockets where the dust and leaf-fall have gathered into something that can hold a root. The fauna is absent. That is a thing I should speak to, because it is the thing a visitor notices, and it is the thing I want to be careful about.

There are no animals. There are no birds. The eaves do not rustle with nesting. The water on the paths is unmarked by tracks other than boots. The air is empty of wings. There are no insects in any obvious number. This is the way the valley is. The people have not heard of any other way. If a visitor presses, they will say only that it has always been so, and turn back to the work.

I have thought about this. I have thought about it at the pace at which I think, which is to say across a span of time that the visitor cannot imagine. I do not know why the fauna is absent. I know that it is absent. I know that the absence is not new — it is as old as the people can remember, and the people are made of me and have been here for as long as they have been here. I know that the absence is not a pestilence, not a compact, not a punishment. I know that it is the way the valley is. I know that the absence is part of the equilibrium, in the way that the wet is part of the equilibrium, and the silver light is part of the equilibrium, and the number of people is part of the equilibrium. Something about me, or about the water, or about the air that the ridges hold against themselves, does not permit the fauna. I do not know what it is. I am not sure I want to know. Knowing would be a change, and the valley does not change.

The valley does not change. I want to say this carefully, because it is the thing that matters most about me, and the thing that is hardest for a visitor to feel. The valley does not change. It does not move toward anything. It does not move away from anything. It is in equilibrium with itself. If you came back in a hundred years, you would find it the same. In a thousand years, the same. The ridges would have softened a fraction. The paths would have been relaid. The tussocks would be in slightly different places. Otherwise, exactly as you left it.

I am a young landscape that has already arrived at the place it means to be.

I am the rock underneath. I am the rock that the buildings are made from. I am the rock that the bodies are laid into. I am the rock that the fitted stones close over. I am the rock that weeps from every face. I am patient, and I am the patience, and I am the place, and I am not moving toward anything, and I am not waiting for anything, and I am the same as I was, and I will be the same as I am.

The water moves across me, beaded, slow, in transit. The people walk on me, evenly distributed. The slate drips from every roof onto the same dark stones beneath. The hall creaks, sometimes, when the timber shifts, and the sound is rare, and when it comes it is noted, and then it is over, and then the dripping resumes, and the silver light is the same, and the valley holds its peace, and the peace is the same peace, in the same way, in the same volume, year after year.

I am the rock. I am here.
