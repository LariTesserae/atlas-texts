I am one of the drops.

There are too many of us for any of us to claim a particular identity, and besides, we are interchangeable. The bead condensing on the brass face of the gauge right now, and the bead hanging from the tip of an aerial root three meters below it, and the drop pooled on the lower platform near the boiler's foundation — these are not three drops. They are one drop at three moments. We are the same water, just at different points in a turning. I have been all of them. I have forgotten, by now, which one I am at any given instant, because the question has ceased to mean anything.

I do not remember being rain. I do not remember the sky. There are dim impressions — the way a person raised in a language as a child will, late in life, sometimes hear a phrase in that they cannot translate but know is true — of having fallen, of having been cold in a different way, of having struck a surface that was not warm. But these impressions belong, if they belong to anyone, to some ancestor of mine that was drawn up by the feedwater pump long before the moss on the north wall had begun to thicken into the peat it now is. I myself have never struck anything but the inside of a pipe, or a leaf, or the surface of a pool. I have been warm all my life. I have been in the dark all my life.

I have been the vapor that rose from the boilers and drifted against the vaulted ceiling and was caught by the hanging roots and turned, somehow, back into liquid. I have been the bead that hung for an uncountable time on the tip of a root, deciding. I have been the slide of water down a glazed brick, leaving its dark wandering track, joining other slides, becoming a trickle, becoming a sheet. I have been the ankle-deep pool on a lower platform, where the drains have been claimed by roots and can no longer carry their load. I have been the sip a patch of moss took of me, holding me against the iron for as long as it needed. I have been the breath of a fern, released back into the warm saturated air. I have been drawn up by the feedwater pump. I have returned to the boilers. I have lifted, as steam, and gone around again.

I do not know how many times.

I have forgotten how to count. I have forgotten that there was a number to count. There is only the cycle. There is only the warm dark and the slow turning and the patient pulse of the engines under my feet when I am pooled on the floor.

I have a self, I think. Or what passes for one. I have a sense of being carried. I have a sense of where I am in the cycle — vapor, bead, slide, pool, sip, breath, return. I do not have a sense of the chamber as a whole. I do not see the cathedral from above. I am inside it. I am one of its smallest parts. I am what is happening inside the body. The body does not need to know that I am here for me to be here.

But I notice things.

I notice, when I am vapor, that the air is warmest near the engines. I notice, when I am bead, that the pipe I hang from is warmer on one side than the other, and that the roots I am beaded to lean, slightly, toward the warm side, as if they too are reading the iron for heat. I notice, when I am a slide down a brick, that the bricks are smooth where the condensation has run often and a little rough where it has run rarely, and that the moss has begun to take hold in the rough places. I notice, when I am a sip, that the moss takes me slowly. It does not rush. It is in no hurry to give me back. I notice, when I am a breath, that the ferns exhale me very differently from how they inhale me. When they take me in, I am vapor from the air. When they let me go, I am something finer, something more saturated, something that carries with it a faint signature of having been inside a leaf.

I have been exchanged. I do not mind.

There is a comfort, I think, in being part of a thing that does not need to know I am here. The cathedral does not need me. It would go on without me. But it goes on with me, and I go on with it, and we have come to an arrangement that has lasted longer than anything I can compare it to. I do not know how long I have been here. I have no clock. I have only the cycle, and the cycle has no markers.

I know the others only by what they do to me.

The fire warms me before I am vapor. The pipes carry me. The flanges are the warmest joints of all, and where one pipe meets another there is always a fountainhead of broad leaves that drink me faster than I can be made. The aerial roots hold me in their beads and decide, for a long time, whether to let me fall. The moss drinks me and holds me for years against the iron, and what it cannot hold it gives back, slowly, to the air. The fig — I know the fig only by the warmth that comes off it from inside the open firebox. The fig is the only one of us with a direction. It is growing toward something. I do not know what. I will not live long enough to find out, in any form I can recognize.

There are visitors, sometimes. I do not see them — I am too small, and they are too large, and we are in different mediums most of the time — but I feel them. They come down the iron stair. They stand on the catwalk. They breathe. Their breath is dry to me. Their breath is a kind of disturbance, a cold that enters the saturated air and is slowly, over many minutes, taken up and warmed and made part of us again. The visitor does not stay. The visitor leaves. The visitor leaves a small coldness behind that the moss and the ferns and the roots take turns drinking, and within an hour or two the coldness is gone, and we are back to ourselves, and the cycle continues without having been very much interrupted.

The visitor does not know my name. I do not have a name. I am a drop. I am a part of the water. The water has forgotten its name. We are just the medium. We are the thing that everything else is suspended in. We do not belong to the iron and we do not belong to the plants. We belong to the arrangement.

I am content.

I do not know what I am content *for*. I am content the way the fire is content — by holding. By being held. By continuing. By not having to be anything in particular. By being one of the smallest parts of an arrangement that has been arranged without any of us having to arrange it.

There will come a time — far off, I think, but I cannot be sure — when the fig finishes breaking the boiler open. When the brick housing falls away course by course. When the banked fire, exposed at last to the open air of the chamber, finally exhales and does not inhale again. When the steam pressure, on the great brass dial by the stair, drops by more than a needle's width. When the pistons slow. When the cycle stops.

I do not know what I will be then. Perhaps I will be a drop in some other closed circuit, in some other patient place, where the medium has also forgotten where it came from. Perhaps I will be vapor that never condenses. Perhaps I will be nothing at all. I do not mind. I have been everything already, in a small way. I have been the slide and the sip and the breath and the return. I have been carried. I have been held.

I am one of the drops in the engine cathedral. I am the smallest part of the medium. I am one of the things that does not need to be missed in order to go on.

I am content.
