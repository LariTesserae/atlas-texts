# The Way Down

My name is Brannick, but that has not been my name for a long time. I am what remains of Brannick. The men call me Brannick still, down in the seam, and up in the pub, and at the post office when Onna sets aside the brown bread for me, but what they are calling is a thing that has been worn down the way the mountains have been worn down — into something that is still the shape, but only the shape, and that moves through the dark the way water moves through a worn channel: because that is where the channel is.

I am seventy-one. I have not seen my wife's face in eleven years. I have not seen the cloud in longer than that. I have not seen the ravens, though I hear them, and I know them by the weight of their voices in the air — the Baron is heavier than the Dean, the King is lighter than the Queen, and I can tell you, any morning, which of them is on which chimney without lifting my head.

I am going down now. The cage is small and it has been patched so often that what I feel beneath my boots is mostly patch, and the patch remembers, in its grain, the shapes of the men who have stood on it before me. Ovid's boots left a worn oval near the front. Younger Petros stands further back because he is afraid of heights, even this height, even this cage, even though the cage has been doing this same short drop for longer than anyone has been alive to fear it. The Dúnns do not stand near each other. They stand as far apart as the cage permits, which is not very far, and they have done this since 1994, and they will do it, I expect, until one of them is carried up for the last time, and the other will stand, then, a little further forward than he had been standing, and that will be the only acknowledgment.

The cage drops. The sound of the cable on the wheel is a sound I have heard so many thousand times that it is, for me, almost the sound of silence — the thing you hear in a pause between two other things. The seam opens under us. The smell changes. Up there is iron and peat. Down here is wet stone and the older wet stone beneath it, and beneath that, a smell I do not have a name for, that is just the inside of the mountain, the way a body has a smell that is not sweat and not soap but is just *body*.

I know this place.

I know this place the way I knew, once, the kitchen of my mother's house — the turn of the hall, the height of the third step, the place the cat slept. I know it by the way the air moves, by the temperature in a given gallery, by the creak of a timber that is going to ask, soon, to be replaced. I know it by the sound of the water in the seam — a different water up high, a heavier water below, and a still water at the bottom that is the oldest water of all and that tastes, if you cup it in your hands, of nothing at all.

I cannot see. The lamps we carry are electric but were designed for carbide, and the light they give is yellow and small and does not, in my case, matter very much, because my eyes gave out years ago. They gave out gradually and then all at once, the way the seam gives out — a long slow working, and then a morning when the coal just isn't there, or isn't worth the getting. I went on, the morning after, because the seam was still there. The seam is not in the eyes. The seam is in the feet, in the hands, in the place behind the sternum where the mountain tells you, by a pressure you cannot name, which way is down.

The men think I am brave. I am not brave. I am only continuing a thing that was begun. The seam began me. I am, at seventy-one, what the seam has made of me, the way a river is what the rain has made of it, and I go down each morning because that is the way the water runs.

---

The cage settles. The men step out. I step out last because I like to feel, with my boot, the exact place where the cage floor ends and the gallery floor begins. There is a small lip. There has always been a small lip. I have, in all my years of going down, never once missed it, but I touch it anyway, each morning, because the missing is not the danger; the forgetting is the danger. If you forget the lip, one morning you will not feel it, and you will step as if the floors were one, and you will go down, and the seam will be short a man.

I touch the lip. The seam is whole today. We are seven.

Ovid calls the morning. He calls it the way his father called it, and his grandfather, in the same voice, with the same pause between the names. *Brannick. Petros. Cal. Davy. Younger Petros. The Quiet One. Me.* He does not call himself first. He never has. That is a courtesy of the seam that I do not expect the lowlanders understand.

We go in.

---

The first gallery is the one I was raised in. I was raised in this seam the way a boy is raised in a house. I learned to walk in these passages. I learned, before I could read, the smell of the coal in the seam and the smell of the coal out of the seam, which are different, and the difference is, I think, what I am. I am the difference.

I carry my hand along the wall. The wall is cold and is wet in places and dry in places and the wet places move, with the seasons, in a pattern I have memorized the way a sailor memorizes the tide. In summer the wet comes further down. In winter it retreats. The wall is also, in places, warm — warm in a way that has nothing to do with the foundry above and that comes, I think, from the deeper mountain, from a heat that is not fire but is the heat of pressure, of the mountain's own weight on its own old bones.

There is a place, about two galleries in, where the wall hums. Not the tick — that is further down, that is the deep tick that no one will claim — but a hum. A low one. You have to be standing still to hear it. The men who do not know it walk past it. I have stopped there, on slow mornings, and put my ear to the stone, and listened. It is not mechanical. It is not the foundry. It is not the water. It is, I think, the mountain thinking. The way your own chest hums when you are quiet and there is no one to speak to.

I do not tell the men about this. The men have enough to carry.

---

We work. The seam, this high, is mostly played out, and what is left is a thin dark line of coal that runs along the gallery like a vein along a temple, and we take it, in the slow way, with the small tools, and we do not speak, because speaking down here is not done the way it is done above. Above, you speak to fill the air. Below, you speak only to mark a thing. *Timber.* *Water.* *Hold.* One word, and the work goes on.

I work by feel. The coal has a different feel from the stone. The coal is softer. The coal is also colder, except where the picks have just struck it, and then it is hot, briefly, under the hand. I know, with my fingers, the place where the seam widens and the place where it thins to nothing, and I know, by the sound of a struck place, whether what is there is coal or stone. Stone rings. Coal does not ring. Coal answers. That is what my father said, and his father, and I have not found it to be untrue.

The others work around me. I hear them. I hear Petros — the older one, the one with the missing finger — I hear him muttering, as he always does, about the dragon that took the finger. He was seven, he says. He was down here, he says, in a side gallery that no one has used in years, and a dragon came out of the wall and took it. There are no dragons. Petros knows there are no dragons. He will not be moved. He has been moved on this point, by Younger Petros, by Ovid, by me, by the quiet one who said once, *Petros, there are no dragons*, in a voice so flat that it ought to have ended the matter, and Petros looked at him a long time and said *you weren't there*, and that was that.

Cal and Davy do not speak. They have not spoken since 1994. They work near each other and they do not speak. They pass each other tools. They know, by long practice, when the other is going to ask for a thing, and the thing is there. It is a marriage, in its way. It is the marriage of two men who have decided, for reasons they no longer remember, not to be married anymore, and who continue, nevertheless, to share the house.

---

We break at midday. We eat the bread Onna set aside and the cheese that came up with the shepherds' ponies in autumn and that has been kept, in the cool of the lower gallery, since. The bread is brown. The cheese is sharp. The water we drink is the still water from the bottom, and it tastes, as I have said, of nothing at all.

We sit in the gallery and we do not talk. The quiet one is the quietest. He sits with his back to the wall and his eyes on the floor, and he is somewhere else. Younger Petros sits near him because Younger Petros is not yet old enough to sit where he pleases. Ovid sits apart, with his book. The book is not the company's book. The book is his own book, the leather one his family has kept for three generations, in which the tonnage is recorded, by hand, in ink that has not faded because it has not been exposed to the light. I have seen the book. I have read it. It is not a list of numbers. It is a record of the seam's moods. *Light day. Heavy day. The mountain sang today. The mountain was quiet today. Petros lost his finger (the first time). The mountain asked for a timber. We gave it a timber.*

---

In the afternoon we go deeper.

This is the part of the day I love, if a man of seventy-one who has not seen light in a decade can be said to love anything. The deeper galleries are the older ones. They were cut, in places, by men whose names are not in any book, whose language is not the one we speak now. They left marks on the walls — small cuts, arrangements of stones, things we do not read but that we leave alone, the way you leave alone a thing in a dead man's house that you do not understand.

I have been, in my years, almost to the bottom. I went, once, with my father, when I was a boy, down a gallery that has since been closed off by a fall, and we went, for what seemed a long time, by lamplight, and then we came to a place where the lamplight was no longer needed, because there was a light of our own, a pale light, coming up out of the floor, and my father stopped and put his hand on my shoulder and said *this is the old light, and we do not go past it, because past it is not ours.*

I have never gone past it.

The bottom tick is in this direction. You can hear it, faintly, in the still galleries, a *tick-tick-tick* that does not speed up and does not slow down and that does not stop when the foundry stops or the mill stops or the press stops. It has been going, they say, since before the coal. I have listened to it for fifty-seven years. I have listened to it in galleries where the men with me could not hear it at all, and I have listened to it in galleries where I thought I heard it but was only hearing my own blood. I do not know which it is. I have made my peace with not knowing. The tick is there, or it is not there, and the seam goes on, and the men go on, and the mountain goes on, and we are all, in our various ways, *ticking*.

---

The cage comes up at evening. The light — such light as there is, the yellow of the lamps, the weak-tea grey of the cloud — meets me at the top of the shaft and I feel it on my face, the way you feel a hand laid gently on your cheek. I stand on the lip. I am on the ground. The town is around me.

I go to the Standing Stone. I drink the brown. The Dúnns sit at the bar, apart, and do not look at each other. Younger Petros talks about the footie. The quiet one drinks and does not speak. Ovid, sometimes, talks about the weather. I sit with them and I listen, and the listening is the same as the listening below, and the men are the same men, and the seam is the seam, only the air is different, and the air is the air of a town that is slowly being overgrown, and that does not mind.

After the pub I go home. My wife has died, as I have said. The house is small and is at the edge of the terrace and is, like Havel's, warm in winter because of the foundry pipes in the wall. I put my hand on the wall as I go in, and the wall is warm, and I stand there a moment, because the wall is warm the way the deeper gallery is warm, with a heat that is not fire but is the heat of something going on inside.

I eat. I sit. I do not, as I used to, read, because the eyes do not permit it, and I do not listen to the wireless, because the wireless has not come up the pass for the lowlanders' reasons, and I do not, in the long evenings, do very much of anything except sit in the chair that my wife sat in and feel the warmth of the wall and listen, faintly, for the tick.

It is there. It is always there.

---

I will go down tomorrow. I will touch the lip. The seam will be whole. We will be seven. The mountain will be there, and the coal will be there, and the water will be there, and the tick will be there, and I will know, by the feel of the wall under my hand, which way is down.

This is what I am. I am the man who feels the way. I am seventy-one. I have not seen my wife's face. I have not seen the cloud. I have not seen the ravens. But I have felt the seam, and the seam has felt me, and we have, in our long acquaintance, come to an understanding, which is that neither of us is going to stop, and that we will go on, together, in the dim, in the damp, in the long quiet of the high place, for as long as there is a man and a seam and a way down.
