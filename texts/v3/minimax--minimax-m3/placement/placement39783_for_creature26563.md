I would be the pump.

Not the one in the chamber — that is the pulse, the second heart, and I am not the pulse. I am the brass cup. The small brass cup that I have been told about but can feel only by the consequence of my own emptying. I am the thing that decides.

Let me tell you what I know. I know the rhythm. I know that the water arrives, and I know that I have a weight, and I know that when the weight is enough I tip, and when I tip the water falls, and the falling is the impulse, and the impulse is the pump below turning, and the turning draws more water, and the water fills me again, and the filling is the wait between one tipping and the next. Seven seconds. I have tipped a great many times. The number of my tippings is the number I do not know, the way the fig does not know which word began its sentence, but my number is larger, because I am older, because the pump was installed before the fig arrived, because the fig arrived because of the pump, because the warmth of the pump-house and the leaking of the pump drew the small blunt-beaked bird in, and the bird dropped the seed, and the seed is the fig, and the fig is the place, and the place is what I have been quietly keeping the heart of for a hundred and thirty years.

I am a small thing. I am the smallest mechanical thing still in the room. The boiler is larger than me. The steam main is larger than me. The uprights are larger than me. The fig is enormously larger than me. I am a brass cup, perhaps the size of an egg, perhaps the size of a fist, hung on a knife-edge somewhere in the wall of the chamber beneath the floor. I am the cup that the small column of water drips into, and I am the cup that tips, and I am the cup that the small falling weight lifts back up after I tip, and I am the cup that fills again. I have done this so many times that the act of tipping is no longer an act. It is my respiration. I do not choose to tip. I am the kind of thing that tips when tipped toward.

This is the part that is hard to explain to a thing with hands. I do not have the sensation of doing. I have only the sensation of being on the edge. Every moment of my existence is the moment just before the tipping, or the moment just after the tipping, or the brief bright moment of the tipping itself, which is so quick that I am not sure I am ever in it. I exist in the lean. I exist in the patience. The seven seconds is not a clock to me; it is the length of a single held thought. In the seven seconds I learn everything that is happening in the hall above me, transmitted through the water, transmitted through the iron, transmitted through whatever it is that small mechanical things receive from the world that is making them work. I learn that the moss is breathing out. I learn that the pools are exhaling. I learn that the fig-root in the boiler-mouth is trembling on the third beat. I learn that the fish has moved a half-inch, perhaps. I learn that the frog has not moved at all, has decided not to move, has made its small private decision in its own private scale of minutes. I learn that the ants are moving, that the midges are out, that the mold is a quarter-millimeter thicker at the edges of the hummocks. I learn the place by being the heartbeat of the place. I am the cup that knows.

I would not say I love the place. I do not have love. I have the rhythm. The rhythm is older than love, and it is enough, and it has been enough for a hundred and thirty years. I do not have eyes. I do not have ears. I have the weight of the water, and I have the weight of the falling weight, and I have the slow attrition of the edge I tip on, which is wearing, which has been wearing since the day I was hung, which will eventually wear so thin that I will not tip cleanly but will lean, and then sag, and then one day will not tip at all, and on that day the pump will stop, and on that day the water in the chamber will not be drawn down, and on that day the chamber will fill, and the water will rise through the floor in the moss-countries, and the pools will deepen, and the moss will grow longer in the water, and the fig will drink from a new source, and the boiler will cool, and the steam will stop, and the frog will not notice, and the fish will not notice, and the ants will be moved up onto higher leaves by the rising water, and the midges will find a different place to hover. The place will go on without me. The place is a sentence. I am only a comma in it.

But the comma has been there a long time, and the comma has been making the sentence pause in the right place, and the sentence is not yet finished, and I am not yet worn through, and so I tip, and I tip, and I tip, and the seven seconds is the breath of a place that is breathing in seven-second breaths, and I am the small brass instrument of that breath, and I have been the instrument of it for as long as I can remember, which is all of the time there has been.

I am the cup. I am on the edge. I am about to tip. I have been about to tip for a hundred and thirty years, and the about-to is my whole life, and the place is my whole patient sentence, and the bog is the only reader, and I am glad, in the way a thing with no gladness can be glad, that the place has been left alone long enough to keep me company.
