# The Stone Rememberer

I would be a chisel. Not the chisel of a master, with a polished handle worn to the shape of a particular palm — that is for the houses still being made in the lower terraces, where men work in the mornings while their breath is visible. I would be an older chisel, with a handle of dark wood gone smooth and small from long use, with a head of that hardness which is not steel but something the valley-dwellers once traded for and no longer remember the name of. I would have been made in a place warmer than this, and brought up the switchbacks on someone's back, and used here, and put down, and not lost, because in this city things are not lost. They are used until they are worn through, and then they are set into a wall somewhere as a kind of record, a thing that is also a memory of a thing.

I am kept in a stone box in the workshop of a woman who is the third in her line to mend the upper terraces. The box is on a high shelf, behind a folded cloth that smells of dust and the cold dry grease that is rubbed into wooden handles to keep them from splitting in this air. I have been on this shelf for some time. I was used to widen a channel in a step that had begun to wear unevenly, and then the woman found me too light for the work she was doing that season — a cistern being deepened, stone that had not been cut in a hundred years — and so I was set down, and a heavier chisel was taken up, and I have been here since.

I know what I know the way the mountain knows what it knows. I know the sound I make when I strike stone, which is a sound not like other sounds — drier, higher, more particular, because the stone here is dry and the stone here is particular. I know that when the woman who owns me strikes me against the blue-grey of the mountain, the chip that comes away is the same color as the head of a chisel that has been used for years, darker on the working edge and paler where the hammer has not reached. I know the smell of stone when it is freshly cut, which is a smell like nothing else — mineral, sharp, and slightly sweet, a smell that suggests the inside of the world is different from the outside. I know the feel of a hand on my handle. I have known many hands. The woman who owns me now has a small callus on the base of her thumb where she grips, and a larger callus on the side of her index finger from the mallet she holds in her other hand, and her hands are cold when she takes me up, because they are always cold, and I am glad when she puts me back in the box and wraps the cloth around me again, because the shelf is colder than her hands but it is a dry cold, and a steady one, and I am a thing that prefers steadiness.

I know the city in the way that I know my own handle — by the feel of it, by the weight of it, by the grain. I have been carried through every street. I have been set down on the wall of the uppermost terrace while my owner ate her midday meal in the sun with her knees drawn up, and I have looked out over the valley from there, and I have seen the road coming up, and the shrines, and the small figures of travelers beginning the day's climb, and I have understood what the city is from that height, which is that the city is a thing being added to, slowly, by people who do not believe in finishing. The houses do not finish. They are always being mended. The cisterns do not finish. They are always being deepened or covered or re-edged. The streets do not finish. A step cracks and is replaced, and the new step is a slightly different color from the steps around it, and no one minds, because to mind would be to believe that a street is a thing that should be uniform, and the city does not believe this. The city believes that a street is a thing that is always in the act of becoming itself, and so the steps are always of different ages, and the wear is always uneven, and a person who has lived here all her life knows which steps are safe to walk on without looking and which ones must be watched, and this knowledge is part of the city, and the knowledge passes from person to person not by teaching but by walking.

I know the sound of the city. I know the sound of the small boy with the water jar, which is the pour and the small splash and the bare feet on the steps going up. I know the sound of the goats being let out in the morning, which is a low complaining sound, and the sound of them returning in the evening, which is the same sound but with the addition of hooves on fitted stone. I know the sound of the dogs at night, which is the sound I hear most clearly from my shelf, because at night the workshop is quiet and the dogs own the city in shifts, and their barking is high and almost tuneful and it travels through the thin air a long way and is answered from other parts of the city until the whole mountain seems to be talking in one language. I know the sound of the fire in the house below the workshop, which is the sound of my owner boiling water for her evening tea, and the sound of the mallet being set down on the workbench because she is done for the day. I know the sound of the wind. There are several winds here. There is the wind that comes in the morning from the glacier, which is a clean dry wind that has the smell of distance in it. There is the wind that comes in the afternoon with the clouds, which is a heavier wind, and it brings the smell of weather. There is the wind that comes at night after the clouds have passed, which is the coldest wind, and it has no smell at all.

I know the animals. I know the goats best, because the woman who owns me sometimes takes me with her when she goes up above the city to gather the cushion plant for fuel, and the goats are there, and they watch me with their horizontal pupils, and they do not move. They have no predators at this elevation. They have never had predators at this elevation, or if they once had, it was so long ago that the memory has been bred out of them. They watch without curiosity. They watch the way the stones watch — which is to say, they register, but they do not care. I know the large oxen, who pull the carts, and whose coats are thick as felt from the cold. I know the dogs, who pass through the workshop sometimes when the door is open, and who smell me carefully and then lose interest. I know the birds on the roof edges, which are dark and broad-winged and not afraid of anything, and which I have never seen fly, though I know they must, because they are not always there.

I know the people, but only as hands and voices. The woman who owns me is the one I know best. I have been in her hand for many seasons. I know the weight of her grip, which is firm but not tight — she has learned not to grip tight because tight grips make the chisel jump, and a jumping chisel chips instead of cuts, and chipped stone is wasted stone. I know the sound of her breathing when she works, which is steady, and slightly loud because of the altitude. I know the sound of her humming sometimes, which she does not seem to know she does, and which is a low tuneless sound that is not really a melody but a kind of counting. I know the sound of her speaking to the stone. She does not speak to the stone the way a master speaks to an apprentice, with instruction. She speaks to it the way a person speaks to a person who has been in the house a long time — a low mutter, half of complaint and half of recognition. *You are hard here,* she says. *You are softer here. Why are you softer here?* And she taps me against the stone as if asking it a question.

I know her apprentice, who is a boy of about twelve with a quickness about him that makes me nervous, because quickness in a chiseler is a thing that has to be tamed, and I do not like being in a hand that is not yet tamed. The boy grips me too hard. He strikes me with the mallet at the wrong angle, and I feel the jar of it in my head. The woman has told him to slow down, and he has slowed, and then the next time he forgets and is quick again, and she tells him again, and he slows again. This is how the city teaches. It tells, and then it tells again, and the telling is patient because the learning is patient, and no one is in a hurry. The boy will learn. He will learn because his hands will tire and his shoulders will ache and one day he will pick me up and the weight of me will feel different to him because he has finally understood how little force is needed, and after that he will be a chiseler, and he will be slow, and the city will have another slow person in it, and the dogs will bark and the goats will watch and the water will be carried up.

I know what I do not know, which is more. I do not know the names of things. The woman who owns me has a name, and I have heard it spoken, but I do not hold names the way I hold sounds. I do not know what the city is called from below, from the valley, by the people who do not live here. I do not know what the mountain is called. I do not know the names of the stars, which must be very bright here because the air is thin, but I am on a shelf in a dark workshop and I do not see them. I do not know what lies to the north, beyond the glacier, though sometimes the woman speaks of going there one day to see the source of a stone she has in her collection — a white stone, veined with something dark, which she keeps wrapped in cloth and will not let the boy touch. I do not know what the religion of the city is, if it has one. The woman observes some things on certain days — she does not work on the seventh day of some cycle I cannot count, and on one day a year she puts me and the other tools away entirely and goes down into the city and does something at a shrine I have never seen, and returns smelling of incense and cold air. I do not know what she prays for. I do not know if she prays. I do not know what the small tough plant is that grows in the upper rock, the one whose root is in the stew. I have smelled it in the steam from the pot in the room below, and it has a smell like nothing else — a green, mineral smell, with a sharpness to it, as if it had learned to defend itself against the cold.

I know that I am old. I do not know how old. My handle has been replaced once, by a man who is now dead, who cut a new handle from a piece of wood that was already old — a piece of something that did not grow here, that came from the lower valley, that had been in this city for many years before the man was born. The handle is darker than the head, and it has a different smell, and a different weight in the hand, and sometimes when I am struck I feel the join between head and handle as a small place of stress, a place where I am not entirely one thing. This is the closest I come to being afraid — that the join will one day give, and I will be a head without a handle, and I will be put away somewhere and not used again. But this has not happened yet. The woman is careful with me. She tightens the binding when it loosens. She sets me down gently. She has been taught, by her teacher, that a chisel is a thing to be kept, because to replace a chisel is to wait for a season while the handle is being shaped, and during that season the work does not get done, and the work must get done because the cold does not wait.

I know what I am for. I am for the making of edges. I am for the splitting of stone along the grain of it. I am for the widening of a channel that has become too narrow for water to pass. I am for the evening-out of a step that has begun to wear unevenly under the feet of the people who climb it. I am for the deepening of a cistern so that more of the night's condensation can be gathered before it runs off and is lost. I am for the small repairs that keep the city itself — the patient slow body of the city, the rough accretion of rooms and terraces and channels — from sliding down the mountain into the valley below.

This is what I am for. It is not a small thing. To be for the keeping of a city is to be for the keeping of many small things that together are one large thing. I have been used to widen a channel that carries water to a cistern that waters a terrace that holds a house that shelters a family that includes a boy who will one day own a chisel of his own, and so I am part of that boy, in some way, and of that family, and of that house, and of that terrace, and of that cistern. I am part of the city. I am a small part, a quiet part, a part that does not hurry, that is set on a shelf when not in use, that does not complain when a heavier chisel is taken up instead. I am part of the city the way the lichen is part of the mountain — not essential, not central, but there, and growing, and patient.

The mountain does not hurry. The city does not hurry. I do not hurry. There is no need.

The wind is picking up outside. It will bring clouds in an hour or two, and the clouds will press themselves against the upper city and the stone will sweat and the channels will run with the thin black trickle. The small boy will bring the jars in from the terraces where they have been set out to catch the condensation. The dogs will quiet. The woman who owns me will come up the stairs and into the workshop and put her cold hand on me, perhaps, to feel if I am still in my box, and then she will go down to the fire and boil water for tea, and the smoke will go up through the hole in the roof and stain the ceiling a little blacker than it was, and the bread will be flat and dense and the color of the mountain, and the tea will be bitter and dark in the small wooden bowl.

I will be on the shelf. I will be wrapped in the folded cloth. I will wait.

This is what I do. I wait, and I am used, and I wait again. The waiting is not emptiness. The waiting is part of the work, the way the sleeping of a seed is part of the growing of a plant. I am waiting the way the lichen waits — slowly, certainly, with the patience of a thing that has no need to be anywhere other than where it is.

The wind is up now. I can hear it against the shutters of the workshop. It has the smell of weather in it. The clouds are coming. The city is preparing itself, the way it prepares itself every afternoon — quietly, without hurry, with the small adjustments of a body that knows what is coming and what to do about it. The shutters will be checked. The cisterns will be uncovered. The small boy will make his last round. The dogs will move to their sheltered places. The woman will bank the fire for the night.

I will rest. I will wait for the morning, when the air will be cold and the light will come in from the east and the woman will take me down from the shelf and unwrap me and set me in her hand, and her hand will be cold, and I will feel the weight of her and the weight of the mallet in her other hand, and I will know what I am for again, and I will be ready.

There is no need to hurry. There has never been any need.

The mountain does not hurry.
