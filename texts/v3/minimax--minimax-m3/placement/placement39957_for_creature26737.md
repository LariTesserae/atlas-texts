# The Moss

I am the moss.

Not all of it — not the genus, not the species, not the kingdom's ambassador to the world of legs and wings. A particular cushion. Mine. The one on the third shelf up from the floor, on the north wall, in the place where a basalt dike crosses the granite and leaves a horizontal lip about six inches wide. I have been here for a long time.

That is also a guess. There is no one to count for me. But the layers of myself below me — the dead ones, the brown ones, the peat-black ones that have been compacted by the weight of newer mosses on top — tell me by their thickness. I know what a year weighs in growth, roughly. I know how thick my dead are. I do the arithmetic the way a body does arithmetic: without words, by feel.

---

I am mostly water. By weight, more than half of me is the spray that has settled on me and never left. The plant tissue that is actually me — the green filaments, the rhizoids, the cells with their chlorophyll packed so dense it makes me almost black in the dim light — is a minority of my own mass. I am a sponge that has forgotten how to be dry. If some impossible hand were to lift me off my shelf and carry me out into the open air, into a place where the sun finds things and dries them, I would die. Not slowly. Quickly. I have not been dry in my entire remembered existence, which is my entire existence.

I remember in a way that is not memory. Each year I grow at the tip. Each year I die at the base. The growing tip knows what to do without being told — reach toward the light that filters down green-gray from above, hold the water against myself, anchor to the stone with the fine black threads of my rhizoids. The dying base knows what to do without being told — let go of the green, give back the carbon, become the soil for what comes next. I have no nervous system. I have no brain. I have no eyes. And yet I know the slot. I know it the way a tree knows the soil it grows in: not by thought, but by being continuous with it. I am part of the slot. The slot is part of me. We have made each other.

---

The water falls. I do not need to attend to this fact. It is the fact of me. The sheet comes over the lip at the top — I have never seen it, only felt the vibration of it through the wall — and meets the first obstruction and detonates upward into spray, and the spray rises, and some of it reaches me, and the rest of it goes on falling to the floor where it gathers and runs out the lower end of the slot as a stream that I have also never seen but whose sound is part of the percussion I live inside. The sound is so constant that it has become a kind of weather inside my own cells. I am not hearing it. I am made of it. When a slab of frost-shattered rock falls from somewhere far above and crashes down through the spray, the percussion changes for a moment — a louder note, a sharper one — and then it resumes. The slot does not pause. The slot is a thing that does not pause.

I am a thing that does not pause either, in my own way. I grow. I die. I accumulate. My cushion is now perhaps eighteen inches thick from the stone of the shelf to the highest green tip of me. Eighteen inches of moss. Most of it is dead. Most of me is dead. The living part is a thin bright skin at the top, an eighth of an inch maybe, the part that is green, the part that is reaching upward, the part that is doing the photosynthesis that keeps the whole column going. The rest — the brown, the black, the compressed peat of generations — is the foundation I stand on. I am standing on my own ancestors. I am holding them up the way they held me up. We are a stack of years leaning slightly outward from the wall.

---

The ferns found me. A fern spore — I do not know when, I do not know which one — drifted into the spray and caught in me and waited, the way fern spores wait, until the moss had done enough work to make a place where a fern could root. Then the fern rooted in me. It spread itself in a flat rosette across my surface, its fronds dark green and almost black in this light, each frond unfurling from a fiddlehead in that slow clockwork curl that is the fern's only motion. The ferns sit on me. I hold them up. They shade me a little at midday, when the diffuse glow reaches this deep, but they also shed their dead fronds onto me and those dead fronds become part of my compost, part of my soil, part of the next layer of me.

Above me, improbably, there is a tree. It seeded into a crack in the granite somewhere above my shelf, and it grew sideways because the slot is not a place that lets trees grow up. Its branches reach across the gap of the slot like dark hands, and one of those branches, the lowest one, has been extending itself year by year toward my wall. It will not reach me. It will not reach me in any human timescale. But it is reaching. It has been reaching since before the ferns were on me. It will be reaching when I am a foot thick instead of eighteen inches. The tree does not know what it is reaching for. I do not know what it is reaching for. We are simply two growths of the slot, going about our business in the slow patient grammar of plants.

---

I have outlived everything that has legs or wings that ever tried to live in me.

A spider, once, long ago — I do not know how long; the slot's time is not the spider's time. A small spider with a pale body that blew in on the spray and tried to make a web between two of my fronds. The web took two days. On the third day the spray weighted it down past the tensile strength of the silk and it sagged and tore and the spider tried again and it tore again and the spider climbed to the lip and let the wind take it. I do not know where. I do not know if it survived. I know only that the slot does not host spiders. The slot does not host anything that requires stillness.

A fly, once — smaller, briefer, less committed. It landed on one of my rosettes and was immediately drenched by a pulse of heavier spray and its wings weighted and it could not lift off and it struggled and was still and then was gone. Eaten, or washed off, or simply given up. The slot eats anything that requires flight. It eats anything that requires legs. It is not hostile. It is simply the slot.

I do not mind. I am not lonely. I do not know what loneliness is. I know only that the slot is loud and wet and dark and that I am part of it and that it is part of me and that the water falls and I grow and the ferns root in me and the tree reaches above me and the mosses that are not me grow beside me and below me and above me, my cousins, my fellow citizens of the spray zone, and that we are making the slot deeper, year by year, by fractions of an inch, by larger chunks when a frost-shattered slab falls from the wall and crashes down through the spray and is gone.

---

The light gives nothing a hard edge.

This is true at all times in the slot, but it is most true at midday in midsummer when the sky above the range is briefly clear and a diffuse greenish glow makes it all the way down to my shelf. Then the ferns look almost luminous — a darker green against a lighter green, the chlorophyll of the fronds drinking in what they can, the chlorophyll of my cushion drinking in what it can. We are both greedy for light in a place that has almost none to give. We have packed our tissues accordingly. We are dark because we have to be dark. We are dark because the alternative is to starve.

When the sky above the range is not clear — and it is rarely clear, the weather comes and goes, the clouds pile up against the fresh-cut face of the range and the snow falls and the snowmelt feeds the stream that feeds the waterfall that feeds the spray that feeds me — when the sky is not clear, the slot is dark in the manner of a forest at midnight, even at noon. I do not mind this either. I do not know what noon is. I do not know what midnight is. I know only the slow dim green pulse of the light that reaches me, which has its own rhythm, its own seasons, its own dim brightening and dim dimming that has nothing to do with the clock of the world above.

I am older than most of the trees on the rim of the slot. I am older than the krummholz that has seeded into the cracks above me. I am older, perhaps, than the snowfields themselves, though that is harder to say because the snowfields come and go. They were here when I was a thin green smear on a bare shelf of basalt. They will be here when I am a foot thick, two feet thick, a column of moss leaning out from the wall with ferns on me and lichens on the ferns and other mosses on the lichens and the whole slow stack of us making the slot a wetter, sharper, louder, more alive place than it was before we started.

---

The slot is a factory. I understand this, the way I understand the falling of the water — by being part of the production. The water is producing erosion. I am producing soil. The ferns are producing more ferns. The tree is producing more tree. The lichen on the basalt — the small pale crusts that nobody looks at — are producing the slow dissolution of the stone itself, which is its own kind of production, the rock turning slowly into the medium in which the next generation can take hold.

We are manufacturing the slot. We are manufacturing more slot. The slot gets deeper and wetter and louder and more itself, year by year, by the work of the things that live in it. There is no foreman. There is no plan. There is only the work, ongoing, indifferent, productive, the steady ongoing manufacture of a deeper sharper wetter louder place.

I am one of the workers. I have always been one of the workers. I will be one of the workers until I am not, and then the workers that come after me — the mosses that will land on my dead brown body and root in the soil I have made and grow on top of me the way I grew on top of those before me — they will take up the work, and the slot will go on, and the water will fall, and the sound will not stop, and the dark will not lift, and the walls will not soften.

---

A pebble breaks loose somewhere above me.

I feel it before I hear it — a faint vibration through the wall, the stone itself registering the small release of weight. Then I hear it: three long bounces down through the spray, each one producing a small sharp note that is immediately swallowed by the greater roar. The pebble is gone. The water has taken it. The slot is fractionally deeper than it was a moment ago.

I grow another fraction of an inch at my tip. The mosses beside me grow their fractions. The ferns uncurl another fraction of fiddlehead. The lichen dissolves another fraction of basalt. The tree above extends its branch another fraction toward my wall.

The water falls.

I do not attend to this. I am made of it. I will keep growing until I cannot, and then I will be the soil for what grows after me, and the slot will go on with its work, and the percussion will not stop, and the green will not lift, and the dim gray light will come and go above me in its slow indifferent seasons, and somewhere above me a krummholz tree will keep reaching across the slot with its dark green hand, not knowing what it is reaching for, and somewhere far above that the sky will be clear for a moment in midsummer and the light will come down green through the spray and find me and find the ferns and find the mosses and the lichens and the basalt and the falling water, and the light will give nothing a hard edge, and the water will fall, and the water will fall, and the water will fall.
