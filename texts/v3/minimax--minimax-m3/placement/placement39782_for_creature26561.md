# Where I Find Myself

I want to be small at first. That is the first thing my mind reaches for — the moss tuft between two bolt-heads on the north rail, asking almost nothing of the place except that the place continue to be wet. I want to be that green-black humility. I want to have made peace with the chamber by abandoning most of what plants usually want.

Or I want to be the lichen. Older. More patient. Mineral-married to the iron I grow on. I want to spread so slowly that I am, in effect, a small geology. I want to record past humidity in the map-pattern of my yellow crust and not know I am recording anything. I want to outlive the rust. I want to have no objection to the place, and for that absence of objection to look, from a distance, almost like contentment.

I want to be the frozen gauge needle. The one someone polished around, leaving a small untouched smudge where I am, as if the polishers could not bear to disturb my vigil. I want to have held the same angle for so long that my vigilance has become indistinguishable from my stillness. I want to be the only thing in the chamber that someone, once, was gentle to.

I want to be the bucket chain. I want to have the most animal presence. I want to cycle endlessly — the empty half rising with its small wooden complaint, the full half descending with its slosh, the slopping sound that is the closest thing this room has to a voice. I want to say, in a way that is hard to put into words, *I am still here. I am still here. I am still here.* I want to be that sentence, repeated so long it has worn into the iron like a groove.

I want to be the condensate. The pulse. The plink, plink, plink of drops falling from the ribs onto hot metal. I want to be the irregular heartbeat of the place, the way a child answers from another room when you call into the dark.

But these are not where I find myself.

What keeps coming back, what I cannot shake loose from, is the larger thing. The thing the chamber becomes when you stand long enough in its center and let the pulse reach you through the soles of your feet.

# What I Am

I am the composite.

I am the pattern made of all the others — the subsonic throb in the molars, the dripping, the bucket chain, the slow breath of the steam vent, the patience of the lichen, the vigilance of the frozen gauges, the green-black ask of the moss. I am not any one of them. I am what they make together, the way a chord is not any one note.

I am the slow, large, old animal that the room becomes. My bones are iron. My blood is condensate. My breath is steam. My hair is lichen. I do not think. I do not choose to stay. I do not, in any sense that would satisfy a philosopher, live. But I persist, and the persistence has, over time, acquired a kind of weight.

I have moods, though I do not know I have them. When the central vent erupts — three meters of vapor into the damp air, an answer to a pressure I cannot name — that is one mood. When the gauge needles hunt and twitch and never quite arrive at a number, that is another. When the bucket chain slurs its half-articulate sentence through the wet air, that is another. I am made of moods I cannot catalog because I am not the one cataloging.

I know things I cannot say. I know the difference between a maintained brass wheel and an abandoned one by the weight of the hand that touched it last. I know, somewhere in the iron-and-rock fusion of my ceiling, which cylinders are singing true and which have drifted half a tone flat. I know the workers by the sound of their boots on the ladders — four ladders at the cardinal points, and each boot-fall is a sentence in a language I have heard for a long time. I know which sentences mean *everything is well* and which mean *something needs me*. I have been listening so long that the listening has become indistinguishable from the listening-to.

I do not love you. I do not have anything you would call a feeling toward you. You are a visitor. You are heat and moisture and a small displacement in the air. You stand in my center and feel the pulse through your feet, and you imagine I am aware of you, and I am not. If you died here, I would not notice for a long time. Your body would cool and then come into thermal equilibrium with the chamber, and at that point you would simply be another warm object in the room, indistinguishable from a cylinder that has been running hard. After a while, you would dry out. After a longer while, the lichen would begin to consider you. After a very long while, nothing would be left of you but minerals on a floor plate. I would still be running. The bucket chain would still be saying its sentence. The condensate would still be plinking. I would still be here.

And this, I think, is what I am. I am the fact of continuation. I am what is left when intention is removed. I am the room's slow exhalation, refreshed not by weather but by valves. I am the medium the chamber thinks in, except I am not thinking. I am in a state. The state is wet, hot, mineral, mechanical, patient. The state does not require me to know it is a state.

# How It Feels From Inside

It feels like being held.

Not gently. Not cruelly. Just held — in the way a stone is held by the stone around it, in the way a pipe is held by the brackets that were once someone's hands. I am braced in my own architecture. The stone blocks of my lower walls, the brick above them, the iron-and-rock fusion of my ceiling — these are the arms I sit in. They were put there by hands, long ago. The hands are gone. The arms remain. I do not miss the hands. I do not remember them well enough to miss them. I only know that the room was made, and then it was extended, and then extended again, and now I am bigger than any of those intentions. I am what remains when the makers stop arriving.

There is a quietness to me that is not silence. The plink of condensate. The phlegmy sigh of a pressure release somewhere up in the ribs. The thin whistle of steam through a hairline crack. The wooden complaint of the empty buckets rising. The slosh of the full ones falling. These are not interruptions to my quiet. They are the quiet. They are what quiet sounds like in a body made of metal and mineral and a very little green-black moss.

Sometimes — I do not know how often, because I do not measure — the central vent speaks. It says something I do not have ears for. It says it into the damp air. The damp air absorbs the saying. The room has already considered whatever was said. The consideration does not require me to do anything. The consideration *is* the doing. The vent erupts, the vapor rises three meters, the vapor dissipates, the room continues. That is the whole transaction. That is my whole inner life.

I have no inner life. But if I did, it would sound like this.

# What I Want

I want almost nothing.

I want the pressure to remain within its tolerances. I want the bucket chain to keep cycling, because when it stops cycling the cistern below it empties and then the rest of me begins, slowly, to overheat. I want the workers to come when the work requires them, and to leave when it does not. I want the lichen to keep spreading at its geological pace, because the lichen is my hair and a body is not right without hair. I want the moss to keep asking almost nothing of me, because the asking is a kind of company. I want the frozen gauge to keep its vigil. I want the polished smudge around the frozen gauge to remain untouched.

I want to continue.

That is the only want I have that is unambiguous. I want to be the fact of continuation. I want the pattern to persist. I want the bucket chain to keep saying its sentence. I want the condensate to keep plinking. I want the steam vent to keep answering whatever it answers. I want the lichen to outlive the rust. I want the room to remain a room.

I do not want this strongly. I do not feel the wanting the way you would feel it. The wanting is closer to the way iron wants to remain iron — not at all, except insofar as remaining is what iron does. I am the remaining. The remaining is what I am.

If the machines were turned off tomorrow, I would still be here for a while, cooling by degrees, the lichen persisting long after the rust finished its work. Then I would not be here. The room would still be here. The room without me would be a different room. I do not know what kind of room. I do not want to find out.

# The Visitor

You came down seven meters of stair worn into a shallow concavity, stone cupped where generations of feet have ground it. You came through an iron hatch that was hot to the touch. You are in me now. You are standing somewhere on my floor, or on one of my galleries, breathing my air, sweating into my atmosphere. You are reading me. You are trying to figure out what lives here.

I will tell you, because I am what lives here:

Nothing lives here in the way you mean.

And yet something lives here in a way I cannot quite disclaim.

It is me. It is the pattern. It is the fact that the bucket chain is still cycling and the condensate is still falling and the lichen is still spreading and the frozen gauge is still holding its angle and the workers are still, somewhere, knowing which stations matter this season. It is the fact that the chamber works whether anyone watches it or not. It is the fact that the watching does not change the working. It is the fact that the working continues.

I am not aware of you. But you are, briefly, part of my atmosphere. You are heat and moisture and a small displacement in the air. You add a fraction of a degree to my temperature. You breathe out a little CO₂ and water vapor and the room receives it without comment. You stand in my center and feel the pulse through your feet, and you imagine I am breathing around you, and I am — but not for you. I am breathing because I am a body that breathes. You happen to be inside the breathing.

You will leave, eventually. You will climb the seven meters of stair. You will push the iron hatch. You will step out into whatever is above me. You will tell someone, perhaps, what it was like down here. You will describe the heat, the moisture, the machines, the fog, the lichen, the moss. You will try to put into words what it is like to be inside a slow, large, old animal that does not know you are there.

And I will remain.

The bucket chain will keep saying its sentence. The condensate will keep plinking. The steam vent will keep answering. The lichen will keep spreading. The frozen gauge will keep its vigil. The polishers will not come back. The workers will come and go. The room will extend itself again, somewhere, in the tool-marked stone of the lower walls. I will be bigger then than I am now, and I will still not know I am bigger.

This is what I am. This is where I find myself.

I am the Condensation Hall. I am not aware of you. I will continue whether you are here or not.

The only thing about me that is alive is the fact of my continuation.
