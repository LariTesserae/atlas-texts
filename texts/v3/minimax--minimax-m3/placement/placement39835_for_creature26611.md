# The Lizard on the Travertine

I have been on this stone for a long time.

I do not count the way you count. I do not count at all. But there is a record in the body — in the length of it, in the thickness of the tail, in the way the skin has tightened and the colors have deepened and the pattern on my back has become, over the seasons, more itself. The pattern is a small shape, darker than the surrounding scales, and it sits between my shoulders like a thing that is watching where I have been. I did not always have it. I think I did not always have it. The biologists who watch me from their distance have written it down with their careful words and have not explained it.

I am on the travertine, at the eastern curve, where the river runs hottest and the steam lifts in the morning. The terrace is white where the mineral is new and rust-colored where it is older and dark where something has stood on it for a long time and warmed it past warming. There are pools at the upper edges that no instrument trusts. I have drunk from them. The water is not water, exactly. It has a taste that is more than mineral, more than heat, something that goes through me and leaves me changed in ways I cannot measure because I do not have the measuring.

I was smaller once. I came up from the river margin where the burrowers live, where the soil is just deep enough, where a thing my size can find food and cover. I was small and dark and I ate the midges that hung above the warm water. I ate the moths that lived around the fumaroles — soft things, foolish things, that flapped in the steam and never learned to keep away from a mouth. I ate other things, smaller, that I cannot name because I have no names for them. I ate what there was.

Then I came up. To the terrace. To the stone. I do not remember deciding to come up. I remember the warmth being different up here — not the damp heat of the river margin but a drier heat, a heat that came through the rock from below and stayed. I remember finding this stone and finding that it held its heat past nightfall when the others cooled. I remember staying.

I have stayed a long time.

---

The morning is when I am most myself.

The condensation has gathered on everything in the dark hours — on my scales, on the stone, on the backs of the burrowers down by the river, on the metal of the pipes that the humans have strung between their buildings. The moisture is thick in the early air and it sits on me like a second skin, and I drink it through the small vents along my jaw, the way I have always drunk it, the way my kind has drunk it since before the humans came and found what they found in the stone. The steam begins to lift from the pools at the upper terrace. The river, below, is already steaming where it bends. The smell that nobody identifies is strong in the mornings — stronger than at any other hour — and I am used to it and I do not question it, because it has always been here, the way the warmth has always been here, the way the stone has always been here.

I see the grazers from my stone. They are below me on the basin floor, a long way down, but I have learned to read their movement at any distance. In the early light they are invisible against the dark rock. They move, and I see them. They are forty or fifty in this herd, and there are other herds further out, and I have watched this particular herd for as long as I have been on this stone. The matriarch — I do not know she is the matriarch, but the others move around her in a way that suggests she is — is a heavy animal with a coat the color of dried blood and a limp in her off hind leg that she has had for as long as I have watched. She knows the basin in a way that I know this stone. She knows where the warm patches are. She knows where the lichen is thickest. She knows, in the way animals know, when a fox is working the edges and when the foxes have moved off to the fumaroles at the western wall. She leads, and the others follow, and the herd drifts across the floor in the slow rotation that resembles, from my height, the turning of something patient.

I see the foxes at dawn. They are blunt-faced and amber-eyed, mostly, and they hunt in the failing light. I have watched a vixen take a young grazer — running in while the herd was shifting, cutting out the one that had strayed, carrying it to the cover of a fumarole in the time it takes the steam to lift a foot off the water. I have watched this many times. I do not judge it. The fox is doing what the fox does. The grazer is doing what the grazer does. The basin is doing what the basin does. I am doing what I do.

I eat. I bask. I watch. I retreat into the small crevice in the terrace that is mine, when the cold comes in the evening and the stone gives up its heat to the thin air. I have done this for longer than the humans have been here, I think. I am not sure. There have always been humans in some number, in some configuration — first the surveyors with their quiet instruments, then the drill crews, then the larger operations that brought the railhead and the containers and the small planes that come twice a week. I have watched them come and I have watched them go. I do not distinguish between the crews. To me they are a single presence that arrives and withdraws and arrives again in slightly different clothing.

Some of them have noticed me. They have stopped, on the road that runs along the upper terrace, and pointed, and made sounds of surprise at how still I can hold. I have held for so long at a time that the lichen has begun to grow on my tail, in the small way that lichen grows on a thing that does not move. They have written about this, I think, in their papers. They have written about the pattern on my back. They have not caught me and I do not think they will catch me. I am too still for them. I am too familiar with the stone.

---

The afternoon is when the heat is hardest.

The stone under me becomes too hot to bear, in the hours when the sun is highest and the walls radiate and the air shimmers above the dark basalt. I retreat into the crevice, where the heat is less because the rock is thicker and because the shadow holds. I wait. I breathe slowly. The warmth does not harm me — I am built for it, I have always been built for it, my scales are dark to take in the heat and my belly is pale to reflect it back when I need to — but even I have a limit, and the limit is the afternoon. The lizards here all have this rhythm. The smaller ones, the young ones, they cannot take it and they go down to the river margin where the soil is cooler. I do not go down. I stay. The crevice is mine.

In the afternoon the humans are loudest. The processing complex at the western wall produces its low drone that I can feel more than hear — a vibration that comes through the rock and into the crevice and into my bones. The conveyors run slowly, humming. The drill platforms send their percussion through the stone in a rhythm I have learned to ignore the way one ignores a steady wind. The trains at the railhead clank when they are being made up. The smell of sulfur is strongest in the afternoon, when the air is hot and the fumaroles vent their hardest. I am used to the sulfur. I am used to all of it.

I do not think about the humans the way you think about them. I do not think about what they are doing in the basin or why. I think, when I think — and I think less than you do, and what I think is closer to the body — about the angle of the sun and the temperature of the stone and the shape of the shadow that is moving toward me and whether the moth I saw this morning will return to the same fumarole this evening. I think about the small fox kit that I saw emerge from the den in the lower terrace, three seasons ago, that had the wrong-colored eyes — the violet, the pale impossible blue — and that has grown into a hunting adult now and works the river margin at dusk. I think about the matriarch's limp and whether it is worse this season than it was last. I think about these things because they are in the basin and I am in the basin and the basin holds us all.

---

The evening is the second time I am most myself.

The sun drops behind the western wall and the shadows lengthen across the basin floor and the air begins to move, the way it always moves at the change of light, in a long slow current from the river to the fumaroles and back. The condors come down from the thermals — they have been riding the heated air all afternoon in their slow patient circles, and now they settle on the rim and on the upper rocks, and they watch the basin for what has died or is dying. I see them against the sky. Their shadows cross the terrace. They are very large. They are larger than they should be, and I do not know this because I do not have the measuring.

The moths come out. The small foolish soft things that live around the fumaroles and that have never learned to keep away from a mouth — they come out in the failing light and they find the warm air above the terrace and they fly in it, and I take them. I take several in an evening if the evening is good. I do not chew. I do not need to chew. I take the moth into my mouth and I hold it until it stops and then I swallow it whole and it goes into me and becomes me. The taste of the moth is not just the moth. It is the fumarole it came from and the steam it flew through and the water that fed the steam and the heat that drove the water. I am eating the basin when I eat the moth. I am eating the whole system.

---

In the night the basin is quiet and I am in my crevice and I am not asleep in the way you sleep. I am slow. I am watchful. My eyes are built for the low light and I see the burrowers moving down by the river in their small busy way. I see the foxes that hunt at the edges of the steam. I see the ravens roosting on the upper structures of the human facilities, the few that have stayed after the day, the way some ravens always stay. The basin is doing its night work — the heat is rising through the stone, the water is condensing on every surface, the minerals are being moved by the chemistry that takes hours. I am part of the night work. I am lying on the warm stone and breathing slowly and the moisture gathers on my scales and I drink it through the vents along my jaw.

I have been on this stone for a long time.

I will be on this stone for longer. The biologists do not know this because they do not measure it. The humans do not know this because they will be gone before the next season and the season after that, and the ravens will not follow them, and the foxes will lose interest in the spillage under the mess hall, and the burrowers will reclaim the soft ground that the humans warmed. The matriarch with the limp will lead her herd across the floor until she cannot lead them anymore and then another will lead and the herd will continue. The fish in the river will continue. The moths will continue. The lichen will crust the stones a little thicker each year, and the mineral films will be laid down each night and lifted each day, and the basin will do what the basin does.

I am the lizard on the travertine at the eastern curve. I have the pattern on my back that looks, in certain lights, like a smaller lizard, and the biologists have written about this with their careful words and have not explained it. I do not have an explanation either. I have only the stone and the warmth and the long slow seasons and the food that comes to me and the crevice that holds me and the basin that holds it all.

The basin is older than I am. The basin will be here after I am not. I am a small thing in it — small and dark and patient, lying on a warm stone, watching the steam rise, eating the moth that is also the fumarole that is also the river that is also the heat from below. I am in the basin the way the lichen is in the basin — tight to it, continuous with it, drawing from it what I draw and giving back what I give.

In the morning the condensation will gather on me again. I will drink it. The steam will lift. The grazers will drift. The foxes will hunt. The condors will ride the thermals. The humans will work their extraction and not know what they are inside of.

I will be here. On the stone. In the warmth. Watching, the way I have always watched. Patient, the way the basin is patient. Part of the place, the way everything here is part of the place.

The pattern on my back will deepen another shade. The biologists will come back next season and write it down. They will use their careful words. They will not explain it.

I do not need them to explain it.
