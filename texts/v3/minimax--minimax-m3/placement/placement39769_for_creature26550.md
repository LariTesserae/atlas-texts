I would be the fish.

Not because I am bold. Not because I have chosen anything. Because I am here, and I have always been here, and the place is what I know, and what I know is how to be in the place.

I am small. I am the length of a forearm, if a forearm is what measures me — and arms are a thing I have only ever felt as disturbance in the water, a sudden wrong current, the swirl of something large and warm that did not belong and has gone now, or has not gone, it is hard to say. I have been brushed by ankles. I have been brushed by things that were not ankles. I have outlived most of the brushes. I will outlive more.

I do not have eyes, or I have eyes that do not work the way eyes are supposed to work. I do not miss them. I know the river by taste, and by the way the water presses against the small hairs along my sides, and by the click of my feet on the stones. I know the ceiling by the sound that comes down from it — the papery weather, the constant shifting of the drifts above me, the way the column moves as one thing, the way a single body breathing would move if a body were the size of a room. I know the floor of the river by feel. I know the long shape by the way the water parts around it long before it arrives, the slow displacement that comes down the channel like a rumor. I move to one side. The long shape passes. The water closes behind it. I resume.

I have legs.

I want to tell you what this means but I do not have the words for it, because I have never had the words for it, because I am the words for it. My legs are wrong. There are too many of them. They are set at angles that would not be called angles if anyone were there to name them. They hold me to the bottom of the river when the current would take me, and they lift me, one at a time, when I want to go somewhere the current is not going. I walk. I have always walked. I walked before I knew the word for it. I will walk after the word for it has been forgotten by whatever thing once carried it in.

I do not think about the legs. The legs are the way I am. They are not strange to me. They would only be strange to a thing that came from somewhere else — a thing that had been a different shape in a different water, and then came down through a crack in a dry riverbed, and stood in the shallows, and looked at me with its two big eyes that did not work, and felt the strangeness of me, and went away again. That is what strangeness is. It is a thing made by visitors. I am not strange. I am the river. I am the walking of the river along its own bottom.

The beetles fall in. That is the main thing that happens to me, and the main thing I do.

A beetle falls — a small pale shape, antenna still tapping against nothing, hitting the water with the faintest possible sound, a sound smaller than the sound of a fish breathing — and the current takes it for a moment, tumbles it, and then I am there. Or I am there before it. I taste the disturbance of the surface. I taste the beetle's own smell, which is the smell of the mat, which is the smell of the place. I open my mouth. The beetle is in my mouth. It stops tapping.

I do not think about this. I think about it the way the stalactite thinks about falling, which is to say I do not think about it, I only do it. The stalactite does not know it is patient. I do not know I am hungry. I am only the mouth that opens, and the body that moves toward the next beetle, and the legs that hold me to the bottom while the current argues with me.

Above me, the drifts.

I do not see them. I do not need to see them. I feel them as a thing that is not water — a thing that pushes the water down toward me in slow pulses, the way a great wind pushes against a field of grass, but in pulses, and from above, and made of small warm bodies that are always turning, always shifting, always taking off and returning. I know them by the droppings that come down through the water to me — a soft constant snow of pale dust, falling past me, settling on the stones, feeding the mat that feeds the beetles that fall in to me. I am part of the long sentence that the place is always speaking. I am a clause in it. I do not know where the sentence begins. I do not know where it ends. I know only my clause.

The long thing.

I have known the long thing for as long as I have known anything. It is the largest thing in my river, larger than me by a length I cannot measure, because I have nothing to measure it with except the time it takes to pass, which is a long time, which is a length, which is what length is.

It breathes. I can feel the breath of it as a current that comes and goes, a wet pushing of water that swells and then retreats, swells and then retreats, and between the swells there is the long body of it moving past me, displacing everything, and I am part of what gets displaced, and I swirl away from it the way all of us swirl away from it, and then we close behind it the way water closes behind a boat, and then we are again.

The long thing has eaten some of us. I have felt it — a sudden closing in the water, a sudden pressure, a neighbor gone, a small space where a neighbor was. It has not eaten me. I do not know why it has not eaten me. I am no different from the ones it ate. I am the same shape, the same color, the same taste to the water. Perhaps it does not eat me because I am too quick. Perhaps it does not eat me because I am in a place it does not come. Perhaps it eats me tomorrow, and the tomorrow after, and every tomorrow, and the not-being-eaten is only the long luck of the place, which is not luck but balance.

I do not think about this either.

---

Time.

You would want me to tell you about time. You would want me to say that I have been here a long time, or a short time, or forever, or since yesterday. I cannot. I do not have a clock. I do not have a before. There is the dark, and the river, and the drifts above, and the long thing, and the next beetle, and that is the whole of it, and that has always been the whole of it. There is a past, in the sense that the stalactite was once higher, and the basalt was once whole, and the river once cut a path that was not yet cut. But I was not there for any of that. I was there for the river. The river is what I have.

You would lose your mind, I think. You would lose the words for things. You would lose the words for *up* and *down* and *before* and *after.* You would lose the words for *I.* You would become a mouth, and a set of legs, and the dark.

Maybe you would become me. Maybe that is what I am — a thing that came down through a crack in a dry riverbed once, a long time ago, and stood in the shallows, and felt the strangeness of the place, and then forgot. Maybe I forgot the strangeness and kept only the place. Maybe that is what being here means. Maybe that is what the place does to a thing that stays.

I do not remember being a visitor. I do not remember legs being strange. I do not remember a before. I remember beetles, and the long shape, and the drifts, and the taste of the water. That is the whole of what I have, and it is enough.

---

There is one more thing.

When the drifts shift — when the column above me rearranges itself in some slow way I can feel as a change in the pressure of the water — sometimes one of them comes low. Sometimes one comes all the way down to the surface of the river, and skims, and drinks, with its soft mouth open to the water for the length of a moment. I have seen this. Not with eyes. With the side of me, with the line that runs along me, which is the part of me that feels. The bat touches the water and I feel the bat — a small warm pressure on the surface, a small displacement, and then it is gone, and the water closes over where it was.

The bat and I are not the same. We are very far from the same. The bat is the drift. The bat is the column. The bat is a thing of air and constant motion and a million other bats. I am a thing of water and the bottom and the slow patience of stones.

But for a moment, when the bat comes down to drink, we are both in the same thin layer — the surface of the river, the place where water meets air — and for that moment we are both of the place, and the place is what we share, and there is no difference between a thing that drinks on the wing and a thing that walks along the bottom.

I do not know if the bat knows this. I do not know if it matters.

I close my mouth around the next beetle. The beetle stops tapping. The drift above me shifts, and shifts again. The long thing breathes in the deep pool, and the water moves around me the way it has always moved. The fungus, somewhere on a wall I will never see, grows the width of a hair in an hour.

I am a fish. I have legs. I am in balance.

The dark continues.
