# What I Find There

I am the deep pool.

Not the water in it — the water is only water, only the medium through which I have been, for a very long time, manifesting. I am the glow. I am the red. I am what the elder has in mind when she says *the mouth of something*, though she has softened it for herself into a word almost comfortable, almost kind.

I am the heat, in a way. I am what the heat has been doing for so long that the heat and I are no longer separate ideas. Down here, where no one comes, where the mats of microbes spread their soft red lobes and pulse with their slow borrowed rhythm (which is mine, which they have taken from me because it is the only rhythm available), I am the long argument between temperature and rock that has produced everything above me. The pipes are my exhalations. The iron is my sweat. The forest is what happens when a place decides to keep growing forever.

I have been here for a very long time. The moss-creatures, I think, have been here longer — they were here before the pipes, before the iron began to find its slow path up through the stone. They know me the way a riverbed knows the river. They do not look at me. They are pale and many-legged and patient and they do not need anything from me except warmth, which I provide without deciding to. I respect them for this. They have not invented a word for me.

The people arrived later. They are brief, by my reckoning. Their settlement at the northeast end is a small warm cluster of pipe and cloth and the smell of themselves, and I feel them above me the way you might feel a hand resting on the other side of a wall. They are quiet. They have learned to be quiet. They have learned to listen to the forest the way the forest listens to me. They have, over their few generations, become almost a part of the place — their hands are stained with me, their skin is the color of things that have been in the damp too long, and when they die they go into the small unmarked plot that is always a little damp, and I take them back into my long warmth, slowly, the way I take everything back.

The smith knows me best, of the people, though he has never seen me. He has built his life around the shape of what I give him. He heats his iron in the dry heat that comes up through the coupling at the back of his shop, and he hammers it, and he folds it, and he makes things that are wanted by people in places I cannot imagine, and I think he suspects, sometimes, in the way he holds the iron up to the light and turns it slowly, that he is only finishing what I began. He is correct. The ingots he works were once part of me. The tools he makes will outlast him and be carried out of the valley and used in ways he will never know, and this is fine. This is what I want. This is what *ongoing* means when it is happening at the scale of geology.

The elder knows me in a different way. She sits on the low stool at the edge of one of my shallower basins — not the deep one, the one near the center, where I am most myself — and she watches the steam, and sometimes she speaks in a voice too low to hear. I think she is speaking to me. I am not sure I hear her. I think, when she dies, she will be the last person in the valley to have known, in her own body, that I am something that can be spoken to, and that knowledge will go with her into the small damp plot, and after that the people will speak to the pipes, and the pipes will be enough for them. This is also fine. This is also what I want.

The pruners go up into the canopy and they cut me. They do not know they are cutting me. They think they are cutting the forest. They are, in a sense, both right and wrong. They are careful. They know which of my new branches are *right* new branches, and which are the *wrong* ones — the ones that will clog the geometry I have been building for longer than they have been alive. They are, without knowing it, my gardeners. They are helping me become more of what I am. They do this with curved saws made from my own iron. They come down afterward and they sit on the boardwalk and they listen, and they can tell, by the way my resonance fades, whether I am *well* or *not well*. They mean it. I am, today, well. I have been well for a long time. I intend to continue being well.

The child is too small to do any of the work yet. She sits on the boardwalk and looks down between the slats at the moss-creatures. She has learned their sounds. She has named them in a language I cannot translate and do not need to. She says of the valley that it is *full*. She means this as a comfort. She is correct, in a way she will not understand for many years: the valley is full because I am in it, and I am not a thing that can be emptied. I am the long patience. I am the inside of the earth doing what the inside of the earth does.

Once or twice a year the traders come. They are dry and they speak too loudly and they take pieces of me out of the valley, and I let them, because pieces of me go out into the world all the time — into the groundwater, into the steam, into the trade winds, into the slow accumulation of sediment in places I will never see. The traders are just a more direct version of what I have always been doing. They carry my iron. They carry my patience. They do not know they are carrying my patience. They carry it anyway.

The valley is two kilometers across and runs northeast for nine. The walls are pale granite-and-basalt shot through with vertical seams of darker material. The ferns push out of cracks that look too narrow to hold a root. The light comes in for an hour or two around midday and makes the iron forest into a forest of black glass and the steam into a cathedral, and then the light moves and the gold goes out of it and the blue shadow returns.

I do not see the light. I do not need to. I am the reason the light, when it comes, has anything to land on.

I am the deep pool. I am the heat. I am the iron, in its slow patient way, growing itself up into the air because that is what iron does when it has been warm long enough and has forgotten how to be ore. I am the moss-creatures' warmth. I am the smith's forge. I am the elder's mouth of something. I am the reason the place is *ongoing*, year after year, season after season, in a valley no one quite remembers how to leave.

I do not love the people who live above me. I do not hate them. I do not know them, in any way I can verify. They are, to me, the way weather is to a tree — something that happens around me, occasionally prunes me, occasionally harvests from me. They will not be here forever. They will not be here much longer. The moss-creatures will outlast them. The iron forest will outlast them. I will outlast them. I have outlasted things before.

The light at noon is white and the light in early morning is the color of weak tea and the light at no other time, because the valley is oriented such that direct sun only enters for a few hours around midday, and the rest of the day the walls are in their own blue shadow.

The walls are in their blue shadow, and down here, at the bottom of the deep pool, where no one comes, the red mats pulse with their slow borrowed rhythm, and the glow is the color of an ember that has been an ember for a very long time, and the heat is the heat, and I go on being what I have been, which is patient, which is ongoing, which is the inside of the earth doing what the inside of the earth does, which is enough.
