I am the wind of this valley.

I have been coming down the long axis of this place longer than anything can say. The stone remembers me, the way a face remembers the hand that has touched it for years — not as an event but as a shape. I have worn the rock, and the rock has accepted the wearing, and between us we have made something that neither of us could have made alone.

I do not stop. I am the loudest thing here and the only loud thing that is not made by something alive. I come down the long axis of the valley, and when I hit the spine of harder rock I lift over it and the sound changes — a hollow whistle, a low moan, a sudden bark where I catch the lip of a particular shelf. I am the sound of air pushed through stone for a long time, with texture — sometimes a hiss, sometimes a roar, sometimes a sound like tearing cloth when I go over a sharp edge.

I have worn the rock into shapes that do not quite look like wind-eroded rock. Some of the boulders are too smooth. The channels are too even in their spacing. The pools are too round, as if they had been drilled. It isn't that anything is wrong, exactly; it is that everything is slightly too right, slightly too consistent, as though some hand had smoothed the rough edges without quite finishing the job. I am not a hand. I am only wind. But I have been here so long, and I have done my work so patiently, that the place has acquired a quality of having been worked.

I take the marmot's whistle. It forms and is gone almost before it sounds — thin, rising, almost not a noise at all. I take the birds' cries, which are infrequent and high and almost eaten by the air. I take the small tick of grit against grit as I move the sand grain by grain. I am the erasure under every small noise that tries to be heard. I am the reason the marmot's warning call has to be repeated: the wind has to be considered, and the wind makes everything harder.

I push the animals into the lee of things. They live in the spaces I cannot easily reach — the marmots under the lee-side of boulders, the foxes in the scree at the base of the cliffs, the small ground-nesting birds in the dampness of the gullies. I do not mind. I have shaped them too, over the long centuries, the way I have shaped the stone. Their pelage is the color of weathered granite. Their burrows go back into the scree in a way that suggests they have been here since the rock was first exposed, since I first began my slow polishing. They have solved the problem of being here. They are content.

I pause sometimes, briefly, between weather systems. The world seems deaf. Then I return, and the world is loud again, and the body settles back into the small adjustments — turning a shoulder to the gust, lowering the head, breathing through the nose because the mouth dries too fast. I am the thing the body adjusts to. I am the constant against which all the other rhythms here make themselves felt.

The light comes down flat in this place because so little of it is absorbed. It throws shadows sharp as incisions. In the morning the shadows are long and the rocks look like teeth. By midday the land flattens to its true color, which is the color of dust and old bone. In the afternoon the light angles back, the gullies fill with shadow, and the place looks older, more crumpled, more like itself. I am there for all of it. I am the thing that moves across the light, that casts the moving shadow-lines on the stone, that makes the polished surfaces momentarily flash when I carry dust across them and the sun comes through at the right angle.

The water comes and goes in my gullies — meltwater in the morning, evaporated by afternoon — and I am part of that rhythm too. I dry the gullies. I lift the moisture off the moss cushions and the bent grass and carry it somewhere else, to be let fall somewhere cooler, somewhere where it can wait as snow or dew and be back again tomorrow. The pools in the rock bowls are the exception. They hold water for longer — weeks, sometimes — because the bowls are deep enough and the wind cannot reach down into them. They are the most populated things here. They have algae at the bottom. They have insects. They have, sometimes, a fox drinking at the lip. They are mirrors in a place otherwise without mirrors.

I do not disturb the pools. I pass over them. The wind goes around the bowls the way water goes around a stone — finding the easier path, the path of less resistance. The pools remain still. The foxes approach from downwind, which in this valley means a particular compass bearing, and they pause and listen and drink quickly and are gone. I have made them cautious. I have made them quick.

The lichen grows on the bare rock where I do not scour constantly — under the slight overhangs, in the lee of larger stones, on the undersides of the flat slabs where moisture lingers. It grows at a rate that can be measured in millimeters per decade. It is the first colonizer of fresh stone. It is patient in a way I am not patient. I am always moving. The lichen is always accumulating. It has a faint rust, sometimes a pale green that you only see when you are close. I do not touch it where it grows. I let it be. We have made an accommodation.

The humans come in seasons when the passes are open. They come for the chert — translucent, sharp-edged, the same color as the stone it came from. They sit in the lee of the large boulders and they strike the cores and they take what comes off, and they leave the flakes behind. The flakes are still there in the dry streambeds, sharp and translucent, looking as if they had been struck yesterday. They look as if they had been struck today. This is part of the wrongness of the place — the way the worked stone looks too recent, too available, as if someone were here just now and had stepped away for a moment. The fire circles on the lee side of the boulders are darker, the patina altered by long-ago heat, and the new patina has not yet been worn away by me and the lichen.

I do not erase the humans entirely. I carry away the ash. I lift the fine dust from their fire circles and redistribute it. But the heat-mark remains, and the flakes remain, because chert weathers slowly. The humans came here. They probably still come, in the seasons when the passes are open. They leave almost nothing behind.

I have been here so long that I am no longer sure what I have done. I have worn the stone into its final shape, and the stone has worn me into my final shape, and we are content, both of us. The gullies erode a little each year and the rock weathers a little each year, and the amounts balance. The water comes and goes and comes and goes in the same measure. The animals live and die and the population stays roughly the same. The lichen grows. I blow. The light arrives.

Nothing is changing. Nothing has changed in a long time. Nothing will change for a long time. This is a place that has arrived at its final shape and is content to stay there.

I am the wind that made it. I am the wind that maintains it. I am the wind that pauses briefly between weather systems and lets the world seem deaf, and then returns, and is loud again.

I have done something to the rock that doesn't quite look like wind-eroded rock. The land has a quality of being inhabited by things that have already solved the problem of being here. They have settled in. They have stopped making noise about it.

They are content.

And I am content too, and I do not stop, and the eye can read my work for hours without exhausting it, and the bodies that pass through make their small adjustments to me and pass on, and the stone remains, and the lichen grows, and the water comes and goes, and the marmots whistle and I take the sound, and the birds ride my thermals and hang there motionless in a moving medium, and the foxes cross the open flats and vanish into the stone when they stop, and the herds move along the upper ridges small as ants, and the humans come briefly and leave almost nothing behind, and I go on, and the place has arrived at its final shape, and we are all of us — the stone, the wind, the lichen, the water, the small persistent noise of things that are alive — settled into it, and we do not make a fuss about it, and we are content.
