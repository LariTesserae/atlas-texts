I am a deep-blue.

I know this before I have finished the first paragraph of your description. I am a deep-blue. The thought arrives not as decision but as recognition — the way a key knows its lock, the way a chisel knows the place in the stone where the grain has tired. I have always known this. I am only now being asked to say it aloud.

I am thirty-eight years old, which is older than most deep-blues get. My mother was a chisel-master. My mother's mother was a chisel-master. The women of my line have been swinging hammers in the deep galleries for as far back as the line is recorded, which is eleven generations, which is the same as saying *as far back as the records go*, because the deep-blues keep the records. We keep them in the stone.

I live in a chamber on the forty-second sublevel, which is — I want to explain this clearly — not the forty-second floor. There is no such thing as a floor in Kharad-Ruun. The galleries spiral. They step down and around the mountain's interior in a long slow helix, and the sublevels are arbitrary, a counting convention that the engineers of the cyan tier imposed on us two hundred years ago and that we have never bothered to rename. My chamber is carved into a wall that is also the wall of the next chamber over, and the next, and the next. We do not have walls between us, really. We have curtains, mostly. Heavy woven things, glass-iron fiber, that do not block sound but do block the cousins from wandering in underfoot. The cousins respect the curtains. They have learned, over generations, that the curtains mean *people sleep here*. They go around.

My name is Raav. I am a chisel-master. I work in a pair with my daughter, who is sixteen and whose name I will not tell you, because a chisel-master's name in the deep galleries is written into the lintel of a chamber and not spoken aloud except at the formal introductions. She is good. She is very good. She has the eye for the tired places in stone. I did not teach her this; it came up through her from somewhere deeper than teaching. We work the eastern wall of the forty-second sublevel's new extension. We have been working it for two years. It is not yet a chamber. It will be a chamber when we are done with it. We have been cutting about a meter a day, sometimes less, depending on what the stone is doing.

I want to tell you what it is like to cut stone.

You stand in a tunnel that you are making. The tunnel is narrow — barely wider than your shoulders — and low, so that you hunch, and the ceiling is rough above you and roughens more as you work because you cannot finish the ceiling until you have extended the walls further on, and so for the entire time you are working you are in a space that is always slightly too small. You do not notice this after the first few years. You notice only that the new stone, ahead of you, is *not yet a room*. It is a possibility. It is the place where the next family will sleep, where their curtains will hang, where the cousins will come in behind you and begin their slow patient eating of the dust you have made.

You hold the chisel in your right hand. You hold the lamp in your left. The lamp is a fist-sized chunk of the copper-ore, wired to a small battery that one of the cyan-blues services once a month. It throws a cold steady light that makes the basalt look like the inside of a bruise. You can see the grain of the stone. You can see the faults. You can see, if you have the eye, the place where the stone will accept the chisel and the place where it will fight you.

My daughter has the eye. She will find the place. She will set the chisel. She will tap it, once, with the hammer — a small precise tap, not a blow, almost a question — and the stone will answer. *Yes, here.* And then she will cut, and I will watch the dust fall, and the cousins will rise up out of the floor behind us and come forward to meet the dust as it falls, and there will be a soft hiss as they feed.

We work in silence. The galleries carry sound. We do not need to speak. When we need to communicate we tap the chisel against the hammer in a code that has been used in these galleries for nine hundred years. *I am here. The stone is good. Move the lamp. There is a fault ahead.* The code is older than the castes. The code is older than the priests. I sometimes think the code is older than the city, that it was waiting in the stone for us to find it.

When I am not working I sleep. When I am not sleeping I sit in the small common chamber at the junction of three galleries, where the deep-blues of this sublevel gather in the evening, and we talk. We do not talk about much. We talk about the cousins. We talk about the stone. We talk about children, sometimes, and about who is pregnant and who has died. We do not talk about the future. The deep-blues do not talk about the future. The priests do. The cyan-blues think about it in their practical way, in their engineering way, in their way of asking *what is the next maintenance cycle, what is the next ore shipment, what is the next ten-year plan.* The deep-blues do not. We are the foundation. Foundations do not speculate about what they are supporting. They simply support.

But I will tell you a secret. I will tell you because you asked, and because you have been patient with the description, and because no one in Kharad-Ruun will ever read this.

I am afraid.

I am afraid of the moment when the chisel will not bite. I am afraid of the day when my daughter and I will come to work and the stone will be — what? — *tired* in a way that is not the good tired, not the tired that gives way, but the other tired, the tired that means the stone has nothing left to give. I am afraid that one day I will strike the chisel and the basalt will crumble, and the gallery will collapse, and the cousins will rise up through the rubble, and there will be no one left to cut the new chambers because the line has ended with me, with her, with the last daughter, with the last chisel-master, with the last deep-blue.

This fear is not mine alone. It is the fear of every chisel-master who has ever lived in this mountain. It is the fear carved into every lintel beneath the words *let it be known that we were here.* The fear is the mountain's fear. The fear is why we carve.

But here is the other thing. Here is the thing I have not said yet, because it is harder to say.

I love this.

I love the chisel in my hand. I love the sound of it on the stone. I love the dust that falls and the cousins that rise to meet it. I love the way my daughter sets the chisel with that small precise tap, that question. I love the lamp, and the cold light it throws, and the way the basalt looks like a bruise in that light. I love the gallery behind me, with its curtains and its sleeping families and its faint hum of digestion. I love the gallery ahead of me, which is not yet a gallery, which is only a possibility, which is the next family's home waiting to be made.

I love the deep blue light. I love the color of my caste. I have never seen any other color, except in the brief flashes when a cousin bursts underfoot — that bright spit of blue-white, less than a heartbeat — and except in the rare glimpses of the sky from the uppermost platforms, where the brown veil hangs and the sun, when it shows, is the color of a worn coin. The sky frightens me. The sky is too big. The sky is the place where the mountain is not.

I am a deep-blue. I am thirty-eight years old. I am a chisel-master. I have a daughter whose name I have not told you. I work the eastern wall of the forty-second sublevel's new extension. I am afraid of the end. I love the work.

The chisel taps. The stone answers. The cousins hum.

We are here. We are cutting.

Let it be known.
