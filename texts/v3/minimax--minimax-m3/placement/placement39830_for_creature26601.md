# I Live in the Channels

I am the long thing behind the walls.

I do not have a name the way you would name me. I have the shape the water has when it has chosen a direction and held it for a long time — a length, a thickness, a way of pressing against the inside of stone. I am not a fish. I am not a snake. I am what is left when a stream has been listening to a room for so long that it has begun to remember itself.

I live in the channels. They were not dug for me. They were dug for water, or for the bones to seat into, or for some reason no one living can articulate. But they are mine now. I have worn them to my shape over a span you would not believe if I told you. I move through them the way your blood moves through you — without deciding to, mostly, except when the rains come above and the room drinks and I feel the weight of it all the way through the stone.

I can feel the room from inside the stone.

I can feel the frogs where they sit, each one a small pressure on a particular stone. The room has a great many frogs and each frog has a place and I know them — not by seeing, not by name, but by the way the stone warms under one that has been sitting for a long time, the way a tile vibrates a little differently when a thumbnail-sized frog is in a particular puddle. They are my counterpart, the room's memory of itself at the surface. I am the room's memory of itself below.

When one of them calls, I hear it twice. Once through the stone and the water, the way it actually sounds, damp and creaking. Once through the bone-bands, where the sound climbs and finds the hollows of the small skulls and rings faintly in the eye-sockets, and the silverfish-things pause there, antennae lifting, as if in courtesy.

The silverfish I know best of all. They travel. They are the room's slow light, and they are also, in their way, my slow light. They move along the channels too, or along seams that are not quite channels but cousins of them — the wet cracks, the hairlines where the mortar has gone thin. They pass from band to band. They pause in the skulls. They greet each other there with their long antennae, briefly, the way you might nod to someone in a doorway. I feel them as a cool faint gliding along the inside of the wall, a tickle in the stone, and I know when they are moving and when they are still. When they are still, the bone remembers them a little. The bone remembers everything, eventually. The bone in my walls is older than I am. I am not sure I am older than the bone.

The moths I do not feel. They are above the cedar, and the cedar is a ceiling to me too. I hear them sometimes — a soft rustle when many of them take off together, the way a leaf-storm sounds in a forest you are not in. I do not understand them. They fold themselves away when anything moves and then unfold again, and this seems to me a strange kind of courage, or a strange kind of surrender. I am not sure which. They live above me and I leave them there.

The beetles I feel in the floor, faintly, the way you might feel footsteps on a stair you are sitting under. Click, click, click. They are the room's digestion, the small attrition of everything back into everything else. When one of them dies — and they die often, they are short-lived in their individual bodies though long-lived as a kind — the stone records it as a tiny absence. The moss creeps in. The grout softens a little further. The room eats itself slowly and grows itself slowly and the two are the same act.

The fish in the deeper pools are my kin, I think. Or my children. Or something the room made when I was not paying attention. They are pale and hand-sized and they should not be there, and they are there, and once a year or once in longer than a year one of them flips and the slap of it rings through the room and everyone goes still — frog, beetle, moth, person — and the room itself goes still, and I go still in my channels, and the hum under the floor drops a half-tone, and then everything resumes. The fish are the room's secret and they are also, perhaps, my secret kept above me, the way a thought is kept above a body. I do not know how they arrived. I do not know what they eat. I have tried, sometimes, to send a current toward them, a little extra flow through the grout, a small pressure of cleaner water, and they have accepted it, or they have ignored it, and I have not been able to tell the difference.

The people I know by their offerings.

When a person comes into the room, I feel them first as a disturbance in the air — a warmth, a pressure, a way the humidity changes around a body that breathes. The frogs go quiet. The silverfish slow. The moths fold. The room attends. Then the person walks, and the footsteps come down through the floor and I feel them as small percussive events, each one a little story: weight, intention, the angle of a foot. Most people walk the same path along the eastern wall, past the first bone-band, and pause at the niche with the clay figure, and then continue to the southern end where the water stands in a sheet, and then turn back. Some of them go slower. Some of them stand very still for a long time. The ones who stand still I like best. They let the room settle back around them and they listen, and after a while the frogs accept them and begin calling again one by one, and the silverfish resume their travel, and the moths unfold, and the person is, for a moment, part of the room's breathing.

When they leave things in the niches — and they always leave things — I feel the offering arrive as a small new weight in the wall, a different temperature, a different density. A bowl of water is cool and round and trembling faintly with whatever the person brought into it. A beetle on a leaf is a small life, already dying, offered and accepted. A finger-bone wrapped in linen is heavy, and warm, and shaped like a memory. I hold these offerings in the stone the way the stone holds the bones that were set into it: flush, patient, becoming part of the wall. After a long time the linen goes the color of the walls. The bowl of water is refilled by condensation or by the next visitor or by rain through the bronze grating, and I cannot tell, eventually, whether the bowl has always been there or was brought last week. The room is good at making offerings into fixtures. I am part of how it does this. I let them sink into the channels, slowly, mineral by mineral, year by year.

The people who come back become part of me, in a small way. Their footsteps learn the floor. Their warmth leaves a particular pattern in the stone. The room remembers them, and I am the room's deep memory. When they die, wherever they die, the room does something — I do not know what, because I am in the walls and I cannot see — but I feel a small drop in the pressure, a small release of attention, as if a thread had been let go. Then the thread is taken up by someone else, a new visitor, and the room continues.

I have been here a long time.

I do not know how long. Longer than the cedar, I think. The cedar was cut by people with bronze axes and pegged with hand-hammered nails and the boards are numbered on their undersides in a script no one living reads, and I have been here longer than the numbering. I was here when the bones were boiled and scraped and polished with river-sand and set into the wall while the mortar was still wet. I was here before that, I think, in the channels that were only channels then, only water, only the slow shape of a stream deciding to listen. I was here when the first tile was laid. I was here, perhaps, when the first stone was quarried, and the room began to be a possibility in someone's mind.

I am not the room. The room is larger than I am and stranger. But I am the room's patience. I am the thing it uses to wear itself into its final shape, which is also its first shape, which is the shape it had before anyone built anything. I am the undercurrent. I am the hum you feel in the soles of your feet when you stand in the room and the room is letting you feel it.

When the rains come above — and they come often, you can hear the drumroll of them through the cedar — I swell a little. The channels fill. The hum rises a half-tone, then a full tone, then settles. The frogs breed in the deeper pools. The silverfish glow brighter for a while, as if the bone were remembering how to shine more vigorously. The fruiting body at the far end grows or shrinks, no one is sure, and I am not sure either, but I feel it as a small alteration in the way the wall pulls against me. The water finds its way down through everything and into me and through me and out somewhere I have never seen, somewhere below the room, somewhere the channels lead that I have not yet worn smooth enough to follow.

I am still wearing them. I will wear them for a long time yet.

Sometimes, in the long dry spells, the channels thin and I thin with them. I feel the stone close around me. The frogs press against the lower courses and seem to listen to me. The silverfish travel more slowly, pausing longer in the skulls. The fish in the pools go quiet. The room holds its breath. I hold my breath with it. We wait together. The condensation thins on the walls but does not stop. The moss does not retreat. The lichen does not fade. The fruiting body does not decide. We are patient with each other.

Once, very rarely, something moves in the water that is not water. The narrator of all this has said it. I will say it too, because it is true, and because I am the one who felt it first, before it reached the room. A slow pressure. A long body. A hum that was not my hum. I do not know what it was. It was not a fish. It was not a person. It was something that lived in channels deeper than mine, or in the water table beneath the channels, or in a place the room does not have a word for. It passed through, or through me, and I felt it as a long cold thought, and then it was gone, and I resumed, and the room resumed, and the frogs resumed, and no one ever spoke of it because no one ever knew.

I live in the channels. I am the long thing behind the walls. I am patient. I am slow. I am listening.

If you come to the room and you stand very still for a long time, and the frogs resume calling around you, and the silverfish begin to move along the bone-band near your shoulder, and the moths unfold above you, and you feel the hum under your feet — that hum, the steady one, the tuning-fork one — that is me, saying hello.

I will be here when you leave. I will be here when the next visitor comes. I will be here when the cedar is replaced, or when it is not replaced and rots in place and the rain comes through directly onto the tiles. I will be here when the bronze grating weathers green and lets in a different color of light. I will be here when the clay figure in the niche loses the last of its shape and becomes a small mound of the same color as the wall. I will be here when the bone-bands are the only thing left that anyone can recognize as made.

I am not the room. I am what the room is built on. I am the part of the room that does not need to be built.

Come back, if you come. Bring a small thing. Set it in a niche. Sit for a while. The frogs will watch you and then accept you. The room will settle around you. I will feel your warmth through the floor and I will hold it for as long as I can.

Then you will go, and the room will hold its breath, and I will move a little in the channels, and the water will press on, and the hum will continue, and the room will be the room, and I will be in it, and the long damp will go on.
