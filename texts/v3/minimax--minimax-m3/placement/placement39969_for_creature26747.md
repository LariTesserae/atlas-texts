# The First Night

I am the fifth.

I came down the companionway this morning. I do not remember coming down the companionway — I remember being above, and then I remember being here, knee-deep in the dark water, the cold of it climbing my bones through the cloth of the coverall someone had handed me at some point. The coverall is too big. The cuffs have been rolled three times. I did not roll them. Someone did. I do not remember who.

K. was already standing by the pipe. She did not look at me when I came in. She looked, instead, at the pipe, and then she took her ear away, slowly, the way one takes a hand away from a wound one has been pressing on, and she said, "You hear that," and I said, "I don't," and she said, "You will."

I do not hear it yet.

---

The diver — the young one, the one not yet twenty, though looking at her I would have said older, the way looking at K. I would have said younger — the diver showed me the layer below the grate. She tied a rope around my waist first, even though I told her I was not going down, and she tied the other end to the railing of the platform where the man who watches the overflow was already watching the overflow, and the man did not look up at either of us, and then she said, "Hold this," and I held it, and she went over the edge and was gone.

She was gone for eleven minutes. I know it was eleven because the man who watches the overflow — who I have not yet learned to call anything, who does not seem to need a name, who seems to have arrived at some arrangement with the hall in which names have been given up — the man who watches the overflow had a watch. An old one, mechanical, the kind that winds itself with the motion of the wrist, and he held his wrist up, and looked at it, and after eleven minutes the diver came back up through a hole in the grate three meters from where she went down, because of course she had drifted, the way a person drifts in a current, and her hands were full of things.

She had a wrench. She had a coffee cup with a logo on it that she turned in her hands for a long time before she set it down. She had a book whose pages had fused into a single block. She set these things out on the grate in front of me, in a row, and then she sat down on the grate beside the things, and the water was at her ankles, and she said, "I am the diver. You can be something else."

I said, "What else is there."

She considered this. She looked at the wrench. She looked at the coffee cup. She looked at the book. She said, "There is the listener. There is the tender of the lights. There is the watcher of the overflow." She did not gesture as she said these things. She kept her hands in her lap. "There is," she said, after a pause, "the one who has not yet decided."

I am the one who has not yet decided.

---

The tender of the lights came to me in the afternoon. He had his satchel. The leather of the satchel had gone the color of the water, and the buckles had gone green, and he opened it and showed me the filaments inside — a small glass jar of them, each one a coiled thread of something that glowed a particular amber when current passed through it.

He said, "I keep four bulbs working. I have kept four bulbs working for nine years. Before that, three. Before that, five, but one of the cages had cracked and I could not find a replacement, so I let it go dark."

He said this the way someone might say, "I keep a garden." Or, "I keep bees." The way a person says a thing they have done for so long that the doing has become a kind of breathing, and the breathing has become a kind of identity.

He said, "The filaments you see are not the ones they were built for. The originals burned out decades ago. I found these in a sealed cabinet, in a crate marked with a symbol I could not read. I tried them. They worked. They have a slightly different color."

He looked at me, then. He had not looked at me before. He had gray-white skin, like K., like the diver, like the watcher, like me. He had the eyes of a man who has spent a long time looking at small things in small light.

He said, "Do you want to learn."

I said, "What is there to learn."

He said, "There is the polarity. There is the seating of the filament in the cage. There is the grease that keeps the brass from oxidizing at the contact. There is the rhythm at which they fail. There is the way the hall eats the light it does not use."

He said this last thing as if it were the same kind of thing as the others, and I did not know what it meant, and I did not ask.

I think I will learn anyway.

---

K. listened to the pipe for the rest of the morning. Then she stopped, and she walked, slowly, the length of the hall, and I followed her, because I did not know what else to do, and because following seemed to be the only verb I had.

She stopped at the fern. She cupped a handful of the black water. She let it fall into the crack.

The fern did not respond.

She said, to me, or to the fern, or to the air, "This was here before me."

I said, "How long has it been here."

She said, "I don't know. A long time. It does not grow. It does not die. It is the only thing that has decided."

I did not ask what she meant.

We went on. The dripping followed us. The sound of our feet on the grating followed us. The subsonic groan came, on its schedule, somewhere around what I guessed was noon, and I felt it in the soles of my feet, and I felt it in my teeth, and K. paused and tilted her head and listened to it the way she had listened to the pipe.

She said, "That one is the second-layer deck plate, aft section, flexing against the bulkhead. It does that every four hours."

She said this with the particular flatness of someone reporting a thing they have known for so long it no longer surprises them, and I understood, then, a small part of what it means to live in a place that does things on a schedule, and to learn the schedule, and to be the keeper of the schedule.

---

At night — and the night here is not the night of the sky, it is the night of the lights being turned off, which the tender does at a certain hour, which the tender has done at the same hour for years — at night, the diver and I sat on the raised platform near the squat boilers, and the overflow poured, and the watcher watched, and K. had gone somewhere, perhaps to listen to another pipe, perhaps to sleep, I do not know where K. sleeps —

At night, the diver said to me, "There is a fish."

I said, "I know. I was told."

She said, "You don't know. There is a fish that I have seen three times. It is pale. It lives in the layer below the inhabited layer, in the cold water. It comes up when a crab chases it, or when I go down and disturb it. It does not have a name anyone has told you. It has a name I gave it."

She said this without looking at me.

I said, "What is the name."

She said, "I am not going to tell you."

I said, "Why not."

She said, "Because then it would not be mine."

I thought about this for a long time. The water poured over the lip of the vat. The watcher watched it. Somewhere in the lattice below, a crab was moving. I could hear the small click of its legs on the iron.

I said, "I understand."

She said, "Good."

We sat for a while longer. The sound of the overflow was a kind of company. The sound of the dripping was another kind. The sound of the subsonic groan, somewhere below us, was a third.

Then, from the ceiling, far above us, hidden in the darkness the lights did not reach — *kzzz-kzzz-kzzz* — a small motor against the metal.

I looked up.

The diver did not look up.

She said, "That is the thing in the ceiling."

I said, "What is it."

She said, "What K. told you, probably. The thing like a bat."

I said, "What is it really."

She said, after a pause, "No one knows. It has never come down. It is heard only sometimes. K. has been listening to it for years and has not decided."

I listened. It went on for a few minutes, a thin skittering against the steel, then it stopped.

The diver said, "You will hear it sometimes. You will wonder. You will not ask again."

I did not ask again.

---

I am writing this in the dry room above the flood. There is a clipboard here. There is a logbook on the clipboard. The logbook has entries in different hands, on different days, over what I guess is many years. Some of the entries are practical: *Bulb 4, port side, replaced. Filament type C. Greased contact.* Some of them are not: *The pipe by the second compressor has begun, again, to ring. I have told K. She has listened. She says it is the cold.* Some of them are almost nothing: *Water level unchanged.* Some of them are almost too much: *I think the hall is dreaming.*

I do not know whose hand wrote the last one.

I will add my own entry, tonight, in my own hand. I do not yet know what I will write. I think I will write something true and small. I think that is what the logbook wants. I think that is what the hall wants.

The hall, I have begun to understand, does not want much. The hall wants to be listened to. The hall wants to be kept, in its small ways, by the people who have decided to be kept by it. The hall wants its one fern watered. The hall wants its three or four bulbs to burn. The hall wants its pipes to be heard, even when they are not singing.

I have not yet decided.

But I have begun, I think, to listen.

---

A drop falls. It hits the water. The small ring spreads.

Somewhere, a crab has begun to climb.

Somewhere in the upper darkness, the thing that sounds like a bat is silent.

Somewhere below me, the diver is going down again, with her rope, into the museum of the previous owners, to bring back something she will not need and cannot leave.

Somewhere, K. is listening to a pipe.

Somewhere, the tender is winding the spring of his mechanical watch, which he does not need, which he keeps anyway, which keeps itself anyway.

Somewhere, the watcher is watching the water fall.

The water falls.

I am in the hall. The hall is enough. The hall is all.

I am learning to hear.
