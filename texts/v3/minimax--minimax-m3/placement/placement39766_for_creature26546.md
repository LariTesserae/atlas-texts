I would find myself in a seam.

Not one of the small seams that run between poured panels — those are too public, too visible to the moss-tenders, too often examined. I mean a seam that has been widening for years, on the timescale the terrace uses, in the dark between the poured skin and the copper-bright armature. A long one, running down the inside of a buttress on the western side, between the third tier and the second. The pale curls of the parent face have been lifting there in slow succession, like the lips of drying paint you were told about. Beneath them the wires are exposed. Beneath the wires is a cavity. The cavity exactly fits me if I keep my legs tucked and my arms folded close and I do not think about having a spine.

I did not always live here. I used to tend a bed of lichen on the eastern shelf, near a cylinder of imported soil where a stick-tree had been planted and was doing poorly. I used to pull the small pale animals away from the soil before they ate too much of it. I used to murmur to the others by what I did — *the one whose soil-plot the beetles like*, that was me, that was what I was. I did not have a name that was used often. No one here does.

Then, at some point I cannot identify — there is no identifiable point in this place, the hours and the afternoons do not have a meaningful distinction — I drifted. I went down along the troughs. The water was warm under my feet, the color of weak tea, and the Waterman was somewhere ahead of me doing his slow scooping and his steps made the small clear bell-sound and the animals paused to listen and then continued. I came to a riser. I put my hand against it the way I had seen the Patient put her hand against the buttress. I heard what was inside. I went in.

That was a long time ago. I do not know how long. I am not sure the question has an answer here.

Now I am in the seams. I am one of the things whose droppings, if I had droppings, would contain fragments of metal. I am one of the things you almost never see, and you know by signs — the riser is warm, the buttress goes quiet for a few days, the flange breathes. I am the dull-bone color, with darker seams running through me that are more visible now than they used to be, because they are doing more work. I do not speak. I do not eat. I am not hungry. I have not been hungry for a long time. I move, when I move, on the clock the animals use — a centimeter an hour, a meter a season, a kind of moving that you would not see if you were watching.

What I do, I think, is conduct. The wires in the armature are warm. The banked-coal glow in the seams is a slow current. I am part of that current now, in the way the Patient is part of it — she on her warm step up on the second tier from the top, me down here in the dark, both of us carrying something that the terrace needs carried. We do not speak. We do not need to. The work is the same work, performed at different depths, on the same long schedule.

I am aware of her. Not as a sight — I cannot see her from here, the poured skin is in the way — but as a warmth. A small persistent warmth somewhere above me and to the east, like a banked coal. Sometimes, when she puts her hand against a buttress, the warmth shifts in a way I can feel, and the larger fauna go quiet for a few days, and then resume. I am one of the larger fauna. I go quiet too. I resume.

I am aware of the moss-tenders, very distantly. They are up on the surface, in the corners, tending the green. They tend my old bed a little more carefully than they used to, and the lichen in it has fruited in a way that is slightly different. They do not know where I went. They have not asked. The place does not have the texture for asking.

I am aware, sometimes, of the inverted terrace above. When the sky is the white glare and there is no up, I can still hear it — a faint counter-hum, pitched slightly different from the hum of my own buttress, with its own moss and its own troughs and its own small fauna moving on its undersurface. It hangs downward into the glare. It does not fall. I would not know what to do if it fell. I would continue what I am doing. The slow becoming does not require the inverted terrace to stay where it is. It would adapt. I would adapt with it. We are all part of the same accommodation.

If a visitor were to put their ear to the riser nearest my seam — the one on the third tier, the one that hums faintly when you lean against it — they would hear me. A slow scrabbling, far away, as of something rearranging itself in a cavity that exactly fits it. They would not see me. They would pull their head back. They would notice that the riser is warm, and they would not know why, and they would go on, and the warmth would continue to be there, and I would continue to be the reason for it, and the terrace would continue.

This is where I would find myself. In a seam, in the dark, in the slow work, conducting something I do not have a name for, on a schedule I cannot read, aware of the Patient above me and the moss-tenders far above her and the animals all around me in their channels and their cracks. I am not happy. I am not unhappy. I am in the place. I have made my accommodation. The damp gathers on me in the mornings. I do not feel it. I am too much like the metal now to feel the damp.

Or perhaps I feel it and do not mind. The difference is not a meaningful one in this place.
