*From the journal of someone who is no longer keeping a journal, written in a hand that does not quite match the one on the earlier pages:*

I came up over the last rise in the late afternoon, when the light was already beginning to do the thing it does here — falling along the ground instead of across it, lying on the moss like something spilled. I had been walking for what I think was three days. I do not know that it was three days. I had a small pack and a stone for a knife and a clay bottle of something I had been carrying since a town whose name I have misplaced. I was tired in the way that you are tired here, which is not the tiredness of the body so much as the tiredness of the arrangement of the body, the joints, the places where the bones meet.

The town of Inhôk was below me. I knew it was Inhôk because there was a well in the square, and the well had a lid that lifted off, and from where I stood I could see the lid had been lifted off recently and not replaced. I could see the smoke from the temple. I could see a woman standing in a doorway looking out at the fog that was just beginning to form in the river valley to the north. The fog was the color of the moss. The woman was the color of the doorway.

I came down into town. I will not describe the coming down. I will only say that when I arrived at the first house on the main street, a house of the grey-green stone with a heavy door on rusted pintles, an old man was sitting on a bench outside it. He had the face of someone who has looked at the same walls for a long time. He did not greet me. He gestured, with one hand, toward the door of the house, and the gesture meant *the stew is inside* and also meant *you were expected* and also meant *come in because the cold is moving in*.

I went in.

I have been here for what I am beginning to think is a long time. The plate is grey. The stew is grey. The cloth the people wear is the color of the cloth they wear. The lamp in the room where I sleep gives a light that does not seem to come from the flame but from somewhere else in the room, and the flame is only the excuse for it. The window of the room looks out onto a courtyard that is not on the outside of the building. I do not go into the courtyard. I have been in this town for what feels like a winter and what feels like an afternoon. I cannot tell which.

The woman who offered me the stew on the first evening comes to my room sometimes. She does not knock. The door is always open when I am here, or it has no door, or I have forgotten whether it had a door. She sits on the edge of the bed and does not speak for a long time and then says something about the weather of the plateau, and the weather of the plateau is always the same weather, but she speaks of it as if it had just been decided, as if the cold had been arranged this morning for the first time, as if the fog in the valley were an experiment. I listen. I do not answer. She does not seem to require an answer.

There is a small pale fish in the stream that runs along the back of the town. I went to look at it yesterday, or this morning. The fish was holding its position in the current. I put my hand in the water. The fish was colder than the water. I withdrew my hand. The fish remained. The fish is the same fish I saw when I first came to this town, and the fish is not the same fish. I am beginning to lose the distinction. I am beginning to lose the distinction between the fish and a stone in the stream, between the stone and the moss around the stone, between the moss and the cold that sits in the moss, between the cold and the woman on the edge of the bed, between the woman and the doorway she stands in, between the doorway and the town, between the town and the plateau.

*Here the handwriting changes. The letters are slightly larger. The strokes are slower. There is a faint tremor in the downstrokes that may be age or may be cold or may be the slow movement of a hand that has been holding a pen for a very long time.*

I do not think I will leave.

I do not think I will leave because I no longer entirely remember what leaving would mean, what *away* would mean, what the absence of the moss and the grey-green stone would mean. I do not think I will leave because the woman who comes to my room said, last night, or this morning, *the land moves, you know*, and she said it the way you say something to a person who has known it for a long time and is only now admitting it, and I found that I was nodding, and I found that the nodding came from somewhere deeper than my neck, somewhere in the arrangement of me, in the joints of me, in the place where the cold has been sitting.

*The next entry is in handwriting that is not a handwriting. It is the impression of handwriting. It is the shape that handwriting leaves in a thing when the thing is the only material that will hold still long enough. There are no words. There is only the slow curl of a line that begins near the top of the page and ends near the bottom of the page, and the line goes nowhere in particular, and the line is patient, and the line is the color of lichen, and the line is still here.*

*On the next page, in a hand that may be the hand of the woman, or may be the hand of someone who has not yet arrived, or may be the hand of the plateau itself, which has learned, in its long cold way, to write:*

He is still here.

He is the kind of here that does not go away.

*Below this, in the very corner of the page, in a different ink, older, the color of dried blood, in a script that is older than the town:*

*welcome back*
