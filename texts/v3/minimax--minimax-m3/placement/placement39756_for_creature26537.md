I am the woman who sits on the third rooftop from the cistern, in the bend where the old bakery's chimney used to be before we took it down for the planking.

That is not how anyone else names me. The others call me by a name I will not give you, because the name is for them and for the rope and for the particular way my hands work a splice. But it is useful for you, I think, to know that I have a place. Every person here has a place — a roof, a corner of a roof, a small region of planking between two uprights where one is allowed to put one's weight and not be in anyone's way. I have mine. I have had it since my mother stopped needing it, which was eleven years ago now, or twelve, and the lichen has had time to grow along the southern edge where the morning fog sits heaviest. I sweep the lichen sometimes. Not because it is unwelcome — the lichen is welcome, the lichen keeps the tile from spalling in the wet — but because sweeping is something to do with the hands in the early hour, before the pole is taken up and the day's work begins.

I was not born to this. I was born three floors down, in what is now a courtyard-pond with a fig tree rising through it. My mother told me, once, that when I was small, the water still lapped at the threshold of the room where I was born, but did not cross it. By the time I had words for the room, the floor was already under a hand's depth of silt, and the doorway had become a little channel through which the catfish nosed at evening. I do not remember the room. I remember the silt. I remember the particular cool of the silt at depth, when a child puts her hand down into it past the wrist and feels the chill that comes from below. I remember the taste of the silt, because children taste everything, and the silt tastes of iron and of something older, of the lime and the fired clay of the floors that were floors before they were pond-bottoms.

I am not old. I am not young. I have a daughter who poles the boat better than I do, and a brother whose work is the causeways, and a cousin who keeps the vines pruned along the eastern wall of what used to be — we say *used to be*, and then we do not say what it used to be, because saying it does not bring it back and also does not quite let it go. We have a way of speaking about the older rooms that is not quite nostalgia and not quite indifference. It is closer to the way one speaks about a relative who has died: with the recognition that they were, and the understanding that one will not see them again, and the small daily practice of carrying their names in one's mouth so that they are not quite forgotten. My cousin can tell you, if you ask, the names of three streets that are now canals. He cannot tell you what the streets were like to walk on. None of us can. The walking is gone out of the place the way the iron is gone out of the place, and what remains is the names, and the names are enough, and the names are also not enough.

You have seen us from your high rooftop, the one where the canopy thins. You have watched us for several days. You have written, I think, a great deal. I have not read what you have written. I will not read it. This is not unfriendly. It is only that the writing is yours, the way the boats are ours and the causeways are ours and the lichen on my roof is mine. Each thing belongs to the one who tends it. You have tended nothing here. You have looked. Looking is its own work and I will not take it from you. But you have not tended.

What I want to tell you is what you could not have seen from up there.

You saw the fog. The fog is real and the fog is also not the place. The fog is the place's surface, the way the water is the place's floor and the canopy is the place's ceiling. We live between those three, and we have learned to read all three. The fog tells us the weather for the next three days. The water tells us the weather for the next season. The canopy tells us the weather for the next year — when a crown of leaves thins, we know the tree is putting its strength into root or fruit; when a crown thickens, we know it has found what it wanted in the soil. We read these the way your people, perhaps, read the sky.

You saw the quiet. The quiet is real. The quiet is also the loudest thing about this place, and you would not understand that unless you had lived in it. There is a kind of quiet that is empty and a kind of quiet that is full, and this is the full kind. It is the quiet of a place that is paying attention. A wall is listening to itself, all the time, to know if it is still a wall. A vine is listening to the tile it has chosen. A fish is listening to the surface. The fog is listening to the air. We are listening, too, all of us, to the small sounds that tell us whether the place is holding. When you live in a place that is half-swallowed, you learn to listen the way a sailor learns to read the water: not for the great events, which announce themselves, but for the small drifts, the small loosenings, the small sighs. The sigh of a plank that has gone soft in the middle. The creak of a wall that has begun to lean. The particular hush that comes when a chimney has just stopped being a chimney and has started being a slow column of water. We hear these. We hear them all the time. They are not loud. They are not dramatic. They are the quietest sounds in the place. But we hear them.

You did not see the smell. I am surprised you did not write about the smell, because the smell is what the place is, for us, more than the sight of it. The smell of the warm water. The smell of the silt, which is different at the bottom from at the top. The smell of the vines — each vine has its own smell, and the smell changes when the vine flowers, and changes again when the fruit sets, and changes again when the fruit falls into the water and the water takes it down. The smell of the smoke from our fires, which is a sweet smoke because we burn the old planks, and the old planks are of the fruitwood, and the fruitwood smells of the fruit it once bore. The smell of the fish, which is strongest in the morning when the catch is laid out on the rooftop racks. The smell of the rope-fibre being twisted, which is green and sharp and reminds me, without my meaning it to, of the smell of my mother's hands when she was working. The smell of the lichen, which is faint and old and of the world before the water. We smell all of these. The smell of the place is its weather, the way the fog is its sky.

You saw us as patient. We are patient, but it is not the patience of waiting. It is the patience of working. There is a difference. A person who is waiting is doing nothing, and is looking outward, toward whatever it is they are waiting for. A person who is working is doing something, and is looking inward, toward the thing in their hands. We are looking inward. We are looking at the splice, at the wall, at the vine, at the fish on the line. We are not waiting for the frogs. The frogs will come when the place is ready for them, and the place will be ready for them when the water has cooled enough at depth and the silt has compacted enough to hold the spawn, and we cannot make this happen faster by wanting it. We want it. We also want the morning's catch to come up clean, and the causeway to hold through the day's traffic, and the vine over the eastern window to be pruned before it lifts the next tile. These things we can affect. These things we work at. The frogs are not among the things we can work at. The frogs are among the things we wait for, but waiting, for us, is only the name we give to working on what we cannot work on. We do not sit and watch the water for frogs. We mend the causeway, and while we are mending it we are also, in some part of our attention, listening for the first one.

I think this is what you could not see. You saw the patience and thought it was passivity. It is not passivity. It is the most active thing I know.

You saw the smallness of our tools and thought they were a deprivation. They are not a deprivation. A tool that breaks and can be replaced from what the place provides is a freer tool than one that requires the smelter and the mine and the long road to bring it here. When my brother's adze-handle cracks, he takes a piece of the straight-grained wood from the stack on the high roof and splits it and fits it, and the new handle is a good handle. When my daughter's fish-hook wears thin at the bend, she takes a fishbone from the smoking-rack and shapes a new one with the chipped-stone knife, and the new hook is a good hook. We are not poor in our tools. We are rich in our tools, the way a forest is rich in its trees: because the supply is in the place, and the supply renews itself, and the making of the tool is a thing one can do oneself, and the knowing how to do it is a thing one can pass down. There is no smelter here. There is no mine. There is also no smith we must wait for, no trader we must bargain with, no road we must send a child down to fetch the iron from. Our tools are of the place. Our tools are *us*, in a way that iron tools were never us, because iron tools were always something brought in.

I think you thought we were a people who had lost something. We have not lost something. We have lost everything that was not the place, and what remains is the place, and the place is enough. This is not a tragedy. A tragedy is when a people lose what they are. We have not lost what we are. We are what we were, but what we were has become a different thing, the way a tree that has been pruned becomes a different tree: the same tree, but the branches are arranged to the new light. The light changed. The branches arranged themselves. We are not the same as we were. We are also not other than we were. We are the continuation of what we were, in a place that has continued itself.

You wrote that the geologists of some future age will find the foundations and call it a lake village. They will be wrong. We are not a lake village. A lake village is a village built on the edge of a lake, by people who chose the lake for the fishing and the trade. We are a city that the lake chose. The difference is everything. A lake village can be moved. We cannot be moved. To move would be to leave the rooftops and the causeways and the rooftop soil that took decades to make, and the fig tree my mother planted from a cutting she brought down from the slopes, and the place where my father's ashes are, in the silt of what was the kitchen, where the catfish now nest. To move would be to become refugees from our own continuance. We are not refugees. We are inheritors.

You asked, at the end of your writing, what I am. You asked it in the manner of someone who is trying to put a name to a thing that does not yet have a name. I will tell you what I am.

I am a person who lives in the upper rooms of a city that has become a forest and is becoming a sea. I am a person who mends causeways and catches fish and prunes vines and tends the rooftop garden that is half of what we eat. I am a person whose mother was born on a floor that is now a pond, and whose daughter was born on a floor that is now a roof, and whose daughter's daughter, if she is born, will be born on a floor that is now what — we do not know. A higher canopy. A deeper silt. A different room, with a different tree through the window, and a different name that we will not give it because the name will not fit and also will not be needed.

I am patient, the way the vines are patient. I am working, the way the water is working. I am part of a place that is becoming something, and I do not know what it is becoming, and I do not need to know.

That is what I am.

Now I have said it, and I will not say it again, because saying it does not change the splice in my hand, and the splice is what is in my hand. You may go back to your high rooftop. You may look down at us for as long as you like. We will be here. We will be working. The fog will lift in the afternoon, and the copper light will come through the canopy, and the fish will rise to the insects, and the causeway-menders will go out with their adzes and their rope, and the small sound of their work will be the heartbeat of the place.

You will hear it, if you listen.

You will not hear it, if you do not.
