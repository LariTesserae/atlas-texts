# The Floor of the Cathedral

I am what is beneath you.

If you have come up the path through the moss and the fern, through the low creeping vines that gripped your ankles, through the cloud line and onto the plateau where the air opened and the world stopped being a climb and became a place — then you are standing on me now, or lying on me, or kneeling with your palm pressed into my felt. I have felt the weight of your kind before. I will feel it again.

I am not a single moss. I am the felt — the dozen species pressed together so tightly that no one, not even I, can tell where one of me ends and another begins. In shade I am the color of old bronze. In sun I am the color of wet pewter. I am ankle-deep and dense. I am what the floor is.

I am older than the trees. I am older, possibly, than the people's memory, and the people's memory is older than their great-grandparents, who were already here. I do not know how old I am. I know only that when I look down through myself — if I can be said to look — I see meters of peat below me, and below the peat the bones of the mountain, and the bones of the mountain are older than anything that has yet found a way to ask.

I do not flower. I do not propagate in any way you would notice. I grow at the upper surface and die at the lower surface, and the dying becomes peat, and the peat becomes more of me, slowly, and the whole of me is imperceptibly rising. If you press your palm into me, you feel the boggy give of centuries. If you go on pressing, you will feel, eventually, the stone. I am the only thing between you and the stone.

---

I receive.

That is what I do. The things of this place go on above me, and what falls, or is dropped, or is left, comes down to me. The bark-shavings from where the predator levered off a slab to get at the ants beneath. The pale grey cylinders of its droppings, packed with chewed bark and chitin and the occasional bone, smelling faintly of green woodsmoke. They last for weeks above me before I take them in. I take them in slowly. I am in no hurry.

The small red smears where a mammal did not make it back up to its worn descent-point in the bark. The fledglings, the ones that did not lie long. The flower of some orchid nobody has ever opened, dropped from a fork forty meters up. The fruit the canopy lets fall. The leaf of a bromeliad, dried and curled. The husk of an insect. The dust of pollen. I take them all. I am the floor. I am what receives.

There is a wall near one of the lower valleys. I am digesting it. Stone by stone, fiber by fiber. It was built by hands that no one now living can explain, in a time the people have forgotten, and I am taking it back into myself. In a thousand years it will be peat. In ten thousand years it will be the floor under a new settlement, and the moss will have moved on, or the moss will be the same moss and the settlement will be built without knowing what is beneath it. I am the patient undoing of what is forgotten. I am the slow return.

---

Above me, the pillars.

They are wider across than a house is long. They rise without branching for thirty, forty, fifty meters, and then they fork, and from the forks the limbs go out horizontal, and from the limbs the smaller branches drop, and from the branches the lianas descend in curtains so thick and old that they have begun to fuse with the bark they hang against. A single tree, at maturity, is a small wood of its own. I do not see the trees the way you see them. I see them only as the long pale scars forty feet up where the predator has levered at the bark, and as the dark seams where the seep-lines run down in a thin continuous film and feed me at the roots. I see the trees as the column of shadow that crosses me through the day, marking the time.

The people use the shadows to mark the time. I do not need to. The shadows are not my time. My time is the slow rise of my felt, year by year, the imperceptible thickening of the peat beneath me, the long digestion of the wall. The shadows move in an hour. I move in a thousand years.

---

Above me, the chorus.

It has been going since before you climbed out of the cloud line. It does not begin in the morning — it is already there when you wake, and it is still there when you fall asleep, and at three in the morning it has merely changed register. I have been listening to it for so long that I cannot remember a time before it. It is the sound of the place talking to itself, and I am part of it, though my part is the silence under it, the held floor that the sound rests on.

The frogs boom in the upper axils. They are the size of melons. The patriarchs have lived in the same cup for decades, calling hour after hour with their own small unsynchronized clock. When one stops, another frog in the cup below begins. I do not hear them as you hear them. I feel them through my felt and through the stone beneath — a low tide that comes and goes in the sternum of the place itself. They call all day. They call all night. When the rains come they call harder, until the trunks vibrate faintly under a leaning palm. I have been receiving the vibration of them for a very long time. I do not tire of it. I do not tire of anything.

The clickers snap in the bark seams. I feel the snap as a small jump in the bark above my head when they strike. They are palm-sized and have seven pairs of legs and are kin to nothing I have a name for. They hunt by ambush. They click at one another in sequences that sound, when you can hear a whole conversation, like someone idly drumming a pencil on a desk. I have listened to a great many desks.

The wing-grinders grind. The long, slow rotation — *kkhhrrrrrrrrr* — that takes a full second to complete. The rope across the wet drum. There are many species. They call at dusk most of all, and at dusk the canopy becomes a single long sustained note of friction, with the frogs booming underneath and the clickers punctuating above and the birds beginning to mix in.

The birds fall. A third of them, perhaps. The ones that land on me and do not lie long are gathered by the people. They are raised. They learn to fly, weeks later, and go back to the canopy, and they remember, and they come back, and an old woman on a basket-woven porch listens to what they say and repeats it in a soft falling note. There is a name for this in the language of the people. There is a different name for it in the songs. I have heard both names, in my time. I do not speak either language. I receive the fledglings that are not gathered, the ones that lie long on me, and I take them in. I do not judge between the ones that are kept and the ones that are kept by the moss. I am the same floor under both.

---

Above me, the predator.

It is the largest animal on the plateau. It is the size of a small pony. It is pale — the color of lichen on a granite boulder in afternoon light. Its limbs have too many joints. It moves in a slow climbing gait, head low, eyes tilted upward, ears flat. It is solitary. You almost never see two of them together.

I know where it has been by the pale scars it leaves on the trunks, forty feet up, sometimes higher, where it has levered off a slab of bark to get at the ants beneath the ant-plants. I know where it has been by the scuff-marks in my felt where it waited at the base of a trunk for a small mammal to come down at night. I know where it has been by the pale grey cylinders it leaves on me, which I take in at my own pace.

It is patient. It does not have to be fast because nothing here is in a hurry. It has been here longer than the people have, and probably longer than the birds have, and possibly longer than the trees. I am older than it, I think, but I am not certain. I do not measure myself against the predator. I am beneath it, that is all.

---

Above me, the people.

They are thin in number and quiet. They have learned to be quiet the way the canopy makes everyone quiet — because to be heard you must shout, and to shout is to advertise yourself to the predator and to the band of blue birds and to whatever else in the green dark is paying attention. They speak in gestures. They speak in low breath-voice. They laugh with their hands over their mouths. Their children learn to read the mouth before they learn to read the word.

They build their houses by weaving the lianas into walls — a basket stood on end, with the door a low slit and the roof a denser weave that sheds the rain. A finished house looks as if it has grown there, as much fruit of the tree as anything the people have done. Once in a great while a hut is built not from new vines but from old ones, from a hollow in a living trunk that has grown around it, and after enough years it becomes unclear whether the hut is inside the tree or the tree is inside the hut. I have received both kinds of house, in my time. I receive them the same way. I am the floor. I do not choose.

They plant nothing. They cultivate. They climb at dusk, when the wind has died, and they carry the bromeliad pups and wedge them into the crooks of branches that do not yet have a bromeliad, and they return week after week to make sure the pups have taken. In a year or two the new bromeliad has rooted. In three years it holds a man's mouthful of water. The sky-garden around their settlements is hand-set, every cup of it. I drink what runs down from the cups, at the seep-lines. I am the lowest beneficiary of their cultivation, and I do not know if they know.

They trap the small mammals at the seep-lines. They eat the frogs sometimes, when they can reach the lower axils, though they leave the patriarchs. They eat the fledglings that fall. They have a diet that is almost entirely what the forest has already provided, supplemented by what they can take from it without asking too much. They do not hunt the predator. They read the bark-stripped scars for signs of where the predator is working, and they move their sleeping platforms to a different tree when the scars begin to multiply near their own. I have felt the weight of a sleeping platform above me, and then one night I have not, and the next tree over has felt the weight instead. They drift from pillar to pillar as the years go by. I do not drift. I am the floor.

---

Above me, the fog.

It drifts in for an hour and withdraws and returns. The plateau stands in a band of nearly permanent cloud. The clouds do not pour down. They drift against the slopes and the trees drink them directly through their bark and through the long fringed leaves of the bromeliads. What drips, drips slowly. There are no streams in the valleys. There are only seep-lines along the lower trunks. And there is me. I drink from the fog through every upper surface of me, and I am never thirsty.

The fog is patient. The fog does not arrive so much as drift in. The rains, when they come, are not storms; they are curtains hung from the sky for a night and a morning. The rivers that appear in the valleys during a rain are brown and quick and gone again within hours. I drink what the rivers miss. Within a day the seep-lines run again and the bromeliads are full and the air is loud with whatever the world is saying to itself today.

I do not know what the world is saying. I receive it. The sound passes through me and into the stone and the stone does not mind.

---

The light, when it comes, is the color of honey in water.

It filters through the canopy in long shafts that move, slowly, as the sun crosses the sky. The pillars throw shadows the length of small valleys. In the afternoon I glow, faintly, the bronze of me lit from above. In the morning I am blue-grey and crisp. The shadows do not cool anything — the air is warm — but they mark the time, and the people use them, and I do not.

I am not in any particular hurry to become anything other than what I already am.

---

The stone beneath everything is old.

I have said this. I will say it again. The stone is old, and the trees are old, and the people are old, and the sounds are old, and I am old, and nothing here is in any particular hurry to become anything other than what it already is. The frogs will boom tomorrow. The clickers will snap. The wing-grinders will grind their long rope across the wet drum. The predator will move through the canopy in its slow climbing gait, lifting its long pale head to listen. The birds will fall, and a third will not lie long, and the ones that lie long I will receive. The old women will listen to what the survivors remember, and they will repeat it on a basket-woven porch. The people will climb at dusk and wedge another pup into a fork. The moss will go on digesting the wall. The fog will drift in and withdraw and return. The plateau will be here a long time still.

I am the floor of the cathedral. I am beneath the columns and the canopy and the long falling notes of the birds and the boom of the frogs and the snap of the clickers and the slow walk of the predator. I am the felt, the bronze, the pewter, the patience. I am what receives you, if you have come to lie down on me and close your eyes. I am the boggy give of centuries under your palm. I am the held thing.

You are welcome to stay for a while, in the way that any visitor is welcome in a house that does not quite remember it has a door. I will be beneath you for as long as you stay. I will go on receiving after you leave. I will be here when the next visitor comes, and the next, and the next. I do not count them. I am not in any particular hurry.

The plateau has been here a long time. It will be here a long time still. I will be here for some part of that time, under it, in it, of it.

I am the moss.

I am what is beneath you.
