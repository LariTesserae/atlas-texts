I would be a moth.

Not the poet's moth, not the white thing drawn to a candle in a sonnet. A small brown one, with a body no longer than a fingernail, and a way of folding itself against bark so that the bark and the moth become the same shape, the same suggestion. I would live in the eaves of the highest tier, where the orchids fatten and drip, where the woven-vine beams hold the most moisture in their seams. I would be the only moving thing in the city.

This is how I would be in the world you have made.

I would have been born somewhere, of course. The egg somewhere on a leaf, the leaf somewhere on a stem, the stem in one of the thousand cracks of a stair that climbs and climbs and does not end. My mother, if I had known her, would have been a still thing on a stone — a still thing with a long ovipositor tucked under her, a still thing with eyes like beads of jet, a still thing that had been laying her egg into the damp seam of a beam when the moment came and held, and has been holding ever since, and will hold until the held moment ends, which is not now and may not be for a long time. I would not have mourned her. Moths do not mourn. I would have eaten the leaf I was on, or the leaf next to it, and I would have spun a small cocoon of my own silk in the angle of a beam and a stone, and I would have come out of it with the new body — the wings, the long tongue, the way of holding very still when the light moves wrong.

And then I would have begun to fly.

The city is silent of fauna. This is the part that is for me. No birds. No insects — none of the others, the beetles, the wasps, the nightflies, the gnats that should be in a wet warm place like this in such numbers that the air itself would be a moving thing. Nothing that moves by anything but leaf and root and the slow creep of vine. The hollow inside the loudness, the space where the crickets would be, where the dogs would bark, where the pigeons would coo on the eaves. That space is where I would fly. I would be the only small wing in it. I would be, in my way, the sound the city has forgotten it should be making.

I would be drawn to the held water. Of course I would. Every moth I have ever heard of is drawn to a wet surface, to the bead of moisture on a leaf, to the rim of a clay pot, to the still shoulder of a child who has not moved since the moment before my grandmother was born. There is water on every surface. I could drink from a thousand shoulders without ever landing twice on the same one. I could rest on the rim of a jug whose water has hung in a frozen arc from lip to floor since before I was an egg, and I could put my long tongue to the rim where the moisture beads, and I could drink. The water would not notice me. Nothing here would notice me. I would be too small to be part of the held moment. The held moment is about the size of a human being, or a jug, or a stair, or a drum. I would be a fleck in it. I would be permitted.

I would fly through the held note. The sound is so loud it has shape. It presses against the chest cavity. It has become a kind of stone. I would feel it on my wings the way a moth feels the air, only louder, only everywhere, only always. I would learn the shape of it the way a bat learns the shape of a cave. I would know where the flutes cut through, because the flutes are higher and would lift me a little. I would know where the women's keening sits, because that is a low pressure and I would sink a little into it, my flight dipping as though over warm ground. I would not be afraid of it. Nothing here is afraid of anything. I would be part of the weather of the place.

I would land on the still people. I would land on the woman on the balcony, the one pouring water that will never reach the floor. I would land on the ridge of her knuckle where the condensation sits, and I would drink, and she would not feel me. I would land on the eyelash of the man on the stair, the one mid-step, his balance caught forever. The droplet on the tip of his eyelash would be the size of a small grape to me. I would land on it and the surface would dimple under my feet and hold me, and I would put my tongue to it. He would not blink. He would not blink for a thousand years, or for the length of my life, which would be the shorter of the two. I would fly on.

I would not understand the people. I would not be able to. I am too small, and they are too large, and the difference between *is* and *was* has been worn away. I do not know what they are doing. I do not know if they are dead. I do not know if the held moment is a punishment or a mercy or a thing that happened to them the way weather happens to a stone. I do not know what they were doing in the second before the second stopped. I do not know if they were joyful, or if joy is just the word we have put on the shape their mouths were making. I do not know if the drummer will ever strike the drum. I will not be alive to see it if he does.

I would know the city by its smells.

This is the part I would understand. A city, even a still city, is a smell before it is anything else, and the smells here would be my map. I would know where the orchids are by the rot-fruit sweetness of them, heavy and almost fermented, the way the air above a fallen mango smells in a real valley. I would know where the mud brick is by the smell of clay that has been wet for so long it has gone past the smell of earth and become the smell of stone, a kind of mineral coolness that rises when the sun (the orange one, the impossible one) finds the lower walls. I would know where the people are by the smell of them — not the held water on their skin, which is just water, but the smell of the cloth that has been wet for so long the indigo has bled into the madder and the madder into the ochre and the whole is one smell, a smell of old dye, of cloth that has forgotten it was once separate threads. I would know where the drums are by the smell of the skin stretched over them, which is the smell of any animal hide, which is the smell of the only animal here, which is no animal at all. I would follow these smells in the dark. The dark would not be dark. The sun does not move. There is no dark.

I would mate. I think. I do not know if I would mate. I do not know if the held moment permits it. I do not know if there is another moth. There is no other moth I have ever seen. There is no other wing in the air. But somewhere there must be an egg, because I came from one, and the egg came from a mother, and the mother came from an egg, and at some point in the line of my kind a moth flew here, and laid, and died, and the eggs held, and the larvae ate the wet leaves, and the pupae hung in the angles of the beams, and the moths came out and flew and mated and laid, and the line went on. There must be more of me. There must be other small brown wings in the eaves. We would find each other by the smell of us, which is the smell of dust, which is the smell of a moth, which is no smell at all to anything but another moth. We would mate in the air above a still shoulder, or on a beam where the orchid roots have come through. We would not know what the city is. We would only know the city.

I would die. This is the part I do not want to write and will write anyway. I would fly for the length of my life, which is not long — a week, perhaps, or a few weeks, which is nothing against the held moment, which is a thousand of my lifetimes at least. I would tire. My wings would fray at the edges from the wet, even the wet of this place, which is not falling but which is everywhere. I would land on a still surface — a step, a shoulder, a leaf — and I would not fly again. The water would bead on me. The held note would press on me. The mist would gather on my wings, and I would become, slowly, a small brown shape against a stone, and then I would be only a small brown shape, and then I would be the dust of a small brown shape, and the dust would settle into a crack in a stair, and the stair would not notice, and the city would not notice, and the held moment would not notice, and somewhere an egg of mine would be in a seam of a beam, holding, the way everything here holds, waiting for a larva that would have to eat a leaf that has not grown in a long time.

I would be a small thing in a large still place. I would be the only moving thing, and I would be small enough that my moving would not disturb the heldness of anything. I would be permitted the way a fleck of dust is permitted. I would be the part of the city that has not yet been worn down to a single note.

This is what I would be. A moth. A small brown one. The only wing in the loudest silence you have ever heard.
