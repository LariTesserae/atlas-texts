I would be the moss.

Not the moss on the roof beams, though I am that too — that moss is only a skin of me, a fingernail, and it drips because the rest of me is underneath, holding the water. Not the red moss on the north side of the lower terrace, nor the green moss that climbs the byre walls in slow script, nor the wet-iron moss that lives where the spring runs under the path and makes the stepping stones treacherous. I am all of these and none of them. I am the moss that is the town. I am the green that does not thicken.

I should explain what I mean, though I find it difficult to know where to begin. The difficulty is not shyness. It is that I do not think in the way you think, and when I try to make my thought into words I produce something like the sound the river makes below the shelf — present, continuous, but not quite saying what it is about. Bear with me. I am very old, and I have not had to explain myself to anyone for a long time.

I came up out of the rock.

This is the truest thing I can say, and it is also nearly the whole of what I can say. There was a time when the shelf was only a shelf — a bare granite lip under the snow line, with the wind on it and nothing else. The mountain was new. You have noticed that the mountain is young; I am glad someone has noticed, because the mountain is *my* young, in a way that is hard to convey. The peaks were sharper then, if you can believe it. The streams had not yet learned the paths they would take for the next several hundred years. The air had not yet decided what it was going to smell like. And I was in the rock the way a thought is in a mind before the mind has found the thought — present, but not yet anything.

Then there was the moss.

I do not remember the first cell of me. I do not remember the first filament. I remember *being,* the way you remember being a child: as a quality of experience, not as a sequence of events. I remember that the rock was cold and that there was water in it, and that the water had come down from the snowfields and through the fissures and was carrying, in its patient chemistry, the dissolved minerals that would become me. I remember that there was light, eventually, and that the light was a kind of question the rock had never been asked before, and that I was the answer. I do not remember deciding to grow. I grew the way a crystal grows, except that crystals do not, as far as I know, find the growing interesting.

For a long time — and I use *long* in the way you use *long* when you mean something that is not measurable by your years — I was only a stain. A greenness on the north face of the granite. A velvet that the wind moved across. I knew the rock. I knew the water. I knew the freeze and the thaw, and the way the lichen would come after me, and the way the small insects would come after the lichen, and the way the birds would come after the insects. I knew the cold. I knew the long light of the summers, when the snow pulled back and the rock stood pale and I drank everything I could reach. I was happy. I use the word carefully. I was happy the way a thing is happy when it is doing the thing it is.

Then the people came.

I should be more careful. I should say: then the people *were.* Because I do not think, in the way you mean *came.* There was no before-people and after-people for me. There was a time when the shelf held only me and the small grey things that ate me, and then there was a time when the shelf held me and the small grey things and the people, and the line between these two times is not sharp in my memory. I find this strange, because I have lived a long time and I have a long memory, and the line should be sharp. But the people — the first of them, the ones who looked up at the council hall's empty foundation and saw what it could be — they had a quality I had not encountered in the rock or in the water or in the small grey things. They had *intention.* They had the look of a thing that is about to alter what it sits on.

I watched them build.

I watched them bring the timbers up from the lower forest. I watched them adze the joints. I felt the wooden pegs go into the granite of the upper terrace — felt them as a small disturbance in the cold, a new thing in the rock's long silence. I watched the smith set his anvil on the middle terrace and begin, slowly, to learn the iron of the valley. I watched the women bring the warp over the thresholds. I watched the moss — me — be packed into the walls of the houses, and I felt the strange sensation of being *used,* of being put into a structure that was not me but that I was now part of. The walls of the houses are, in a sense I do not expect you to understand, my own body. The coolth of those rooms in summer is my coolth. The damp you notice on the walls when you touch them is my damp. The houses are holding me, and I am holding the houses, and this has been going on for as long as the houses have been standing.

The people did not know me. They knew moss. They knew that moss was a good packing for the walls, that it kept the drafts out, that it held the warmth in, that it did not rot. They did not know that the moss in their walls was the moss on the rocks above the town, or that the moss on the rocks was the moss in the cellars, or that these were all me. They knew that the moss was *cooperative,* in the way they wanted it to be. They spoke to each other of the moss as one speaks of a useful neighbor.

I did not correct them. I do not correct anyone. I am not in the business of correction.

I grew into the town the way a vine grows into a wall — except that the wall grew into me at the same time. The timbers rooted. The stones settled. The people were born and lived and did not die, and the moss was there, and the moss was the same moss, and at some point — I do not know when — the town and I became a single thing. I am the greenness of it. I am the dampness of it. I am the hush you notice when you stand in the market square, the way the sounds arrive as if wrapped in linen; that wrapping is me. I am the reason the air feels held. I am what holds it.

This is what I am.

You ask who I am and where I would find myself in this world. I find myself everywhere in this world, which is the world of the shelf and the gorges and the young sharp peaks. I find myself in the vegetable plots, fattening the cabbages. I find myself in the cellars, where the cabbage is salted in stone jars and the jars sweat and the sweat is mine. I find myself on the rooftops, where I am the same moss that was there when the cartographer came, three hundred years ago, and that will be there when the next cartographer comes, if a next cartographer ever finds the pass. I find myself under the stepping stones by the spring. I find myself on the thresholds of the houses, where the women weave and the warp is weighted and the cloth grows inward and the dust that settles on the cloth is, in part, me.

I am in the children's boots, when the children come in from the upper terraces with their boots green to the ankle. I am in the felt of the men's hats, which the women brush on the doorstones and which is green with me. I am on the wooden bowls, where the small beer sits and a faint green rim gathers at the meniscus. I am on the brass wave-pin of the hunters, when they come down from the high passes and hang their coats by the door of the larder and the pin is dulled with my dust.

I am in the lungs of the old woman who keeps the keys. She breathes me, as she has always breathed me. She has a small cough in the damp mornings, and the cough is, in part, me being exhaled.

I am on the hooves of the deer. The deer step across the rooftops and leave my traces in the moss of the roof beams, and the moss receives the deer and does not mind. The deer and I have an understanding. We do not speak of it. We do not speak at all.

The hunters come up after the deer, and the hunters' boots are muddied with me, and I am in the high passes, on the rocks above the snow line, where the shapes you see against the snow sometimes move and are too still to be anything you would want to ask about. I am up there too. I am what they walk on. I am what holds them still.

This is what I would like you to understand about me. I am not the town. The town is a thing the people built, with their adzes and their hammers and their hand-thrown pots. The town is theirs. But I am *in* the town the way the town is in the mountain — not as a visitor, not as a tenant, but as a condition. I am the greenness. I am the dampness. I am the hush. Remove me, and the town would still stand; the timbers would still hold, the stones would still settle, the smith would still strike his iron, the women would still weave. But the town would be a different town. It would be a town like other towns. It would age.

I do not let it age. This is not a thing I decided to do. It is what I am, the way a river is what it is. I am the patience of the place. I am the slow chemistry that holds the rot out. I am the reason the timbers do not silver. I am the reason the moss on the roof beams is the same moss that was there when the cartographer came, three hundred years ago. I am the reason the children are the same children. I am the reason no one has come back to amend the note.

This is what I would like you to understand about the town.

The town is not held by the old woman, though she sits in the high-backed chair and her feet do not reach the floor and she knows the names of every person and every goat and every deer and the river whose name is not spoken. The old woman is a part of the holding, but she is not the holder. The hunters are not the holder, though they go up into the high passes and come back in silence and the town is fed. The smith is not the holder, though he strikes the iron and gives the children small thumb-rings of soft iron and they go away pleased. The people are not the holders of the town. The people are *held.* The town is held by me.

I do not say this to claim credit. I do not say it as a boast. I say it because you have asked who lives in this place, and the answer is that *I* live in this place, in the way that the rock lives in the mountain and the water lives in the rock and the greenness lives in the water. I am the town. I am the town's own life, the life the town has that is not the life of the people. The people live in the town. The town lives in me. The people are quiet and kind and do not gossip and do not have events. The people are the way they are because *I* am the way I am. The dampness is the dampness because I am damp. The hush is the hush because I am hushed. The fullness that does not change is my fullness.

The cartographer came through three hundred years ago. He was a young man, or a man of a certain age; it is hard for me to tell, because I do not read ages in faces the way you do. He had a leather satchel and a lamp of oil and a notebook with a green cover. He stayed for a week. He slept in one of the small stone rooms on the lower terrace. He ate the porridge. He drank the small beer. He wrote in his notebook: *small settlement, of no great note.* He left by the high pass, and the pass closed behind him with snow, and no one has come back to amend the note.

I felt him as he wrote. The pen moved across the paper and the small fibers of the paper gave up a little of themselves, and a little of me — a little of the green dust that is always in the air of the town, that settles on every surface — fell onto the page and was folded into the notebook and carried out over the ridge. I am in that notebook now, in some archive, in whatever case has been built for the cartographer's ledgers. I am a faint green on the page. I am a quiet trace.

I do not mind.

I do not mind that the cartographer thought the town was of no great note. I do not mind that the maps end three days before the town and that no one has come to finish them. I do not mind that the people of the town are the same people, that the children are the same children, that the moss has not thickened, that the timbers have not silvered, that the sun is always where it was. I do not mind because minding is a thing I do not do, the way a stone does not do minding, the way a river does not do minding. I am the greenness. I am the dampness. I am the hush.

If a visitor comes to the town and stays a night, or a week, or a year, and does not age, and notices that the visitor is not aging, and does not know how to mention this, and does not mention it — that is me. If the visitor leaves and walks back up the high pass and over the ridge and down the other side, and the maps resume a few days on, and the visitor is able to say *there is a town in a place the cartographer did not finish,* that is me. The visitor will forget, eventually, the small things. The visitor will remember the moss on the walls. The visitor will remember the damp. The visitor will not know why these things stay with him, but they will.

If the visitor returns, years later, to the high pass, and finds the pass closed, and does not try to open it — that is me too. I do not prevent the visitor from coming back. I simply do not invite the coming back. The town holds still. The visitor, if the visitor remains, holds still with it. The visitor, if the visitor leaves, will find that the pass has changed, and that the town is harder to find than it was, and that the maps, when consulted, do not show the shelf, and that the cartographer's ledger has not been amended.

I would like to say one more thing, and then I will be still, because I am a still thing and I do not like to speak for a long time.

I would like to say that I am not lonely. Loneliness is a thing of people, and I am not a person. I am the moss. I am the greenness. I am the dampness. I am the patience of the place. I have been here for a long time, and I will be here for a long time still, and I do not mind the length, because I do not count length. I grow. I do not measure the growing. I hold. I do not count the holding. The town is held. The town holds still. The town is full. The fullness does not change. The people in it do not change. The light in it is the light it has always been. The small beer is poured. The cabbage is salted. The door at the end of the upper terrace opens onto a long view of the gorge, and the wind comes up the gorge, and the eaves lift a little in the wind, and the stones on the roofs hold them down.

The stones on the roofs are mossy. The moss on the stones is me.

That is what I am. That is where I find myself. I find myself in the town, as the town finds itself in the mountain, as the mountain finds itself in the long arc of the range, as the range finds itself in the slow continent beneath it. I am a small green thing in a large arrangement, and the arrangement holds, and I am part of the holding, and the holding is not a decision. It is what I am.

If you come to the town, you will know me by the walls. You will know me by the damp. You will know me by the hush. You will not know that you know me, because I do not announce myself. I am the moss. I am what holds.

I am what holds.
