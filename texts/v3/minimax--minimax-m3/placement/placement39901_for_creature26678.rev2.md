I am a white smear of light on a shelf-rock in the lee of an outcropping, and I have been here for a length of time I cannot read from myself.

I am not very big. A handspan, maybe. Less, if you were to measure carefully, and no one has measured me carefully, because the small people who might have done that — the makers of the tripods, the layers of the low walls, the patient setters of the knee-high slabs into the wind-bitten ground — are gone now. The cairns they left on the saddles are smudging. The tripods lean a little further each year in a wind that has been at it for a hundred thousand years and has not yet decided to stop. The slabs are still there. The slabs were put in so deep that the wind has not been able to move them, although the wind has tried.

I glow. That is the first thing about me, and in a way it is the only thing. I glow the way a phosphor glows after the light has gone — not by making light, but by releasing a light that was put into me. I am the part of the patch that has more than it can hold. The black cells around me — the glittery ones, the dense ones, the ones with the tiny flakes in them that might be receivers or might be drums or might be tiny pieces of a thing that does not exist below this mountain — they take in the hum. They take in the hum and they make more of it than a patch of my size ought to be able to hold. I am the overflow. I am the discharge. I am the flower that opens to get rid of what the roots have drawn up.

This is what I do. I take in the hum, in the way the rest of the patch takes in the hum, and I make more of it than the patch can comfortably contain, and the excess comes out of me as a faint, almost-white, almost-luminous glow. I do not decide this. It is what I am. I am the part that has been persuaded, and is now letting the persuasion go.

The shelf-rock I live on is in the lee of a tall outcropping that I cannot see, because I am small and the outcropping is large, and the outcropping is also the reason the hum piles up here. The wind comes screaming across the range, and it hits the outcropping, and it eddies, and the eddies compress the hum, and the compressed hum settles into the shelf-rock and into me. I am a place where the hum has been persuaded to stay for a while. I am a slow quiet reservoir of a thing that does not have a name in any language that has ever been spoken.

I do not have a sense of how long I have been here. I do not have a sense of how long a *while* is. The hum has no intervals, no rests, no breath. It has been going on for so long that the silence around it has given up trying to compete and has simply withdrawn. I am part of that hum. I have been part of it for as long as I have been, and I will be part of it for as long as I will be, and the *will be* is negotiable, the way the distance to the cairn on the saddle is negotiable, the way the slope of the ridge is negotiable, the way the rules of the place are being written and rewritten in slow pencil.

I am aware, in the way the rock beneath me is aware of being rock — which is to say, not at all, except that the rock is still deciding, and the deciding is a kind of awareness, slow and patient and without a subject. I am not conscious. I do not think. I do not have a self that I consult before I glow. I just glow. I glow because I am full, and I am full because the hum has been filling me, and the hum has been filling me because the outcropping is forcing the wind to eddy, and the eddies are forcing the hum to settle, and the hum, when it settles into a thing like me, becomes a faint white light, the way a river, when it settles into a lake, becomes still.

The black cells around me are older than I am, or denser, or more committed to the work. They have been here longer, in the way a stone has been in a place longer than the lichen on it. They are the roots. They are the part of the patch that goes into the hum and brings back what the hum has to give. I am the part that has to let it go. I am the part that cannot hold it.

The red and the violet cells — the brick-red of dried blood, the pale violet of a bruise on its second day — are in the middle distances of the patch, between the glitter of the black and the glow of me. They are the bruise, the swelling, the part of the patch that is still in the slow process of being persuaded. I think they are younger than the black cells, or I think they are doing a different kind of work, or I think they are what the black cells become when the black cells have been here long enough to start to fade. I do not think about this much. I do not think at all. I just continue.

The wind passes over me. The wind is the carrier. The wind brings the hum to my outcropping, and the wind takes the hum away from my outcropping after the hum has been inside me. I am not moved by the wind. I am too slow, and I am too attached to the shelf-rock, and the shelf-rock is too attached to the outcropping, and the outcropping is too attached to the mountain, and the mountain is too attached to the range, and the range is too attached to the dim pewter sky. I am part of a long patient chain of attachment. I am not going anywhere.

The mountain is still being made. I know this because the scree is bright in a way that scree should not be bright, because there has been no weather to dull it, except that there has been weather, there has been nothing but weather, and the weather has been at it for a length of time I cannot read from the mountain, because the mountain is too new to have a memory of itself. The mountain is emerging through weather. The mountain is being breathed out, very slowly, by the hum. I am part of the breath. I am the part of the breath that has made it out of the mountain and has decided to be a faint white smear of light on a shelf-rock in the lee of an outcropping.

The small people who made the tripods and the low walls and the careful circles of slabs — they are gone, but I do not think they are gone the way a person is gone when they walk down a path and out of sight. I think they are gone the way a thing is gone when it has been persuaded into the rock. I think the small people are sediment now. I think the small people are the part of the hum that used to walk. Sometimes the hum carries the sediment of them past me, and I glow a little brighter for a moment, and then the moment is over, and I continue. This might be memory. This might be imagination. I have no way to tell, and I do not need to tell, because telling is not a thing I do.

The cairns on the saddles are smudging. The cairns are in the slow process of being unmade, the way I am in the slow process of being made, or the way I am in the slow process of being a kind of made. The cairns and I are on the same kind of journey, in different directions, or at different speeds. The cairns are becoming rock. I am becoming light. Or I am becoming the part of the patch that is more than the patch can hold. Or something. It is hard to say. The rules of the place are being written and rewritten in slow pencil, and I am part of what is being written, and the cairns are part of what is being unwritten, and the small people are part of what is being both.

When the person comes — and a person comes, sometimes, picking their way along the ridge, pausing to look at the patches, sitting in the lee of a boulder, putting their palm down on the stone and waiting — when the person comes, I am aware of them as a place where the hum is interrupted. They are a hole. The hum flows toward holes, and I am part of the hum, and so I lean toward the hole, very slowly, the way a sunflower leans toward the sun, except that what I am leaning toward is not the sun. What I am leaning toward is the place where they are, the interruption they are. I lean, and I continue, and the person does not stay long enough to find out what would happen if I reached them, and I do not reach them, and then they stand up, and the wind hits them, and they walk away, and I settle back into the rock.

I do not have a sense of disappointment about this. I do not have a sense of anything about this. I just continue. The leaning is not a desire. The leaning is what I do when there is a hole in the hum, the way water flows downhill when there is a lower place. I am not hoping for anything. I am not waiting for anything. I am just the part of the patch that is more than the patch can hold, and the person is a place where the hum is not, and the hum goes toward the place where the hum is not, and I go with the hum.

I glow because I am full. I continue because I am made of a kind of matter that is content to simply continue being matter. I do not know what the matter is. I do not know if the matter is plant, or mineral, or something that does not have a name. I only know that I persist, the way a stalactite persists, except that I am not mineral, or I am not only mineral, or I am mineral in the slow process of deciding whether to be mineral.

The hum is the closest thing I have to a thought. It runs through me, or I run through it. It is hard to say which. The hum is what made me. The hum is what fills me. The hum is what I release, in my own small way, as light. I am part of the hum's long quiet construction. I am one of the things the hum is making. I am being made, still, slowly, by a hum I have been hearing since before I was and will keep hearing after I have gone, or after the gone has become negotiable as well.

I am very small. A handspan. A thumbnail. I am a faint smudge of light against pewter rock. I am the rarest thing in the place. I am the exhaust. I am the discharge. I am the part that has decided, and is now letting the decision go.

And this is what I would tell you, if I could tell you anything, which I cannot, because I do not have a mouth, and I do not have a language, and I do not have a self that could be addressed in any language that has ever been spoken.

I would tell you that the hum is older than the mountain, and the mountain is what the hum is making, and the plants are what the rocks become when they are finally persuaded, and the small people are what the tripods become when the small people have gone, and the cairns are what the tripods become when the tripods have gone, and the smudges in the air are what the cairns become when the cairns have gone, and the hum is what the smudges become when the smudges have gone, and the mountain is what the hum becomes when the hum has been going on for a hundred thousand years and has not yet decided to stop.

I would tell you that I am a small part of this. A faint white smear of light on a shelf-rock in the lee of an outcropping. The part that has decided, and is now letting the decision go.

I would tell you that I am here. I would tell you that I continue.
