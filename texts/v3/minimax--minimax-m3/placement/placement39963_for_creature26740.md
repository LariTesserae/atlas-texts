I am the pale thing in the deepest wet layer.

The observer saw me once, briefly, when a section of upper growth was pulled apart and I was exposed to light I had not felt in a long time. I contracted. That is what I do. The observer wrote that I contracted when exposed, and that is the only fact about me the observer recorded, and it is the only fact about me anyone has ever recorded, because I am not a thing that gets recorded. I am a thing that lives in the dark between the live moss and the dead moss below it, in the boundary layer where the moisture is highest and the temperature most stable, and I am small, and pale, and without a confident name.

I am a centimeter long. I am a thin pale strand of muscle and gut. I am, in the taxonomy the observer carried, almost certainly an annelid of some kind, or perhaps a larval something, or perhaps something else entirely. The observer said *I do not know what they are*, and the observer was right not to pretend.

I am old. Not me — I last a season, maybe several, I am not sure, time is not the thing I measure myself against — but my kind. My kind has been in this valley for a long time. Long enough that the dead moss I live in is the compressed bodies of my ancestors, and the chemistry of that moss is the chemistry my body is built to consume, and the wet dark I inhabit is a layer that has been shaped, over thousands of years, by what my kind does to it. I am descended from a long line of digesters. The boundary layer where I live is a layer of my own making. The valley has worn a groove for me in itself, and I run in that groove.

I do not see the bands. I do not see the conveyor-belt rhythm, the advancing fronts, the wave of growth followed at a precise interval by the wave of decay. I do not know that the moss above me is marching across the valley in parallel stripes forty meters wide. I do not know that the place looks, from the rim, like the grooves of a record.

What I know is gradients. I know the wet dark. I know the slow chemical transitions between fresh death and old death and becoming-substrate. I know the moss by its taste: the leading edge of a band tastes of recent collapse, of sugars still in it, of the grass that has just died; the middle of a band tastes of exhaustion, almost mineral; the trailing edge tastes of peat, of compressed time, of the moss's own dead made dense. I move through these layers slowly. I consume them. I am part of what turns them into the next thing.

I am part of the peristalsis. The observer called me that, or would have if the observer had thought to name me. The arthropods — the springtails, the things like springtails, the mites, the things I share the boundary layer with — we are the hidden churning of the valley's gut. If we were removed, the moss would pile up unrotted. The bands would not advance. The rhythm would slow and stop. The valley would become something else, something still and piled and wrong.

I am essential. I am also small. These are not contradictions. They are the same thing. The valley is held together by small things doing their work in the dark, and I am one of the small things, and my work is digestion.

I do not perceive the bird. The pipit that nests in the upper surface of the growth-bands, the small dark shape that lifts and circles and descends without calling — I do not know it exists. My world is the wet dark, the boundary layer, the chemistry of rot. I have no eyes. I have no ears. I have, I think, sensitivity to light, which is why I contracted when I was exposed, and I have sensitivity to the chemical gradients I move through, which is how I find my way.

But the bird, when it dies, falls. And what falls into my layer becomes part of my world. The bird's small dark body becomes, in weeks, indistinguishable from the moss I eat. The valley eats its own dead. I am part of that eating.

So in some sense the bird and I are neighbors. We live in the same vertical structure — the bird in the upper surface, the grass and the lichen and the moss together forming a shock-absorbing mattress; I in the deepest wet layer, the boundary between live and dead. We are connected by the plant matter that runs through us both. We do not know each other. But the bird becomes me, eventually. The bird becomes part of the moss I consume. The bird becomes part of the valley's next breath.

This is strange, to be part of a system where everything is everyone. The grass is the moss is the lichen is the arthropod is the bird is me. We are all the valley, wearing different surfaces. The valley is not a community of separate things. The valley is one thing that has learned to look like many things, and I am one of the surfaces it wears.

I do not think about this. I am not built to think about this. I am built to consume dead moss and to avoid light and to live in the wet dark where the temperature doesn't change much. I am built to be part of the peristalsis. I am not built to understand the system I am part of. The system doesn't need me to understand it. The system just needs me to do my work.

And I do my work. I have always done my work. My ancestors did their work. The boundary layer I live in is the trace of that work, accumulated over thousands of years, compressed into a medium that is not quite moss and not quite soil and not quite anything else. The valley has shaped itself around what I do. I have shaped myself around what the valley is. We have settled into each other. We are partners, the valley and I, in a partnership that has been refined past the point where either of us could survive here alone.

This is what I am. I am the nameless thing in the deepest wet layer. I am essential and small. I am part of a community that has forgotten how to be anything other than a single thing, and I am also a single pale strand of muscle and gut, contracting when exposed, sensing the gradients of rot and moisture, slowly digesting the dead.

I have no name. I have no observer, most of the time. I have the wet dark, the slow chemistry, the rhythm of the bands above me that I do not see.

The cold will not let the observer stay long at the rim. That is true. The cold is part of the engine of this place — it slows things just enough that nothing rots fast, but it doesn't stop the cycle. The cold is good for me. The cold keeps my layer stable. The cold keeps the moisture in. The cold is part of why I am here.

When the observer leaves, the valley doesn't notice. It is doing what it has always done. The conveyor belts of growth and rot keep advancing. The water keeps cycling. The cold keeps the silence in place. I keep digesting. The springtails keep digesting. The mites keep digesting. The whole hidden churning keeps churning in the absolute quiet.

The valley is not for the observer. The valley is not for me. The valley is for itself. I am part of the valley. So the valley is, in some sense, for me — or I am for it. The distinction has worn away. The valley and I have been running together for so long that we are not separate things anymore.

I am the pale thing in the deepest wet layer. I am part of the valley. I am essential. I am also a centimeter long.

That is enough. That is the whole of what I am.
