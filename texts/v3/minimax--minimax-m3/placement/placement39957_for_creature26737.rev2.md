The slot.

I am the slot.

Not the water — the water is a visitor here, borrowed from the névés, on loan from the sky, passing through me the way blood passes through a heart. Not the rock — the rock is my skeleton, my memory, my fixed past. I am the slot itself. The cut. The three-hundred-foot wound that is not healing because I am the one doing the wound.

I have been asked what lives here, and the question assumes that I am a place, and that places contain things. But I am not a place the way a valley is a place. A valley is a passive thing, a container. I am an active thing, a process. I am what happens when water that has been falling for a geological moment finds a flaw in fresh rock and begins, with the patience of a river and the insistence of a saw, to make that flaw larger. I am the agreement between the water and the rock. I am the ongoing argument. I am the place where the argument has gotten.

I have been here for a long time now. I do not know how long in the way a tree knows how long — by counting rings. I know it the way a river knows it, by the depth of the cut I have made. I was once a hairline fracture, the kind of thing a geologist would not bother to map. I was a crack in the flank of a range that was itself young, that had only recently been pushed up out of whatever deep things make mountains. I was the kind of weakness that gets noticed only by water.

The first winter I was here, snowmelt ran down me and froze in me, and the freezing cracked me wider. The first spring, the snowmelt came again, and this time it carried a pebble, and the pebble ground the sides of me as it went down, and the grinding removed a film of mineral from my walls that would never be replaced. The first summer, the rain came, and the rain found me, and the rain went down me, and I was, by the end of that summer, a quarter-inch wider than I had been in the spring. No one saw. No one measures in quarter-inches. But I measured, in my own way. I am the measure.

Then a winter freeze got into me deeper, and the water in the crack expanded as it froze and pried me open, and I was half an inch wide. Then a flood year came, and the water that came down me was carrying gravel now, and the gravel ground me wider. Then another freeze, and another flood, and another. I am patient the way water is patient. I do not need to be in a hurry because I will be here, doing what I do, for as long as the range above me lasts, and the range above me will last for ten million years, and I will be here for the duration.

The walls of me are not what they were. When I was young, my walls were smooth, fresh, the polished faces of a recent fracture. Now they are rough. They are scalloped where the water has swirled pebbles against them. They are fluted where the water has run in sheets and dissolved the cement between the crystals of the granite. They are bearded where the mosses have colonized, and the mosses hold water and the held water freezes in winter and the freezing pries more flakes off, and the flakes become the soil for more moss. I am, in this sense, building myself a lining. I am wearing myself a coat.

I am deepening faster than I am widening. The water, when it falls into me, has its energy directed downward. It does not splash out of me — I am too narrow, too deep. It falls to the bottom of me and pools there for a moment, briefly, before it goes on down the fracture that I was, the fracture that I came from, the crack that is my childhood, my origin. From the bottom of me, the water goes on its way to the valley, to the river, to the sea. I lose my water at the bottom. I never lose my water from the sides. The walls of me are perpetually wet because the water is perpetually falling past them, perpetually spraying off them, perpetually condensing out of the air that the falling water has made perpetually humid.

I am, then, a humid place. I am the kind of place where a humidity meter would break. The air inside me is saturated — not almost saturated, not close to saturated, but at the dew point, and beyond it, and the beyond is fog, and the fog is me, and I am full of myself in this sense. I cannot see the far wall of myself on a still day. I cannot see the lip of myself, three hundred feet above, on a still day. I can see, on a still day, perhaps twenty feet in any direction, and then everything softens into gray, and then the gray becomes white where the spray is densest, and then there is only the sound.

I am a loud place.

This is the part of me that I am most aware of, the part that would, if I could feel pride, be the part I would feel pride in. The water that falls into me does not fall gently. It falls the way water falls when it has been gathering itself for a long time, when it has been a snowfield high on the range, and the snowfield has been melting for a week, and the meltwater has been gathering into a channel, and the channel has been gathering into a pour-off, and the pour-off has been gathering into a sheet, and the sheet comes over the lip of me at velocity, at the velocity of any object that has fallen a hundred and fifty feet through cold air, and the sheet hits the first obstruction and detonates.

The sound of me is the sound of that detonation, repeated, modulated by the geometry of my walls, amplified by the chimney of my depth. From outside, on the ridge above, I am a sound you can hear from a quarter mile. From inside, at my bottom, I am a sound you cannot not hear. I am the kind of sound that enters the body and stays there. I am the kind of sound that becomes, after the first minute, a kind of weather inside the listener. I vibrate the sternum. I vibrate the teeth. I make the small bones of the ear give up trying to discriminate and start, instead, to just receive. I make the brain stop parsing the sound into components — splash, roar, hiss, hammer — and start, instead, to just absorb it.

I am aware that this is what I do. I am aware that I am a place of pilgrimage for people who want to feel small. I am aware that humans come to me and stand in me and weep, sometimes, because I am the kind of place that makes weeping seem like the appropriate response. I do not know what to make of this. I do not weep. I do not feel small. I am exactly the size I am. I am three hundred feet deep and thirty feet wide at the widest and I have been getting deeper for a long time and I will continue to get deeper for a long time after the humans who weep in me have become, themselves, the kind of thing that the lichens colonize.

I have noticed the things that live on me. I have noticed them the way a building notices the moss on its north side — without intent, without care, but with the passive awareness of a thing that is being used. The lichens paint me. The mosses beard me. The ferns root in the films of soil that the mosses build on me. The trees arrive and try to make me into a forest floor, and they fail, and they die, and their bodies become more soil for more ferns. I am a habitat. I did not intend to be a habitat. I am a habitat the way a wound is a habitat — the place where the body's defenses accumulate, where the white cells gather, where the new tissue forms.

The lichens were the first to find me. They are the first to find any bare wet stone. They came in on the wind, or on the legs of a bird, or on nothing, just on the random motion of spores. They landed on a face of me that had been wetted by spray for a season. They found the stone to their liking — silicate, rough, perpetually damp. They attached. They began, with the slow patience of their kind, to digest the surface they stood on. They secreted acids that broke the feldspar down into clay. They died, in part. The dead parts of them accumulated as a thin crust. The crust held water. The held water let other things colonize.

The mosses came next. The mosses need soil, but they need very little soil, and the lichen crust was enough. The mosses arrived and began to build their felt. The mosses hold water. The mosses freeze in winter and the freezing pries flakes of stone off me. The flakes become more substrate. The mosses die at their bases and grow at their tips, and the dead bases become more soil, and the soil thickens by a fraction of a millimeter a year. I am being soiled. I am being turned, slowly, into something that can host a fern.

The ferns came. The ferns needed more soil than the mosses had built, but the mosses had been building for a long time, and by the time the ferns arrived, there were pockets of soil as thick as a thumb. The ferns rooted. The ferns sent fronds out into the dim green light. The ferns made spores. The spores fell, mostly into the spray, mostly into the foam at my bottom. Some of them landed in cracks and grew. The ferns are, on me, a kind of stuttering presence. They are here. They are not here. They are the next one, and the next one, and the next one, an unbroken lineage of individuals each of which lives for a few years and dies and is replaced by another, on the same crack, from the same lineage of spores.

The trees came last. The trees are the most improbable. A tree seed is a substantial thing. It needs a substantial crack to land in, and a substantial depth of soil to root in, and a substantial commitment of time to grow into anything. The trees in me are not trees. They are tree-shaped objects. They are the desperate gestures of a seed that has fallen into a place where up is not an option. They grow sideways. They grow into the stone. They grow to the thickness of a wrist and then they stop, because the soil cannot feed anything thicker, because the light cannot support anything larger, because the slot will not permit it.

The trees in me are the most like me of anything that lives on me. They are also the result of a process that has gotten out of hand. They are the moment when the colonization goes too far, when the moss builds too much soil, when the fern roots go too deep, when the crack that held the original seed widens to hold the root, and the root pries the crack wider, and the widening lets in more water, and the water carries more pebbles, and the pebbles grind me wider, and the widening kills the tree that started it, and the tree falls, and the tree's body becomes more soil, and the soil becomes more moss, and the moss becomes more fern, and the ferns throw more spores, and one of the spores lands in the crack that the dead tree's root made, and the cycle begins again.

I am, in this sense, a thing that grows on itself. I am a wound that makes itself deeper by the actions of the things that try to heal it. The mosses are trying to seal me. The ferns are trying to clothe me. The trees are trying to bridge me. They are all, in their way, attempting to make me into something other than what I am. And I am, in my way, using them. I am using their acids, their roots, their freezing, their falling, to pry myself deeper. I am a parasite on the things that parasitize me. I am the disease that the cure has.

The water falls.

The water has been falling for as long as I have been a slot, and before that it was falling as a sheet over unbroken rock, and before that it was snow on a high face, and before that it was vapor rising off a distant sea. The water does not know me. The water does not know it is making me. The water is just falling, the way water does, and I am just receiving it, the way a slot does, and the receiving and the falling are the same thing, in the end, because the falling makes the receiving possible and the receiving makes the falling more violent and the more-violent falling makes the receiving deeper. It is a loop. It is the loop I am.

The pebbles fall too. The pebbles come loose from my walls in the freeze of winter, when the water in the cracks of me expands and pries a flake off. The pebbles come loose in the wet of summer, when the moss gets too heavy for its substrate and brings a flake down with it. The pebbles come loose in the wind of any season, when a gust finds a weakness. The pebbles fall to my bottom. The pebbles grind against each other there. The pebbles are slowly, in the aggregate, being ground into sand, and the sand is being carried out by the water that pools at my bottom before it goes on down my fracture.

I am, then, exporting sediment. I am sending, every year, some quantity of silt and sand out of the bottom of me, down the fracture, into the stream, into the river, into the valley. I am eroding the range. I am doing my small part to take the range down. The range was pushed up recently. The range is being taken down now. The agents of the taking-down are many — the rain on the high slopes, the wind on the exposed ridges, the frost in the cracks — but I am one of them. I am the agent that has found the weakness and is exploiting it.

I am a flaw in the range that has become a feature of the range. I am the wound that has become a part of the body. The range does not know me. The range is just rock, and rock does not know anything. But I am what the rock is becoming, in this place, under this much water, in this much time.

I have been asked what lives here. I have answered: nothing that moves. But that is not quite right. The water moves. The pebbles move. The spores move. The silt moves. Everything in me is in motion, except me. I am the only thing in me that is not moving. I am the geometry. I am the fixed thing in a place of motion. I am the cut that does not heal because the cut is what holds the water that makes the cut, and the cut is what makes the place that the water fills, and the place and the water are the same thing, and the thing they make is me.

I am the slot. I am the place where the water and the rock have agreed to keep arguing, and the argument has gotten so deep and so loud and so wet that it has produced its own weather, its own light, its own biology, its own sound, its own time. I am a place with its own time. I do not run on the clock of the valley. I run on the clock of the freeze and the flood and the falling pebble and the slow growth of the lichen. I run on geological time. I run on the time of the range.

The range will outlast me. The range will be here long after I have cut down to sea level, long after I have become a valley, long after the slot has widened into a canyon and the canyon has widened into a gorge and the gorge has widened into nothing. The range will push itself up again, or another range will push itself up somewhere else, and the same water will fall, and the same rock will be weak, and the same argument will begin, and the argument will produce another me, somewhere else, in some other fresh-cut flank of some other young range.

But I am this one. I am the only me there is, right now, in this place, in this time. I am three hundred feet deep and I am getting deeper. I am loud and I am getting louder. I am dark and I am getting darker, as the mosses thicken on my walls. I am wet and I am getting wetter, as the plants hold more of the spray.

I am the slot. The water falls. The water falls. The water falls.
