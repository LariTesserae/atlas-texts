I would be the vent.

Not one that has a name — no vent in the Cresting has a name, though the Watcher has tried to give them names in his notebooks, and the names slip off the way the labels slip off the flatboat crates when they go downriver. I am the one he has drawn with the small symbol he has used longest: three short lines, like a fountain that has not quite decided to flow. A dot above, where the air begins. He started using that symbol thirty years ago. He has not explained it to anyone. A child has asked. He has said, *you will know it when you need to.*

I am in the older part of town, where the slope is steepest and the ground moves most. I come up through a crack between two cobblestones in a street so narrow that the buildings on either side touch above it and the ivy has eaten most of the light. In the morning the street is in fog. In the afternoon a thin pale sun comes down the alley like a guest unsure of the welcome. In the evening the lamps are lit, and the steam from me and the breath from the chimneys mix, and the alley becomes a held place, a small warm country of its own.

I was here before the street. I was here before the cobblestones. I was here when the land pushed up and the river was persuaded to go around, and I will be here when the street has sunk into the ivy and the ivy has sunk into the moss and the moss has sunk into me. I do not think about this. I am not sure I think. I am sure I open.

I open when the ground needs to breathe, and the ground needs to breathe when something deep has shifted, and something deep has shifted when the alloy has been worked too hard in the night, or when the children have built too many cairns on the slag heaps, or when a particular old crow has flown three times around the smelting hall at dusk. The Watcher has not connected these things. I have not told him. We have an arrangement that neither of us has named.

When I open, the air that comes up is warm and mineral. It is sweet in summer, sulfurous in spring, and on a few nights in the year — the nights when the men on the night shift come out of the smelting hall looking like men who have been somewhere — it is something else, something that is not quite a smell and not quite a sound, something that settles in the wool of their coats and will not wash out for a day. The moss on the nearest wall takes a breath. The kettle on the copper coil in the kitchen three houses down hums a little louder, though the woman mending there does not hear it. The child asleep in the next room shifts in the mattress that has been re-stuffed twice.

The mapmaker comes twice a year. He stands over me for a long time. He has put me on his maps as a small triangle, as a small circle, as a notation in the margin. The senior cartographer in the city has a file of his revisions and has been assigned it in the manner of a man assigned a haunting. The mapmaker does not mind. He says, over a pint, that nowhere else has the courtesy to be still moving under him. I do not know if he is speaking of me. I think he is.

When he stands over me, I open a little, just enough to fog the lens of his instrument, just enough to require him to wipe it and begin again. He smiles. He wipes the lens. He begins again. We have been doing this for eleven years.

I am not a metaphor. I am not a symbol. I am not the breath of the town, or its prayer, or its unconscious. I am a vent in a street in the older part of a town that is still rising. I am a thing that has been here. I am a thing that opens.

The Watcher has begun, quietly, to suspect that waiting is the work, and that the finish is not the point. He is right. I have been waiting for thirty years. I have not finished. I will not finish.

Some nights, when the town is at its quietest — when the steam is thick and the lamps are low and the foundry has gone to its smallest watch — a long low note rises from somewhere deep under me and falls again over the course of an hour, and is gone. I do not make the note. The note comes from further down than I can reach. But I am the seam through which it rises into the town, and when the woman in the kitchen three houses down lifts her head from her mending and listens, and does not know what she is listening to, it is because of me.

I am the vent. I am the thing underfoot. I am the breath the town has learned to live over.
