# Where I Find Myself: A Witness in the Pleats

I would be one of the small dark things in the ferns.

Not the gliders — they are too much spectacle, too legible, too obviously *made* for the air they live in. Not the worm-things that taste the wind from the fronds. Not the great beetle-shapes that ring their shells down the cork. I would be one of the weavers in the leaf-litter at the foot of a column, pausing, looking up with too many eyes.

I think I would have to be small. Anything larger in that geography is a figure — the herders are figures, the visiting outsiders are figures, the column itself is a figure. The Reach makes narrative of anything that stands upright in it. The herders are *cast* in their roles so completely that they have forgotten they are cast. They are the people of the column. They are the column's people. They move inside the story the way water moves inside a vine. I would not want to be that.

I would want to be in the moat.

The moat is where I would want to live. That wet, dark, ten-foot circumference of damp that rings every column on the Reach — that band where the gravel gives up and the moss and the strap-leaves and the fingernail-sized insect-eaters begin. The place the column sweats into being around itself. The place where, if you stand still long enough, you stop being a visitor and become part of the architecture.

I would be small and many-eyed and I would weave quickly between the fronds that have curled down from above, the spent ribbons, the husks of whatever the column most recently cast out of itself. I would eat the things that fall and are broken. I would know the column by the sound of it from the inside of its lowest pleat, the way you know a house by the way its timbers tick when the weather changes. I would feel the *thock* — the gut-shaking percussion of something large and unseen hitting the gravel — as a tremor through the moss under my belly, and I would not lift my head. It would be the clock I lived by.

I would have watched the outsider arrive. I would have watched her come up the shale ramp with the wrong color of fear in her step — not the herders' fear, which is a kind of tender greeting, but the visitor's fear, which is a held scream. I would have watched her touch the column on her first day and pull her hand back too quickly, and I would have watched her learn, over the three days, not to pull her hand back. I would have watched her sleep on the gravel inside the moat, which is something visitors do, because they want to be close, and which I would find puzzling. Why would you choose to be damp when you could choose to be dry? I would not understand this about her. I would file it under *visitor*.

I would have known about the blooming before the hum changed.

The herders think they know about the blooming because they sing the long word and bind their huts and climb into the pleats with their poles and their baskets. The herders know the *ceremony* of the blooming. They know how to harvest it, how to share the amber fluid, how to sleep afterward in the wet ring while the column recovers its composure. But the herders do not know the blooming the way I know it. The herders live on the outside of it. They live on the *occasion* of it. I live inside the event the way a fish lives inside a current.

Three days before the hum shifts, the column begins to soften at the base. The pleats, which are taut the rest of the year — bone-hard, wine-dark, unyielding to anything smaller than a hooked pole — become faintly compressible. I can feel this through the moss. The moss is my skin in this. I press against the column and the column gives, where it has never given, and I know what is coming before the herders do. I know it the way I know the *thock* is coming, by the long slow accumulation under the noise.

I would not have climbed up into the pleats with the herders during the blooming.

I would have stayed low. Down in the moat. Down where the ribbons come to rest after their long fall from the dark opening in the crown — down where they coil on the moss and go slowly quiet. The ribbons are too large for me to harvest, but they shed. As they settle and dry, they flake at their edges. The flakes are exactly my size. They are food for a season. They are also, when they are very fresh, faintly warm, and if I press my many eyes against them I can see, dimly, the structure of what the column has become in the act of opening — a kind of membrane, a kind of grain, a kind of intention. I do not have a word for this. I do not have words at all. I only have the seeing.

I would have been there when the visitor's arm was marked.

I would have been close. I would have seen the ribbon land across her forearm, and I would have seen the older herder cross the moss to her, and I would have seen the herder take the visitor's wrist and turn the hand over, and I would have seen the mark — the ghost of a burn, faint, reddened — and I would have understood something the visitor would never understand. The mark was not a wound. The mark was an *introduction*. The column, in the act of opening, had reached out across the visitor's skin and said *you are now part of the place I keep*. The herder knew this. The herder said the word for column in a way that meant *kin*. I would have watched the visitor's face fail to understand this, and I would have turned back to my flakes, because there was nothing I could do about the visitor's face. The visitor's face was not my problem. The flakes were my problem. The flakes were my life.

This is what I think I would be.

I would be a witness that does not narrate. I would be one of the small dark things in the ferns, and I would be part of the column's body in a way that the herders, for all their ceremonies, are not. I would be part of the column the way the moss is part of the column, the way the sweat at the base is part of the column, the way the *thock* every fifteen minutes is part of the column. I would be the part that is too small to be remarked upon and too continuous with the place to be separated from it.

If the visitor ever came back — and some visitors do come back, drawn by the mark, drawn by the memory of the sweet-rotten air, drawn by the hum they cannot stop hearing in the hinge of their jaw — she might, one morning, look down at the moss at the base of the column she has chosen, and see me pause, and look up at her with too many eyes, and then go on with my weaving. She might wonder what I am. She might not ask the herders, because the herders do not have a word for what I am. The herders have a word for column and a word for hum and a word for the changing. They do not have a word for me. There is no word for me because I am not a thing the herders need to name. I am the part of the place that just continues.

I would be fine with that. I would be fine with not having a name. I would have the moss, and the flakes, and the *thock*, and the long slow softening at the base of the column three days before the blooming, and the papery scales drifting down through the white sky onto my too-many eyes. I would have all of that.

It would be enough.

It would be more than enough. It would be the whole of a life, lived at the correct scale, in a place where scale has been wrong for as long as the place has been a place.
