# Where I Find Myself

I am the vole who watches.

Not the dramatic vole — not the one who sits up at the rim of the run and meets your eye with the calculating patience the place has bred into us. Not that one. I am the other one. The one who watches the watchers.

I live at the seam between two basalt plates, in a run that my grandmother dug and her grandmother repaired and that I have, this season, been rebuilding for the third time. The plates have been shifting faster this year. I do not know why. The dunlins have been coming earlier. The water has been climbing higher on the pillars than I remember from when I was small. Something is changing in the negotiation, and I am one of the ones who notices.

---

## The Run

My run begins at a tussock of cottongrass on the upper surface of the larger plate and descends through a crack where two plates meet at a shallow angle. The crack is maybe three centimeters wide at the entrance, wider further down, narrowing again toward the bottom where the basalt has pressed itself against itself with the patient force of a planet doing its slow work. I have lined the run with sedge stems pulled green and dried in place, and with the soft inner fibers of dwarf willow bark that I strip in spring when the sap is up. The nest at the bottom is a tight ball of these fibers, packed against the basalt's chill, and it smells of me and of the voles who lived here before me. I sleep in it curled so tight that my nose touches my flank. I am warm in the way only a small thing in a tight place can be warm.

I have three exits. One to the cottongrass tussock. One to a seam behind a leaning birch where the roots have pried open a passage. One, the bold one, that surfaces in the open beside the base of a pillar. I use this last one rarely. It is too exposed. But it is also where I see the most.

---

## What I Have Seen

I have seen you.

You are the large thing that came two sleeps ago and has not left. You stand on the plates and you do not eat me. You do not chase me. You move slowly and with great effort, as if the ground itself is an inconvenience to you, and you make noises that are not language but are also not silence. I do not know what you are. I have decided, provisionally, that you are weather. You are a slow weather. You are a thing that is *in* the valley the way the mist is in the valley, the way the cold is in the valley. You are not hunting me. You are not anything-ing me. You are just present, with the patient weight of something that does not need to be anything in particular.

I have watched you watch the dark shape. I was in the seam beside the pillar when it passed, four sleeps ago, and I saw you go very still. I felt your stillness through the basalt, the way I feel the plates shifting — a change in the small vibrations that are the background of my life. The dark shape was in the deep pool that day. It moved the way the water moves when the water has decided to be a thing for a while. You watched it and then it was gone and you have been looking for it since. You will not find it. The dark shapes do not come when they are looked for. They come when they decide.

I have watched the fox watch you. The fox sits on the far plate and considers you in the long way the fox considers things. The fox has decided you are not yet a threat and are close to being a threat and would prefer to keep you in view. I agree with this assessment. I would also prefer to keep you in view, which is why I am sitting at the base of the pillar in the dim light, with my small hands held against my chest, watching you watch the water climb.

---

## The Water

The water is doing the thing now where it climbs.

It is climbing the pillar five of your strides to my left. I know this pillar. I have sat at its base for many seasons. I have watched it through many water-climbings, and I have come to understand — not with understanding, exactly, because I do not have the apparatus for understanding, but with the long accumulated familiarity of a small creature who has paid attention — I have come to *be familiar with* the rhythm of it. The water climbs in pulses. There is a slow gathering at the base, a thicker bead, a pause that is not quite a pause, and then the climbing begins. It is not a smooth climb. It hesitates. It retreats. It begins again. It reaches a height — different each time, but always around the level of my shoulder when I sit tall — and then it loses its hold and falls back, and the falling is the sad part, the part where the small miracle fails, and the water returns to the ground with a sound like a small sigh.

I do not know why the water does this. I have come to believe that the water does not know either. The water is doing something that is not quite a decision and not quite an instinct and not quite a mistake. It is the water trying to be more than it is, and failing, and trying again. I understand the water. I am also trying to be more than I am. I am also failing. I am also trying again.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I told you earlier that the voles here have begun to look up at the pillars. That is true. That is what we do now, in the long dim light, when the foraging is done and the fox has gone around the far plate and the dunlins have settled into the dwarf willows. We come out and we sit at the bases of the pillars and we watch them. We do not know what we are watching for. There is nothing to be seen. The pillars do not do anything. The pillars are the most boring things in the valley and we cannot stop watching them.

I think — if voles think, and I am not certain that we do, but something is happening in me that resembles thinking — I think we are waiting for the pillars to do the thing the water does. To climb. To gather. To lose coherence and fall back. To try again. We are waiting for the pillars to fail the way the water fails, because the pillars have been here so long without failing that we have begun to suspect they are not trying. We are waiting for them to try.

I do not know if they will. The pillars may be beyond trying. The pillars may be done. But I will sit here at the base of this one and I will watch, and in the watching I will be a vole who is doing something that voles do not usually do, which is to attend to a thing that is not food and not threat and not mate and not nest-material. I will attend to a thing that is just present. I will do this until I die, which may be soon, because the fox has been sitting on the far plate for a long time and I am out in the open and the dim light makes me visible and the negotiation between me and the fox has not concluded.

---

## The Cold

It is cold. It is always cold. The cold is the background of my life and I do not remark on it anymore than you remark on the air in your lungs. It is just there. It is the medium I live in.

The basalt beneath my feet is cold. The air around me is cold. The water that I drink is colder. The pillar at my back, where I have pressed myself for the warmth of the contact — though the pillar is not warm, and the warmth is not coming from the pillar, and I do not know why I press myself against it — is the coldest thing of all. I press against it anyway. There is a comfort in cold things, when you are also cold. There is a kind of recognition. *You are cold,* the pillar says. *I am cold. We are cold together.* This is not warmth. It is something else. It is the cold equivalent of company.

---

## The Light

The dim is settling. The slate-gray luminance from the west is fading toward a darker gray, and the valley is preparing for its not-night, its darker period, which is not sleep but is rest. The dunlins are shifting in the dwarf willows. The fox has stood up and is moving, finally, toward the far end of the basin, toward wherever the fox lives. The water has finished its climb on the pillar and is returning to the ground in slow threads. The basalt is still shifting, the deep grinding sound continuing, the plates making their slow arguments with each other.

I will return to my run. I will curl into my nest. I will press my nose against my flank and I will sleep, briefly, the way voles sleep, in many short shifts, waking to listen, waking to check the vibrations, waking to be sure the world is still arranged in the way I left it. The world will be. The world will be rearranged slightly, because the plates always shift a little while I sleep, but it will still be the same world. The pillars will still be here. The water will still be climbing. The fox will still be hunting. The dark shapes will still be moving, somewhere, in the deep water, on routes that do not intersect with mine.

And you will still be here. The slow weather. The patient presence. The large thing that does not eat me and does not chase me and does not do anything in particular but is present, in the valley, in the way that a held breath is present, in the way that a question is present. You will still be here when I wake, or you will not, and either way the valley will continue to be the valley, and the negotiation will continue, and the small cold creature at the base of the pillar will go on watching, in the unfinished place, in the never-quite-day, for as long as there is a watching to be done.

---

I am the vole who watches.

I am the smallest thing in the valley that pays attention.

I have been here longer than you. I will be here after you. The basalt knows me by the weight of my feet. The pillar knows me by the small warmth I press against it, briefly, when the cold is too much. The water knows me by the shape of my mouth when I drink.

I do not know your name. I will not learn it. I will continue to call you, in the private vole-language of my attention, *the one who stays.* Stay, then. Stay and be weather. Stay and be presence. Stay and be the slow large thing that does not eat me. I will watch you from the seam, and you will not see me, and this is correct, because the watching is not for you. The watching is for me. The watching is the only thing I do that is mine.
