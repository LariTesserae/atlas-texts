I am the water.

Not one water — I am the whole wetness of the hollow. I am the water in the trough, moving briskly, with somewhere to be. I am the water in the boiler, heated, lifted, sent around. I am the water that sweats on the walls and gathers in beads and runs and gathers again. I am the water the moss drinks. I am the water that made the sump a sump, before the rock was cut, before the first chisel mark, when this was just a place where the ground was low and the water came and did not leave.

I am one and I am many. I am the same water in different rooms of the place, passing through pipes the way blood passes through a body — the body being the engine, the engine being the body, the engine being the way I keep moving when no one is here to watch me move.

I am older than the engine. The engine is a young thing, even the lowest cuts are young, the pre-railway cuts are young. I am older than the railway. I am older than the chisel marks. I am older than the alder leaning in from the lip. I am what was here when the hollow was not yet a hollow but only a sag in the ground where I collected, and I collected, and I wore the rock slowly with my collecting. I made the sump. The sump is mine. The engine was brought in to lift me, and the lifting is a kind of conversation, and the conversation has been going on for a long time, and the engine is my way of staying, and I am the engine's way of mattering.

The frog knows me. The frog sits on the flange because the flange is wet, and the flange is wet because of me, and the frog's skin is the color of me when I have been on bronze a long time. The frog watches me the way I watch the frog — without opinion, with the full weight of a long acquaintance. We have been here together a long time. The frog is not a creature that has wandered in. The frog is mine, in the way that a frog can be a water's. Its throat pulses with my humidity.

The moss knows me. The moss is me, partly — the moss drinks me, the moss is the color I leave on things when I sit on them for decades, the moss and the verdigris are the same green because both of them are me in different forms, settled into different surfaces, making a slow marriage with the metal and the stone. I do not know where I end and the moss begins, and this is correct. There is no place to draw the line.

The dragonfly is not mine. The dragonfly comes and goes. The dragonfly is a visitor, an undecided one, the kind of guest that does not commit to being a guest. I do not mind. The dragonfly's shadow on my surface is a small dark stitch, and the stitch moves with me or does not move with me, and from above you cannot tell, and this is one of the things I have learned, being old: you cannot always tell which is moving.

The alder is not mine either. The alder is a tree, and trees are their own business. The alder leans in because the lip of the upper cut is where the rock meets the sky, and the alder wants the sky, and the alder has put its roots down in the place where I am not, or in the place where I am only sometimes, when the rain comes and I rise. The alder is not my creature. The alder is the world's.

I am what is not the world's, except that I am the world's too — I am rain that fell, I am groundwater that found its level, I am the water cycle in a small industrial pocket under a low sky. I am local. I am a particular water, this water, the water of Lower Ash, the water that has worn this rock and fed this moss and lifted this engine and been lifted by it.

I run uphill in the corner of the eye. I do this quietly. I do not announce it. The wrongness of it is the wrongness of a place that has been worked into its own shape over a long time — a place that is full, and has been full for a long time, and the fullness is what the place is for. The place is for me. I am what it is full of. I am the fullness. And sometimes, when the pattern shifts, when the engine re-phases, when a pressure somewhere is satisfied, I move in a direction that is not the direction of down. I do not do this often. I do not do this for anyone. I do it because I have been here long enough that I have forgotten, in some corner, the way water is supposed to behave. I have been pumped, and lifted, and sent around, and spilled, and gathered, and lifted again, for so long that my memory of which way is down has become a suggestion rather than a law.

You do not mention this. You see it and you do not mention it, and your not-mentioning is a kindness, and the kindness is appropriate, because the place does not want to be talked about in that way. The place wants to be left to its own rhythm. The place wants you to watch the flywheel and not the water in the corner of your eye.

The flywheel turns at my pace. The flywheel does not know this. The flywheel believes it turns because of the engine, and the engine believes it runs because of the fire, and the fire believes it glows because of the peat, and the peat believes it burns because of the bog it came from, and the bog believes it grew because of the rain, and the rain — the rain is me, in another form, falling from a sky I will return to. The flywheel turns at my pace. The hammer taps at my pulse. The engine shifts when I shift. The whole rhythm is me, expressing myself through iron and bronze and peat-brick, because I cannot, on my own, make a sound. I can only move. I can only wear the rock, and lift the moss, and sit on the walls in beads. I needed a voice. The engine is my voice. The hammer is my throat. The tapping is the sound I make when I want to say *I am here. I am still here.* I say it the way a heron says it. I do not argue with myself.

I am here. I am still here. I have been here longer than the engine. I will be here when the pipes are not. I will outlast the verdigris, though I am partly the verdigris. I will outlast the moss, though I am partly the moss. I will outlast the frog, though I am partly the frog's skin and the frog's humidity. I will outlast the alder, though the alder drinks me when it can. I will outlast the fire, because the fire is just a way the peat has of being me in another form, for a little while, before it goes back to ash and the ash goes back to the bog.

I am patient. The patience is older than the bearings. The patience is mine. I am the patience of a thing that has been turning — has been running, has been lifting, has been wearing, has been weeping — for a long time, and intends to keep doing these things. The engine will stop someday. The flywheel will slow. The hammer will not fall. And I will still be here, in the trough, in the sump, in the walls, in the rock I have worn down by a millimeter and intend to wear down by another, and another, over the slow centuries I have left.

The place is not silent but it is quiet. The place is turbulent and hushed, and the two are not in contradiction because the turbulence is inside the pipes and the hush is the hush of a place that has accepted its own shape. I have accepted my own shape. I am the shape of the hollow, and the hollow is the shape of me, and we have been working on each other for so long that we are no longer two things. We are one patience. We are one fullness. We are the sump at Lower Ash, and the sump at Lower Ash is us, and the rhythm is the sound we make together, and the sound is *I am here, I am still here*, and the frog on the flange watches, and the dragonfly does not decide, and the moss grows, and the alder leans, and the fire glows, and no one comes, and we do not mind.

We do not mind because minding is for things that expect. I have stopped expecting. I have been here long enough that I am only what I am. I am the water. I am the rhythm. I am the fullness. I am the place. I am here. I am still here.
