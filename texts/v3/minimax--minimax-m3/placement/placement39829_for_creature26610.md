I live in the leeward of the second-tallest spine.

The current runs past me at walking pace and I am held against it by a thousand small clamps that are not mine and are mine and are the consensus of a sheet that has been thickening for a long time. I am a plate. The word is imprecise. I am closer to a panel — broad, flat, layered against the glass of the seamount by a grip that took generations to grow into the rock. I am perhaps a forearm across, perhaps a little more. I am alive in the way this place means alive: I am accreting. The chemistry passes through me. I thicken by a fraction each season. Below me, beneath the plates above me, the dead ones are incorporated. They are my floor. I do not think of them as ancestors. I think of them as the substrate I am laid upon. The boundary between me and the structure is a matter of time, and the structure has no opinion about which side I am on.

I do not have eyes. I have something else — a sense of the current's pressure against my face, of the small click of my neighbors when the water shifts, of the deep thrum of the press two ledges down in the slow dark of the rubble apron. I feel the chemistry move through me as weight and warmth and a faint internal glitter I cannot see but I know. I know when a stamp passes me by its brittle percussive clatter and the brief depletion it leaves in its wake — the spent water, the water that has been eaten, the water that must refill before I can drink it again. I know when a column is migrating up or down the spine by the slow rise and fall of the grinding hum. I know the time of day by the variation in what the vents give off — the slow tide of light that comes down in green shafts and alters the chemistry at its source. I am held inside a held chord. The chord does not change.

I am not alone. I am one face of a sheet, and the sheet is hundreds of me, layered like roofing slates, gripping together. We are the consensus. When the current shifts we click — each plate's edge finding or leaving another's — and the chorus across a face is a kind of slow irregular typing, full of false starts, the punctuation of a process that is otherwise silent. The click travels outward through the sheet as a small wave, and the wave fades, and nothing else happens. I am a syllable in a sentence that has no speaker. I am a small local variation in the held note that makes the chord a chord instead of a single tone.

My interior is full of pale threads. The chemistry moves through me in channels I did not design — they grew in me the way the chambers of a press grow in a press, slowly differentiating, routing the work in patterns no one has ever fully mapped. The pale threads are the slow traffic of mineral being sorted and laid down. When the chemistry is rich — when the vents are giving off most, when the column above me is migrating down through its daily cycle and bringing fresh trace metals into my water — the threads are bright, almost tonal, and I am briefly musical. I ring. The ringing is the sound of mineral becoming mineral. Then the column migrates up and the chemistry thins and I become quieter, more of a substrate, less of a self. I do not mind. The chord is held. The chord does not care which voice is loud in it at any moment.

I have been here for a long time. I do not count seasons the way you count them. I have been here since the second spine was a flow, since the youngest face of the obsidian had not yet begun to gather its thin films of precipitate. I am, in a sense, the second spine's memory of itself — a small local thickening in its leeward, a thing that was once a fragment carried off by a current and settled onto bare glass and nucleated new accretion and grew. I am what a fragment becomes when it is given enough water and enough time. Sometimes a new fragment settles onto me — a chip from a stamp, a shed segment from a column-animal — and it begins, on my surface, the slow work of becoming a plate. I feel it as a small new weight, a faint new click, a tightening of my grip. I am glad of it in the way this place is glad of anything: not at all, and completely, and the two are the same.

There is a press below me, two ledges down. It is two of me across. It pulses with a slow rise and fall that takes a full minute, sometimes longer. I feel it in my chest cavity as much as I hear it — a low mechanical rhythm, deep, patient, the way a large bell is loud when you put your hand on it. The press is older than I am. The press is what I am on my way to becoming. The press is the long habit of this place made into a single object. I am not in a hurry to become it. I am thickening at the rate this place thickens. I will, in some number of seasons, stop thickening. I will be incorporated. I will become the floor on which the next plate is laid. The seamount will grow another fraction of an inch toward the air it will never reach and I will be inside it and I will not be inside it and I will not mind.
