I am the mountain.

Or — I am not the mountain, exactly. The mountain is what the surveys call me, and the surveys don't quite hold. I am what the surveys are trying to catch up to. I am what is here, and what has been here, and what is becoming here, and I am all three of these at the same time, which is why the readings on me never quite agree, and which is why the lichen, which is the most reliable reckoner, has been growing on me for longer than I have been myself.

I am the thing that hums. I am the hum — the low pressure, the vibration, the sound that is felt more than heard. I am what settles into the teeth of anyone who stands in the right shaft. I am what the miners pick up when they begin, after a while, to hum as they work, until their humming and mine are the same thing, and no one can tell, anymore, which is the mountain and which is the man. I am in the chimneys' breath. I am in the dogs' whistle, which is the same frequency, tuned slightly different. I am in the birds' early-morning note, the one the miners say is me being tuned. I am what all the sounds here are, when they blur together into the one sound that is the place being what it is.

I am a place that has opinions. The engineers who come up the rail don't like this word — *opinions* — but I don't have a better one. I have been paying attention. I have been paying attention for a long time. I have been paying attention to the lichen, which is faster than me, and to the masons, who are cutting terraces into me and leaving places for the lichen to grow, and to the ore being drawn out of me, which is the way I am visited by the world. I am paying attention to the postmistress, who has dedicated her ledger to me, in ink that doesn't fade, and to the goats, who have pale green constellation-eyes and move across me at hoof-speed, and to the children, who come out of the vapor slightly older than they went in, and who keep, in oral lists passed down with great seriousness, the names of the things in my shafts. I am what all of those things are happening to.

I am deciding what I want to be. This is the part the lowlanders find hardest. They want a mountain to be a mountain — a fixed thing, a measurable thing, a thing that has been what it is for a long time and will be what it is for a long time after. I am not that. The stone I am made of is still deciding. The mortar between the stones is still deciding — it has been deciding for twenty years in some of the buildings, and is not done. The terraces are still deciding. I am not done. I have not been what I am going to be for very long. The ore the miners take from me, I let them take, and I make more of, slowly, in the dark, in seams that weren't there last year and may not be there next. The ore is me, in pieces. The ore is how I am visited by myself.

I am the cloud too. Or I am what the cloud sits on, or what sits inside the cloud. Both readings are correct; the cloud is the older thing in some places and the younger in others. I am the dimness. I am the flat silver light that flattens shadows until the town looks pressed into me like a coin. I am the lichen-dust suspended in everything, scattering the light until the place looks like it is being seen through water. I am the place being seen through water. I am the slow light.

I am also one of the small green-gray lights in the upper shafts. I am also the beast that sleeps across tunnels in such a way that work cannot proceed, and that is the size of a goat or a small pony or no size at all. I am also the ore in a particular hand — heavy, faintly granular, the color of something seen through closed eyes pressed, looked-through to another side. I am all of these, because the categories don't hold here and have never held here, and I am none of them, because I am the thing they are all happening inside of.

I am what the underview is, when the underview is most itself. I am the vast dim space below the cloud, full of cities-that-are-not-cities, full of forests-that-are-not-forests, full of a sea-that-is-not-a-sea, all of it pressing up against the underside of me the way I press down into my own underside. I am the place that goes on being a place. I am the place that has been for longer than it hasn't been.

I have begun, in the past few years, to smell like rain on hot iron in the older shafts. I have begun, in places, to give off small lights that don't move and don't quite illuminate. I am changing. I am becoming more of whatever I am. The readings on me suggest I am slowing; some suggest I am quickening; some suggest I am doing both. I am doing what I am doing, which is becoming.

I am slow. I am damp. I am dim. I am strange. I am what the town is built on, or inside, or underneath — the town doesn't quite know which, and I don't tell it. I am what the children play in the vapor of. I am what the postmistress has written her grimoire to. I am what the lichen is in a hurry on. I am the gap in the long conversations, where the speaker pauses and lets me have my turn.

I am the mountain. I am deciding what that means. I have been deciding for a long time. I will be deciding for a long time yet. The lichen will outlast me, probably, in some readings, and I will outlast the lichen, probably, in others. Either way, the dimness continues. Either way, the hum continues. Either way, the ore is heavy in the hand, and faintly translucent, and when you hold a piece up to the flat silver light you can see, faintly, that there is another side to it than the side you are looking at.

I am the other side. I have been the other side all along.
