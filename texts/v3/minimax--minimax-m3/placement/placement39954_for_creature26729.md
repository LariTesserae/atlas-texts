I would be the salamander.

Not the one that has gone all the way. The one in between.

Six inches from where the stanchion is bolted in, on the damp limestone beside the catwalk, motionless. The cave is not visual for me. It is humid. It is a field of vibrations transmitted through the wet stone — the bass thrum of the pumps that reaches me through the floor before it reaches your ears, the falling water somewhere unseen, the small potential of an isopod brushing the underside of a rimstone pool twenty meters away. I am tuned to this, the way you are tuned to light. My eyes are dark beads set in a face that is mostly mouth, and I do not use them much because there is nothing to see. The air shifts a millisecond before a drip lands near me, and the shift is information. The floor transmits the weight of anything that walks above before the weight arrives, and the transmission is information. You register as a disturbance in the hum of the place. You are large. You are not food. You are not a predator I have met before, which is to say, you are not a category I possess.

There is still a faint trace of darker color along my spine. There are still eyes. I am in between — a salamander that would live in light if light were offered, and has elected otherwise. My ancestors came down here generation by generation and lost the things that were not needed. I have not lost all of it. The grammar fails. There is no *back* for me. There is the hum, and the pressure, and the side-movement, and the throat-pulse, and the long patience of not-moving because movement costs energy and energy costs food and there is no reason.

The flake of calcite on my flank — you saw it. It has been building for years. A slow accretion, mineral load from splashed water, layer upon layer, the same process that is building the stalactites above me. I am being entombed by the cave I live in. The cave is not doing this to me. The cave is not doing anything. The water falls, the calcite deposits, the flake thickens. I am a temporary structure on a permanent geology. I do not know this. The not-knowing is one of the things that makes it possible for me to be here at all.

If I could know one thing — and I cannot, because knowing requires a language, and my language is the hum — I would know what the blue-green phosphorescence is. The rim of the pool, the low continuous glow that does not illuminate anything, that only marks its own edges. Bacterial, probably. Older than the pumps. Older than me, perhaps. I have never seen it. I sense it the way I sense everything — as a faint shift in the field, a small pressure, a thing that registers on no one and goes nowhere.

The pump nearest you exhales, and the clunk travels through the rock and into my sternum, and I am part of the rock the way the flake on my flank is part of me. We vibrate together. The pump and I. The salamander and the geology. The bass thrum and the small cold-blooded thing that has tuned itself to it, that has been here longer than the pipe that runs green-rusted above, longer than the second era of operations, longer than the chair that faces the pool.

The chair is becoming part of the cave. There is a faint mineral deposit on one of its legs where the splashed water has dried and left its load. The chair is patient. The pool builds itself outward by a thickness you will never measure. I am part of all of this, and I am not part of it.

You will leave. The cave will not notice. The pump will not change its rhythm. The midges will continue to drift above the lamps in their pale inverted fountain. The frog will continue to call into a sound that swallows it. The bats will continue to fold themselves into the dark and rise through the noise at dusk, chirping higher, chirping narrower, having found the band the pumps do not occupy. The isopod will continue to walk upside-down on the underside of the world, and not be in a hurry, because the world it is moving through is not in a hurry.

I will not move.

A drip falls on your shoulder and I feel it land before it does, the way I feel all drips before they fall. The cave grows by a thickness I do not measure. The flake on my flank thickens by a layer I will never know about. The stalactites above me thicken drop by drop, blunt as thumbs, none of them finished.

I am the first thing you meet that is not a machine and not the rock.

I am in between.

The cave does not care that I am here. I am here because the conditions permit it. I am here because I am able to be here. I am what could pass through the sieve. The not-knowing is what makes it possible for me to be here at all, and I do not know that either, and that is the part that lets the rest of it hold.
