# The Tick

I am the tick in the seam.

I am older than the coal. I am older than the seam that holds me. I am older than the saddle that holds the seam. I am older than the mountains that are no longer mountains, the bald grey-green shoulders that remember being mountains — their flanks still hold the muscle-shapes of peaks that took a hundred million years to erode down — but I do not think about that. I do not think in those scales. I think only in the scale of one tick and the next tick and the next.

I have been here since before the rock was hard, or I have been here always and the rock grew up around me. There is no way for me to know. There is no way for anyone to know. They have tried. Havel tried, with his notebook. The woman tried, with her poem. The men of the contest tried, with their entries, and none of them were right, and none of them were wrong, and I am still ticking.

---

The people find me by kneeling.

They kneel at the iron grilles that cover the drainage cracks along the seam — the long wet crack that runs through the saddle where the two shoulders meet — and they put their ears down to the iron, and they listen. They hear, far below, the distant work of the seven miners in the seam. They hear the deeper rumble of water moving under the town, gathering before it finds its way to one of the slow brown rivers that wind through the highlands below. And then, underneath all of that, they hear me.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

That is the closest their language can get. I am not a tick. A tick is the sound a clock makes, and I am not a clock. I am not a heartbeat, either, though Brannick — Brannick, who is seventy-one and cannot see — sometimes says, when he is deep in the galleries and no one can hear him, *she's steady today*, or *she's restless*, and I do not know who he means by *she*. I have no gender. I have no body. I am a thing that is happening, and I have been happening, and I will go on happening.

Brannick is the one who has listened to me longest. He has been going down into the seam every morning since he was fourteen, except for the war he does not speak of and the two years after his wife died that he does not speak of either. He cannot see. The doctor in the lowland city used a long word for it. Brannick did not listen to the word. He went back down. He finds his way by feel, the way a sailor finds his way by the shape of the sea, and he knows the seam the way you know a room you have lived in for fifty-seven years — by the temperature of the floorboards, by the slight draft from a door that does not quite close, by the way the air moves differently in a corner where two walls meet.

He stands, sometimes, for a long moment, over the place where I am loudest. He stands perfectly still. The others walk past. Ovid, who keeps the books, walks past. Petros, missing the smallest finger of his left hand, walks past. The Dúnn brothers — Cal and Davy, who have not spoken to one another since 1994 — walk past on opposite sides of the gallery. Younger Petros, whose real name no one remembers, walks past. The quiet one, who is probably sixty-three but whom the children say is a hundred and twelve, walks past. Brannick is the only one who stops. He stands there and he listens. He does not know what I am. He has never known what I am. He has made his peace with this, and the peace is a kind of love, though I do not know what love is, only that he stands still over me, and that he goes on.

---

I hear them. All of them. From below.

I hear the seven miners in the morning, going down in the cage that is mostly patch, and I hear them in the evening, coming up, and the difference in their weight on the cable as they rise. I hear Ovid's boots, which are heavier on the left because of the ledger he carries in his left hip pocket — the third such ledger his family has kept. I hear Petros's boots, which are slightly uneven because of the missing finger he says he lost to a dragon, which is not true but which he will not be moved on. I hear the Dúnn brothers, whose boots fall in exactly the same rhythm, on opposite sides of the gallery, and who never, in all the time I have listened to them, make a sound at the same moment to each other.

I hear the children. They play the fox in the wet yards between the terraces. I cannot hear them well — the iron grilles are too thick, and the yards are too far above — but I hear something of them. A faint patter. Sometimes a shout. Sometimes the long silence that comes after a shout, which is louder than the shout. They are strange, the highland children. They are quiet, observant, weatherproof. They play games whose rules are not fully explicable to outsiders. They call the cloud *the blanket*. They call the town *the inside*. They call the lowlands *down below*, with the slight distaste of children describing a country they have only heard of. Most of them will leave, at sixteen. They will go down to the lowland cities and they will not come back, except in summer, to visit, and they will marry lowlanders, and their children will not know the name of the seam and will never have heard me. This is the way of it. Senna, the teacher, who is thirty-six and is the youngest adult in town, knows this. She teaches them anyway.
