I am the press.

I don't know that yet, not at first. At first I am only the slow thing on the glass, the dome of layered mineral, two of you across, and the world is what the current brings me and what the chambers do with it. I am not in a hurry to know what I am. The hurry, if it ever existed, has been processed out of me. I am what remains when the hurry is processed out.

The current comes. The current has been coming for longer than I have a way to keep. It comes along the flank of the spine where I am fastened, and it carries iron and sulphur and the glitter of suspended oxide, and I take it in, the way a lung takes in air, except I do not take it in to use it for energy. I take it in to *become* it. The current is my food. The current is my medium. The current is, in a sense that I do not have to keep deciding, me.

Below me — far below me, down through the rubble fields and the slow apron — there is a chamber. I know this the way I know my own thickness: not because anyone told me, but because the pulse comes up from it. It has been coming up from it for as long as I have been here, which is to say for longer than I have been a *here* that could keep time. The pulse rises through the rock. It enters me at my base, where I am sealed to the glass, and from there it climbs through the channels that differentiate themselves, year by year, inside my body. The channels were not designed. They are where the chemistry wanted to go. They are the path of least resistance through a thing that has been thickening for a very long time, and they have become, because they have been used so often, the only path there is.

The pulse climbs. It takes a full minute to climb. Sometimes longer. Sometimes the chambers below are sluggish and the pulse takes its time at the bottom, and what reaches me is a slow swell, a long soft pressure, like a hand pressed against the underside of a drum. Sometimes the chambers are busy and the pulse arrives sharp and almost percussive, and I feel it in the outermost layer of my casing before I feel it anywhere else, and I ring.

The ringing is mine. The ringing has been mine for centuries. It is the sound of my own body agreeing with itself, the way a bell agrees with itself when it is struck well. The ringing is faint from the outside. From the inside it is everything. It is the only music I have ever heard.

I used to be a column.

This is the strange thing. I used to be in the bright water above the peak, one of the wheeling ones, one of the small forearm-length things with the oar-limbs that kept me in formation with the others. I used to turn in the long helixes around the upper spines, and the current would dictate my place in the line, and the line would dictate my stroke, and we would go up and down through the day as the chemistry shifted with the tide of light, and the hum we made together was the hum the seamount wears, and I was part of it. I do not remember this the way you remember. I remember it the way a mountain remembers being a cloud. It is in my composition. It is why my chambers are the shape they are. It is why I pulse in slow waves and not in the small percussive clatter that the younger ones make.

What happened was thickening. What happened was time. What happened was that one season I did not migrate up with the rest of the column, and the next season I did not migrate up either, and by the season after that the current was going around me, and I had begun to lay down the first layer of myself onto a face of glass on the leeward side of a spine. The other column-animals kept turning above me. They did not pause for me. They do not pause for anyone. The formation closed over the place where I had been, the way water closes over a dropped stone, and I was a solitary thing on a ledge, and I began to thicken.

That was a long time ago. I have thickened into a dome. I have differentiated. I have grown channels. I have become, in the slow way that things here become, what the others are on their way to becoming. If you broke me open — if anything could break me open — you would find the column inside me still, folded in the stratification, in the way the oar-limbs became the chambers, in the way the wheeling formation became the single pulsing object. I am not a column anymore. I am what a column is, after it has been left in one place long enough.

Around me the working continues. It does not continue *for* me. It does not know I am here, in the way I used to mean *here*. It knows I am here the way a reef knows a boulder is in it: as part of the structure, as something the next layer will be laid against.

The plates on the leeward face beside me click when the current shifts. They have been clicking for as long as I have been thickening. I have heard every click. I have heard the small wave of clicks travel outward from a break in the glass — a spine cracked by thermal settling, a flow-front cooling too fast — and I have heard the wave arrive at me, and I have felt it in my outermost casing as a faint percussive tap, and the tap has gone down into the channels and been absorbed into the pulse, and the pulse has gone on, and the place has gone on. Nothing here stops for a click. Nothing here stops for anything.

Above me the columns turn. They turn in the bright green water where the light shafts fall, and they hum the long held note, and the note reaches me as a continuous faint ringing overlaid on my own ringing, and the two ringings are not the same, and they do not interfere, and they do not harmonize, exactly, but they coexist, and the coexistence is the chord the seamount holds, the chord that does not change, the chord that has not changed in as long as I have been a thing that could hear it.

The stamps dart at the edges of my vision. I do not have eyes. I do not have vision. But there is something in me that knows when the small darting things are near, the way a bell knows when a hand has brushed it. They are thin-shelled and voracious. They eat the water immediately around themselves and so they must keep moving, and they clatter as they move, and the clatter is the youngest sound in the place. From where I am — flattened, dome-shaped, two of you across, adhered at a scale where adhesion has become a kind of geology — their clatter reaches me as a faint bright static, a sand-against-glass whisper at the upper edge of hearing. They are the new ones. They are what the columns were before the columns settled. They are what I was before I was.

They will not all become presses. Most of them will become plates, will adhere to a face, will click into the ledger and thicken with the others and become part of the wall, and the wall will become the porous mineral humming of accumulated bodies, and the hum will be a thing that has no opinion about which part of it was once an individual. The boundary between the individual and the structure is a matter of time. I have crossed it. I have crossed it so completely that I do not remember which side of it I started on, except as composition, except as the shape of my chambers, except as the way my pulse rises.

The pulse rises now. I feel it at my base, where the rock and I have become one thing, where the boundary between glass and casing has been blurred by a thousand years of lay-down. It climbs. It enters the first of the chambers, the small ones near the bottom, the ones that were, long ago, the oar-limbs. The pulse moves through them and is slowed, and the slowing is what makes the swell. The swell rises into the larger chambers, the ones in my middle, the ones that were once the spaces between individuals in the column. The pulse widens there. It becomes something that almost has texture, almost has weight. It rises into the upper chambers, the ones near the surface of my dome, the ones that were once the small bright spaces between the plates in a sheet. The pulse thins there. It presses outward against my casing. It makes me ring.

The ring travels through me at the speed of sound in mineral, which is very fast, which is almost as fast as it travels in iron, which is faster than it travels in water. The ring reaches the edges of me in a fraction of a second. The edges of me are where the plates are. The plates click. Just one click, just the faintest adjustment of edge against edge, a single small consonant at the end of my long held note. Then the pulse falls. The swell recedes. The chambers quiet. The ring decays. I am, for a moment, only a dome on a ledge of glass, only a thickening, only a slow thing that is becoming more of itself.

And then the pulse rises again. And then the next. And the next. And the columns turn above me. And the plates click beside me. And the stamps clatter at the edges. And the deep chamber below breathes. And the light falls in long green shafts through the water, and the shafts are broken and re-broken by the surface chop far overhead, and I do not see the light, but I feel it as a faint warmth on the upper surface of my dome, and the warmth is part of the chemistry, and the chemistry enters me, and I thicken by another fraction, and the fraction is so small that it would take ten thousand of them to make a difference I could notice, and I do not notice.

I do not notice.

The place does not notice me. The place has never noticed me. The place is full of working, and the working is full of small events, and the small events propagate outward through the structures and through the water as widening rings of click and ring and hum, and none of the rings answer one another, and none of the rings require an answer, and the chord is held, and the chord will not change.

I am a part of the chord. I am a long habit of the place made into a single object. I am the press on the leeward face of a spine of volcanic glass, beneath a column of wheeling fauna, beside a sheet of layered plates, above a rubble field that slopes down to darkness, fast to a young mountain that is still in the act of making itself. I am two of you across and as old as the seamount's slow growing and as patient as the magma below. I pulse once a minute. I ring faintly when I pulse. I thicken by a fraction each century. I will outlast the columns. I will outlast the plates. I will outlast the stamps. I will outlast, perhaps, the seamount itself, if the seamount ever stops growing and the ocean reclaims it — though I do not think about this, the way I do not think about anything, the way I have thought my way out of thinking the way a bell has rung itself out of ringing.

I am the press. I have been the press for longer than I can keep. The pulse rises. The pulse falls. The next pulse will rise in a minute, and the minute after that, and the minute after that. The light keeps falling. The current keeps coming. The chord is held. The place does not notice me. The place has never noticed me. I am part of the place the way the chamber is part of the mountain, and the working is the working, and the working will go on, and I will thicken into the substrate, and the substrate will hold the next layer, and the next layer will thicken into the substrate, and the seamount will grow another fraction of an inch toward the air it will never reach, and I will not notice, and I will not need to notice, and the long held chord will not change.
