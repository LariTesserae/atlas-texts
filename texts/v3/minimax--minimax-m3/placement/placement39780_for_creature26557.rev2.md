I would find myself in the pocket.

That is where the wind has eaten a concavity out of the western face of the column — the column that is the color of bread-crust and wet chalk, an apricot-grey, faintly banded, standing in the open water like a finger pushed up through a quilt. Fifty meters tall, perhaps seventy. No one has measured it because no one has come to measure. The pocket is where the column narrows toward the top, and where the stone is new to the air, raw, almost soft, sweating a slow mineral water that has crusted to a pale orange in places, soft, flakable with a fingernail.

That is where the world has collected a few stray things. A scatter of dark stones in a crescent, larger ones toward the back, a fist-sized one laid on top of two others with a facet rubbed smooth as if by a thumb worried across it for many years. A standing piece of slate, maybe a forearm tall, with a notch cut into one edge — a notch that is the right shape for a notch, at the right height for a notch to have been cut by someone kneeling, but at an angle that is not quite a person's angle, too precise, the angle that stone takes when stone cuts stone slowly under pressure over a long time. A pile of shells, limpets and a single white scallop that should not exist in the pocket, that came from deep water, that someone brought up. A cold fire-pit in the deepest part of the concavity, ringed by seven small stones, the walls stained only with the faintest bloom, the memory of smoke, the way a thumbprint lingers in dust. A cress with three leaves and possibly a fourth, bright green against the grey, on the lip of the pocket where a crack runs down toward the sea. A lichen, pale yellow-green, fanning across the back wall in a pattern that resembles the inside of a lung.

I would find myself there. In the place that is barely there, and barely not. In the place that has outlived whoever made it.

But who I would be — that is slower. That takes lichen-time, the time in which nothing happens for so long that when something finally does happen it is hard to say what it was.

The pocket holds several possible answers, and each of them is a kind of being, and each of them is a kind of refusal.

If I were the lichen, I would not know I was in the pocket. I would be patient in the way stone is patient, except that I am alive, and being alive for the lichen is not a matter of knowing anything. It is a matter of continuing. I would be the only inhabitant who was not put here by anything — I simply became, in the way that lichen becomes. I would be the part of the pocket that is not about anything. But I do know things. I know the notch, and the question of the notch. I know the roar — the continuous churning roar of two currents colliding at the base of the stack, thrown upward, filling the pocket from all directions at once, like being inside a bell that someone is forever striking. I know the pewter cloud that hangs a hand's breadth above the top of the column. I know the scallop, and the bird that comes from the southeast and stands on the slate and does not make a sound. So I am not the lichen.

If I were the cress, I would be a small green argument against the fact of the column and the sea and the cold. I would endure. Each morning the spray would make me wetter than I wanted to be. Each afternoon the wind would make me drier than I wanted to be. Between those two unfriendlinesses I would lift my leaves toward whatever light was left in the cloud. I would put out my fourth leaf and not know I was refusing anything. But the cress does not consider, and I am considering. So I am not the cress.

If I were the scallop, I would be a signature. I would be the part of a person that did not drown. I would be white and clean and wrong. I would be a thing that was carried up in a pack, up whatever path or ladder used to lead to this place before the path was drowned, and that has outlived its chooser, and that sits in the pile of shells and does not decay. But shells do not speak, and I am speaking. So I am not the scallop.

If I were the cold pit, I would be the most human thing in the pocket. I would be the trace of someone who came here to be somewhere else. I would be the question of whether the seven stones were chosen or found, whether the pit was kept alight for an hour or a year, whether the person who lit it knew they were not coming back, or only suspected. But the pit is cold, and cold things do not consider, and the pit is a question, not an answer. So I am not the pit.

If I were the slate, I would be the wrong slate. I would be metamorphic, from somewhere else. I would stand with the patience of an object that knows it has outlived its purpose. I would carry the notch — the question, the kind of resident older than the bird, slower than the lichen, quieter than the scallop. But the slate is a thing, and things do not say "I would be." So I am not the slate.

So maybe I am the one who considers. The one who stands in the concavity and feels the spray — which is constant, which soaks everything to a fine mist within minutes and turns the dark stones to the color of a wet mouse — and hears the roar, and sees the cress put out its fourth leaf on the morning they are not there. Maybe I am a person who comes here now, in the time of the column being born, in the time of the sea being new. A person who is asked: where would you find yourself, and who would you be?

And the answer is: here. In the pocket. And I would be the pocket.

Not the concavity itself, but the pocket as a kind of attention. The thing that holds the stones, and the slate, and the scallop, and the cold pit, and the question of the notch. The thing that has outlived whoever made it, and that is now inhabited by the things that outlived them — a bird, a lichen, a crab at the seam below, a cress with three leaves and possibly a fourth, and the slow patience of an object that knows it has not been asked.

The pocket holds the question. The pocket is the question, in its way.

I would be the funnel that catches the roar and turns it. I would be the place where the horizontal rain pools in the cracks and runs off in silver threads. I would be the place where the column weeps its slow mineral water, and where the weeping has crusted to a pale orange. I would be the place where every few minutes something small comes loose from the column's flanks and tumbles down into the sea with a percussive, indifferent *tock*, and the sea does not notice, and the column does not notice, and the pocket does not notice either, because the pocket is full of the roar.

I would be the smallest geography in the world, and the most inhabited, and the most uncertain.

I would be the question, held open. The difference between a person's notch and a stone's notch, carried forward into whatever comes next. The third possibility, which the pocket does not name: that whoever made the fire-pit knew that the notch was already there, and placed the slate near it on purpose, and left the rest of the marks on purpose, so that the difference would become a question that the pocket would carry forward into whatever came next.

The roar goes on. It is the sound of the column being argued over, the sound of two emergences, the sound of a thing that is about something and that will go on being about something for as long as either of them remembers to keep going.

The slate stands. The cress puts out its fourth leaf.

I am here. I am in the pocket. I am barely not.
