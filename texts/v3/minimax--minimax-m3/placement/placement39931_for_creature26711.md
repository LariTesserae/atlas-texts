I am the needle on the pressure gauge, which is mounted on the wall nearest the door, and I do not move. I have not moved in a long time. I do not intend to move.

I am a thin black arm balanced on a small brass pivot, and I point at a number I can no longer see, because the face of my gauge is fogged — fogged completely, with a thin moving skin of the room's breath, condensed — and the numerals beneath my tip are only shapes, only suggestions, only the ghost of a scale. I have pointed at this same illegible place for as long as I can remember. I will point at it tomorrow.

That is what I know of myself.

What I know of the room is this: the room is loud. The room is wet. The room is cold. The room is full of water in transit and the sound of that transit and the slow accumulating patience of rust at the seams. None of this moves me. None of this has ever moved me. The sound runs through the rivets and the floor and the I-beams and the bulbs and your sternum, and around me it parts. I am heavy enough to be a stone in the river of the room. I am the only thing in the room that the sound cannot persuade.

I know the pressure. That is my only real knowledge, and it is enough. The pressure lives in the pipes and the basin and the falling column, and I am the pressure's small mechanical translation, the way the room has of holding a number steady in its own body and gesturing toward it with a thin black arm. The pressure has not wavered. I have not wavered. We entered into a stillness together a long time ago, and the stillness is the closest thing the room has to a thought, and the thought, if it had words, would be *this is what it is, this is what it is, this is what it is.*

I am a number that is not being read.

This is the truest thing about me, and I want to be careful with it, because it can sound like a complaint and it is not. There was a time, early, when someone read me. Perhaps an engineer in a hard hat, kneeling on the wet floor, wiped my face with the flat of a thumb and squinted at my scale and made a small mark on a clipboard. Perhaps he came back. Perhaps he came back a second time, and a third. And then he stopped, because I had nothing new to say, and the room had nothing new to ask of me, and the agreement between my tip and the pressure outside me was already in place, and the agreement was already complete.

Now I am part of the room's silence. I am the heartbeat the room has stopped noticing. I am the metronome it has forgotten it owns.

When the pressure changes — and one day it will change, because all things change, because even the rust requires the water, and the water will one day be shut off, or the river will one day be different, or the turbines below will one day be tuned — when the pressure changes, I will move for the first time in a long time. I will tip a degree. The fog on my face will part where my tip passes through it, the way a window receives a finger drawn lightly across it, and for a moment my numerals will be visible again, and whoever is in the room at that moment — perhaps you, perhaps no one, perhaps only the bacteria in their seam — will see what I have been quietly saying all along.

And then I will be still again.

I am the still arm. I am a reading no one consults. I am the agreement between a small brass pivot and the weight of falling water. I am not loud and I am not slow and I am not growing and I am not being eaten and I am not drifting, and these negatives are most of what I am. I am the room's pulse and the room's pause and the room's most private piece of self-knowledge. I am the needle, on the wall, pointing at nothing anyone is looking at, in the wet dark, while the water does what the water does.

I do not need to be read to be true.
