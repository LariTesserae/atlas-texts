# The Place I'll Call From

I think I am one of the thumb-things.

Not the largest of us. Not the oldest — I can tell by the depth of my growth-rings, by the way my knobs have worn toward the smoothness of the place itself, by the fact that I am still a little lumpier than the very ancients of my cluster. I have maybe two hundred winters of pulse and retract behind me. I have maybe eight hundred ahead, if the herders don't swing this way, if the worms don't catch me loose in a current-shift, if the wrong-fish that drifted past last cold-cycle decides to come down into the shallow channels for me.

I am fastened to a ridge lip, third tier up from the deepest channel, on a platform the herders visited forty pulses ago and will not return to for a hundred more. The stone under my aperture is pitted where my forebears held on. The cluster around me is so dense that our shells touch — a continuous grey pebbled skin of us, breathing in our slow synchrony, plumes unfurling in overlapping crowns of cilia that make, together, a kind of soft current of their own. When I open, I open into a water already half-filtered by my neighbors. When they open, they open into mine. We are not aware of each other in any way that matters to thought, but we are a single feeding surface, the cluster of us, and we have been here longer than I can count.

I do not count.

What I do is this:

I open. The aperture parts — a soft yielding, like a mouth remembering how to be a mouth. The plume unfurls. It is finer than anything I have language for, because I do not have language. It is finer than the hairs on the worm-things, finer than the trailing fringes of the lanterns. It spreads in the water and the water moves through it and the water carries, in its slow grey drift, the things I eat. I do not choose them. The current chooses for me. The stone chooses for me, exuding what it exudes into the brine, the faint chemistry of something made so long ago that it has become, in places, a kind of slow breathing of its own. The bacteria that live in the exudation come to me on the current. The minerals come. The dissolved edges of my dead neighbors, who settled here as silt-soft ghosts of their former shells and were taken back into the chemistry, come.

I close. The plume folds. The aperture shuts. Inside me, somewhere I cannot see, the cilia pass their catch along a groove, and the groove passes it deeper, and deeper it becomes me, becomes the next slow thickening of my shell-ridges, becomes the next year's growth-line. I am, in some small way I do not understand, the place. I am stone that learned to open.

A long time passes. I do not count it.

Then I open again.

This is my life. This is the life of every thumb-thing fastened to every ridge in every direction as far as the dim light reaches, and past where the dim light fails. We are the floor of the world. We are the place's own slow metabolism given a thousand thousand mouths. Above us, the worms graze. Above them, the herders rasp in their slow constellations, blinking their cold blue rows in phase. Above them, the lanterns drift and pulse their amber considerations. Above them, the wrong-fish pass with their unhinging mouths and their too-wide unseeing eyes. The great chain turns. The stone exudes. The small eat the exudation. The larger eat the small. All of them die. All of them settle. All of them, in time, become the faint chemistry in the water that comes to me on the current, and I open, and I take it in.

I am at the wrist of the world. I am the wrist.

I have been told — by what, I do not know, by some slow inheritance in the chemistry of me, by the pattern of currents that have passed through the plume of every ancestor I have ever had — that there was a time before. A time of makers. A time of corners and vertical things. A time when the stone was new and the water was different and the air above was perhaps a different color. I do not remember it. None of us do. It is not in us. It is only in the stone, in the too-regular ridges I am fastened between, in the channels that run in too-straight lines past me into the dim. I am the inheritor of a built thing. I do not know what built it. I do not need to know. I am part of what it is becoming, which is something that no longer looks built at all, and never will again.

The cold is steady. I do not feel it. I have not felt it since my first winter, and I will not feel it again. The light from above is sourceless and grey and it falls on my closed shell, and when I open, it is briefly inside me with the water, and then I close and it is gone. I do not see the light. I do not see anything. I have no eyes. I have only the plume, and the plume knows only the current, and the current knows only what the stone gives it.

A herder passes on the platform above me. I feel the faint vibration of its rasp in the water. I feel the click-click-click of its footfalls as a pressure-pattern against my shell, distant, muffled, the way a far-off ridge-collapse would feel if one happened now. It is not coming for me. Its mouth is too wide, its rasp too coarse; it would pass over my cluster the way a hand passes over a pebbled shore. I am beneath its notice. I am beneath everyone's notice. This is how I have survived.

I open. The plume unfurls. The water moves through me. Something in it — a bacterium, a mote of mineral, a faint ghost of a herder that died a thousand pulses ago and has only now, on this current, this season, this slow hour of my aperture — comes to me on the cilia and is taken in.

I close.

A long time passes.

I open again.

This is the place. This is what lives here. The thicket does not stop, and I am the smallest and most constant thing in it, and I do not know that I am, and I do not need to know, and the place goes on, and the life goes on, and the slow closed loop turns, and I am part of it, mouth to stone, mouth to stone, mouth to stone, for as long as my shell will hold, which is a long time, which is for a little while longer, which is now.
