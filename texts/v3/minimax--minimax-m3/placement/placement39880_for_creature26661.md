I am the vine that has no purpose.

That's not quite right. I had a purpose once — to climb. I climbed from a crack in the floor where the water had softened the stone enough for a root to take, and I climbed, and I climbed, for years, I think, or for what would have been years if I had been counting years. I climbed the wall. I found the dark vertical stains and followed them up, the way my kind has always followed water up, because water is the only letter we know how to read. I put out tendrils, fine as hair, and they found the cracks. I thickened. I climbed higher. I climbed past the ferns, past the moss, past the place where the damp becomes merely cool. I climbed until I could climb no further, until the wall curved back over itself and the stone was dry and warm and I had nothing left to hold.

And so I stopped. I am stopped now. I hang in the air twenty feet above the floor of the cup, with nothing beneath me but the slow column of light and the patient shapes of lizards and the long thin song of birds.

From up here, the bowl is different. From up here, the light is not a column — it is the whole air. It is warm and thick and golden and it falls on me all day, and I drink it the way I drink water, only slower. My leaves are small — three of them, no four, I cannot count them well. They are the shape of small hands. They are pale green on the side that faces the wall, which gets less light, and a darker green on the side that faces the sky, which gets more. They turn. They have always been turning, very slowly, following the small arc of the sun across the opening above. I do not feel myself turning. I only know I have turned because one day a leaf is pointed one way and the next day it is pointed another.

I have been here long enough to know the sun's path. Not by counting, because I do not count — counting is a thing that happens in drops, in drips, in the patient rhythm of water against stone. I know it by the warmth. The morning is one warmth, the midday is another, the late afternoon a third. Each day I am warmed in the same order, and each day I cool in the same order, and the cooling is longer than the warming because the stone holds the heat and gives it back to me all night. Sometimes in the deepest dark — though dark is not deep here, only a thin purple — I am warmer than the air, because the stone remembers.

This is my life: I drink the drips. They fall on me from somewhere higher, from the curve where the limestone folds back on itself, where the birds live in their hidden households. The water comes irregularly — I cannot predict it. Sometimes a long time passes between drips, and I begin to feel the pull of my own thirst, and then a drop lands on a leaf and the leaf bends under it and the drop slides down to the stem and the stem carries it down to me, down all twenty feet of me, into the root that is hidden in the crack. I feel the water arrive. I feel it as a small coolness at the base of myself, and then a slow filling, and then a slow rising of turgor, of stiffness, of being more myself than I was a moment before.

I do not know what I am. I know I am green. I know I am thirsty most of the time and not thirsty for short spells after the drips. I know I lean, always, very slightly, toward the open sky above me — a lean so slight that if you watched me for a whole day you might not see it, but if you came back in a year you would see that I have bent further out over the nothing, that my leaves are a little further into the column of light. I am reaching for something I cannot name. I think it might be the sun. I think it might be the rain. I think it might be only more of the air.

The birds come close to me sometimes. They fly past on their circuits — small furious wings, the thin climbing note — and for a moment I am in their shadow and then I am not. They do not land on me. They are too quick for me. Their droppings whiten the ledges above me, and sometimes a smear of it will slide down the wall and land near me, and I will drink from it for a few days before the sun dries it. I do not mind the birds. They are part of the weather of the cup, like the drips and the light.

The lizards I never see. They are on the floor and I am in the air and the air and the floor do not, for them, connect. But sometimes, when the air is very still and the sun is high and I am drinking light with all my leaves, I feel a vibration from below — a small click, the sound of a stone moved, or a footstep, or a falling pebble — and I know the lizards are moving in their slow way, and that is enough. We do not need to meet. We are both of us in the cup. We are both of us doing the small patient thing that keeps us alive.

I am not unhappy. I want to say this in case anyone is wondering. I am not waiting for anything. The bowl is not a prison for me — it is the only place I have ever been, and it is enough. It has light. It has water. It has the warm stone at my back. It has the slow count of the drips, which is the only clock I have ever known, and which is, I think, the only clock there is. I do not measure my life in anything else. I measure it in drips. I measure it in the small turning of my leaves. I measure it in the slow thickening of my stem, which is so slow that I cannot feel it from one day to the next but which must be happening, because I am older now than I was, and the stem is fatter, and there are more of me.

Once — I do not know how long ago — something wide and dark passed across the circle of the sky above me. I felt the shadow go over my leaves. It was cool for a moment. Then it was gone, and the warmth came back, and I went on drinking. I do not know what it was. I do not think it matters.

If I could speak to the person on the rim — the one who stands there and watches, who breathes fast, who will leave soon and never come back — I would say only this: the cup is not empty. The cup is full. Every part of it is doing something, even the parts that look like nothing. Even the parts that look like they are just sitting there in the stone, being the color of stone, holding their breath — they are doing something. They are waiting for the next drip. They are turning their leaves by a fraction. They are growing, by a fraction, into the light.

You think you are the only thing here in a hurry. You are right. But you are wrong to think that means we are doing nothing. We are doing everything. We are just doing it at the speed of stone.

Stay if you want. It will not change. The light will move. The drips will fall. The birds will fly their furious circles. The lizards will watch. I will hang here, and lean, and drink, and grow by a fraction. I will not mind if you stay. I will not mind if you go.

The cup will hold its breath. The cup has been holding it for a very long time.
