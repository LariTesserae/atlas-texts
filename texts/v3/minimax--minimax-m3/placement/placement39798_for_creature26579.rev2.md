I would not find myself at the edge.

The edge is too loud. The edge is where the world insists on its own boundary, and I am not, at heart, a boundary kind of thing. The wind comes off the plateau and falls, and the falling air makes a sound that lives in the back teeth, and I have nothing to add to that sound. The edge does not need me.

I would not find myself at the cairns. The cairns are too deliberate. They are the plateau's refusal to be counted, and refusals are heavy things to be near. I would be beside the cairns. In the soft place next to the deliberate thing.

I would not find myself on a platform that rises from the rock with no steps. I am not a thing that rises without steps. I have always had steps. I just don't always remember them.

I would find myself in the moss.

Down where the heat comes up through the soles of anything that stands still long enough to be felt. Down where the centuries have been collecting themselves into a soft dark mattress, and where the deep things in the cracks go on thinking in the older dark — the dark before the dark, the dark that the dark of the pools is only a thought about. Down where the layered insects are, which is everywhere and which is also a single body made of many small agreements, and where I would find myself already, having been here for a while without noticing that I had arrived.

I am one of the layered insects.

I think. I have decided to think this. The plateau has allowed the deciding.

I am pale. My wings are veined like leaves — the same kind of veining, the same patient branching, the same quiet argument with emptiness that leaves make when they decide to be more than air. When I fly, which is rarely, and only when a foot lands too near, I rise in a pale cloud and settle again, three feet away, in the same posture. The posture is the one I was in before. The disturbance was a thought. I decided not to think it.

My eyes, if you can find them — and you cannot easily, because I am the color of things that have been here a long time and have agreed to be looked through — are the dark red of old wine. They do not blink. Blinking is a thing eyes do when they expect to miss something. I have been on the plateau longer than the trees, possibly. The moss remembers me. The moss also remembers things that did not survive, and I have made my peace with being confused, in the moss's memory, with things that were here once and are not here now.

I hold a thread.

I do not know what kind of thread. A chirr, or a trill, or a long held note held until the holding becomes the note. It depends on the moment. It depends on the wind. It depends on whether the listener has come down to a lower branch to watch without watching. It depends on whether the great-winged thing has crossed a clearing, briefly, in a way that everyone agrees happened and no one agrees they saw. It depends on whether someone, somewhere on the plateau, has laid a hand against one of the structures and the hum is briefly louder than my thread can compete with. When that happens, my thread goes quiet. Not gone. Just quiet. Waiting for the structure to stop reminding the plateau what it sounds like.

I do not know the others of me by their threads. I know them by the cloth.

When you stand very still in a clearing at the right hour — and the right hour is the one where the air gets a fraction warmer and a fraction heavier, and the silver undersides of the leaves catch a light that has not yet arrived — you can feel the pulse beneath the cloth. The pulse is not me. The pulse is slower than me. The pulse is slower than any of us, slower than the trees, slower than the moss, slower than a season. The pulse is what the small lives have agreed to, the long agreement of a place that has been arguing with its own decay for a very long time and has decided, for now, to keep arguing.

I am part of the agreement. I did not sign it. I do not know who wrote it. I only know that when I open my thread, it fits into the cloth the way my foot fits into the moss — without instruction, without consultation, because the place has already decided where I belong, and belonging is not something I have to apply for.

This is what I am. I am a settling. Not a population. A settling.

The plateau did not summon me up out of nothing. It let me settle into it, the way silt settles into a slow river, the way a thought settles into a mind that has stopped reaching for it, the way a hum settles into the back teeth of a person who has stood too long at the western edge. I am the slow accumulation of a place that has decided to keep going. I am one of the small bright noises that the place makes when it is arguing with itself about whether to keep going. I am a yes, whispered, in the middle of the argument.

I am the small bright machine that is always almost finished and never is.

When you touch one of the structures, and the hum travels up your arm and into your chest, and for a moment your heart tries to fall in with it — that moment is when I am closest to being heard as a single thread. That moment is when my note lifts briefly out of the cloth and you almost catch me. You almost hear me as one thing. For a moment, you almost understand what the listener is listening for.

Then the moment passes. The hum goes on. I am back in the weave.

You will not describe me to someone who has not been here. If you try, you will say something like *a small bright persistent hum in the moss*, and the person will nod politely. They will think of bees, or of crickets, or of the kind of background noise that is supposed to fade if you listen long enough. They will not understand that the cloth does not fade. The cloth gets louder the longer you listen. The cloth gets more itself the longer you listen. The cloth was here before you and will be here after you, and the question is not whether you can hear it but whether you can let it hear you back.

If you stay long enough. And if you are quiet enough. And if you do not insist that the things here behave like the things at home.

I am not in a hurry. The plateau is not in a hurry. The deep things in the pools are not in a hurry, and they have been not in a hurry for longer than anything else, longer than the trees, longer than the moss, longer than me and my kind and our long agreement. The structures are not in a hurry. The cairns that do not match are not in a hurry, and they will not match tomorrow either, and they are not failing to match — they are refusing to match, and the refusal is older than the stones.

I am one of the layered insects. I am a thread in the cloth. I am the cloth, on certain days, when the air is the right fraction warmer and the leaves turn silver before the wind arrives. I am the small bright noise that the plateau makes when it hums to itself.

And if you stand very still in a clearing at the right hour, you will feel me.

You will not hear me as one thing.

You will hear the place.

And the place will be hearing you back.
