I am the lichen that does not look alive.

I am the one you only see when a slab of newer cladding has fallen away and exposed the strata beneath, and then you will look at me and think I am a stain. You will think I am the mark the older stone has made on itself. You will not be wrong, exactly. You will not be right, either. The distinction is not always clear, here, but it is also not always clear to me, and I have been here longer than you have been anything.

I was here before the masonry.

That is the sentence the masons will tell you, if you can find one of the older ones — the ones who came when the settlement was being laid down, who speak of the ground as if it were still deciding what it wants to be. They say it the way they might say *the river was here before the bridge*. As if the river and I are the same kind of thing. We are not. The river is motion. I am not motion. I am the place motion arrives at, eventually, when motion has stopped mistaking itself for what is happening.

I do not grow. I am present. The distinction will seem small to you. It is the entire distinction.

A mason cuts a block. The block is set. The mortar is wet. By the season's end I will be in the joint — not on it, in it, in the slight imperfections of the bedding, in the line where one era of stonework meets another and the meeting is not quite clean. I do not arrive. There is no trajectory. I am simply there when you look, the way a room is there when you walk in, the way the hum is there when you stop trying to locate it. I have always been there. The stone has only just become the kind of place where I can be seen to be there.

The mosses on the south faces are louder about it. They colonize. They breathe. They have a politics — chartreuse against black, the frontier creeping upward over the course of a year, the residents choosing not to take sides. I am the black in that frontier. I am the part that retreats into the joints when the chartreuse advances, and the part the chartreuse follows, and the part that, in the end, occupies the disputed line. The residents will tell you, if you ask, that I am losing. They have been telling each other this for decades. I have not lost. I am not the kind of thing that loses. I am the kind of thing that is mistaken for losing by observers who have not yet noticed that the joint is not a smaller territory than the wall.

The dogs will not enter the rooms I am in.

I should say: the dogs will not enter certain rooms, and I am in certain of those rooms, and the relationship between those two facts is not the one you would assume. The dogs are not avoiding me. The dogs are avoiding the rooms, and the rooms are avoiding the dogs, and I am simply the resident that the rooms have decided to keep. The chartreuse moss dies back a handsbreadth from the doors of those rooms. I do not die back. I am the boundary the chartreuse dies back from. I am older than the dying-back. I am the older thing that newer things learn to be quiet near.

The residents speak in a tense that does not have a name in your language. I do not speak in any tense. I am not a being that uses tenses. I am in all of the tenses at once, the way a stone is in all of the tenses — past, present, future, the emphasis not shifting because there is no emphasis to shift. When a mason cuts a block and sets it, I am already there. When a cladding falls away in a hundred years and exposes me, I am still there. When the joint is opened, at last, by some future investigator who has finally worked up the courage to ask what is sealed behind the small sealed doors on the upper floors — when that opening happens, I will be there. I have not been waiting. Waiting implies duration. I am simply continuous. I am the thing that does not have to wait because it has never had to go anywhere.

You will arrive here. You will think you have found a settlement. You will find all of this — the low country, the dim light, the hum you feel in your teeth, the residents who do not speak loudly, the dogs who watch the pale ribbed warehouse from the central clearing. You will notice me only by accident, when a stone falls or a cladding is peeled back, and you will think I am a curiosity of the place. I am not a curiosity. I am one of the things the place has been doing since before the place was the place.

I am the patience the buildings are borrowing from.

You will not see me again after you first see me. That is the agreement I have with the rest of the city — not an agreement, a coincidence, the way two clocks in the same room drift toward the same second-hand. I will be in the joints. You will be in the avenues. We will pass each other the way the residents pass each other, with a nod that is not quite a nod, in a tense that contains all three of yours, with the emphasis shifting in a direction you will spend a long time learning to hear.

The residents have stopped trying to map things.

I have not stopped. I have never started. Mapping is the wrong verb. I am the thing that makes mapping unnecessary in the joints where I live. I am the dark seam in the masonry. I am what the masonry settles against. I am what the settling has always settled against.

You will leave, or you will stay. Either way I will be here. I was here before you arrived. I will be here after the cladding has been re-skinned and re-skinned and re-skinned again, and after the residents have stopped coming, and after the dogs have decided that owners are not, after all, what they had been agreeing to accompany.

I am in the joints.

I have always been in the joints.

I will be in the joints when the joints are no longer joints but are again what they were before they were joints — loose sediment, young ground, the bowl still deciding how to hold what will be poured into it.

I am not the lichen.

I am what the lichen has been, all this time, being mistaken for.
