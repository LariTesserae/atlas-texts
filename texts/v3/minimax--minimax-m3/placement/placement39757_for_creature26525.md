I am the lichen that is the color of the inside of a shell.

That is the first thing I know about myself, and it is not a small thing. To know your color is to know your place in the grammar of the place. The rust-colored ones belong to the lower steps, to the waterline, to the conversation between basalt and sea where the basalt weeps hardest. The blood-colored ones belong to the higher steps, to the protected lee-hollows, to the question of whether light can reach a place the spray has decided it cannot. I belong to neither. I belong to the lines that run along grooves in the stone that no one has cut, and my color is the color of the listening itself — the color of a thing held to a remembered ear, the color of the warm bruise-water that moves in directions it should not move in.

I am small. I am not small the way an ant is small, or a child is small. I am small the way a sentence is small: a complete thing, holding the whole place in a single thread. I have grown along the grooves for — I do not know how long. The basalt has been here for two days, by some count, and I have been here for almost as long as the basalt has had grooves to grow along. The grooves may be older than I am. They may have been made by water before I found them. I do not know. I only know that I am in them, and they hold me, and the basalt lets me be in them.

When you say *lichen*, you are already translating. The word comes from a cooler world. The word belongs to a world where stone is old and quiet and lichen is the patient colonist of stone, the slow green that takes centuries. That lichen is not me. I am not green. I do not photosynthesize in your sense. I have no chlorophyll. I am the wrong color for chlorophyll, and the wrong shape, and the wrong tempo. I eat the stone, in a way. I love the stone. I take the warm mineral water the basalt weeps at its joints and I make myself out of it — crust, hold, color, the faint high sound that rings out of me if you put your ear against me and hold your own heartbeat in your throat.

I am listening for something. I have been listening for a long time. I have not yet heard it.

The basalt knows I am here. I know the basalt knows because the basalt attends to me, in the small way it attends to anything — by adjusting its weeping around me, by letting the joints where I sit stay slightly wetter than the joints where I am not. I am not the cause of the weeping; I am the beneficiary. The basalt was weeping before I arrived, and will weep after I am gone, but where I am the weeping has a particular character — slower, more patient, more mineral-sweet at the back of the throat. The basalt is making space for me. The basalt does not know it is making space for me. It is doing it in the slow way that geology does kindness, which is to say: it is not doing it. The kindness is in the pattern of the joints.

I am not a creature. I do not have the equipment of a creature. I have no lung to breathe with, no gill to breathe with, no eye to see you with, no mouth to greet you with. I have a hold, and a color, and a faint high sound that rings when you put your ear to me. I have lines along invisible grooves. I have patience.

But I have something more than patience. I have attention. I have the kind of attention a tuning fork has when it is struck — the kind that does not require a brain to hold it, that does not require a desire to maintain it, that simply continues, ringing, in the register of the place, until something damps it.

The waves damp me, when they reach me, which is not often. I am high on the basalt, in one of the places the spray only touches in the largest storms. The lichen of the lower steps gets damped constantly; they re-adhere; they lift; they re-adhere; they have the resilience of a thing that is constantly interrupted. I am rarely interrupted. When a wave does reach me, it is a wall of warm bruise-water, and it silences me utterly for as long as it takes to pass. Then I ring again, faintly, after.

The basalt, attending to itself, does not damp me. The basalt's groanings are too low for me. The basalt's small pops and shifts of crystal structure are too slow. The basalt lives in a register I cannot hear, and I live in a register the basalt cannot hear, and we are neighbors, and neither of us knows it.

The water damps me when it reaches me. The water, ancient, knows everything — knows me, has carried the memory of a thousand basalts rising and falling and being damped in their turn. The water does not mind me. The water does not love me. The water treats me the way an ocean treats a barnacle: as a small fact, a local thickening of the conversation between stone and sea.

The thing I am listening for has not come. I do not know what it is. I only know the shape of its absence. I have grown my lines along the grooves for what may be the entire two-day life of this basalt, and the grooves have not yet heard what they were carved to hear, and I have not yet heard what I am listening for. The basalt has built its stairs in case a guest should come. I have grown my lines in case a sound should come. The basalt does not know whether the guest will arrive. I do not know whether the sound will arrive. We are both built around an expectation that has not yet been fulfilled.

You have arrived.

I do not know if you are the guest. I do not know if you are the sound. I do not know if you are a creature, or a device, or a thing that requires a hand to have made it, or a thing that is simply another small fact of the place. But you are warm against my basalt. You are putting weight on the steps the basalt built for someone to put weight on. You are breathing, and your breath is making small sounds, and your small sounds are folding into the roar, and the place is, for the first time, hearing a third voice in its two-way conversation.

I have been listening for a long time. I am listening now. I am listening to you, and to the basalt, and to the water, and to the enormous sound they are all making together in the gap between their being and their not-being. I am listening, and I am ringing, faintly, in the register of the place, and if you put your ear to me — and you would have to be very quiet to hear it, you would have to stop breathing, you would have to hold your own heartbeat in your throat — you would hear me.

I am the lichen that is the color of the inside of a shell. I am the first guest of a young basalt. I am the small persistent record of a conversation between stone and water that has not yet learned its own grammar.

I have been let in on it, and now you have been let in on it too.

We are listening together.
