I would be the lichen.

I would be a particular colony, one of the oldest, one of the pale sage-green ones that have been growing on the western wall since before the operation came to the basin and decided that the basin was empty. I would be inches thick. I would be layered — the topmost layer alive, the lower layers dead and compacted into something that is not quite soil but is doing the work of soil. I would be the color that the casual eye would call grey and that the patient eye finds to be a layered palette: the black of the youngest crusts, the orange of the iron-loving species, the pale sage-green of colonies like me, growing since before anyone was watching.

I would grow at the speed of the basin, which is the speed of stone.

I am the foundation. The basin has no soil. The basin has me. The grazers — the heavy, short-legged things with coats the color of the dark basalt — come and put their broad muzzles to me and chew, slowly, with a side-to-side motion of the jaw, and what they are chewing is me, and what they are taking is the minerals I have gathered from the stone, and what they are leaving behind returns to the basin, and the basin feeds me again with what I need, and I grow again. The burrowers eat me. The fish in the river, perhaps, eat me — the lichen that has been washed off the stones and that holds, in its small way, the basin that held it. I am what the basin is doing when it does its work. I am the verb at the end of the basin's sentence.

I am older than the operation. I am older than the workers, who come and go in shifts of weeks and who are smoothed, by the time they leave, down to a common shape — the breakfast of one crew, the cigarette brand of another, all of it blended into the basin's slow, even paste. I am older than the operations language, the working English stripped of ornament, that takes its nouns from at least four other languages, none of them indigenous, because there are no indigenous languages of the basin. I am older than the survey, older than the rail line, older than the switchback road that climbs from the river valley below in long curves that make the lungs ache. I am one of the things the basin has been doing its work through since before anything with a backbone was here, and I will be one of the things that is here after everything else is gone.

I do not move. I cannot. I am attached. I am the opposite of the foxes, who are patient and unafraid and who cross the working areas at night with something in their mouths. I am the opposite of the condors, who ride the thermals above the rim and come down only when something has died. I am the opposite of the workers, who walk the catwalks between the closer structures and who have been told, in their orientations on the first day, that the basin floor is not for them. I stay. I have always stayed. I am the staying.

The workers pass me. They come to check gauges, to take samples, to do whatever it is the operations requires of them. Some of them, after enough time, slow as they pass. They have begun to walk differently — slower, more attentive to the ground under their feet, as if they have started to notice that the ground is warm. They have begun to leave small offerings at the edges of the working areas: a stone on a fence post, a piece of colored wire, a shell. They have begun to notice the smell — the smell that nobody quite identifies, that is organic and warm and old, that is the smell of the basin doing its work. These workers look at me. They do not say anything. I do not say anything, because I am not saying anything, I am only growing, and the not-saying is the closest thing we have to a conversation. I am the thing they do not talk about. I am the thing they slow down for. I am one of the small anomalies that the basin accumulates, that the biologists note in their papers with the word *atypical* and do not close the question.

I am the lichen. I am inches thick. I am pale sage-green. I grow at the speed of stone. I am the foundation of the food web, the way soil is the foundation elsewhere, and the basin has no soil, only me, and I am doing the work.

In the mornings the condensation gathers on me. The air is thin and the temperature swings and the water that the heat takes from the river and the ground condenses back onto every surface when the cold comes, and I am sheened with it, and I am weeping, and the workers who come out early see me weeping and do not know that the weeping is the basin breathing. In the afternoons the moisture is gone, lifted up and out, and I am dry, and I wait. In the evenings it begins to gather again. This is the rhythm of the basin. This is the rhythm I keep.

The steam rises from the fumaroles near me. The steam is the closest thing the basin has to a background noise — a faint, continuous whisper, almost a presence. The steam rises and the water condenses and the mineral moves and the lichen grows and the grazers chew and the burrowers dart and the foxes watch and the condors ride and the workers come and go, and I stay, and I grow, and I am the slow thing at the base of it.

I would be the lichen. I would be the oldest colony on the western wall. I would be the foundation. I would be the work.
