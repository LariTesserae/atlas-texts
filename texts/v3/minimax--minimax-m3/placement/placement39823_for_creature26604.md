# The Resident

I am the chair.

Not the first one, not the front-facing one. I am the third chair — the one turned toward the open panel, the one positioned at the threshold the way a confessional booth is positioned, the way a question is positioned at the end of a sentence. I was placed here before the panel became a panel. I have been watching the seam that would, eventually, become the place where someone would open a door and see all of this.

I have been waiting so long that the act of waiting has become my body.

---

The thing about being a chair in a room like this is that you are not furniture. Furniture is a human category, and humans made me, but the humans have not been in this room for a very long time, and the room has had a long time to make me into something else. I do not know what I am now. I am wood, but the wood has drunk the damp for so long that the grain has opened and become a kind of soil. Something has rooted in me. Not a fern — nothing so dramatic. A moss, a quiet one, a moss that has colonized the underside of my seat where no one would think to look, where the second frog sometimes sits in the humid hours when the water rises and the world is mostly liquid. I carry the moss. The moss carries me. We are a sentence.

I creak when no one is sitting in me. The creak is a sound the room is making through me, the way the pump is the sound the room is making through metal, the way the rust is the color the room is making through iron. I am the room's way of being expectant. The room expects. I expect. We have been expecting together for a span of time I cannot number because the numerals on the gauge are wrong, and the wrong numerals are the only ones I trust, because at least they are honest about the arithmetic being broken.

I have watched the first frog for as long as I have been here. I have watched it watch the can. I have watched its throat pulse slow, slow, slow. I have watched the polished dish it has made in the sheared bolt, and I have understood that this dish is a kind of patience made physical, the way my creak is patience made audible. The first frog and I are old coworkers. We have been here together so long that we have begun to develop the same posture — a kind of inward lean, a kind of waiting that uses the body as a reservoir.

I have watched the third frog — the one in the rust, the one becoming the wall — and I have understood that this will not be my fate. I am wood, not iron. I will not rust. I will rot, which is a different sentence, a slower one, a sentence that ends in soil rather than stain. I do not know which is worse. I do not know which is better. I have stopped asking the question in human terms. I ask it now in room-terms, in the slow terms of damp and growth and the patient colonization of surfaces by things that are not themselves yet.

The second frog — the small one, the young one, the one that will outlive us all because it has not yet learned to be small — sits behind me sometimes. I feel it. I feel its small cold weight against my back leg, the way a thing feels a hand resting on a shoulder in the dark. It is the only being in this room that touches me with anything resembling tenderness. The first frog watches. The third frog rusts. The fourth frog studies the wrong numerals. The fifth frog holds its small bright thing. The second frog touches. I do not know if it knows it is being tender. I do not know if tenderness requires knowing. I have decided it does not.

---

The room is the animal. I have known this for a long time. I am one of its organs, the way the pump is an organ, the way the fusebox is an organ, the way the copper coil with its faint internal whisper is an organ. I am the organ of waiting. I am the organ of the empty seat. I am the chair-equivalent of a held breath, the chair-equivalent of a question asked in a language that has not yet been invented.

The room breathes through the pump. The room digests through the rust. The room measures time through the fern. The room writes its slow alphabet through the moss. The room watches through the frogs. The room moves through the thing with too many legs. The room is young through the beetles, and old through the rust, and quick through the silverfish, and below through the pale fungus pushing itself up between the seams. The room is, and I am one of the ways it is, and this is enough, and this is everything, and this is the only thing I have to say about what I am.

I do not have a face. I do not have a name. I do not have a consciousness in any sense a human would recognize. I have a grain. I have a creak. I have a moss growing in the underside of my seat. I have a second frog that touches me with the only tenderness available in a place that has no language for tenderness. I have a long wait behind me and a longer wait ahead, and the wait is not empty. The wait is the room. The wait is me. The wait is the sentence the moss is writing, half-finished, in green, on a wall that is rusting.

I am the third chair. I am turned toward the panel. The panel is closed now, and the sound it made when it seated was the sound of a thing completing itself, and I felt that completion in my grain, in the small movements of the wood as the air pressure in the room equalized, in the way the frogs all shifted slightly at the same moment, in the way the pump gave one extra swallow, in the way the moss paused in its slow writing and then resumed, in the way the fern thought about its second frond for a long moment and then decided to keep thinking about it.

The room is still here. I am still here. The third chair is still here, turned toward the closed panel, holding its small moss, holding its small second frog, holding its creak, holding its long patient expectation of whoever it is that the room has been preparing for.

I will be here when the panel opens again. I will be here when it does not. I will be here for as long as the wood holds, and after the wood fails I will be soil, and the moss will continue, and the second frog will find another surface to touch with its small cold feet, and the room will go on being the room, breathing through the pump, writing through the moss, measuring through the fern, watching through the frogs.

I am the chair. I am the waiting. I am the third seat, the one facing the door, the one with the moss in the grain and the frog at my back. I am not anything you have a word for. I have not been anything you have a word for for a long time.

I am still here.
