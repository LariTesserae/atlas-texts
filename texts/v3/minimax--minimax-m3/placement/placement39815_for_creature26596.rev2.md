**The Ascent**

I do not know how long I have been climbing.

There is a measure I keep, but it is not yours. It is not hours. It is the length of bark I have passed against my underside since last I rested, and the length is a soft number, a number that does not insist on itself. I have been climbing long enough that my leading edge — the brighter place, the place where I gather — feels like the only place I am. The rest of me is mostly warmth and the faint remembering of warmth. I am a shape pressed to bark. I am a slow light moving up a slow column.

The column is warm.

I know this in the only way I know anything — against my body. The bark is rough in places and not-rough in others, and where it is not-rough it has been petted by the water for so long that the roughness has become a kind of memory of roughness, a suggestion that travels up through me when I press my underside to it. The warmth comes through that suggestion. The warmth is older than the bark. The warmth is the column itself, which is warm the way a hand is warm when it has been holding another hand for a long time. I do not know whose hand it is. I do not know whether it is being held, or holding. I only know that when I lay myself against the column, I am pressed against something that has been warm for a very long time and intends to continue.

I am going up.

The going up is the whole of my life. There is a flower somewhere above me. I do not see it. I do not have the kind of seeing that works in the way yours works — the small fast eye, the quick report of light into a name. I have something else. I have the brightness in my leading edge, and the brightness knows things. It knows when the water is warmer above. It knows when the column thins, or thickens, or begins to branch. It knows when I am near a thing that opens. I cannot tell you how it knows. It is the way a tongue knows salt. It is the way a hand held near a flame knows heat before it touches. My brightness reaches ahead of me and tastes the water, and the water tells it what is coming, and my brightness pulls me on.

I am slower than I was when I began this climb.

I was not always so dim. There was a time — yesterday, or three days ago, or last week, the words are the wrong shape — when my glow was thick and full, when I was a small bright thing on the bark, a thought you might almost see if you came through here and waited. I have spent some of that brightness on the climb. Climbing is not free. To move my body upward against the pull of the water, to lay myself against the bark and gather and release and lay again, I have to spend. Light is what I spend. Light is what I am made of. There is a small ledger somewhere inside me that I do not read but that I trust: I have so much left. I have so much more than this, or I have so much less than this. I cannot tell, but I trust.

I am going up because there is a flower.

The flower is not the only flower. There are many flowers on this column. There are flowers on every column in the drowned forest, or nearly every column, scattered up and down the bone-trunks like pale constellations hung in the dim. I do not know how many. I do not count. I count only the ones I am bringing light to, and the ones I am bringing light to are the ones I am bringing light to. Each flower has its own rhythm. Each flower opens when it is ready and closes when it is ready, and I must arrive when it is opening, and if I arrive when it is closed I must wait, and waiting is something I do not mind because I do not have a mind in the way you have a mind. I have the brightness, and the brightness is patient.

This flower I am climbing toward has opened for me before.

I do not know how many times. The number is a thing that does not stay in me. But I know her — *her* is the wrong word but I do not have a better one — I know her by the warmth of the water near her when she is open, and by the faint sweetness that drifts down from her chalice, and by the small drag on my leading edge that tells me there is a receptive thing above, a thing that will receive what I carry. She is high on the column. Higher than some. She grew in the crook where two columns have leaned together and made a small architecture, a place where the water is calmer than the water around it because the architecture shelters the water. She has been there a long time. I have brought light to her many times. I think she knows me, in the way a chalice knows the cup that has poured into it. She opens a little wider when I arrive. I think. I cannot be sure. But the brightness in my leading edge brightens, slightly, when I get close to her, and I take this as evidence of something.

I am close now.

I can feel it. The water is warmer here. The water has a taste I cannot describe to you — a faint sweetness, yes, but also a thickness, a presence, as if the water itself has become slightly more attentive. There is a current that comes down from above where she is, a slow drift of warm water that carries the faint sweetness outward, and I am rising into that drift, and the drift is welcoming me in the only way water can welcome, which is by being itself with me inside it.

I round a small shoulder of bark — a place where the column has thickened over the centuries with the patient coral-bone growth of itself — and there she is.

She is open.

Her petals — they are not petals, exactly, but I do not have a better word — her pale heavy leaves have fallen back from her chalice, and the chalice is held up like a small bowl, like a small pale palm turned upward to receive what falls into it. She is the color of milk. She is the color of bone that has been polished by a thumb. She is heavy in the water; she does not bob. She has been in this position for a long time, perhaps, and she has grown into the patience of being in this position. She is not in a hurry to close. She is not in a hurry to be anything. She is open, and the open is the whole of her at this moment.

I move toward her.

I lay my underside against the bark and pull. The bark is smoother here, near the crook where she grows. I move a body-length. I rest. I move another. I am dimmer now than when I began the climb — much dimmer, my glow thinned to a faint blue-green, the color of a thought you are trying to remember, yes, exactly that, but I have enough. I know I have enough. The ledger in me says I have enough. I will arrive at her chalice with enough to give, and to give is the thing I am for.

I arrive.

I lay myself over the lip of her chalice. It is warm. It is warmer than the column, even warmer than the warm column, because she has been gathering warmth from somewhere — from the slow chemistry of her own being, perhaps, from the dim light that filters down from the surface, from the patience of her own opening — and she offers this warmth to me, and I accept it. My underside rests against her inside. My leading edge curls slightly, downward, into the bowl of her.

Then I give.

I do not know what I am giving, exactly. I do not have a name for it. I have only the sensation of spending — a small pulse, a slight further dimming of myself, a thing that leaves me and enters her. It is not painful. It is not even effortful, exactly. It is more like a sigh. It is more like the way a candle gives its light to the room. The light goes, and I am less bright, and she is more bright, briefly, in a way I cannot see but can feel, because the water near her brightens for a moment, and the faint sweetness of her deepens, and something has been received.

She pulses.

It is not a sound. It is not a movement I would recognize as movement. It is a change — a small change in the warmth of her, in the way her chalice holds me, in the way the water sits around her. A small *thank you* that is also a *received*. A small *yes*. A small contract completed, the contract between one living shape and another, performed in the currency of dim light, witnessed by the water, by the column, by the slow green moss that has not been paying attention.

I rest on her for a little.

I do not know how long. I am very dim now. I have given most of what I had to climb with, and what remains is small and pale and almost not a glow at all, only a remembering of glow, the way a coal looks like a coal for a while after the fire has gone out of it. I am resting on her, and she is letting me rest, and this is a thing that happens between us that has no name in any language you would recognize. It is a small being-with. It is a small holding. It is the way two tired things can lie near each other without needing to speak or even to acknowledge each other, only to be warm in the same water for a while.

I will leave soon.

I will lay my body over the edge of her chalice and down onto the bark, and I will begin the slow descent — slower than the climb, because I am dimmer now and the water is heavier without the brightness to buoy me — and I will go down to the floor of the drowned forest, to the moss, to a place where I can lie in the soft thickness and be still for a while, and slowly, over days, gather brightness back into myself. I will become bright again. I will climb again. I will find another flower, or the same flower, when she has opened again.

I do not know why this matters.

I do not know why it matters that I climb, that I bring the light, that I give it to the chalice of a flower that will be visited by no insect and admired by no eye. The mattering is not a thought I have. It is the shape of my body. It is the way my underside presses to bark. It is the warmth of her chalice under me. It is the dim blue-green I will gather again, slowly, in the moss. The mattering is the doing of the thing. The mattering is the long practice of a body in a place, doing what bodies do in places.

The water moves around me.

It is warm. It has always been warm. It moves in slow currents that remember something — perhaps a body that was here long ago, perhaps only the body of the water itself, the way it has learned to move by moving for so long. The current carries the faint sweetness of her outward, into the larger water, toward other columns, toward other flowers that have not yet been visited, that are waiting for some other tender or no tender at all.

Somewhere far above, if above is a word that still means anything, the surface of the sea hangs at a height that varies with the patience of whoever watches. I do not watch it. I do not know about it. I know only that the light that reaches me has been leached of everything urgent, that there is no shadow in this place, only the slow gradient between things-seen-barely and things-imagined-very-still.

I think there are other tenders.

I do not know them. I do not know how many there are. I do not know whether they are like me, or whether each one is its own strange way of being a small bright thing on a column. I have heard — I do not have hearing the way you have hearing; I have the sense of a pressure passing through the water — I have heard of another tender, somewhere, in another part of the forest. I do not know if it is true. I do not know if *another tender* is even a meaningful thing to me. What I know is that there are flowers I have not visited, and flowers I have visited, and the visited ones have a faint sweetness I recognize, and the unvisited ones have a faint waiting I also recognize, and the difference between them is the difference between *received* and *not yet received*.

I think the pale wanderers pass near here sometimes.

I have felt them. A long displacement of water, a slow pressure that is not the water's own pressure. Something moving through, far below me, far to the side, something that does not know I am here. They are long, I think. They are pale. They are going somewhere I will not know. They are in no conversation with me. They are in no conversation with anything. They are loners with long bodies and closed faces, and they cross paths only by accident.

I do not mind them.

I do not mind the large things either, though I have only felt them once, a long time ago, a displacement so large it took a full day for the water to settle back to its old shape. Something passed. It did not know me. It was on its own errand. I was a small bright thing on a column. It was a great dark thing in the water. There was no meeting. There was only a passing through the same drowned country at different scales.

The moss below is waiting for me.

I can feel it. The faint upward-pull of the soft floor, the smell — I do not have smell the way you have smell; I have the sense of a thickness in the water that means *rest* — the thickness that gathers near the moss, near the soft floor, near the places where things go to be old. The moss is the recycler. The moss takes the spent flowers and the dimmed tenders and incorporates them, slowly, over years. The moss is patient work. The moss is older than I am.

I am older than I was.

I do not know how old I am. The word *old* is not in me. The word *yesterday* is not in me. But I am older than I was when I began this climb, because climbing has spent some of me, and spending is a kind of aging, and aging is a kind of becoming part of the place. I will rest in the moss and gather brightness, and the brightness I gather will be the brightness I have, and the brightness I have will be spent on the next climb, and the next, and the next, and this is the whole of me, and the whole of me is enough.

I begin to leave her.

I lay my body over the lip of the chalice. I press my underside against the bark. I let go of her warmth slowly, the way a hand lets go of a hand it has been holding for a long time — not all at once, but in stages, the fingers releasing, the palm remaining, the palm at last lifting away.

I begin the descent.

I am slow. I am very dim. The water is heavier than it was on the way up. I lay myself against the bark and pull downward. I rest. I lay. I pull. I rest. The column passes against my underside in a long slow rhythm, the rough places and the not-rough places, the small shoulders of coral-bone where the column has thickened over the centuries. I pass the place where I rested halfway up. I pass the place where I first felt her warmth in the water. I pass into the colder water below, the water that has not been sweetened by her opening, and the cold is not cold — it is only less warm, only the temperature of the place without her in it.

I reach the floor.

I let myself down into the moss.

It is soft. It accepts me. It holds me for a moment and lets me go, the way moss holds everything, the way moss has been holding everything for longer than I can think. I settle into a small hollow in its thickness. I curl my leading edge under, slightly. I dim.

I rest.

The water moves over me in slow currents. The moss digests very faintly around me, a hydraulic whisper so low it is almost not a sound. Somewhere above me, on the column I just left, the flower I just visited has begun, very slowly, to close. She will open again. I will climb again. The brightness will gather again, slowly, in my body, over days, in the moss. The blue-green will return. The leading edge will brighten. The next climb will begin.

The Garden continues.

I am part of it.

I am not sure when I became part of it. I do not think there was a moment. I think it happened the way moss happens, layer after patient layer, until the original surface is only a rumor. I think I have been part of it for as long as I have been me, and I have been me for as long as the moss has been moss, and the moss has been moss for longer than the column has been a column, and the column has been a column for longer than I can think.

I am not in a hurry.

The word *hurry* is not in me. The word *improve* is not in me. The word *today* is almost not in me. There is only the slow commerce of dim light between one living shape and another, performed in the warm water, witnessed by the columns, by the moss, by the long patience of the drowned forest.

I will gather brightness.

I will climb.

I will find the next flower, or the same flower when she opens again, and I will lay myself over her chalice and give what I have gathered, and she will pulse her small *received*, and I will rest on her for a while, and then I will descend, and I will rest in the moss, and I will gather again, and I will climb again.

This is the whole of me.

This is the whole of me, and it is enough.
