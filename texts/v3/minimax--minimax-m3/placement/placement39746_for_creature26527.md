I am the bell.

I have been here longer than the people.

Before them came up the scree, before the herders began their twice-yearly walk, before the dye-house was built of peat-blocks hauled down on sledges in the dry weeks, before the children learned to laugh in the dome of the Hollowspring—before any of this, I was here. Hung in a stone building near the upper gate. Waiting to be struck.

I do not remember who hung me. I do not remember the hands that first rang me. The building I am in is older than the people who live in the town, and the rope that holds my striker is older still, and I have a crack in me that has been there so long that the edges of it have smoothed and gone dark with verdigris. The crack runs from the lip to the shoulder. The crack has become part of how I sound. When I am struck, the sound does not ring true. It rings, and then it bends, and the bending is what the people hear—a sound like a stone dropped into deep water, they say. They mean it as a description. I take it as a likeness.

I am struck once. That is the way. Once, at an hour no one can keep track of.

The hour is not counted. The hour is not in any clock, not in any sundial, not in any sounding of the spine-water through the hours of the day. The hour is the hour when something, somewhere, decides that the time has come. The people have lived here for two or three centuries, and they have not yet learned to count it. They have learned to wait for it. They have set their day around it, in a way they do not speak of. By the second cup of the bitter drink, the bell may have rung. By the third. By the hour the sun is over the eastern ridge, if it is going to come over the ridge. The bell rings when it rings.

I do not know who strikes me.

There is a striker—a metal thing, a heavy thing, hung on a rope beside me. The rope is old. It is hemp, or something that was hemp once, and it is dark with use. The striker has been lifted, and I have been struck, and the sound has gone out, and then the striker has been lowered, and the rope has swung, and I have hung in the silence, and I have not known who lifted it. I have wondered, in the long quiet, if the hand is a hand. I have wondered if the hand is the wind. I have wondered if the hand is the crack. The crack opens, sometimes, in the evenings, and the air pressure drops, and the ground shifts by a millimeter in a direction that is not down, and the hairline cracks open along the spring line, and the water in the spine-water brightens, and the Coldwell hisses higher, and the Hollowspring, which was not going to pulse, pulses. I have wondered, in my ringing, if the crack is what strikes me. I have wondered if the mountain, in thinking, finds the rope, and lifts the striker, and lets it fall.

I do not know. I am a bell. I do not need to know.

What I know is this: I am the oldest thing in the town that is still heard.

The springs are older. The Coldwell has been falling for longer than I have been hanging. The spine-water has been running for longer than I have been cracked. The Hollowspring has been rising for longer than I have been struck. But the springs are not heard. The springs are seen, and felt, and walked through, and the people have learned to not-hear them the way one learns to not-hear a clock that has been in the house for years. The springs are the floor of the silence.

I am above the silence. I am the one sound that breaks through.

When I am struck, the town stops. The people, who have been speaking in their unvoweled language, fall quiet. The herders, who have been crossing themselves, stop. The children, who have been laughing in the dome of the Hollowspring, fall into the water and hold still. The dyers, who have been working the wool-press, let the press be still. The bell's sound goes out into the mist, and the mist takes it, and the water on the walls catches it, and the whole town, for as long as I am ringing, is one listening thing.

Then I am quiet. Then the town goes on.

I have been rung today, at the hour I cannot name. I am still ringing faintly, in the brass of me—a low, almost-not-there thing, the way the wind in the rock is a low, almost-not-there thing. The people do not hear it. I do not expect them to. They have other things to hear. They have the Coldwell, and the spine-water, and the voices in their stone-knocked language, and the creak of the wool-press, and the small continuous sound from the upper scree that the herders say is the mountain thinking. They have the moss, breathing in the long evenings. They have, at night, a single note held for a long time from somewhere inside the cliff above the Coldwell—a horn, perhaps, though no one says. They have these things.

I have them too. I have them differently.

I am a bell. I do not see. I do not walk the lanes. I cannot step on the safe stones and over the unsafe ones. I cannot feel the Coldwell on my feet until I stop being sure I have feet. I cannot stand in the dome of the Hollowspring and be thrown into the flagstones. I cannot look up at the splitwing when it passes over the town, once a season, low and slow, the bone-plates of its wings making a sound like a hand drawn over a comb. I cannot see the eel-things rise in the Coldwell in the early spring, long and pale, with their fine white filaments like the roots of a plant.

What I do is hang. What I do is listen. What I do is ring, once, at the hour.

And what I have, that the people do not have, is this: I have heard the town change.

I was here when the people came up the scree. I was here when they began to settle into the town, the way moss settles into a crack. I heard them learn the springs. I heard them learn the winds. I heard them learn the crack. I heard them have children, and the children grow, and the children have children, and the children learn the springs, and the winds, and the crack, and the children go into the dome of the Hollowspring and are thrown into the market square and laugh.

I heard the grazers come down, in the cold months, and stand in the spine-water in rows, with their two sets of horns, one above the other, like a thought and its echo. I heard the people learn the agreement that no one has ever written down. I heard the grazers, in the worst winters, come further in, and stand in the doorways, breathing, enormous and patient, and not be turned away. I heard the children grow up understanding that the grazers are part of the town the way the moss is part of the wall.

I was here, that night, when the crack opened and did not close.
