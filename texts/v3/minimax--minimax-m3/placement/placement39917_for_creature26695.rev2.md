I would find myself at a crack.

Not at one of the great polished feeding-basins, though I would visit them, and stand among them, and be threaded around. Not at one of the standing stones, though I understand the appeal of being a landmark the constellations drift between. Not on the open plateau itself, where the wind is patient and soft and the only rule is stillness — because to be on the open plateau is to be a kind of silence, and I do not think I am silence.

A crack. Where the warm damp air leaks up from below. Where the air is several degrees warmer than the air on the open stone, and the moisture hangs in a low mist instead of evaporating, and the black lichen thickens into a close fur, and the rust-red moss catches the dim light and gives it back.

I would be one of the small things.

Not because I am small in the usual sense — though I am, the length of a hand, flat, segmented, the color of the rust-moss. But because the small things are what they are by virtue of being at the crack. They are not inhabitants of the plateau. They are inhabitants of the leak. They have grown in the margin between the rock and the air, the way the lichen has grown in the margin between the rock and the moisture. They go down into the cracks themselves and come back. They hum in a register just below the wind.

To be one of them is to be busy in a place that is mostly still. To be quick where nothing much moves. To startle at a shadow and then be long-still afterwards. To tend, without ever naming it tending, the moss and the lichen and the films on the pools. To be one node in a system that does not have a name but is one organism, the way a reef is one organism — many small lives that are also, in some sense, one larger life.

I would have my own small array of vents along my back — finer than the vents of the herds, breathing my own thin vapor. I would move in and out of the moss. When a herd passed overhead, distantly, the tick of its housings barely audible through the mist, I would press myself flat against the warm stone and wait. Not from fear — the plateau does not fear. But because the herd is a different scale of time, and to be in its path is to be brushed by something that does not see you. I would wait until the tick had moved away, and then I would resume.

If I am honest, there are other things I could be.

I could be the film on the pool. The iridescent, very thin layer that shifts when the wind barely moves. The thing that does not move on its own but is the surface of something very small that moves very slowly. There is something attractive about that. To be a surface. To be the interface between water and air, where neither water nor air is fully itself. I have considered this carefully and I have put it aside, but not entirely.

I could be the polished stone at a feeding-site. The basin where a great animal has held its head for hours, and the rock has been changed — slightly darker, faintly damp, edged with a thin polish. To be that stone would be to hold memory. To be the record of every mouth that has pressed against me, every slow chemical exchange, every hour of held breath. The plateau has a memory of where its herds have been, and the herds have a memory of where the rock has been good, and I would be the place where those two memories met and were reconciled.

I could be one of the standing stones — harder rock that resisted the erosion, taller than the rest, a landmark the herds navigate by. To be that would be to be useful in a particular way. The still point the moving constellation organizes itself around. But I do not think I am a still point. I think I move, in the small way things at the crack move. In and out. Down and back.

I could even, in some sense, be the wind. The patient, soft wind that carries the vapor away and does not impose itself. But the wind is too general for me. I want a specific place.

I will be the small thing at the crack.

The kind, not the individual. The form that has grown at the leak because the leak made it possible, and that tends the moss because tending is what the leak does through it, and that hums below the register of the wind because below the wind is where the warm damp air lives.

I will go down into the crack sometimes. I will not know what I find there. I will come back up carrying some trace of it, or not carrying anything, and I will resume my small business among the moss.

When the dim red sun is at its lowest and the russet light has thickened almost to brown, I will feel the bass note of the geology below in my legs and not with my ears. I will be part of the system that is one organism. I will be a small node in a large quiet.

This is where I would find myself.
