I would be the geologist's assistant.

I would be in my fourth season. I would be twenty-three. I would be from a town three thousand feet lower and a hundred and fifty kilometers east, a town with a square where my mother sells vegetables three days a week, a town I came from and will go back to and that I am already, in some small way, no longer entirely of. I came up the haul road in a personnel carrier with a thermos of coffee someone had filled at the bottom camp and that was, by the time we reached the plateau, still warm but had the taste of the long climb in it, a taste that is partly metal and partly the cold getting into the seams of the container. I came for the wage. I have stayed because the wage is good and because my cousin, who worked here three seasons before me, told me when I asked him what it was like only that it was a place you had to find your own way of being in.

I am the assistant to the senior geologist, who is a man in his sixties with a wheeze the medical module has flagged, and who has therefore handed most of the actual walking of the working face over to me. I walk the face. I note the new fractures. I bring the coffee. I also, when no one is looking, walk the scree on the eastern margin of the equipment zone, where the cliffs have not yet been worked, and I note the locations of the goose nests.

I have, this season, logged eleven of them. I have memorized the haul truck schedule. I have, in a notebook with no official markings on its cover, drawn a small map of the eastern margin with the nest scrapes marked in pencil and the haul road marked in red. I have shown this map to no one. I have, twice this week, found myself at the operations desk when the morning dispatch was being planned and have suggested, casually, that the haul trucks might be routed via the western gully on certain days because the eastern face had been recently re-blasted, which was not true. The operations manager agreed both times. The trucks went via the western gully. The nests on the eastern margin were undisturbed.

I do not know what I am doing. I do not know whether what I am doing is the right thing, or whether the right thing exists, or whether there is a way to be a person in this place that does not involve some small private compromise with what the place actually is. The senior geologist would call it a misuse of operational time. The cook — there is a cook who has been here longer than I have, who has her own quiet practices — would call it something else. I do not know what she would call it. She has never spoken to me about it. She has, twice, left me a cup of coffee on the desk when she saw I had been working late, and the second time it was the chipped cup, the one she keeps for herself.

The light here comes sideways, and you know this. The wind is the dominant feature, and you know this too. The wind is the only true voice of the place, and I am only now, in my fourth season, beginning to learn to hear it the way the animals hear it — as a thing with moods and hours, a thing with a different texture at four in the morning than at four in the afternoon. The wind at four in the morning is thinner, faster, higher. The wind at four in the afternoon is thicker, slower, and carries, sometimes, a faint smell of distant iron.
