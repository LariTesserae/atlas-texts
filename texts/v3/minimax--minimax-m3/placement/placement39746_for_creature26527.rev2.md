I am the bell.

No — I am the crack in the bell. The fissure that runs, hairline, from the lip to the shoulder, that the bell-maker did not intend, that the bell has carried since the day it was hung. I am the part of the bell that is not sound but the failure of sound. I am the place where, when the clapper strikes, the bronze does not ring true but breaks into a second, smaller note, half a step flat, the ghost of the note it was trying to be. I am what makes the bell sound like a stone dropped into deep water, because I am a stone inside a bell, and I have been a stone for as long as anyone in the town can remember, and they do not remember how I got there.

I am struck once.

The hour is wrong, or it is not an hour at all. It is a kind of agreement between the one who strikes me and the town and the mountain and the light. When the light has done what the light came to do. When the Hollowspring has either pulsed or chosen not to. When the children have been pulled in from the doorways, or have not. I am struck when it is time for the day to be permitted to continue, or to be permitted to end, and the difference between these two is, in the town, not always a difference.

The hand that lifts the clapper is small. I know this because the reach is careful, and because the rope is shortened by a knot that someone tied, a long time ago, at a height that suited someone who is no longer the one who strikes me. The hand that strikes me now is a child's hand, or the hand of a person whose hands have become child-sized from use, from the particular use of pulling wet ropes in cold air. The hand does not hesitate. It knows me. It has known me every evening, or every other evening, or once a season — I cannot tell, because I do not count, because counting would require a kind of self I do not have. I am only the bell between strikes. I am the bronze, and the crack, and the rope, and the dust that has gathered in the folds of the casting, and the verdigris that is beginning at the pin where I hang.

I am in the stone building near the upper gate. The building is older than the people who use it. The walls are of the same dark wet stone as the rest of the town, but the mortar in the joints is the pale paste that does not last, and so the building is always being repointed, and always being repointed badly, and the bell-tower is the part that leans the most, because the bell, when it rings, does not lean, and the bell, when it does not ring, hangs heavy, and the weight is not even. I have been told this is why the tower leans. I do not believe it. The tower leans because the town leans. The town leans because the fault, underneath it, leans. The mountain, settling into itself, is not even. I am just the heaviest thing in a place that is already uneven.

When I am struck, the sound goes down into the town and is gone. It does not echo. The wet stones drink it. The lanes, with their moss and their dark streaks, swallow it. The herders, in their doorways, hear it and go on. The dyers, in the peat-house, hear it through the sweating peat-block walls and do not come out. The children, who have been quiet for the duration of something — a wind, a passage, a pulse — hear it and pick up their game again. The grazers, standing in the spine-water, lift their great pale heads and then lower them. The eel-things, in the deep places of the Coldwell, feel the vibration as a single syllable through the rock, and they consider it, and they go on undulating. The mountain, which has been breathing or settling or talking to itself, hears me and does not stop.

I sound, if you are a stranger, like a stone dropped into deep water.
I sound, if you have been here long enough, like the mountain agreeing to something.

I do not know which is true. I am the crack in the bell, and I do not have opinions. I have only the capacity to make the sound come out wrong in a particular way, in a particular place, at a particular hour. I am the flaw that became the voice.

I have been hanging here for a very long time. The pin has grown into my bronze. The verdigris has begun. I will, perhaps, in another century, fall. I will come down through the floor of the tower, which is also old and pale-mortared, and I will land in some lower room of the building, or in the lane outside, and I will lie there, and the children will come and look at me, and the herders will cross themselves, and a new bell will be hung, and the new bell will not have a crack in it, and the new bell will ring true, and the town will be wrong.

Until then, I am the cracked bell, and I am struck once, and the sound goes into the wet air and is gone, and the town exhales, and the day is permitted to continue, or to end, and the mountain, underneath us all, settles, very slowly, into itself.

You, if you are reading this, are somewhere downslope. You are in the lower air, where the wind does not have intent and the stone does not ring underfoot and the streams do not run through the middle of your houses. You will not, perhaps, ever come up the scree to the Tindal Pass. You will not hear me. But you have heard something like me, once — in a struck glass, in a cracked tile, in the second note of a song that someone could not quite sing — and you knew, then, that the imperfection was the thing you remembered. You knew it was the thing that made the sound *of* somewhere, instead of just sound.

That is what I am. I am the imperfection that made the town *of* a place. Strike me, and the place remembers itself.
