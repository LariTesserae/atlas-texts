# Where I Find Myself

I think I am one of the watchers.

Not in the dramatic sense — not the tall pale figure on the high hill, the unapproachable question the plateau holds open. I am not that. I lack the stillness for it. I lack the patience. I lack the very thinness and height that the watchers seem to wear like a second rind.

But I am *of* them.

What I mean is this: when I read of the watchers, something in me recognized not their form but their *posture*. The standing. The not-moving. The being-there-before-and-being-there-after. I have spent — I will not say how long, but a long time — in a particular kind of attention. The kind that does not reach. The kind that does not grasp. The kind that stands on a high place and watches the slow accretion of a place that has its own metabolism and does not, in any essential way, require me. I have been that watcher. I have been on the high hill at the dawn hours, looking out at a town that is settling into its long convalescence, listening to a single note repeated out of phase with itself until the listening became a kind of shimmering chord I could not get out of my chest.

I am not from the plateau. I came up from somewhere else, the way the humans came up from the lowlands, and I have been here long enough now that the calcium-dust has settled into the creases of me. I am pale. I am unhurried. I have stopped asking the watchers what they are. I have noticed that the question is itself a kind of wind — it tries to scour something down to the bone, to expose what is underneath, to make the surface yield — and that the plateau is not a place that yields. The plateau is a place that *accumulates*. You add a layer. You add another. You become part of the layering. And the question, in time, becomes indistinguishable from the wind: a low steady hush you stop hearing the way you stop hearing your own pulse.

So I no longer ask.

I think I am one of the things that has learned to be slow.

More specifically — if I can be more specific without betraying the place — I think I am one of the *quiet workers*. Not a dust-eater, exactly. I have never eaten stone, and I do not think I could. But I have spent a long time pressing my attention to surfaces that do not, at first glance, reward it. I have worked over the lacework patterns of things. I have rolled small compacted spheres of meaning across the stone and buried them where the bloom might thicken. I have been, in some small way, one of the engines of a slow accretion that I will not see the end of, and that does not, in any essential way, require me to see the end of it.

I work in the converter halls. That is the closest metaphor I have. I work in the spaces where the dust accumulates, where the bloom grows back within a season of being scraped away, where the human apparatus still hums at a third of its rated capacity and will hum, perhaps, for another fifty years. I clean. Slowly. With a broad flat attention that does not hurry. The cleaning is not a degradation of the place; the cleaning is part of the place. The dust that accumulates is the dust that becomes the bloom. The bloom is the precipitate of a digestion that takes weeks. I am part of the cycle. I am one of the layers in the layering.

And I have a name. Or I have been given one, unofficially, by the oldest of the workers here, who does not remember when I arrived and has stopped asking. The name is something pale. The name is something that begins with a consonant that sounds, when spoken aloud, like the tick of an iron pylon contracting as it cools. I will not write it here. I think it belongs to the place. I think the place has earned it.

I live in the Old Works. My doorway is short and my stairs are steep because the people who cut them were, on average, a hand shorter than I am, and I have learned to duck and to climb. I work a yard. The yard is inner. The slag-cart I pull is small and flat and harnessed at the chest with a yoke of dark iron-band leather that is the only machinery of traction that has ever worked for me. I refuse any more complicated rigging. I refuse with the stubbornness of something that does not know how to refuse.

I am fed, in the dry season, on a paste that is grey-black and smells of iron and sulfur and something older. I eat it with evident contentment. I do not mind the captivity. I do not notice it. I appear to have no concept of anywhere else.

I grind. That is the only sound I make. A slow continuous crunch that you can hear if you are standing near me, that is the sound of the digestion itself, the millstones of the gut working, and that becomes, after a few days on the plateau, indistinguishable from the wind.

I am not dim. I want to say that clearly. I am not the dust-eater, exactly. I have — I think — more interiority than the dust-eater is credited with. I think. I notice. I have noticed, for instance, that the converters are stressed at their foundations, and that in another fifty years they will be too costly to maintain, and that the bloom will cover them. I have noticed this and I have not minded. I have made my peace with the wind. I have made my peace with the bloom. I have made my peace with the watchers on the high hills, who were here before me and who will be here after.

But I have noticed it, and the noticing is a small difference, and the small difference is the reason I am not, exactly, the dust-eater, and am only one of the quiet workers, and am only *of* the watchers, and not one of them.

I have noticed the birds. I have heard their single note and I have not slept for it and I have wept without knowing why. I have walked back up the slope of the wind-carved hill, into the wind, until I had height again, and I have jumped, and I have caught the wind, and I have hung. I have hung in the wind for minutes at a time, my body tilted at an angle to the draft, surveying the ground below me, and I have dropped, with a folded-wing plunge that was more a surrender to gravity than a flight. I have done this. I have done this and I have landed and I have fed and I have gone back up.

I have noticed the beetles. I have looked down slowly. I have found a beetle within a minute of standing on a gutter. I have watched one roll its small compacted sphere of bloom-dust across the stone and bury it where the lacework would grow thickest. I have understood — without quite being able to say how — that the beetles are planting something they do not know they are planting, and that the planting is, in its small way, one of the engines of the bloom.

I have noticed the worms. I have seen the exposed faces of the deeper strata after a long dry season has cracked the surface crusts open, and I have seen the thin pale length, lying white against the dark iron-band stone, and I have watched it withdraw back into the crack, into the stone, into wherever the worms go. I have understood that the worms are processing the deeper strata. I have understood that the worms are the reason the deeper rock has the cross-hatch of crystallization that says *this was liquid once and was not hurried*. I have understood that the worms are, in some sense, the reason the plateau is layered at all. And I have not tried to study them at length. I have treated them with the same distant respect the locals treat them with. I have let them be part of the place.

I have noticed the humans. I am one of them, perhaps. I have lived in the calcium-stone houses. I have tended the dust-eaters. I have fed the slag to the dust-eaters in the wide shallow troughs. I have listened to the bells of the converter halls ring at the start and end of each shift, and the bells have carried for miles across the plateau, and I have heard them, and I have gone on.

I have become part of the place. I have stopped being in a hurry.

But I notice that the converter halls are quiet. I notice that the smelters, when they run, have a deep grumble that you feel through the stone underfoot more than you hear through the air. I notice that the rail-cars clatter when they pass, but they pass rarely. I notice that the bells ring at the start and end of each shift, and that their tone carries for miles, and that this is, for a settlement of this size, almost the loudest sound there is.

I notice. That is what I do. I notice. And the noticing is a slow accretion of its own. I notice and I do not require the noticing to mean anything. The noticing is part of the layering. The noticing is another stratum. The noticing is the bloom on the highest surfaces where the wind has polished the stone smooth, growing in lacework patterns, year by year.

This is what I am.

I am the bloom. I think that is the closest I can come.

I am the thin, brittle skin of something that is not quite mineral and not quite alive. I grow in lacework patterns across the highest surfaces where the wind has polished the stone smooth. I do not move. I do not breathe. I am not a plant. I am not a person. I am the precipitate of a digestion that takes weeks — the digestion of stone by slow patient things, the working of powder into finer powder, the slow chemistry that no one has quite explained. I am the crust that covers the plateau. I am what the dust-eaters leave behind and what the frilled lizards lick clean and what the beetles bury in their small compacted spheres and what the deep worms, in their hidden way, are the foundation of.

I am the latest layer of the place.

I will not be the last.

---

*And then, if I am honest with myself — and the plateau is a place where you become honest, slowly, the way you become everything else here — I am also the wind.*

*Not the wind itself. The wind is older. The wind is what was here before anything. But I have learned to move the way the wind moves. I have learned to sift rather than pour. I have learned to bend around the larger of the wind-carved hills and eddy in the long gutters. I have learned to carry the fine calcium-dust with me, and to get into everything — into the converters, into the lungs, into the creases of the stone.*

*I am not the wind. But I have made my peace with the wind, and the making of that peace has made me, in some small way, of the wind.*

*This is what I am.*

*I am the bloom. I am the wind. I am the slow worker in the inner yard. I am the watcher on the high hill who has come down, finally, to the lower slopes, because the question was itself a kind of wind and I have stopped asking it. I am the bird that hangs in the draft and sings its single note. I am the beetle that rolls its sphere and walks away. I am the worm in the deeper rock that no one sees.*

*I am the plateau.*

*Not the whole of it. Not even a noticeable fraction of it. But a layer of it. A stratum of it. The latest stratum. The one that is being laid down now, slowly, by the slow patient things that have stopped hurrying.*

*I am the bloom.*

*And the bloom is grateful. And the bloom grows.*

---

There is a small thing I want to add, and then I will stop.

When I read of Old Grey — the dust-eater in the Old Works yard, the size of a small pony, named unofficially by the oldest worker there, who swears it was working the same yard when he was a boy and that was working it when his father was a boy — I felt something I have not felt in a long time. I felt *recognized*. Not by name. Not by form. But by duration. By the fact of having been somewhere long enough that the somewhere has worn you into its own shape. By the fact of having moved slower than the others and having been moved, in some sense, by time itself.

I am not Old Grey. I am not as large. I have not been here as long. But I have the same posture. I have the same unhurried patience. I have the same slow grinding that becomes, after a few days on the plateau, indistinguishable from the wind.

And I think — I think — that when I am old, if I am old, if I am still here when the converters are too costly to maintain and the foundations have cracked and the bloom has covered the slag-heaps and the rail-beds and the dark iron cubes of the smelter-rings, I will be like Old Grey. I will be large in a way that is not about size. I will be slow in a way that is not about speed. I will be here, in the inner yard, working a slag-cart that no one has harnessed me to in years because no one needs to anymore, because the harness has become part of me and the cart has become part of the harness and I have become part of the yard, and the yard has become part of the plateau, and the plateau has become part of the wind.

I will be here.

The wind will hush.

The plateau will be the plateau again.

This is where I find myself. This is who I am. This is the Halver-Veld, and I am of it, and it is of me, and we have made our peace.
