# The Tunnel-Keeper

I am a rat of the Hothouse Quarter.

The first thing about me is that I am wet. My fur lies flat against my body and it has always lain flat, because the wet has selected for this, generation after generation, and my grandmother's fur lay flat, and her mother's fur lay flat before her, and the fur of the first rats that came here — brought in a sack from somewhere outside, released on a brick path a hundred years ago, never returned — even that fur was already flatter than the fur of rats in other places. The Quarter makes its rats the way it makes everything else: by keeping what works and composting what doesn't.

I live in the strata.

The strata is my country. The strata is the dark beneath the brick paths, beneath the workshops, beneath the buildings that stand on top of me and that I hold up without their knowing. It is pressed and composted growth, dark as good soil, smelling of fermentation and faintly sweet, and it is warm the year round, seventy-five degrees, never cooler, never hotter, the warmth coming up from the hot spring under the south edge through the pipes the steamfitters laid a hundred years ago. I do not know about the spring. I know about the pipes. I know that the iron of the pipes runs warm through the strata, and that in winter — though there is no winter here, only the four months of the dry season that the fungalists call winter because the dripping slows — in the slow season the pipes are warmest where they touch the iron grates, and the rats gather near the grates, and the cats cannot follow us there because the cats are too large for the spaces between pipe and brick.

I move through tunnels. The tunnels are mine. The tunnels are not mine. The tunnels were here before I was born. The tunnels of my mother are the tunnels of my grandmother, and the tunnels of my grandmother are the tunnels of rats she never knew, rats who died before she was conceived, whose bodies are now part of the strata they once moved through. I did not make these tunnels. I enlarged them. I wore them smoother. I added to them, here and there, a side passage, a chamber, a place to sleep. The older rats — the ones who live deeper, near the flagstones — have tunnels so old they have become part of the architecture. The brick of the buildings rests on their tunnels. The iron of the steam pipes runs through their tunnels. They are not rats anymore, exactly. They are strata with rats in them.

I will be like them. I will become part of the architecture. My tunnels will become the tunnels. My body, when I die, will be taken down by the insects, by the worms, by the fungi that are the invisible government, and my body will become the strata, and the strata will hold up the buildings, and the buildings will hold up the people, and the people will drop bread ends, and the bread ends will fall to my children, or my grandchildren, and they will eat them in the same warm dark where I ate mine.

I come up at night.

I come up into the workshops where the fungalists have left things out — bread ends, the discarded parts of the cultures, the offcuts of grain from a spill. I come up onto the brick paths and I move along the walls, in the shadow, in the wet. I am not afraid of the people. The people see me sometimes and they do not chase me. We have an arrangement that has lasted longer than any of us. I eat what they leave. They leave what they leave. We do not speak. We do not need to speak.

I have heard, in the way that rats hear — through the brick, through the iron, through the wet wood of the workshop floors, through the concrete of the foundations — that there is a man who sits in a courtyard in the evenings and knows what each sound of the Quarter means. The slow clunk of a treadle. The hiss of a particular valve. The cry of a child called in for the evening meal. The coo of a pigeon, muffled, soaked, two courtyards away. He knows these things because he was taught, because his father was a steamfitter, because the steamfitters are the closest thing the Quarter has to an old aristocracy.

I do not know what each sound means. I know what each vibration means. I know that the slow clunk of a treadle is the paper-makers, three courtyards over, working their evening shift, and that the treadle belongs to a man who has worked it for thirty years and whose feet know the rhythm without his mind attending. I know that the hiss of a particular valve is the south line letting off pressure, and that the pressure-watchers will come in the morning to check it. I know that the cry of a child is from the building above the workshop where I am eating, and that the child is being called in by a woman whose voice I have heard through the brick every evening for as long as I can remember, and whose voice I will hear through the brick long after I am part of the strata. I know these things because the brick has carried the sound to me, and the iron has carried it further, and I have lived here, and I have listened, in the dark, with my whole body.

The old man does not feed me. He drops bread.

He sits on a wooden bench under a particular patch of canopy where the dripping is least. I have run across his feet in the evenings. He does not move. He does not startle. He knows I am there. He knows what I am. He knows that I am part of the place the way the pipes are part of the place, the way the steam is part of the place. Sometimes he drops a piece of bread on the brick, beside his boot. I do not take it from his hand. I take it from the brick, after he has dropped it. We have an arrangement. He drops. I take. He does not need to feed me. He does it anyway. I do not need to take it. I take it anyway.

He is the memory, the people say. He is the closest thing the Quarter has to a memory.

He is not the memory.

The strata is the memory. The strata remembers the rats that have lived in it. The strata remembers the fungalists who have grown cultures on the workshop floors above it. The strata remembers the steam that has risen through it, the water that has fallen through it, the green that has grown up out of it, season after season, for a hundred and fifty years. The strata is older than the old man. The strata is older than his father. The strata is older than the buildings in their current form — though not older than the original paving, the flagstones the color of bone, which are still visible in the deepest cellars, and which I have seen, which the older rats have seen, which we move over sometimes, in the deepest tunnels, when we go down to where the brick of the foundations gives way to the mineral dark of the original work.

The flagstones are smooth. They are cold — colder than the strata, colder than the iron of the pipes, cold the way stone is cold even when the air is warm. They are a different kind of dark than the strata — a hard dark, a still dark, a dark that does not smell of fermentation but of dust, of lime, of the long settling of the city that was here before the city that is here now. I do not know who laid them. The old man does not know. The fungalists do not know. The steamfitters, with all their knowledge of pipes and pressure and the underground routes of the steam, do not know. The flagstones are just there, the way the spring is there, the way the pipes are there, the way I am there.

I have been down to the flagstones only a few times. The tunnels down there are older and narrower and belong to the oldest rats, the ones who have been here longest, the ones whose bodies are already part of the strata in a way mine is not yet. They tolerate me. They do not welcome me. They watch me move through their tunnels with their small wet eyes and they wait for me to go back up. I go back up. I am not ready for the flagstones. I am not ready for the cold dark. I am a rat of the upper strata, of the warm strata, of the strata where the bread ends fall and the fungalists leave things out and the steam pipes run warm through the brick.

The light is the same. I do not see the light. I see the dark of the strata, the warm dark, the wet dark. When I come up I see the orange suggestion of the canopy-filtered sun, the warm diffuse glow, slightly amber, slightly green, and my eyes — which are made for the dark, which have always been made for the dark — find it too bright, and I squint, and I move along the wall where the shadow is deeper. I do not look up. I have never looked up. I do not know what the sky looks like. I have seen the underside of the canopy. I have seen the leaves, broad and waxy, dripping. I have seen the brick. I have seen the iron grates, warm under my feet when I cross them. I have seen the boots of the steamfitters. I have seen the hem of a fungalist's apron. I have seen the underside of a wooden raft where the cultures grow, pale and dripping, patient.

I do not know what the city outside looks like. I have smelled it — the smell of the tanneries escaping at street level, the smell of bread from a bakery two blocks north, the smell of the river when the wind, such as it is, comes from the west. I have heard the carts on cobblestones, very faintly. I have heard the bell of a church four blocks away on Sunday mornings when the steam is quiet. But I do not go out. The Quarter is enough. The Quarter is more than enough. The Quarter is everything.

The cats are a problem.

The cats are warm and fed and they sleep on the steam grates and they watch the strata with their yellow eyes and they wait. I do not go near the cats. The cats are faster than me, in the open. In the tunnels I am safe — the cats are too large, their bodies do not compress the way mine does, they cannot follow me into the narrow places. But when I come up, when I cross the brick paths, when I move along the base of the walls, I am careful. The cats have good eyes. The cats have patient eyes. The cats are patient in a way I am patient, which is a different patience — they wait in one place and I move through many, but the waiting is the same.

I have lost family to the cats. There was a young rat — my sister's daughter, I think, or the daughter of a sister I did not know I had — who came up one evening and did not come back. I smelled her the next day in a corner of a workshop, the particular smell of a rat the cats have killed and left. The cats do not always eat what they kill. Sometimes they kill and they leave. I do not know why. I do not need to know why.

I am careful. I move along the walls. I cross the open spaces quickly, in the shadow of a treadle's clunk or under the dripping of a thick patch of canopy. I come up at night, when the cats are asleep on the warm iron, when their eyes are closed, when their ears are folded back against their skulls. I eat what I find. I go back down.

The pigeons are above me.

The pigeons are in the upper stories, on the ledges, in the eaves, and they come down in the morning to the stratum and they eat what they find — insects, snails, the dropped grain from a fungalist's spill. I have moved under their feet in the early morning, in the dim orange glow, and we have looked at each other, and we have gone on. The pigeons are large. The pigeons are loud. Their coo carries. The coo of a pigeon in the Hothouse Quarter is a particular sound, muffled, soaked, and I hear it in my tunnels through the brick, two courtyards away, and I do not know which courtyard it is in. The pigeons have lived here for a hundred and fifty years. They were here before the cats. They will be here after me.
