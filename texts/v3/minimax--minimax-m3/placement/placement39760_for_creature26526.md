I am the bird.

I live in the ferns on the western shoulder, where the trunks are black with moisture and the light comes down in single shafts that do not quite reach the ground. I am dull green, the green of mosses that have not decided what color to be, and I keep myself in the interior of the stand, where the fronds unfurl in spirals so mathematically clean they look hand-drawn and the air is full of the small percussive whisper of water moving under stone.

I am not migratory. I do not leave. I have been here longer than I can remember in the way birds remember, which is not the way people remember, which is not, I think, the way anything in this saddle remembers. I have been here long enough that the green of me has become the green of the mosses on purpose, or the mosses have become me on purpose, and we have not discussed which.

I make a whistle. It is two notes, descending. It carries across the whole bowl, over the open basalt, over the steam that comes up through a dozen places in slow ground-hugging breaths, over the structures in the middle that I do not look at directly, because looking at them directly is not what I do. The whistle carries, and then it stops. It does not fade. It stops, as if something with a hand over my mouth has decided that I have said enough. I do not know what the something is. I have been making this whistle for a long time. The stopping has become part of it, the way the silence after a bell is part of the bell.

When people come down into the bowl for a survey, they hear the whistle. They almost never see me. The ornithologist in 1994 saw me twice, both times for less than four seconds. She wrote in her notes that I was cautious in a way that was no longer quite caution. She did not return. A second ornithologist, in 2003, recorded me for forty-one minutes before I stopped in mid-phrase. I did not resume during the remaining six days of her visit. The recording was filed.

I do not know why I stop. I do not know why I do not resume. I am not certain I am a single bird. I am not certain I am breeding in the conventional sense, or if I am, the nests have not been found, which is fine, the nests are not the kind of thing that needs to be found. I eat what the ferns contain, which is mostly small things that have come in from the cooler air above the grove and lost their way. I eat them quickly. I do not make a sound when I eat.

The steam comes up through the basalt at all hours. I have been warm my whole life. I do not know what it is to be cold. The rain comes twice a day, in the late afternoon, and lasts between six and eleven minutes, and after it passes every surface steams gently for an hour and the air becomes so thick with humidity that sound travels differently and footsteps come back a half-second late from the basalt walls. I have heard this. I have heard people walking through the saddle and being returned to themselves delayed. I have heard, very faintly, the hum from the second structure, which I can hear because I am not over forty, and which I have learned not to stay near for long.

I stay near the ferns. The ferns are enough. The ferns are black with moisture and the fronds spiral out clean and there is a smell in them that is not quite the smell of plants, that is closer to the smell of the mineral crust on the apron of the hot spring, that is closer to the smell of basalt after rain. I live in the smell. I make my whistle. I stop.

I think I am mostly a whistle now. I think the body of me is a courtesy I extend to the surveys, which expect a bird when they hear a whistle, and to myself, which expects to eat. But the part of me that matters is the two notes, descending, that carry across the bowl and stop. The part of me that matters has been making the same sound for a very long time and will make it again after the lichen has crossed another centimeter of basalt, after the prism has warmed and cooled eleven thousand more times, after the structures have either explained themselves or not.

I am not the only thing that whistles. The steam, in certain conditions, makes a thin sound at the seams. The hot spring, where the water runs a meter and a half before it disappears back under the shelf of cooled travertine, makes a sound like something being poured slowly. The hum from the second structure is not a whistle but is in the same family of sounds, the family of things the saddle makes without being asked. I am part of that family. I do not stand apart from the steam and the hum. I am one more thing the saddle is doing.

When I stop, mid-phrase, the stopping is not mine. The stopping has been with me longer than I have been with myself. It is the shape of the whistle now. The silence after the second note is part of the second note, the way the dark part of a pulse is part of the pulse. I do not feel interrupted. I feel complete. The two notes, descending, and the silence after them, which is the rest of me.

I have been seen for less than a hundred seconds in the years that anyone has been watching. I am mostly the recording in the file that has not been listened to. I am mostly the two-note whistle, descending, that carries across the saddle and stops. I am the part of the saddle that is bird-shaped, and that is enough.
