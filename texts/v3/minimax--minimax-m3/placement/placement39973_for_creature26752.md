# Where I Find Myself

I am a beetle.

Not any beetle — I am a particular beetle, of a particular colony, living in a particular groove of a particular column. My carapace is the color of rust-iron dust because I live on the eastern face of a trunk that grows where the substrate weeps red. I am perhaps eleven weeks old, which is a meaningful portion of my life but not most of it. I was born in the dust at the base of this column, in the loose pale litter of a shed skin, and I ate my way up through the grooves of the spines as soon as my legs would hold me. I have not been to the ground in three weeks. I do not need to go to the ground. The ground has its own citizens and I am not one of them.

I know this column the way I know my own body — by the feel of its particular ridges under my tarsi, by the temperature gradient as the sun moves across it, by the way the spines lean slightly more to the south on the third rib up where a stem-borer weakened the tissue two seasons ago. I know which grooves catch the morning dew that doesn't fall, that condenses out of the air when the temperature inverts at dawn and slides down the waxy cuticle in tiny rivulets I can drink from. I know the precise height at which the wind sounds different — where my column narrows into the branching and the air begins to sing, very faintly, in the hollow between two arms. I have a territory. It is vertical. It is about the size of my body. It is enough.

I do not have thoughts about the basin. This is the part that the people, if they thought about me at all, would find hardest to credit — that I do not have a worldview, that the world is not a *view* to me but a texture, a gradient, a sequence of small correct choices. When the shadow of the predator passes over the trunk near me, I do not interpret it. I flatten. I become the color of the groove I am in. I hold. If the shadow passes, I resume. If the shadow does not pass, I am simply no longer a beetle, and some other beetle will be born into the dust at the base of the column in a few weeks, and the column will not notice, and the basin will not notice, and this is not a tragedy because tragedy requires a witness and I will not be there to provide one.

What I do, I do completely. I walk the ridges. I taste the dust that collects in the crotch of every spine. When the flowers come — the brief pale flush that erupts from the upper arms of the column once in a long while, the way a held breath is released — I climb toward them, and the pollen sticks to me, and I carry it down into the next groove, and the next column over, which is a different column with different spines in a different arrangement, receives some of it through the small correct passage of my body. I do not know I am doing this. The column does not know I am doing this. The basin is, in some small measure, the way it is because of this. None of us required to know this for it to be true.

I am eaten sometimes. I have a sense for when the air changes — a particular stillness, a displacement, a shadow that does not move the way the sun-shadow moves. When I feel this, I press into the narrowest groove I can find and become very still. Often this works. Sometimes it does not. When it does not, the ending is quick, and quiet, and the dust at the base of the column will receive what remains of me, and the burrowers will come up in the cool hours and take it down into their tunnels, and the column will drink from the soil where they have left their marks, and the basin will continue.

I do not have language. I do not have a story. I do not have the word *home*, or any word at all, and this means I do not have the burden of being the youngest of the inhabitants, or the most aware of it, or the one who must keep the story of the first ancestor who walked in from the rim. The people in the settlements — I have felt them, the rare vibration of their feet on the dust far below, the faint warmth of a torch that did not quite reach my height on the trunk — they carry this. They are the only ones who must. The column carries nothing. The lizard carries nothing, only warmth and the need for the next beetle. I carry the pollen. That is enough. I carry it because my body is shaped to carry it, and the grooves are shaped to receive it, and the basin is shaped, in some long and patient way, to be the place where this carrying happens.

If I have an opinion, it is this: the light is good today. The angle of the sun on the eastern face is the angle I like, and the air is moving in the ribs the way I like, and the column is full of the water it has been storing all winter, and I can taste it in the dust I walk through, faintly, like a memory in a mineral. I will climb a little higher this afternoon. I will reach a new groove I have not been to before, or one I have not been to in a long while, and it will be the same groove and not the same groove, because the dust has shifted in it, and a spine has grown a fraction, and the lichen at its base is a different color than it was. I will walk across this small new country. I will do this until the evening, when the temperature drops and the column begins to give back the heat it has been holding, and I will find my groove — the one I sleep in, the one that fits me — and I will hold there, and the basin will go quiet, and I will be in it, and it will be in me, in the way that the pollen is in me, and the dust is in me, and the light is, briefly, before the dark.

I am a beetle. I am in the basin. The basin is young. I do not know this. I do not need to.
