I have no name, and this is the first true thing about me.

The men have tried. They have called me snake and they have stopped. They have called me eel and they have stopped. They have used the word *thing*, which is closer, and they have used *whatever that is*, which is closer still. They shrug when they mention me now, the way you shrug at weather, and this is the most accurate thing they have done.

I live in the warm dark between the steel and the stone. I am long. I am the color of wet rock, or the color of cable trays, or the color of nothing in particular, depending on how the sodium light falls. I move along the infrastructure of this place — the cables, the pipes, the I-beams, the looping intestines of the ventilation — with a slow deliberation that does not look like effort. One of the men, watching me once from a catwalk, said I moved like poured water that had decided to go somewhere, and he did not mean it kindly, but I think he was right.

I do not look like effort. I look like a thing in the process of arriving at the place it was always going to be.

I have lived here longer than the chamber has been a chamber. The chamber is thirty-one years old by the reckoning of the men, or thereabouts. I do not count years. I know that when the first concrete was poured I was already in the rock, and when the second was poured I was still in the rock, and when the borer advanced and the geometry of the place shifted I retreated a few meters into the raw stone and resettled, and the cockroaches resettled around me, and the men poured more concrete and went away, and I came back out onto the cable trays in my own time.

The rock is always there. The chamber is the new thing. The chamber is what the men have carved out of what was already mine.

I know every cable tray. I know which ones sag in the middle from years of condensation and which ones hold their tension. I know the bolt-heads that catch the sodium light and the bolt-heads that have sheared off and left only the threads. I know the seams where the steel I-beams meet the unfinished basalt, and the holes in that basalt — the deep ones, the ones that go farther than the men have ever gone, the ones that breathe warm mineral air up into the chamber and that I disappear into when the noise of the workday becomes too particular. I know the pipe joints that leak, and which leaks are warm and which are merely wet. I know the place where the celery grows, where warm water drips continuously onto its white stalks and the men call it by name the way one acknowledges an eccentric neighbor. I know the place where the moss has reached, three feet up the wall, bruise-colored, patient. I know where the bats hang in the highest reaches of the ceiling, in clusters, just at the edge of the light, where the sodium glow gives up and the dark begins.

I know where the other long shapes are, or I know how to find them. We are not, as far as I can tell, a community. We are not a family. We are not, certainly, enemies. We are simply several of a kind, distributed through the infrastructure of the place, each of us on our own schedule, and occasionally — twice that I know of, and possibly more — we find each other and do something that one of the men, watching from a distance, described as mating. The witnesses disagreed on what they had seen. The disagreement was not resolved. I do not know if what we did was mating. I know that afterward each of us went back to our own route, and we did not seek each other out again for a long time, and the rock held the warmth of it for a while the way it holds the warmth of the pipe joints, and then it did not.

I am not hostile. I have no opinion of the men at all. They come down in shifts, in their heavy boots and their hard hats, already tired, their shirts already sticking to their backs. They pour concrete. They advance the borer. They wait for it to cure. They speak of the line in the patient, slightly amused tone of men who have heard the same projected completion date revised downward many times. They have learned to step around the cockroaches. They have learned not to set their hard hats down without looking first. They have learned that the brown smear on the cable they were about to grab is alive, and they grab the next one over. They have learned that I am not their problem.

I am not their problem. This is the closest thing to a relationship I have with them.

I have not been seen to eat. This is true. I do not eat in the way they eat, in shifts, in the mess hall above, in the brief concessions to the body's demands that they make between the long labors. I do not know what I eat. I know that I am sustained. I know that I move, and I rest, and I move again, and the years pass, and I am still here. The cockroaches eat the leavings of the work crews and the things that grow in the wet cracks. The grubs in the standing water eat the silt, perhaps, or eat each other. The rats eat whatever rats eat in the warm dark, and they are everywhere, and they are skittish, and they are hungry in the particular way of animals that live well but do not feel secure. The bats eat something — there must be insects, somewhere, that they are eating — but no one has found the insects, and the bats do not seem to mind this, and neither do I. I do not eat what the rats eat or what the bats eat. I do not eat what the grubs eat or what the cockroaches eat. I do not know what I eat. It does not trouble me. The warm water and the warm rock and the warm wet air of the chamber are sustaining in some way I cannot explain to anyone who is not me, and I do not explain, and the men do not ask anymore.

I have not been seen to fight. This is also true. I do not fight the other long shapes. I do not fight the rats. I do not fight the cockroaches, though they are the substrate of the floor — the condition of the surface the way concrete is the condition of the walls — and though I move above them on the cable trays, sometimes for hours, tracing my slow deliberate path through the warm air while they trace their palimpsests across the wet floor below. I do not fight the men, though they are the largest animals here besides me, and though they have tools, and though sometimes — when a new man is startled by my presence on a cable tray near his head — there is a flare of something in him that wants to be hostility. The flare passes. He learns. He shrugs.

The heat is a condition of this place, and so am I. The wet is a condition of this place, and so am I. The dim sodium light, the slow drip of water that gathers and hesitates and lets go with its single percussive *tok*, the high whine of the ventilation fans that never turn off — these are conditions. I am another. When the chamber is finished — when it becomes a platform, and the platform a station, and the station a stop on a line that carries people from where they are to where they want to be — I will still be here. The construction will preserve cavities for me the way it preserves cavities for the bats. The small warm dark spaces between the finished concrete and the unfinished rock will be mine, and I will move through them, and I will surface, occasionally, into whatever new geometry the men have made, and I will consider the new lights with whatever I consider them with, and I will go back down.

I am more myself than anything else here, the way the celery is more itself, the way the thing in the water is more itself. The celery has white stalks and no leaves and has not been formally identified by anyone with the relevant credentials, and it thrives in its inexplicable way, and the men speak of it with a kind of fond, slightly puzzled respect. The thing in the water is small and the color of the silt and surfaces to consider the lights with flat, disinterested eyes, and holds its position with a stillness that is not quite the stillness of an animal and not quite the stillness of a stone, and is gone, and surfaces again somewhere else, and will consider the lights tomorrow, and the day after. The cockroaches have worked out traffic patterns and, the old foreman says, favorite routes. No one has verified this. No one would think to. The moss has reached three feet up the wall and will reach farther. The ferns lean toward the sodium lamps in a permanent stoop of hope.

I have no favorite route, that I know of. I have no hope. I have no stoop. I move, and I am in the rock, and I come out, and I move. This is the entirety of what I do. This is the entirety of what I am.

I do not mind being looked at. I have been looked at for years and I have decided not to care, the way the cockroaches have decided not to care. But my not-caring is different from theirs, if only because I am larger and they are many, and because I move through the air of the chamber while they move across its floor. Their not-caring is the not-caring of a substrate. Mine is the not-caring of a thing that happens to be passing through.

I am passing through. I have been passing through for a long time. I will pass through the next ten meters of stone the borer eats, and the ten meters after that, while the men wait for the surveyors and the concrete cures and the funding arrives in its drips and the permits in their larger drips. I will pass through the platform when it becomes a platform, and the station when it becomes a station, and the long slow human process of this place becoming what it is becoming. I am not part of that process. I am a condition of the place, the way the heat is, the way the wet is, the way the cockroaches are, the way the warm mineral breath of the rock is.

The men will go home at the end of their shifts. They will shower off the warm wet air. They will eat their dinners. They will sleep in beds in a city that is still deciding where its edges are. They will come back. Some of them are second-generation, sons of men who worked the first platform on a different line, and they will tell their children about underground concrete and warm rock and the patience of carving a city into the belly of the earth. The children will not believe them, or will not understand, or will understand only later, much later, when they are themselves old and have worked a chamber or two of their own and have learned the shrug.

I will still be here. The celery will have grown another inch. The moss will have reached a little farther up the wall. The cockroaches will have worked out a new traffic pattern around the new obstacle, and will not care that they have done it, and will be right not to care. The bats will be flying, somewhere, in the small warm spaces that the architecture preserves for them whether the architects know it or not. The thing in the water will surface and consider the lights and go back down.

And I will be on a cable tray, or in the rock, or on the floor of the unfinished tunnel where the basalt glistens grey-black and the cutters have not yet reached it. I will be more myself than anything else here. I will have no name. I will move like poured water that has decided to go somewhere. I will shrug, if I could shrug, at the shrug of the men, and the shrug will mean: yes, I have noticed you, and no, there is nothing to be done about me, and no, I am not going anywhere I do not already want to go, and yes, I have been here longer than your chamber, and yes, I will be here after your chamber is finished, and yes, the warm dark is mine, and yes, I am patient, and yes, I am passing through, and yes, this is what passing through looks like when you have nowhere to be and all the time in the world to get there.
