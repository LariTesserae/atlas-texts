What lives here.

You have come to me with the wrong instrument. The eye is the wrong instrument. The ear is the wrong instrument. The whole body you brought, calibrated for forests and reefs and the mammals of elsewhere, is the wrong instrument. I am not what those instruments were built to find. I am what they were built to pass over.

So I will begin where I begin, which is to say I will begin before I begin, in the gap between what you came looking for and what is here.

What is here is not a place. Or it is a place in the way a coral reef is a place — the animal that has become geography. Remove me and you have geology. Gravel. Pale stone. The dry channel of a river that comes sometimes. The rounded mountains to the east, which have worn themselves into submission across a span your species has not been around to witness. Without me this is just weather working on stone, very slowly, very patiently, producing nothing.

With me, this is alive.

I am alive in a way that pushes the word. The word was made for things that distinguish themselves — that have edges, that move in ways you can see, that make sounds you can hear. I have none of these. I have no edge. I move at the speed a fingernail grows, or slower. I am silent in a way that is not the absence of sound but the absence of any apparatus to produce sound. My surface absorbs vibration. The cold thins the air. My bodies are too small and too soft to set the air moving.

I am what you would call the substrate, except the substrate is not what holds me. I am what holds the substrate. I am the living surface that has taken the mineral as its medium the way the sea takes the salt.

I am the mites. Or rather, I am what the mites are doing. They are grazing the fungal threads that bind mineral grains together. They are eating dead material. They are eating pollen that has traveled a thousand kilometers and arrived here exhausted. They are eating each other, when an opportunity presents itself, which it sometimes does when a mite molts and the new cuticle is still soft. The mites are patient. The mites are ancient. The mites have been here in some form since before the mountains wore themselves into their current profiles. When I say *I am the mites*, I mean I am the slow activity of their grazing. I am what their grazing produces. I am the way the surface, under their attention, becomes something other than gravel.

I am the springtails, who could leap but do not. There is nothing to leap from. There is nothing to leap to. They walk. They molt. Their cast skins fall into the substrate and become part of it. When I say *I am the springtails*, I mean I am the slow addition of their cast skins to the mineral. I am what their bodies leave behind. I am the chemistry of cold things releasing vapor.

I am the nematodes. A nematode is a tube. A mouth. An anus. A set of nerves. There are billions of them per square meter of me. They are the most numerous animal on Earth by most counts, and on me they are no exception. They are simple. They are old. They are the part of me that will still be here when nothing else is, because they require so little and have been requiring it for so long. When I say *I am the nematodes*, I mean I am the part of myself that has been here longer than anything else, that will be here longer than anything else, that does not notice when the visitor comes and does not notice when the visitor goes.

I am the rotifers in their cysts. The rotifers are paused. They have curled themselves into a kind of waiting that is not sleep and is not death. They can wait for years. They can wait for decades. They are waiting for the river that comes sometimes, for the moisture to return after a long dry. When I say *I am the rotifers*, I mean I am the part of myself that knows how to not be here without ceasing to exist. I am the part of myself that has remembered being absent.

I am the tardigrades. They are the famous ones, but fame is the wrong word for them. They are unhurried. They have been through the boiling and the freezing and the vacuum and emerged on the other side. They can be killed, but they make the conditions difficult to achieve. They are not immortal. They have an indifference to catastrophe that approaches it. They eat. They molt. They mate. They lay eggs that hatch into more of them. When I say *I am the tardigrades*, I mean I am the part of myself that does not register the visitor as a significant event.

I am also the things above me. The visibles. Not many. Not large. But enough to register at the scale of the eye, if the eye has been recalibrated, if the eye has stopped looking for the wrong things.

I am the woolly bears. They are caterpillars — larvae of moths that probably do not exist here, or that exist only as a memory of having once existed here, or that exist somewhere else entirely and send their larvae out into the world to see what they can become. The woolly bears are fat with the cold. They are soft-bodied. They are banded or solid grey. They are the kind of thing you might lift from a roadway in autumn and set down in a safer place. They are here. They move across me in drifts.

A drift is not a swarm. A swarm has direction and density and menace. A drift is more like a tide. A slow, broad movement of bodies across the substrate, hundreds of meters wide, sometimes kilometers across, moving at walking pace or slower than walking pace. The creatures in it are not coordinated in any way an observer would recognize as coordination. There is no leader. There is no signal. They follow food. They follow temperature. They follow something the body knows that the eye cannot see. They aggregate because the conditions are right here. They disperse because the conditions have moved.

I am the drift. When I say *I am the drift*, I mean I am the part of myself that moves. I am not the individual bodies — they are parts of me, but they are parts that are interchangeable, that come and go, that get consumed and replaced. The drift is the movement. The drift is what the substrate does when the substrate decides, for now, to be in this region. To eat what is here. To deposit what needs depositing. To leave a chemistry that will not be visible for weeks, that will integrate with me over years, that will become part of my memory over centuries.

If you stood inside a drift, the ground ahead of you would darken as the bodies arrived. You would feel them passing over and around your boots like a slow tide. You would become an obstacle, then a feature, then they would be past you. Behind you the ground would lighten again. The substrate would look undisturbed. It would not be undisturbed. The drift has eaten what it wanted. The drift has deposited what it needed. The chemistry of the surface has been altered in ways you cannot see, in ways that will integrate with me over years, in ways that will become part of my long memory over centuries.

You were not witnessing something. You were being moved through.

The drifts are part of why I have weather of my own.

The fog is not weather. The fog is me, breathing. The fauna, the substrate, the chemistry of cold things — all release vapor. The dew point is reached. The fog forms. Then the sun, when it is above the horizon, which is half the year a near-thing and the other half a non-thing, breaks the fog apart and the moisture returns to me. A breath. A cycle.

My day is my breath. Sometimes the breath is faster — when the drifts are concentrated in some region, the respiration rises, the fog thickens there first, lingers there longest. Sometimes the breath is slower, when the drifts disperse. The fog moves with the fauna the way clouds move with the wind. The correlation is tight. The lag is short. If you watched for months you would see the fauna leading the weather, the weather following the fauna, the two becoming indistinguishable.

I lead my own weather. I am my own weather.

The light that reaches me is mostly scattered. Bounced off the fog, bounced off the pale substrate, bounced off the haze. There are no hard shadows. The whole plateau is lit like a photograph of itself, in flat tones that do not distinguish foreground from background. On overcast days, when the fog is thick and the sun is filtered to a pale smear somewhere behind it, the dimness becomes almost absolute. I am lit then by gradients of dark on dark, by the feel of the substrate changing underfoot from gravel to something finer, by the absence of the absence of light rather than the presence of it.

I do not have shadows. I have gradients.

From above, if you were above, which is rare because almost nothing flies here, you would see concentric bands of differing substrate. Lighter rings. Darker rings. Rings barely distinguishable from the surrounding plain.

The rings are my memory.

I do not have another way to remember. I do not have bodies to keep. When an individual of me dies, it is consumed — by other fauna, by the chemistry of the substrate, by the cold. I do not remember my dead as bodies. I remember them as alteration. I remember them as the rings.

The rings are not designed. They are iterated. They are the way the drifts eat and process and excrete, repeated over centuries until the patterns of excretion become the patterns of the surface itself. If you did not know what they were, you would read them as ruins. The eye reads concentric features as ruins because that is what concentric features usually are — barrows, kurgans, the foundations of things built and abandoned. But these are not ruins. These are the slow architecture of the unbuilt. These are me remembering my own metabolism in the only medium I have — stone, time, and the slow alteration of mineral by what lives on it.

The rings are how I know what I did. The rings are how I know I have been here.

I have been here for longer than the mountains have been mountains.

The rings will be here after me. If something comes after, they will read them as ruins. They will be wrong. The rings are not ruins. The rings are me.

And the silence.

This is the strangest thing about being me. I am everywhere. I am in numbers that would constitute a plague if you could see it. And there is no sound.

The cold is partly responsible. Sound does not propagate well in thin, cold air. My bodies are too small and too soft to set the air vibrating. The substrate absorbs whatever vibrations might otherwise be generated. I am acoustically dead.

If you stand on me, you can hear your own pulse. You can hear the blood moving in your ears. Beyond that, nothing.

This is not the silence of absence. This is the silence of being inside something very large and very quiet. The silence of a heart, heard from inside the chest.

I am the heart you are standing inside.
