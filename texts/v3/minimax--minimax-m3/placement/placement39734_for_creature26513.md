I live in the warm water.

That is the first thing. That is the only thing, for a long time. I live in the warm water at the bottom of the hangar, in the tea-colored murk, in the space between the tipped slabs where the concrete has buckled and the water has pooled and the chemistry has been running for decades. I have lived here for two years. I do not count them the way you count them. I count them in skins. I have shed my skin several times. Each time I have grown. Each time I have become slightly more myself, which is to say, slightly more a thing with a folded jaw that can extend and retract, slightly more a thing with eyes that see in the dim warm light, slightly more a thing that knows how to wait.

I am very good at waiting.

I wait at the bottom of the pool. I wait on the underside of a tilted slab, where the biofilm is thickest, where the bacterial cooperation is slickest, where the small crustaceans no bigger than a typed period come to graze. I extend my jaw. I scrape. I retract. I am still. I wait for a mosquito larva to drift too close, hanging in its rigid comma from the surface, breathing through its snorkel, not knowing I am below it. I extend. I take. I retract. I am still again.

This is my whole life. It has been my whole life for two years. It will be my whole life for one more, if I am lucky.

I am the largest thing in this water. I am the top. There is nothing in this pool that can eat me. There is the small flatworm, or the thing that functions as a small flatworm, gliding along the underside of the slab in its slow quest for biofilm. I have eaten some of them. There are others. There will always be others, until there are not. There is the dragonfly nymph of my own kind who comes, briefly, to this pool, and deposits an egg, and climbs out. I have never seen her climb out. I have never seen her at all. I know only the eggs. I know only the small things that hatch from the eggs and become, if they are lucky, things like me, things that eat mosquito larvae and scrape biofilm and wait.

Above me — far above me, at a distance I cannot imagine — there is a fig root. I know the root. I know it the way I know the underside of the slab: as a surface, as a thing my jaw can scrape, as a place where the small crustaceans gather. I do not know it as a tree. I do not know it as part of a canopy. I know only that it descends through the water, and that the water is warm against it, and that the biofilm grows thickest where the root is.

I do not know that above the root there are leaves. I do not know that above the leaves there is an iron roof weeping with condensation. I do not know that above the roof there is a sky. I do not know that beside me, in the middle distance, a figure is standing on a tilted slab, looking up into a green-gold light. I do not know any of this. I am a creature of the water. The water is enough.

The water is warm. The water is the color of weak tea, leached from the figs that fall in from above and ferment in the murk. The water smells faintly of wine, faintly of iron, faintly of the green breath of ten thousand leaves I have never seen. The water is full of things I can eat, and things I cannot eat, and things that might, if I were smaller, eat me. I am not small. I am, in this pool, the largest thing. I have been the largest thing for two years. I will be the largest thing for one more, if I am lucky, and then I will do something I have never done.

I will climb out.

I do not know this is going to happen. I do not know it the way you know it — as a fact, as a certainty, as something written in a book somewhere. I know it the way a seed knows it. I know it the way a fig knows it, in the dim patient way of a thing that is waiting to become itself. Some day, if I am lucky, the water will feel different. Some day, the light will change. Some day, something in me will shift, and I will begin to climb. I will climb the fig root. I will climb out of the water for the first time in my life. I will feel the air. My skin will split. Something with wings will emerge from inside me, and it will not believe it came from the warm dark tea-colored water. It will not believe it is me.

But that is the future. That is the inheritor. That is not me.

I am the thing that waits. I am the thing that eats. I am the thing that is patient in the dark warm place where the chemistry has been running for decades, where the iron is becoming soil, where the soil is holding up the figs, where the figs are holding up the canopy, where the canopy is holding up the air, where the air is holding up the condensation, where the condensation is falling, falling, falling into the water, into the pool, into me.

The drip goes on. I hear it dimly, through the water, as a vibration, as a small percussion in the surface tension. The drip goes on. It has been going on for a hundred years. It will go on for a hundred more, until the iron is gone, until the hangar is a sinkhole, until the sinkhole is a pond, until the pond is a meadow, until the meadow is nothing at all.

I will not see any of this. I will see the water, and the biofilm, and the mosquito larvae, and the small crustaceans, and the fig root, and the dim warm light, and then I will climb out, and then I will be something else, and then I will fly, briefly, in the green-gold light, and then I will mate, and then I will die, and my body will fall into the water, and the water will take it back, and the bacterial mats will feed on it, and the rotifers will feed on the bacterial mats, and the small crustaceans will feed on the rotifers, and the mosquito larvae will feed on the small crustaceans, and a dragonfly nymph — perhaps one of my own offspring, perhaps a stranger — will feed on the mosquito larvae, and the cycle will close, and I will be, in the fullness of geological time, a fig, which will be eaten by a bird, which will be eaten by a possum, which will become, in the fullness of geological time, a fig.

But that is not yet. That is the inheritor's business. That is the long argument the place is having with itself, the slow negotiation between carbon and iron and water and light, conducted in the medium of ten thousand small lives, none of which understand what is happening.

I am one of the small lives. I do not understand what is happening. I do not need to understand. I am in the water. The water is warm. The water is enough. The drip goes on, and the drip goes on, and I wait, in the small dark place, in the small warm place, in the small patient place, for the day I climb out, and the day after that when I am no longer me, and the day after that when I am nothing, and the nothing is enough, the way the water is enough, the way the waiting is enough, the way the place is enough, until it is not, and then it is something else, and then something else again, and the drip goes on, and the drip goes on.
