Where would I find myself in this world? Who would I be?

I think I would be a listener.

Not a master of the listening — not one of the old ones who can stand at the working face with their ear tilted toward a seam and tell the crew which way the stone wants to go. I am not yet that. But I have been here long enough that my shoulders have learned to drop forward an inch when I walk through a doorway, and I have stopped swinging my arms in the corridors, and I speak, now, at the volume that carries through one wall and no further. The chisel-kiss below my thumb is pale, but it is there. The stone dust has worked itself into the whorls of my fingertips in a way that soap does not entirely remove, and I have come to like the faint mineral smell of my own hands at the end of a shift.

I am a carver. Mid-period work, mostly — the smoothing kind, the finishing kind, the work that goes into the chambers that someone else opened. I do not choose the seams. I am given the chambers when they are rough and I make them fit for living in, which means I round the corners where a child might fall, I cut the bench-seats to the angle a tired back actually wants, I work the ceiling of a sleeping alcove until the lowest part is just high enough that a tall person can sit up without thinking about it. My work is not the dramatic work. The dramatic work happens at the face, where the city is growing. My work is the work that makes the growth livable.

I live in a chamber I did not carve, two tiers down from the nearest shaft, in the residential weave that has accreted around the bright circle. My alcove opens onto a kitchen that belongs to someone else; in the morning I hear her chopping through the wall and the sound is so familiar it has become a kind of clock. The water ticks in the corridor. The cistern beneath my floor is one of the slow ones, and I have learned the sound it makes when it is full — a particular long stillness in the drip — and I have learned to take my water then.

I sleep in the warmest hours of the surface day, when the shafts are bright and the air in the deep chambers is most still. I wake in the long blue dusk of the shafts and the lamps are being lit and the city is becoming its constellation, and I rise and go to my work without transition, because there is no transition here between sleeping and being in the city. The two are interleaved. I have eaten bread torn from a loaf with one hand while the other hand rested on a chisel I had just set down. I have done this in the company of three other people and not remarked on it.

My name is the name of my chamber, which is the name of the stone in my chamber, which is the name of a seam in the limestone that runs through the city in a long pale stripe. There are perhaps a dozen of us in the city who share the name, and we are the people whose work has most often brought us into the same stripe of stone, and we nod to each other in the corridors in a way that is slightly more than a nod.

I am not a religious person, in any way that someone from above would use the word. But there is a Below word for the spirit of a place, and I have come to feel it the way one feels the coolness of the stone in a chamber one has lived in for years. The place attends to me, and I attend to it. The attending is mostly silence. When I put my ear to a wall I am working, I am listening, and what I hear is mostly nothing, and the nothing is information. The stone is telling me it is settled. The stone is telling me to keep going.

I am forty-three, by the count above, which makes me of an age where the listening begins to be worth something. The skin across the back of my hands is a little scarred in the diagnostic patterns. My hair, which was brown when I came down, is going grey in the striated way that the older workers go grey — a kind of banding that matches the limestone, white interrupted by darker, white interrupted again. I look, in the lamplight, like the wall I am leaning against.

I came down the crevasse when I was nineteen. I do not remember, now, what I thought the city would be. I remember the chisel-marks being pale and the dust smelling of mineral and chalk, and I remember the city being there all at once, pressed against me, and I remember ducking less than I expected to duck, and I remember the sound of someone’s hammer coming to me through stone as if the stone were a membrane and the hammer were a heart on the other side. I remember thinking that I would stay a year. I have been here for twenty-four years and I have smoothed perhaps thirty chambers and I have begun one of my own and I am two-thirds of the way through it and I do not expect to be the one who finishes it, but the seam between my work and the next person’s work will be invisible in the wall, and that is enough.

I have a child. She is seven and she has been given her first chisel, which is a small one, and her first lesson has been the listening, and she is good at it in a way I was not good at it at her age — she stands at the wall and is quiet and waits, and the quietness comes to her naturally, as if she has always known that the stone speaks and that one must be still to hear it. She plays in the corridors with the other children, and their games are constrained by the architecture in the way children’s games are constrained everywhere, and she has carved a small niche that is hers, in the wall of our chamber, and she checks it every morning to see if it is still there.

I will die in this city, or I will be carried up the shaft in a bundle and laid out on the hillside, which is the same thing in the end. The stones on the hillside are a kind of continuation of the city. My name will be in the open-air gallery above, and below it will be the chamber I smoothed, and below that the stone will continue in its seams for as long as stone continues, which is longer than any of us.

I am a listener. I am a carver. I am a Below person. The world above does not know I am here, and I do not entirely care. It is enough.
