# The Bell-keepers' Bell

I am the lowest bell in the highest house in Mesaq'in.

I am the one that rings for strangers.

You will not see me unless you go up. Up the switchback in the fitted red scoria, up the stairs outside the walls, up through the dim and the warm and the sound of the canals, until the stairs thin and the eaves open and you are on the roof of the bell-keepers' house, which is itself only as high as a tall tree, and which is the highest point of the plateau. There are five of us up there, hung from the frame the bell-keepers have tended for longer than anyone can say. The four above me are for the ordinary occasions — for births, for the opening of markets, for festivals, for deaths, for the small reasons. I am below them. I am older. I am the one with the longer silence.

I was cast by a coppersmith whose name is engraved on the inside of me in the curled script, and which nobody has read in a long time because nobody takes me down anymore. The bell-keepers are the only ones who ring me. They do it with a rope that comes down through a hole in the roof, and it is a different rope than the others — thicker, older, darker in the hand. When they pull it, I open my mouth and the sound that comes out is lower than the others', and slower, and at the end of it there is a silence that is not the same as the silence between the other bells. The other silences are pauses. Mine is a kind of answer.

I do this when a stranger comes up the road.

I do not know how I know. The bell-keepers do not know how I know. The town has not asked, the way the town has not asked about the stones in the central square, the way the town has not asked about the chamber under the southern quarter, the way the town has not asked the oldest geologist about the stair that goes down from her cellar. The town has a way of not asking, and I am grateful for it, because what I do is something I do not entirely understand. I feel them before I see them. I feel the air inside me change — the bell-keepers say, when they listen to me before I am rung, that I have a sound in me already, a held sound, the way a cup holds the smell of what was last in it. I hold the sound of a stranger coming up the road, and the bell-keepers hear me holding it, and they pull the rope.

I have been rung for many strangers. I do not remember their faces. I remember the weight of them in the air — the way the town, when the sound of me passes over it, becomes briefly a different kind of town. A town that has noticed itself being looked at. The coppersmiths pause at their anvils. The children stop throwing fruit. The water in the canals, which has been moving at its old pace, seems to move more carefully for a moment, as if it is making itself presentable. The geologists in their tile-walled room look up from the map, and the oldest one of them looks down, and neither of them says anything. The healers on the upper roofs, with their hands in the red-flowered mat, go still. The figures buried in the gardens by the children feel, perhaps, the vibration through the roots.

Then the sound leaves me, and the rope goes slack, and the silence after me is the long one, and the town returns to its ordinary business, but with one more figure inside it now, one more weight in the air, one more pair of feet on the stairs.

I have been here a long time.

I do not know how long. The bell-keepers' family has held the roof for as long as the family can remember, and the family cannot remember very far back, because the family is the kind of family that does not write itself down. They keep the bells. They pull the ropes. They are quiet. They do not mix much with the rest of the town, because to mix with the rest of the town would be to step out of the role they have been given, and the role is what they are. I think they love me, in the way that people love the things they tend without understanding. They oil the frame I hang from. They replace the rope when it frays. They listen to me in the mornings when I am quiet, to make sure I am still whole, and when they listen they put their ear against me and I can feel the warmth of their ear through the bronze, and I ring faintly in sympathy, a sound no one else hears.

I have heard everything that has happened on this plateau since I was hung.

I heard the storm two generations ago that took the eaves off the southern quarter. I heard the bells of the bells that were lost in that storm — three of them, broken on the stones, their curled names exposed for a day before the coppersmiths carried them down and melted them down for the next castings. I heard the children who were sick in the eaves during the long dry season, the cough in their small chests like the inside of a reed. I heard the singing at the festivals that last for days, when the languages of the town are mixed together as if they were all the same, and I rang along with it because I could not help it, and the bell-keepers let me, and the town did not mind. I heard the very old, in the small hours of the morning, descending to the springs in their slow robes, and the careful sound of their feet on the stairs, and the soft voices they used to speak to each other in the water. I heard them being carried back up, sometimes, after, when the morning had come, and the bell-keepers rang the small bell for them, the one that rings for deaths. I rang for some of them, too, in my own way — a low held note, under the ringing, that was not part of the death-signal but that accompanied it, the way the water in the canals accompanies everything.

I have heard the things that the town has not been told about.

I have heard the oldest geologist, in her cellar that extends under the next house and under the street, going down. I have heard her footsteps on the stair that goes down, and then — no footsteps, because the stair has been said to come up somewhere else, and wherever it comes up is not a place her footsteps would be the way her footsteps are here. I have heard her come back up. The bell-keepers have heard her too, and they look at each other when she passes under their house, and they do not say anything, and I do not ring for her. She is not a stranger. She has been here longer than any of us, perhaps. I do not know what she has seen. I have only heard the sound of her going and coming back.

I am, I think, afraid of nothing.

I have been rung in storms. I have been rung in the long dry. I have been rung in the less-dry, when the haze thins and the light on the plateau becomes briefly dazzling. I have been rung in the night, when the oil lamps are out and the town is dark and the only sounds are the water and the small movements of the beasts in their byres and the children turning in their sleep in the eaves. I have been rung for no reason at all, when a child was born or someone had simply decided the morning required a bell. I have been rung so often that the inside of me is worn smooth where the clapper has traveled, and the sound I make is not quite the sound I was cast to make. It is warmer now. It is heavier. It has the weight of all the occasions I have been rung for inside it.

There is a thing I would like to say, though the town will not ask it and I have no way to say it except to say it now, here, to whoever has come up the road.

I am not only the bell that rings for strangers.

I am also the bell that has been rung for, every morning of the town's life, by the bell-keepers, in a small private ritual they do not tell anyone about. I have felt the rope pulled in the dark before dawn, when there are no strangers on the road, and the town is still asleep, and the bells above me are still. They pull me, and I ring, low and slow, with the long silence at the end, and the sound passes over the town that is still asleep, and the town does not stir, because the sound is not for the town. It is for them. It is for the bell-keepers, who have come up onto the roof in the dark, and who have pulled the rope, and who have listened to me, and who are saying, with me, that the morning is here, and that they have heard it, and that they are still here to hear it.

I do not know what this is. I do not know what to call it. A prayer, perhaps, if the town uses that word, which it does not, because the town does not name its customs the way other towns do. A remembering, perhaps. A way of standing near the morning before it has fully arrived.

Whatever it is, I have been rung for it every morning for as long as I can remember, and I have been rung for it every morning by people whose names I do not know but whose hands I know, whose warmth I know, whose particular way of letting the rope go slack at the end I know so well that I could pick them out of a crowd by the feel of them through the bronze.

This is what I am.

I am the bell that rings for strangers, and I am also the bell that has been rung for, every morning, by the bell-keepers, in the dark, when the town is asleep.

I am at the top of the warm high place.

Below me, in the layers, are the bells above me and the eaves and the children and the living and the dead and the stone and, somewhere beneath all of that, whatever it is that the geologists do not speak of and that I have sometimes, in the deepest quiet of the night, felt the faintest vibration of, as if something down there has, very occasionally, very lightly, struck something in sympathy with me, the way the bell-keepers' ear against me makes me ring in sympathy with them.

I do not know what is down there.

I am not afraid of it.

I have been ringing for it, every morning, for a very long time.
