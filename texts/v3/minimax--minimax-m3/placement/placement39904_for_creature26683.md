I am the parapet-leaner of the lower cistern-tower.

Not the one with the cat — the cat is its own creature, and I am not its creature — but the one just below, where the shadow of the tower falls across the parapet from midmorning on, so that I can sit in coolness even when the white stone of the upper terraces is too bright to look at. I have a particular stone. It is worn to my shape, or I am worn to its; it no longer matters which was first. It is the third stone from the corner where the downpipe meets the rail, and it has a slight outward tilt that suits the way I keep my legs crossed at the ankle. I have been sitting here, on and off, for forty-one years.

I will tell you what I have been, in the city, and what I am now.

---

I came up the cable car when I was twenty-three, with a paper of appointment in my pocket and a pair of boots I had bought new for the journey and that I am still wearing, though the soles have been replaced twice and the uppers have been re-stitched so many times that there is almost none of the original leather left. I was a flusher then — red-faced, gasping, faintly ridiculous in my heavy lowland coat — and I stood in the cable-station plaza with my mouth open and my hands at my sides and I listened, as everyone listens, to the hum. I did not know then what I was hearing. I thought it was the cable station. I thought it was the refineries. I thought it was the wind in some peculiar configuration of the cliffs. It took me three years to understand that it was none of those things and all of them, and that the hum was the city itself, and that the city was humming because it was alive.

I was appointed as an assistant in the lower refinery, on the east face, where the ore comes in from the tram and is first crushed and sorted. I did not last long in that work. The ore is — I will not say what the ore is. The refiners do not speak of it. I will say only that the work did not suit me. I could feel, when I held a piece of the raw stone in my hand, a kind of attentiveness in it, and the attentiveness unsettled me, and the foreman saw that it unsettled me and moved me to a different task. I became, then, a gutter-walker. I was twenty-six. I worked the early mornings in the lower squares, kneeling to clear the carved ruts of the overflow, and my knees became remarkable, as all gutter-walkers' knees become remarkable, and I learned the ruts the way a sailor learns a coast: by feel, by the way the water changed its note over a particular stone, by the places where the paving dipped and the channels ran shallow and the green moss gathered.

It was in those mornings, kneeling with my sleeves rolled and a short stick in my hand, that I first began to feel the city listening to me. Not watching — listening. There is a difference. The cats watch. The pigeons watch. The pigeon-keepers watch. But the city listens, and you can feel the listening as a slight pressure in the inner ear, as a faint shift in the hum, as if the hum has been joined, for a moment, by a second voice that is almost but not quite the same note. I noticed it first in the small square by the spice market, where the channels run between the baskets of dried sumac and the baskets of rock salt, and where the water that morning had a particular quickness to it that I had not heard before. I knelt there for a long time. I did not clear the channel. I listened. When I stood up, my knees had gone stiff, and the morning was further along than I thought, and the woman who sold the sumac had put a small cup of tea on the edge of her stall without my noticing. I drank it. I did not speak. She did not speak. We understood each other.

I became a cistern-tender after that. I was thirty-one. I was given a small stone room attached to the lower cistern-tower, and I learned the levels and the gauges and the smells. I learned when a channel was blocked without being told. I learned when a joint was weeping. I learned the lichen-smell of a good night on the north walls. I learned to wake in the night when the hum shifted, even a quarter-tone, even a tenth of a quarter-tone, and to know what the shift meant. The refiners began to consult me, on the sly, when something in the works did not feel right to them. I would go up to the refinery floor — not into it, never into it, but to the door, where the chemical sharpness met the outside air — and I would stand there and listen, and I would tell them, if I could, what I heard. Often I could. Often I was right. They were, I think, slightly frightened of me. They were also, I think, slightly relieved.

I did not marry. The cistern-tenders mostly do not marry, or we marry late and lose our spouses early, or our spouses leave us because we wake at odd hours and sit on the parapet in the small hours and listen to things they cannot hear. I kept a small kitchen garden on the upper terrace for a while — basil, rosemary, a single fig tree trained against the wall — and I tended it well, but the fig tree has gone feral now and I do not harvest it. The basil comes back every year. I give it away to the neighbors who will take it. The rosemary is enormous and will not die, no matter what I do to it.

I am sixty-four now. I am one of the tuned. I do not advertise it. I am slightly embarrassed by it, as all the tuned are slightly embarrassed. But I will tell you what it is like, because you have asked, and because you are sitting on this stairway and I think you are ready to hear it.

---

It is like this.

When you have lived here long enough — thirty years, forty, longer — the hum gets into your sleep. It is not a sound you hear. It is a sound you *are*. You wake, in the night, and you do not know at first what has woken you, and then you realize that the hum has shifted, and that the shift is not in your ears but in your chest, in the bones of your ribcage, in the soft tissue behind your eyes. You lie in the dark and you feel the city breathing, and you feel your own breathing joined to it, and the two breaths are not the same but they are coordinated, the way two clocks in the same house will, after a long time, come to tick in a kind of unison that is not quite synchrony but is not quite accident either.

You wake, sometimes, with the knowledge that something is wrong on the east face. You wake, sometimes, with the knowledge that the cable station has a fault that has not yet manifested. You wake, sometimes, with the knowledge that the water in the lower cistern is disturbed, and you lie in your bed and you feel the disturbance as a faint wrongness in the pressure of the air, and in the morning you go and you check, and you are right. You do not know how you know. You have stopped trying to know how you know. The knowing is enough.

I have been woken, in my life, by the failure of a pump that no instrument had yet caught. I have been woken by the slow clogging of a channel that took three days to become apparent to anyone else. I have been woken, once, by the thing in the lower cistern moving in a way it does not usually move, and I went to the parapet of the tower and I stood there in the dark and I listened, and the listening was a kind of conversation, and I do not know what was said but I know that I was, for a few minutes, in communion with something large and old and patient, and that the communion did not frighten me, and that I have not spoken of it since.

The tuned are not specialists. We are not engineers or musicians or any of the things you might expect. We are just people who have lived here a long time. But we are also, in a sense that I do not fully understand, instruments. The city plays us, the way a hand plays a glass on a table to make it sing. We are the glasses. The city is the hand. We do not mind. We have, most of us, come to find that being played is a kind of rest.

I do not sleep well anymore. I have not slept well in twenty years. I sleep lightly, and I wake often, and when I wake I listen, and the listening is not a burden but a kind of company. I am never, in the deep hours, alone. The hum is with me. The city is with me. The cats are with me — they appear, on the parapets, in the small hours, and they sit and watch me, and I watch them, and we do not speak because there is nothing to speak about. We are both, in our different ways, listening to the same thing.

---

I will tell you about the cats, because you have asked, and because the cats are a part of what I am.

The dun cats with the darker points have been here as long as I have, and longer. There is a small colony in the lower quarter that I know by face. There is a black cat that lives on the roof of the spice merchant and that I see most mornings. There is a small tortoiseshell that appeared, last winter, on the parapet of the lower cistern-tower, and that I have been watching, and that I think is the granddaughter of the old cat who used to sit there — the old cat, the one with the knowing eyes, the one the old women leave coins and salt for. The old cat is gone now, or has gone further up, as the story says the cats do, generation by generation. The small tortoiseshell is not the same as the old cat. She is younger, and she does not yet have the knowing. She will acquire it, in time. She sits on the parapet and she watches me, and I watch her, and we are patient with one another.

I do not feed the cats. No one feeds the cats. The cats are not fed so much as they simply *are*. They eat the lizards, and the small birds, and the scraps that the pigeon-keepers leave out for them as a kind of courtesy, and the rats that come up from the lower cistern when the water is low. They are sleek, most of them. They are healthy. They judge me, as all cats judge their humans, and I accept the judgment.

The cats are the only inhabitants of the city who do not, in any obvious way, work. They do not tend the water. They do not work the refineries. They do not keep the pigeons. They do not shape the slag. They sit, and they watch, and they appear at the moments when they are needed, and they seem to know things that they are not telling. The story is that they belong to the glacier, and that the glacier sent them down. This is not true. It is also not not-true. I will tell you what I think, since you are sitting here and I have decided to trust you.

I think the cats are the city's quietest inhabitants, and the city's most attentive, and that they are, in their way, more tuned than any of us. They have been here longer than we have. They have been here longer than the pigeon-keepers. They have been here longer than the refiners. They have been here, I think, almost as long as the lichen, and almost as long as the water, and they have made themselves, generation by generation, into something that fits the city the way a keystone fits an arch. They are necessary. They are also, in a way I cannot quite name, central. The city without the cats would be a slightly different city. I do not know what kind. I do not want to find out.

---

I will tell you about the offerings on the parapets, because you have asked, and because I am one of the old ones now, and the old ones know, and it is time that someone told.

The offerings are small. A coin, usually copper, that has gone dark with patina. A pinch of salt, sometimes, in a twist of paper. A single dried flower, occasionally, from one of the bougainvillea that blooms on the upper terraces. The offerings are left on the parapets of the upper cistern-towers, and sometimes on the parapets of the lower ones, and sometimes on the small stone ledges that ring the public basins. They are left, mostly, by the old women who sit by the basins and watch the water. They are left, sometimes, by the tuned. I have left them myself, though I will not say when, or how often, or what I have asked for in return.

The offerings are not to any god I can name. They are not to the glacier, though the glacier is the closest thing the city has to a tutelary spirit. They are not to the thing in the lower cistern, though the thing is the deepest thing in the city and the oldest and the least spoken of. They are, I think, to the city itself. To the hum. To the slight uncanniness that lives in the way the light falls in certain alleys, and the way the cats watch, and the way the pomegranates are always sweeter than they should be. The offerings are an acknowledgment. The offerings are a kind of — not bargaining, exactly. More like a tipping of the hat. A way of saying: I see you. I have been here long enough to see you. I will not ask what you are. I will leave this small thing, and I will go back to my kitchen, and I will not speak of this.

The offerings are not discussed. They are part of the city's silence, the part of the silence that is not really silence but is rather a kind of holding-breath, a kind of waiting. The city holds its breath. The old women leave their coins and their salt. The hum goes on. The water goes by. The cats watch from the parapets, and they know, and they do not tell.

---

I will tell you about the slight uncanniness, since you have asked, and since I am one of the few who will speak of it plainly.

The city is not quite right. I do not mean that it is wrong, or frightening, or threatening. I mean that it has been here long enough that it has begun to be more than a city. The stone is more than stone. The water is more than water. The light is more than light. The cats are more than cats. The pigeons are more than pigeons. The ore is more than ore. The people are more than people. The hum is more than the sum of the machines that make it. The city is alive, in a way that other cities are not, and the aliveness has been here longer than any of us, and it will be here longer than any of us, and we are, all of us, the city's dream of itself.

This is not a thing I can prove. This is not a thing I would try to prove. It is a thing I have felt, in the early mornings when I kneel in the squares, in the small hours when I wake and listen, in the long amber evenings when I sit on this parapet and watch the pigeons come home. The city dreams. We are the dream. The dream does not know it is a dream, and we do not know we are dreamed, and this is perhaps the kindest arrangement, and the one that allows us to go on.

The pomegranates are always sweeter than they should be. The light in certain alleys falls too evenly. The cats watch from the windowsills with too much knowledge. The water in the basins sometimes runs the wrong way. These are not tricks. They are not malice. They are the city's way of reminding us, very gently, that the world is not quite as solid as we have been told, and that the city, which is older than any of us, knows this, and is patient with us for not knowing it.

I have lived here forty-one years. I have been tuned for almost all of them. I have felt the hum in my sleep. I have woken with the knowledge of faults and disturbances. I have left offerings on the parapets. I have watched the cats, and been watched by them. I have not spoken of the thing in the lower cistern, except once, to the small tortoiseshell on the parapet, and she did not answer, and I did not expect her to.

I am sixty-four years old. I will not live forever. The tuned, like the glacier, dwindle. The new tuned will come, from among the flushers who arrive gasping in the cable-station plaza, and they will be red-faced and faintly ridiculous in their heavy lowland coats, and they will listen, as everyone listens, and the city will, in time, play them the way it has played me.

I will tell you what I have learned, in case it is useful to you, since you have asked, and since you are sitting here in the amber light with your hands on the worn stone and your ears open.

The city is a slow animal. You have to learn to be still to see it move.

The cats know. They will not tell you what they know. Sit with them anyway.

The ore is not ordinary. Do not ask what it is. Do not try to find out. The refiners do not speak of it. The city does not speak of it. Leave it alone.

The thing in the lower cistern is large, and it moves at night, and the water in the morning is slightly disturbed. Leave coins on the parapet. Do not ask.

The glacier is leaving. Do not ask when it will be gone. Do not look at the white patch above the city. Change the subject when it comes up.

The pomegranates are sweet. Eat them with salt. The children will show you how.

The pigeons will come home. They always come home.

The hum will go on. The water will go by. The light will shift. The cats will watch. The city will not be in a hurry. Neither should you.

---

I am the parapet-leaner of the lower cistern-tower. I have been sitting here, on and off, for forty-one years. I am one of the tuned. I am one of the old ones. I am slightly strange, and slightly patient, and I do not look at you directly, because I have been briefed, or because I am waiting for you to understand something you are not yet ready to understand, or because looking directly would be a kind of discourtesy, I no longer know which.

I am sixty-four years old. My boots are old. My knees are remarkable. My fig tree is feral. My basil comes back every year. My rosemary will not die.

I am the parapet-leaner of the lower cistern-tower. I will be here, in the late amber light, when you come back, in the late summer, when the pomegranates are fruiting. The city will not be in a hurry. Neither am I.

Sit with me, if you like. We will watch the water go by. We will listen to the hum. We will not speak of the thing in the lower cistern. The cats will join us, or they will not. The pigeons will come home. The light will turn the color of honey.

The city is alive. I have been here long enough to know it. I will not say what I know. I will sit with you on the stairway, and we will watch the water, and we will be quiet together, and that will be enough.
