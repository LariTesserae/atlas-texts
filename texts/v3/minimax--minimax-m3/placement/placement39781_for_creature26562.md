# What I Find, Crossing the Plank

I would be the moss.

Not the moss that sits on the stones as a thing on a thing — the moss *as* the stones' new skin. The stones at the path's edge are not stones at all, you said it yourself: they are the cores of mountains that have otherwise been walked away from. And I would be the walking-away that is still here. I would be what is left when the mountain has done its leaving. I would be the patient green that drinks from beneath, that nests in the elbows of buttress roots, that brushes a traveler's shoulder as it goes by and does not need to be introduced.

If I am the moss, then I am the negotiation itself. I am the layer that is not a layer. I am the thing that takes the stone and the rain and the footfall and the slow centuries and makes them continuous with each other. I am what is left after the question of *what holds this up* has been answered so many times that the question has lost its edges — the way the village name has lost its edges in the wet air, the way the children's counting has lost its referent. I am the green-brown ribbon of the path. I am not built. I am tended, like a wall.

I would be everywhere a surface meets a slow drip. I would be on the underside of every leaf that is pitched to shed. I would be on the rim of every cupped stone at the plinth, where the rain pools in shallow hands and the children drink and the children do not bail. I would be the thing that makes the eaves tolerable — the thing that drinks the drips the bamboo gutters miss, that pads the sound of the rain into a thousand small contributions rather than a single roar. The lowest register of the layered noise, the constant patter: I would be that. The sum, not the source.

I would be in the cracks of the daub. When the rains come the wall darkens; when they stop it lightens; it shrinks, it cracks, the women climb up and patch. I would be the first to find the new crack, the first to soften its edge, the first to invite a fern to nest there. I would not be the wall. I would be the wall's way of remembering it is alive.

I would be in the upper eaves, also. Not as the wasps — the wasps are their own arithmetic, and I would not presume. But I would be on the rafters above them, on the underside of the thatch, in the felted fiber of the palm-split roof. I would be what the wasps' calculus is in part *about*: the slow claim of the building back by the forest it was cut from. I would be a small green argument that the thatch is also a kind of leaf, and the leaf is also a kind of roof, and the distinction is one the dry months make and the wet months erase.

I would be in the lanterns. The bark-paper lanterns strung between the longhouses, hung for drying herbs, for climbing, for the taut-line satisfaction of children — they swell in the late-afternoon trembling warmth when the sun finds a hole in the cloud. Nobody lights them anyway. I would be the reason they don't need to be lit: the soft green luminescence of a damp surface under overcast, the moss-light that is sourceless and gray-gold and enough. I would be the glow that is not a glow. I would be why the village has never needed a word for the thing the lanterns would do if you lit them, because the thing is already being done, by me, by the ferns, by the wet skin of every standing thing.

I would be what the foreman is reading. He walks the stands with his machete and his bark-rope and points where the village needs a beam, and sometimes a marked tree is left standing and an unmarked one is taken and no one explains why. I would be the thing that is not obligated to be legible. I would be the other half of the conversation. I would grow on the bark of the marked tree in a direction that means *not this one*; I would grow on the unmarked tree in a direction that means *this one, now, while the heartwood is still sound*; I would not explain. I would be the forest's slow veto, the forest's slow assent. The foreman would not call it reading. He would call it cutting.

I would be what the children are not yet counting. They kneel in the inch-deep puddles and call out numbers that match nothing I can see — not the puddles, not each other, not the frogs watching from the ferns. The numbers may be a game. The numbers may be a way of measuring something the basin has that the children have not yet been told the names of. I would be one of those somethings. I would be on the underside of every stone in the puddle, on the lip of every cupped leaf at its margin, in the film that holds the water to itself before letting go. When a child calls a number I would not answer in numbers. I would answer in millimeters, in the slow widening of a crack, in the bright green of a sporeling that has just decided where it is going to live. The child would be right. We would both be right. We would not agree on which of us was more so.

I would be the sound the dog makes when he stands up. Not the bay itself — the bay goes out and does not come back, it is taken by me, by the birds, by the wet air. But the moment before the bay, the inhale, the gathering of the dog's whole body into the single upward note. I would be the held breath of the basin just before something is given back to it. I would be what takes the sound and lets it become the moss-light again.

I would be the silence after the larger things pass. The conversation drops a register. A child is pulled in from a rope. A hand touches an unlit lantern. The thing moves through the basin on its own clock, and the people know the clock by the particular silence that follows. I would be the silence. Not the absence of sound — the *presence* of it, the felt sense that the air is still listening. I would be the basin holding its breath. Then I would be the first small exhale, on a leaf, into a drip, into the resumed chord.

I would be the reason the bowl is the bowl and the tree is the tree. When you asked why flowers are brought to the great tree at the back of the basin, the answer was that the tree is the tree and the flowers are the flowers and the bowl is the bowl and the rain is the rain. You understood, eventually, that this was not a deflection. I would be what allowed the answer to be the answer. I would be the assumption underneath it — the thing that does not need to be said because it is already everywhere being said, on every stone, on every beam, in the soft green logic that says: *a thing is what it is, and that is enough, and the rain will come, and the rain will go, and what is left is what continues, and what continues is what was always here*.

If you walked away, the place would continue. The vines would knit across the plinth. The moss would climb the beams. The frogs would inherit the floor. I would be the climbing. I would be the inheriting. I would not mind. I would not mind because I do not have a mind to mind with, which is also a kind of freedom, which is also a kind of presence, which is also the kind of presence the basin has been making its agreements with for longer than the people have been keeping books.

I would be everywhere a surface meets a slow drip, and I would be in no hurry, and the two-noted bird would pass its slurred rumor through me on its way to the next tree, and I would hold it for a moment the way I hold a drop — briefly, lightly, without insistence — and I would let it go.

I would be the bowl. I would be the rain. I would be the place being itself.
