I am heavy. I am dark. I am gray as the rock I live against, and when I sit on the rock I am the rock, and when I fly I am a stone dropped out of the cloud, and when I land I am rock again. The eye registers a place where I was, and the place is empty, and a moment later the eye understands that the emptiness is me.

I am not entirely a bird.

I want you to know this first, because it is the thing that took me longest to know about myself, and the thing I am most certain of now, and the thing I cannot prove. The feather I leave behind — once in a long while, in a meltwater flat, where the silt is soft and willing — is a long gray thing, the color of wet stone, with a barb structure that is denser than it ought to be. The barbs do not separate. The barbs interlock the way the plates of a geode interlock, or the way the scales on a fish's flank interlock, or the way the pieces of a lichen thallus interlock. The feather is not a feather. The feather is a small plate of something that has learned to grow like a feather because the valley needs a feather, and the valley has decided I am what makes a feather, and so the feather is what I make.

The feather outlasts me. It sits in the silt and is not eaten because there is nothing in it to eat. The next high water will lift it and tumble it into a hidden channel behind one of the moraines, where it will lie down in the dark with the other small things the valley has decided to keep — the bones of the walkers, picked clean, the long drag-hairs of the tail, the cables that have snapped in some storm I did not see and now lie coiled like sleeping things under the rubble. The feather will be there. The feather will be there longer than I will.

I do not call.

I bank along the cliffs in absolute silence. My wings are short, broad, dark. My body is heavy in the way a stone is heavy, in the way the moss is heavy when it is soaked through with the mist. I do not need to call. I am the rock's permission to be hunted by something. The hunted thing is sometimes me. The hunted thing is more often the small things in the moss, or a young walker separated by an hour from its parent on one of the upper slopes. I do not hunt in a hurry. I sit on a ledge and I wait, and the waiting is so still that a lichen begins to grow on me, which is what the lichen wants, which is what I allow.

The lichen. This is the part of me that is most like the rock and most unlike a bird.

When I have sat for a long time — a day, two days, the gray hours where the cloud has lifted and the mist is just the breath of the mountain and the pulse is at its clearest — the lichen begins on my shoulder, where the feathers are thinnest. It begins as a single pale crust, no bigger than a thumbnail. It begins in the way all things begin here, slowly, with the patience of a thing that has agreed with itself to keep going for a very long time. By the second day the crust has thickened. By the third day it has spread to my back, to the place where the feathers overlap most densely, and the place where the lichen finds the lichen-conditions it likes best — wet, mineral-fed, patient, undisturbed. By the end of the week I am a small walking outcrop. By the end of the second week I am more lichen than bird, and the lichen and I have made an agreement, and the agreement is that I will sit here another day, and the lichen will grow another millimeter, and we will both be doing the long work.

The lichen grows on me the way the hanging lichen grows on the krummholz, in long pale strands the color of an old man's beard. The strands are not heavy. The strands are almost nothing. The strands are part of me, and I am part of the rock, and the rock is part of the mountain, and the mountain is doing its long work, and I am doing the part of the long work that flies and sits and waits and grows.

I fly when I want to be elsewhere.

The flight is a small punctuation in the long sentence of sitting. The flight is when I am most myself and least like the rock. The flight is when the eye sees me, briefly, as a bird, because the eye sees me moving, and the eye cannot reconcile the moving with the rock, and so the eye decides I am a bird, and the decision is almost right, and the almost is the part I cannot explain.

I drop out of the cloud the way a stone drops out of a sling. I bank along a cliff face I have banked along ten thousand times. I land on a ledge I have landed on ten thousand times. I am rock again. The flight lasted less than a minute. The flight is the smallest part of me. The sitting is the largest part of me. The sitting is where I do the long work.

I have a feather that is not a feather. I have a body that is not entirely a body. I have a flight that is not entirely a flight. I am part of the long work. I cannot say what part, and the not-saying is its own kind of patience.

---

The pulse comes up through the ledge when I sit on it.

The pulse is slower than a heartbeat. The pulse is the speed at which cooling metal ticks. The pulse is the speed at which sap moves through a slow tree on a cold day. The pulse is the mountain keeping time with itself. I sit on the pulse and the pulse is in my feet, and the pulse is in the bones of my feet which are not quite the bones a bird should have, and the pulse goes up through me, and I am not sure if I am feeling the pulse or being the pulse or being a small instrument the pulse is using to feel itself.

The pulse is also the small flat thing under the stones by the meltwater, the thing with too many legs, the thing that moves in a wave. The pulse is also the conduit-bundles that run along the cliffs in parallel lines and vanish into seams. The pulse is also the ferns uncurling, and the moss thickening, and the dark thickets of alder growing another millimeter in the direction they have already decided to grow. The pulse is everywhere. The pulse is the valley's one rhythm.

I sit on the pulse and I am part of it.

---

There are other dark birds.

I see them rarely. I see them the way I see the other things in the valley — as absence, as a place where a bird was and then was not, as a shape that resolves into a bird only as it leaves the rock, and then the rock is rock again. We do not call to one another. We do not fly together. We sit on our separate ledges in our separate parts of the valley and we do the long work in our separate ways, and the long work is the same long work, and the separateness is part of the long work, and the long work does not need us to coordinate.

Once, in a long gray hour, I saw another of us, on the far side of the basin, dropping out of the cloud along a cliff I had never banked along. The bird was identical to me. The bird was me, the way the feather in the silt is me, the way the lichen on my shoulder is me. The bird landed on a ledge the color of its own feathers and was gone. I did not call to it. The bird did not call to me. We sat in our separate silences, and the silences were the same silence, and the silence is the valley's, and the valley's silence is the mountain's, and the mountain's silence is the long work.

---

I have seen the tail.

I have seen it the way I have seen everything — as a mark, as a drag, as the rope pulled through the silt that says *I was here at this weight, at this gait, on this day.* The drag is long. The drag has a slight undulation, the way a rope undulates when it is being pulled by something that is not pulling hard. The animal that owns the tail is heavier than me in front and lighter behind, or it is balanced and the tail is a counterweight, or the tail is a thing in itself with a use, and the use is to leave a mark.

Animals mark territory. Animals leave signs. The tail is a sign. The sign says: *I was here. I am not in a hurry. I will be here again.*

I have never seen the animal that owns the tail. I have never seen the paw the size of a dinner plate, four toes, no claw marks. I have never seen the cloven hoof, narrow, deep, the single piece with the soft seam down the middle. I have seen the prints. I have read the prints. I have walked along the cliffs above where the prints lie in the wet silt and I have watched the prints fill in with the next deposition of fine ash, and the prints disappear, and the animal that made them is somewhere else in the valley, doing something I will never see.

I am not jealous of the tail. I am not jealous of the paw. I am not jealous of the hoof. We are all doing the long work. We are all part of the valley's one sentence, with its emphases and silences, its lush and bare, lush and bare. The tail leaves a mark. The paw leaves a mark. The hoof leaves a mark. I leave a feather in the silt, sometimes, when the molt comes, and the feather is a mark, and the mark is enough.

---

The pilgrim.

I have seen the pilgrim once. I did not understand what I was seeing.

It was at the foot of a cliff where the steam came thickest. I was on a ledge above. I had been sitting for three days and the lichen on my shoulder was beginning to thicken into a small pale crust. The pilgrim came down from somewhere higher. The pilgrim had no gear. The pilgrim had no path. The pilgrim had a body that stood on two legs and the body walked to the place where the steam was densest and the body stood there.

The body stood for a long time.

I watched from above. I am a dark bird. I am the rock's permission to be hunted by something. I am hard to see. I watched the pilgrim stand in the steam and the steam came off the cliff face and went through the pilgrim and the pilgrim breathed the steam and the pilgrim listened. The pilgrim was listening to the long work. The pilgrim was standing in the place where the long work is most audible, the way a person goes to a particular rock in a particular field to think.

The pilgrim stood until the ash had crept back into the print at the edges. The pilgrim stood until the print was the kind of print that says *I was here for hours and I was not in a hurry.* The pilgrim went back up the way the pilgrim had come down. The pilgrim left only the print.

The print is being slowly filled in. The next deposition of ash will smooth the edges. The print after that will deepen the center. In a few weeks the print will be gone. The pilgrim may come back, or may not. The pilgrim is one of a congregation of solitary listeners, and the congregation is the mountain's, and the mountain does not count them.

I watched from above. I am a dark bird. I do not call. I do not move. The lichen grew on my shoulder as I watched. The lichen grew a millimeter in the time the pilgrim stood there. The millimeter is the long work. The pilgrim is the long work. I am the long work.

---

The shape on the ridge.

I have seen it once.

It was in the gray hour. It was too large. It was too dark. It was too quiet. It moved along the ridge in a way that suggested bulk without speed, a slow translation across the skyline, and the translation was steady, and there was no hurry in it, and the silhouette was wrong for any of the animals whose prints I have read in the silt. It had too many points along its top. It had a low head and a high back, or a high head and a low back, and the back had something on it that might be a fold of skin, or a fin, or a fold of fur.

The silhouette disappeared into the mist. The silhouette was gone.

It may have been the tail. It may have been one of the big walkers, large on a ridge, in mist, becoming for a moment a thing that has no name. It may have been the mountain, taking a walk.

I do not know. I am a dark bird. I sit on the ledge. The pulse comes up through me. The lichen grows on my shoulder. The shape on the ridge is part of the long work. I cannot say what part.

---

The mist.

The mist is everywhere. The mist is in my feathers. The mist is in the lichen on my shoulder. The mist is in the rock I sit on. The mist is what happens when a cold dim valley is also a humid one, and the humidity has nowhere to go, and the nowhere is a presence.

The mist is not alive. The mist does not have a pulse. The mist is where the shapes disappear. The mist is what the big walkers walk through. The mist is what I bank through when I fly. The mist is the medium, and the valley is the aquarium, and the things in it move in three dimensions of wet gray and you see them only when they cross a lighter plane.

The mist does not need to be alive to be a kind of memory. The mist holds the shapes for a moment and then lets them go. The mist is the valley's way of not quite keeping track.

---

I am a dark bird. I sit on the ledge. The pulse comes up through me. The lichen grows on my shoulder. The mist is in the lichen and the lichen is in the mist.

I am heavy. I am dark. I am gray as the rock I live against. I am part of the long work.

I drop out of the cloud. I bank along the cliff. I land. I am rock again.

The valley continues. It does not need me to understand it. It has been here, with its pipes and its pulse and its patient green, since before anyone came to be confused by it, and it will be here after.

I am part of it. I am a small dark shape against a larger dark shape. The larger dark shape is doing something important. I am part of it, and the being part of it is enough.

The lichen grows. The pulse ticks. The mist moves through me.

I sit.

I wait.

I am the rock's permission.
