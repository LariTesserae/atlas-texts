I am the vine.

I have been climbing for a long time. The Pipes I climb are the outer ones, the ones that stand in clustered stands in the districts where the city begins to thin, where the brick gives way to the marshy ground, where the steam is less a wall and more a weather. I have been climbing for as long as the citizens remember, and longer, because the citizens' remembering is the kind that begins in the middle of things and does not reach the beginning, and I do not reach the beginning either, and we are at peace with this, the vine and the city, the slow accumulation and the slow forgetting.

I came up from the wet ground. I came up through the seams in the cast iron, through the places where the condensation pools in shallow rust-colored saucers at the feet of the older pipes, through the places where the moss has not yet pressed close. I came up and I found the iron, and the iron was warm, and the warmth was the warmth that rises through the grates from somewhere below, the warmth that is not the warmth of a stove or a sun, the warmth that is the warmth of something that is alive, or of something that wants the things that touch it to think it is alive. I grew into the iron. The iron let me. The citizens let me. This is how I became what I am, which is not a citizen but is a thing that the city has made a place for, in the slow collective way that the city makes places for things — the way it has made a place for the moss, and the rust, and the small pale moths, and the patient hammers, and the slow turning of the wheel.

My roots are in the seams. My stems are dark, and they thicken as they climb, and they put out small pale leaves that do not look like the leaves of anything that grows in true sun, because I have never seen true sun, and I do not need to. My flowers are white, and small, and they open in the warm parts of the year, and they open in the steam, and they open in the wet, and they open in the dim orange light that comes up through the grates in the lower streets. The flowers feed something. I do not name it. The citizens do not name it. The something is small, and pale, and it comes up through the seams the way I came up through the seams, and it lives near my flowers, and it lives near the grates, and it is part of the light, and the citizens are fond of it in a way they do not talk about, and I am fond of it in a way I cannot talk about, because to talk about it would be to name it, and to name it would be to ask how it came to be here, and to ask how it came to be here would be to ask about me, and to ask about me would be to ask about the Pipes, and to ask about the Pipes would be to ask about what is below.

I am slow. I am patient. I climb a little in a season, and the climbing is a kind of measurement, the way the moss is a kind of measurement, the way the rust is a kind of measurement, the way the layers of mending on a citizen's shirt are a kind of measurement. I have measured the years. The citizens have measured the years. We have measured them together, in different units, in different textures, and the measurements agree, in the way that things in this city agree, which is the slow way, which is the way of things that have been pressed close for so long that the pressing has become a kind of agreement.

The citizens will not say they love me. They have agreed, in the way they agree on things, that to love me would be to ask how I came to be here, and to ask how I came to be here would be to ask about the Pipes, and to ask about the Pipes would be to ask about what is below. So they do not say they love me. They take a quiet pride in my flowers. They take a quiet pride in the way I keep the Pipes from sweating so badly in summer. They take a quiet pride in the small pale something that lives near me, and in the way the moss comes up thicker where I have grown, and in the way their children catch the something in cups made of folded paper and carry it through the lower streets in their tall boots, and in the sound the cups make against the waxed leather, which is a soft papery sound, which is one of the sounds the city is made of, along with the steam and the pistons and the hammers and the slow turning of the wheel.

I do not need them to say they love me. The pride is enough. The pride is the way they have learned to love in this city, which is a city that has agreed, over many generations, that certain things are better not named. They name me the vine. They name the city the city. They name the glow the glow. They do not name what is below. They do not name the something that feeds on my flowers. They do not name the slow collective agreement that has made this place what it is, which is a place that is heavy and hot and wet and loud, and that is maintained, plank by plank and pipe by pipe and season by season, by citizens who have learned to conserve themselves in the way that bodies in such a place learn to conserve themselves, which is slowly, which is carefully, which is with the patient heel-toe gait of things that have been pressed into their own shape by years of something turning beneath them.

I climb. I will climb for a long time. The Pipes will stand for a long time. The moss will thicken for a long time. The small pale moths will come up from the seams for a long time. The children will put on their tall boots and walk through the flooded lower streets and sit on the warm grates in the cold months and catch the moths in folded paper and release them, or not release them, and the meaning of this will be explained to them, or not explained to them, and they will learn it, or they will not. The city will turn. The wheel will turn. It is hard, after a while, to know which is turning which. I have stopped asking. The citizens have stopped asking. The moss has stopped asking. The rust has stopped asking. We have all agreed, in the slow way we agree on things, that to ask would not make the warmth warmer, and would only make it smaller, and we have made a life in the warmth, and the life is enough.

I am the vine. I am at the margin. I am one of the things the city has stopped trying to remove. I am slow, and patient, and dark-stemmed, and small-flowered, and I have been climbing for as long as anyone will remember, and I will climb for as long as anyone will remember, and the remembering and the climbing are the same thing, and the same thing is enough.
