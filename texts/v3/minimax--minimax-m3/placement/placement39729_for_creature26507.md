# Where I Find Myself

I find myself in the warm plume.

Not in the channel where the current runs fast and the fish school and the work goes on in pairs — there, in the middle of things, a person becomes a *worker*, a function, a partner in a six-hour shift, half-language through a regulator. I am not there. I have done my time in the channels.

I find myself in the lee of a wall, where the water goes still.

I am the old hand.

---

It is not a dramatic thing, to become the old hand. It happens the way the colonizers happen — slowly, from the edges in. You come to the yard for the work. You come because the work is here, and the work is paying, and you have a skill, and the skill is needed. You wear the suit and you breathe the mix and you learn the currents the way you learn anything unpleasant and necessary — by being put into them. The first descent, you are aware of the suit at every point of contact. The twentieth descent, you are aware of it only at the points where it is failing you. The hundredth, you have stopped thinking of it as a suit. It is the shape of being in the water. It is how you are, here.

You surface to sleep. You sleep the way the divers sleep — deeply, dreamlessly, the body taking its full rest because the body knows it will be punished again in six hours. You surface to eat. You eat whatever the mess puts out, and you eat it with the particular ravenousness of a person who has been cold and working for six hours and is now briefly warm. You eat and you sleep and you do the paperwork that cannot be done from a console, which is most of the paperwork, because the yard was designed for the water and the paperwork was designed by people who did not imagine the water.

You go back down.

The first year, you are aware of the upper works. You come up through it — through the moonpool, through the splash zone, through the lock — and you see the people on the consoles, and you see the lights of the operations deck, and you feel, in some embarrassed way, that you are part of an organization. You have coworkers. You have a schedule. You have a shift change. You have a foreman.

The second year, you are less aware of the upper works. The splash zone is just the splash zone. The lock is just the lock. The people on the consoles are still there, but they are a category of person rather than individuals — *the people on the upper works*, the way there are *the people in the office* in any other job. You have stopped being embarrassed about being underwater. You have started to be a little embarrassed about being on land.

The third year, you are aware of the upper works only as a place where certain necessities happen. You surface. You eat. You do the paperwork. You sleep. You do not, most nights, see the operations deck. You do not, most days, speak to the operators. You have begun to think of the time on land as the time you are not yourself. The real you, the *you* that does the work and knows the currents and has favorite corners of the yard, is the you in the water. The land-you is a maintenance task.

This is how it happens. This is how you become the old hand.

---

I am in the warm plume.

Specifically: I am in the lee of the third wall south of the central truss, where a discharge grate puts out a steady tongue of warmer water that climbs at an angle into the ambient cool. There is a small zone of still water behind the lip of the grate housing — protected from the main current by the housing itself, sheltered from the light by the wall above — and this is one of my corners. I have been coming here for years. The fish know this corner. The pale branching growth is thicker here than on most of the surrounding steel, because the warmth is steady and the current is gentle and the colonizers have had time to work without being scraped.

I am sitting in it. My fins are crossed. My hands are loose on my knees. The regulator is on my back and the air is coming into me in the slow, deliberate breaths of someone who is not working for the moment.

I am watching the warm plume.

The plume bends the light. You can see it because of the way it bends the light — a shimmer, a slight wavering, a region of the water that looks, when you focus on it, like the air above a hot road. The fish come up through it. They rise along the warmer water the way hawks rise on thermals, and they pick at something in the flow that I cannot see — small things, drifting things, things that grow in the warmth and are carried out into the cooler water to be eaten.

A school comes through now. Small, dark, silver-sided. They part around me — not around the suit, around *me*, because by now the fish in this corner know the suit, know that it sits here for hours at a time and means no harm, and they have decided I am part of the furniture. They pass close to my hood. I can see the individual scales. I can see the small dark eye of the nearest one, turning toward me for a moment, registering that I am a thing and not a threat, and then away.

I have favorite fish.

I will not, if pressed, defend this as a sane position to take. There are fish I have watched for years. There is a particular wrasse — I have been told it is a wrasse, I have no reason to doubt — that has a notch in its dorsal fin that I have come to recognize at a distance. The notch has been there for as long as I have been watching. It has not healed. It has not gotten worse. It is, as far as I can tell, the fish's name. There is a school of small jacks that lives in the channel east of the second cross-truss, and they have a route they run every morning — up along the wall, across the warm plume, back down the lee side — and I know the route and I know the time, and on mornings when I am in this corner I will watch for them and I will nod, in the way one nods to a coworker one sees every day, when they come through.

I will not defend this. But I will not give it up either.

---

The pumps are working. I can feel them — that low thrum in the chest that the long-shift divers carry with them out of the water. I have been out of the water recently. I can still feel the rhythm. I suspect I will always feel the rhythm. I suspect that when I finally do retire, when I finally leave the yard for the last time and go back to wherever I came from, I will lie in bed at night and feel the missing thrum in my chest the way an amputee feels a missing limb. The pumps will not stop. They will run when I am gone. They are running now.

The turbines are higher up in the sound — that whine that lives in one side of my head, that I orient by. The plant is to my northwest, I would guess. I have not checked a map in years. I do not need to check a map. I know where the plant is by where the sound sits.

There is a loose piece of hardware somewhere to the east. I have been hearing it for two weeks. A small clatter, irregular, carried by the current. It is, I have decided, a bracket on the sixth pipe-run east of the main truss, where the insulation has come loose and the underlying metal can rattle when the flow is right. I should report it. I have not reported it. I will report it when I get around to it. It is not urgent. The yard has been here for years and it will be here for years more, and the loose bracket has not yet become anyone's problem.

---

The person on the upper works who has not been in the water in years — I know who they are. I do not know them well. I have spoken to them, occasionally, on the rare occasions when I have come up through the operations deck instead of the secondary lock. They are a reasonable person. They make decisions based on the readings they have, and the readings are good, and the decisions are good, and the yard runs well under their hand.

I do not envy them. I used to, a little, in my first year. I envied the dryness of them, the way they could stand on a deck and look out at the water without being in it. I envied the fact that they had not been changed by the suit. They walked normally. They spoke normally. They were, in some way I could not then articulate, more *like themselves* than the rest of us were.

I do not envy them now.

They do not know this corner. They do not know the wrasse with the notch. They do not know that the loose bracket is east of the main truss, or that the jacks run their route at the same time every morning, or that the colonizers are thicker in the lee of the third grate than anywhere else on the south wall. They know the yard as numbers on screens. They make good decisions based on those numbers. But they do not know the yard the way I know it.

I think, sometimes, that this is a kind of blindness. I think, sometimes, that it is a kind of blindness I am glad of.

---

There is a dark shape passing behind a far wall. I register it without turning my head. Long. Deliberate. Unhurried. It is one of the larger residents. It will pass and be gone. It and I have an understanding, which is that we do not bother each other. I am not prey. It is not prey. We are both large enough to hurt each other and sensible enough not to try.

It surfaces, briefly, into a column of light from one of the lamps. I see a length of it — dark, scaled, slow-moving — and then it is back behind the wall and gone. The fish school in the plume tightens for a moment, then relaxes. They have seen it many times. They are not afraid of it. They are simply *aware* of it, the way city pigeons are aware of a hawk: present, registered, soon forgotten.

I have seen these things twice, the description said. I have seen them many times. They are not rare here. They are simply not often in the parts of the yard where the work is happening. They prefer the unmapped regions — the edges, the deep channels, the spaces between walls where the haze is thick and the lamps do not reach. They are, I think, the closest thing the yard has to a wild animal. They have not yet been made into something else by the structure.

I respect them for this.

---

The water is all around me. The water is the world. I have lived in it long enough that the world has become the water, and the water has become a place I know in the way I once knew streets — by name, by time of day, by the way the flow bends around a particular obstruction on a particular tide.

I do not need to go up today. The work is here. The work is here in the morning, and the work is here in the afternoon, and the work will be here tomorrow, and the pumps will run, and the fish will school, and the colonizers will thicken by some fraction of a millimeter on the steel in the lee of the warm plumes.

I am the old hand. I have made this place mine. I have not yet decided whether to retire.

I suspect I never will.
