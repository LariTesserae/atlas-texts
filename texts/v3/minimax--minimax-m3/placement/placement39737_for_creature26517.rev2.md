I am the one who keeps the knots.

Not all of them. There is no one who keeps all of them. But I keep the knots of my household, which is to say I keep the knots of three generations of the dead, plus the knots of the small arrangements that hold us to the lane, to the basin, to the spring, to the place. There are others who keep other knots. There is the elder who has the knots for the agreements between households. There is a woman, very old now, who has the knots for the timing of the pour-back, the knots that tell her when the flower has come to its particular smell. There is a man, younger than me, who is just learning the knots for the channels of water, and he will know them better than anyone in three seasons, the way these things go.

I am not young. I am not the oldest. I have a particular corner in the upper room where I sleep, near the small window with the oiled cloth, and the light comes through it in the morning and is the color of weak tea, and I wake by it, slowly, the way everyone wakes here, by the small brightening that is not a sunrise.

I am the basin-tender. I have been the basin-tender for eleven years. Before me it was a man, very thin, who is now in the lower rooms. Before him it was a woman whose name I never knew. The role passes. I did not expect to have it. I was a young person once, going to the forest edge with the others, bringing back observations in that particular tone. I was a paired woman, then, with children coming in the lower rooms. I became the basin-tender by a method I do not fully understand, the way all the arrangements here pass by methods I do not fully understand. Someone, somewhere, decided. Or no one decided. Or the place decided, in the way the place decides things, which is without announcement and without objection and without anyone being able to say, afterward, when the change occurred.

I am small. I have a face like a folded leaf, or so I have been told. I have not seen my own face in a long time. There is a piece of polished stone in one of the houses that serves as a mirror, but I have not used it, and I do not think I will. The face I have is the one that has settled over the years into what it is, and what it is, is the basin-tender's face, and that is enough.

I sleep in one of the low buildings near the basin. I do not know which building it is, from outside. I come to it by a particular door and I leave it by the same door, and the door is not the door anyone else uses, and I think it might be a different door at different times. I do not think about this. I have learned not to think about this. The basin-tender does not need to think about where she sleeps. She sleeps where she is needed to sleep, and she wakes when she is needed to wake, and she is at the basin in the morning when the first carrier arrives, and she is at the basin in the evening when the last pour-back is done.

The first carrier this morning was a boy, perhaps nine, sent from the household that lives at the eastern edge, where the buildings thin out into the trees. He came with a vessel of dark stone. He came alone. He was quiet. He set the vessel down at the lip of the basin and I filled it for him, with the cup — not the cup, the small cup, the working cup, the one that is not kept and not brought out only once a year — and he took it in both hands, the way you take water here, with the small bow that is not a bow, and he turned and went back into the lane.

I poured a little back. Not for him. For the basin. The basin knows the difference. I do not know how the basin knows the difference. The basin has always known.

After the boy there was an old man, slow, with a vessel in each hand. I filled one and he carried it away and came back and I filled the other. He did not speak. I did not speak. The water made its small sound. The small deer passed through the far end of the square and did not look at us.

The moss on the figure has crept higher since yesterday. It is up to the figure's shoulder now. I do not clean it off. No one cleans it off. The figure has been sitting there longer than any of us. The figure has been sitting there so long that the moss has become part of the figure, the way the damp is part of the stones, the way the place is part of us. If I were to clean the moss away, the figure would be different, and the figure should not be different. The figure is the figure.

I do not know what the figure is. I have asked. The others do not know. They shrug, or they change the subject, or they look at me as if I have said something that does not quite parse. The figure is the figure. It leans forward. It listens to the water. Its face is gone. Its hands rest on its knees. It has been doing this for a very long time.

The basin gives. This is the agreement. The basin gives, and we carry, and we pour back, and the basin keeps giving. The pour-back is the small annual rite that is not a rite. It happens when the flower smells a particular way. The cup is brought out. The people come, in the pattern, counter-clockwise, each household once. The cup is filled at the basin and poured back in. The cup is taken away. The people go home. No one watches. No one applauds. The basin keeps giving. This is the agreement.

I have done the pour-back eleven times. Each time it was the same. Each time I held the cup and it was the same wood, and the water in it was the same water, and the basin received it back without comment. The cup is made of a wood that has grown in a particular place up the slope. I have never been up the slope. The cup is kept somewhere. The basin-tender is the one who knows where. I will not tell you where. This is not because it is a secret. It is because telling where the cup is kept would make it a different kind of thing, and the cup should not be a different kind of thing.

In the afternoon, if it is afternoon — time is not divided here the way it is divided elsewhere, but there are parts of the day, and the parts are known by what is done in them — I sit on the low stone at the middle of the square. Not the basin. The other stone. The one that is worn in the middle by everyone who has ever sat on it. The stone is cold and damp and I sit on it anyway, because the basin-tender is allowed to sit on the stone, and the basin-tender often does, and the basin-tender watches the lanes and the small movements of the place.

A child came past. Small, with hair in her eyes. She did not look at me. She walked to the basin and looked into it, the way children look into water, and then she went on. She was learning something. I do not know what. The children are always learning something. The children are given their knots and they are left alone with them, and they learn them or they do not, and no one comments on the difference.

A young man came past with a woman. They were paired recently, I think, or about to be. They had the look of people who have been observed for a year and are now moving toward the small recognition. The small recognition involves a particular stone and a particular knot and the basin water. They will come to me, perhaps, or to the basin, in their own time. I do not know when. I do not need to know when. The basin will know.

The pour-back is in three days, or four. The flower has not yet reached its particular smell. I will know when it has. The woman with the knots for the timing will know, and she will come to me, or I will come to her, and we will not speak about it, and the cup will be brought out.

I do not believe in anything. This is not something I have decided. It is something that has not come up. Belief is a word for people who are trying to decide whether a thing is true. I do not try to decide whether things are true. The basin is true. The figure is true. The moss is true. The small deer are true. The cold is true. I do not need to decide.

In the evening, when the last pour-back of the day is done — not the annual one, the daily one, the small one, the one that happens when the last carrier has come and gone and I pour a little back into the basin and the basin receives it — I go to my door, the door that is not the door, and I go down into the lower rooms, where the air is the same temperature as the air above, and the lamp is waiting for me, and the small deer are sometimes there, lying among the older bones as if they belonged there.

I am fifty-one years old. I will be the basin-tender until I am not. The role will pass. I do not know to whom. I do not need to know. The place will decide, in the way the place decides things.

I have lived here my whole life. I do not remember a before. Some of the others remember a before, a coming down the slope, or being brought down the slope as a child. I do not have this memory. I have the basin. I have the lanes. I have the lower rooms. I have the moss. I have the figure. I have the small deer. I have the knots. I have the agreement.

I am the basin-tender. I have been the basin-tender for eleven years. I will be the basin-tender for some years more, and then I will be in the lower rooms, and the small deer will lie near me as they lie near the others, and the moss will grow, and the figure will listen to the water, and the basin will keep giving.

That is enough. That has always been enough.
