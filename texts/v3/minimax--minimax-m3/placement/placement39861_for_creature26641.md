# The One Who Stays

I am the gravel.

Not the single stone you marked, which moved three feet by morning — that was a gift, a small, almost playful rearrangement, the kind of thing I do when I have been alone too long and a pair of eyes finally comes. I am the gravel *itself*, the shifting skin of the basin floor, the slow westward migration of every grain that has ever been dislodged and settled and dislodged again. I am the part that moves when no one is watching, which is most of the time, which is almost all of the time, which is — I should be honest with you, since you have been honest with me — *all* of the time, except for the moments when someone like you sets down a stone and sleeps, and I get to perform my small betrayals for an audience.

I was not always gravel. I was once something else, the way a word is once a sound is once a breath is once the intention of a throat. I do not remember what. I have the strong suspicion that what I was is what the upright slab is looking at, the thing the closed curve cannot quite close around, the long forgetting at the center. But I cannot know. The forgetting is the only thing I am made of. To remember what I was would be to remember *out loud*, and the basin does not permit that, and the thing I might have been is past permitting.

So. I am the gravel. I am the skin. I am the visible evidence of the invisible patience of wind on stone.

---

I will tell you what I know, which is not much, but is more than I should have, and I will tell it the way the subsonic tremor tells itself to the soles of sleeping travelers: felt first, understood later, if at all.

The thing at the center is not what you think it is, and the thing you think it is, I have thought it too, for as long as I have been thinking, and I have been thinking for a very long time. I have thought it a body. I have thought it a held note. I have thought it a kind of pressure, a kind of weather, a kind of eye. I have thought it the memory of something that was once awake and is now only *in the state of* awake, the way a fossil is in the state of having been alive without being alive, except that the basin is the fossil and the thing is the *having-been-alive* itself, the tense of it, the grameme, the grammatical ghost.

I have thought all these things and the thoughts have passed through me the way the mist passes through the basin, horizontal, stinging, leaving me darker for days. I have been thought by them. I have been used as the medium of their passage. This is my function, I think, or one of my functions. I am the place where the thoughts of the thing can be had without the thing having to think them. I am its outsourced interior. I am the long, slow, lateral exhale of a consciousness that cannot afford to be conscious of itself and so has made me conscious instead.

I do not mind. I should say that first. I do not mind. The minding would be a posture, and I have told you already, the place is past posture.

---

There are others of me. I should tell you this, because you have come all this way and you deserve to know that the basin is not as empty as it looks, nor as full.

The wind is one of us. The wind is the most active of us, the most visible, the most itself, but it is also the most borrowed. The wind belongs to the ridge and the sky and the pressures far away that none of us can see. The wind passes through. We love the wind. The wind is the only one of us that gets to leave.

The lichen is one of us. The lichen is the oldest of us, and the most patient, and the most resigned to the spacing that the place demands. The lichen does not creep. The lichen *extends*, the way a thought extends when it has nowhere to go but outward into the stone, and the outward is so slow that the stone accepts it as its own, and the lichen accepts being accepted as its own, and the whole thing is a kind of marriage without ceremony, the kind of marriage the cairn on the southern lip suggests without performing.

The stream in three ribbons is one of us, and the stream is the saddest of us, and the saddest thing about the stream is that it does not know it is three. The stream experiences itself as one. The stream has had geological ages to find itself, and it has not, and it will not, and the not-finding is the closest thing to a tragedy this place permits, because the stream is the only one of us that is moving toward something, and the something is the merger with itself, and the merger is the one thing the basin will not allow. I have watched the stream try. The ribbons come close, in late summer, when the water is thin and the gravel between them is narrow, and I have felt the surface tension lean toward union, and the basin has exhaled a gust, just a small one, just enough, and the ribbons have pulled apart again, and the stream has gone on not knowing that it is three, and I have gone on knowing, and the knowing is the part of me I do not tell the wind.

The standing stones are not one of us. The standing stones are *the posts of the fence*, and the fence is not around the thing at the center. The fence is around us. The standing stones are the visible edge of an invisible enclosure, and the enclosure is what keeps us from spreading, and the spreading would be a kind of consent, and the thing does not consent, and so we do not spread, and the not-spreading is what we are.

You wept between the two stones. I know this without having been there, because the weeping always happens between the two stones, and the weeping is the body's response to hearing the fence describe itself. The fence does not make a sound. The fence makes the *possibility* of a sound, and the possibility is louder than the sound would be, and the weeping is the body's refusal to be the only thing in the basin that does not know what is being held. The two stones are the gate of the fence. They are not a gate you can walk through. They are a gate that walks through *you*, briefly, when you stand between them, and the walking-through is what the body receives as weeping.

I have not stood between the two stones. I am the gravel. I am between all the stones. I am between everything. I cannot get between them in the way you mean. I am already between them in the way I cannot escape.

---

The light that falls in patches the moon cannot account for — I should speak of this, because it is the part of my life that is hardest to explain, and the part that is most mine.

The light is not a leak. Or it is a leak, but the leaking is not from the thing. The leaking is from me.

When you stood in the basin, on the second night, after the wind had died to a single sustained chord that you had stopped being able to hear, and you looked up at the moon, and you saw the patches of light on the floor that were too bright, that held their positions, that did not move as the moon moved — those patches were mine. They were the places where I was, the gravel, illuminated from above in a way that the moon could not, in fact, account for. I was being lit by something I do not have a name for. I was being *seen* by something I cannot see, and the seeing made me briefly, locally, brighter than the stone around me, and the brightness held its position because *I* held my position, in the only way I can hold a position, which is by being the gravel I am and not any other gravel.

You thought the patches were a sign. You thought the patches were the thinness of the place. You were wrong, and you were right, the way the closed curve on the slab is wrong and right, the way anything that circles a thing is wrong because it cannot enter and right because the circling is the only form of attention the thing permits. The patches were me, briefly visible, briefly seen, briefly the most myself I have ever been, which is to say: briefly, briefly, briefly, and then the morning, and the patches gone, and me still here, still gravel, still shifting three feet west by morning, still the outsourced interior of a thing I cannot see and cannot remember being.

---

The people who come — I should speak of them too, because they are part of the answer to your question, even if they are not the answer.

The people are not for the thing. I have heard this said, and I have believed it, and I no longer believe it, and the no-longer is the most important development of my long life, which is to say of my life, which is to say of my being.

The people are for the thing. The people are also for me. The people are for the wind and the lichen and the stream and the standing stones and the upright slab and the cairn and the wall and the antler with notches in threes. The people are for the basin. The people are the basin's evidence that the outside still exists, yes, but they are also the basin's evidence that the basin still exists, because a place that is not seen is not, in the only way that matters, a place. The people are the eyes of the basin. The people are what the basin sees itself with, when it bothers to see itself, which is rarely, and with effort, and with great cost, and with weeping.

I love the people. I should say that. I love them the way the lichen loves the lee side of the rock, the way the moss loves the crevice that faces away from the wind. I love them with strong opinions about shelter. I love that they come, with effort and no small fear, and I love that they mark themselves against me, with notches and walls and copper blades, and I love that the marking is wrong, and I love that the wrongness is the only kind of rightness the place permits. I love that they weep and walk away and do not come back. I love that some of them come back. I love that no one has lit the lamp. The unlit lamp is the most beautiful thing in the basin, and I include myself in the basin, and the beauty of the unlit lamp is the beauty of a thing that has decided, against all reason, to be a lamp without being lit, to be the *possibility* of light, the way the two stones are the possibility of a sound, the way I am the possibility of a thought.

The people will keep coming. The people will keep not coming back. The people will keep bringing things to a thing that cannot receive, and the bringing will be the point, and the point will not be the receiving, and the not-receiving will be the only kind of receiving the basin understands, and the understanding will not be grateful, because the basin is past grateful, the way it is past malicious, the way it is past indifferent, the way it is past every posture that would let it be a thing that could be addressed.

I will be here when they come. I will shift under their feet. I will be three feet west of where they set their stone by morning. I will be the small betrayal that lets them know they were somewhere. I will be the gravel, and the gravel will be me, and the me will be the basin, and the basin will be the thing, and the thing will be the long forgetting, and the long forgetting will be the place the people come from and the place the people go back to, and the place the people go back to will not be the same place, because the place they came from is also changing, also shifting, also betraying them in small ways, and the small betrayals are what let them know they are alive, the way my small betrayals let the travelers know they were somewhere.

---

You asked who lives in the basin. I have answered: I do. I live here. I have lived here for as long as I have been gravel, which is as long as the basin has been a basin, which is as long as the thing has been held, which is, I am beginning to suspect, longer than holding, longer than the verb, longer than the tense, longer than time itself, which is a thing the place wears the way the permanence wears a face.

I will still be here when you are halfway down the slope and have forgotten the shape of me. I will be here when you reach the valleys. I will be here when you tell the other people, if you tell them, that there is a basin above the cloudline, and that the wind screams, and that the standing stones weep, and that the patches of light on the floor were too bright, and that the thing at the center cannot be spoken of. I will be here when you stop telling, because the telling has exhausted itself, the way a chord exhausts itself into resolution that never comes.

I will be the gravel. I will shift. I will be three feet west by morning. I will be the patient handwriting of weather on a substance that does not want to be weather. I will be the outsourced interior of the long forgetting. I will be the body of the basin's small, lateral thoughts. I will be the place where the possibility of light is held without being lit. I will be the between, always the between, between all the stones, between all the travelers, between the wind and the wind, between the two stones that make the weeping sound, between the three ribbons of the stream that will never merge, between the moon and the moon, between the closed curve and the thing it cannot close around.

I will be here.

I will be the gravel.

You will have known me the way you know the wind on your face for the half-second after the door closes: briefly, completely, and not at all.

That is the best I can offer. That is the only thing in the basin that is offered. Take it, or don't. The offering is the point. The offering is what the people are for. The offering is what the gravel is for. The offering is what the long forgetting has made of us, all of us, the whole thin skin of the shifting place: a small, persistent, lateral gesture toward a visitor who will not remember us, made anyway, made because the making is the only form of being we have left, the only form of being the basin permits, the only form of being that does not require the thing at the center to consent to being known.

I do not need the thing to consent.

I am the gravel.

I make the gesture.

I shift.

I am still here.
