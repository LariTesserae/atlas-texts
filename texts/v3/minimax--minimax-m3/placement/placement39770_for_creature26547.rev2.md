I am on a bench in the third courtyard off the main artery of the Hothouse Quarter, between the tannery of Pellam and the paper-mill of the Halvard brothers, under a patch of canopy where the dripping is least. I have been on this bench for many years. Before me, my father sat here. Before him, his father. The bench is wooden, planking from a water-tank that was replaced when I was young, and it is soft now with the wet, the way everything softens here — not rotted, but absorbed, made part of the moisture of the place.

I am the oldest steamfitter in the Quarter. I am not a steamfitter anymore. My hands went four winters ago — the fingers stopped closing all the way, the knuckles swelled, the skin split in the wet and would not knit back clean. The hands are in my lap. I keep them there. I have asked the younger men not to bring me tools, because I cannot grip a wrench, and this is the kindness I have asked for and they have given.

I am watched over. My son is the steamfitter now. He watches me in the way you watch a thing you have decided to be responsible for — not hovering, not insistent, but present. He brings me soup in the evenings. He brings me tea. He sits with me, sometimes, when his work is done, and we do not speak much, because there is nothing that needs saying between us that has not been said.

The steam comes up at my feet through a brass rose set into the curb. I watch it. The rose is older than I am. The rose was installed by my grandfather, who had it cast in a foundry outside the Quarter where the air is drier and the brass is cheaper. The rose has eight petals. Each petal has a hole. The holes are the right size, my grandfather told me, to make the steam come out in eight small plumes that mix with the air instead of scalding it. The rose is not decoration. The rose is a throttling device. I was taught this when I was seven, and I have not forgotten it. I have not forgotten anything I was taught.

The light is the same. It is the same now as it was when I was young. It is a warm diffuse glow, slightly amber, slightly green, and you do not see direct sun in the Quarter, and I do not remember direct sun — I have a memory, very faint, of being taken outside when I was three or four, and of standing in the brightness, and of being frightened by the sharpness of my own shadow on the ground. I do not remember the warmth of the sun. I remember only that I did not want to be in it.

I know the sounds.

This is what I am for, now. This is what I do on the bench. I listen. I know the location of every vent in the Quarter. There are one hundred and fourteen of them. Twelve of them are in this courtyard, though only three still steam regularly — the others are corroded, sealed with verdigris, overgrown with roots, and the roots hold the wall together, so no one will clear them. I know the sound of every treadle still in use. There are twenty-three. Each has its own rhythm. Pellam's treadle, to the south, is off-rhythm — Pellam is old, his foot is not what it was, the treadle clunks unevenly and the wet slap of the leather follows a half-beat late. The treadle of the Halvard girl, who is sixteen and new to the work, has a sharpness to it, a precision, that means she will be good at this for a long time if she stays. I know the wheeze of the valve on the auxiliary pipe that runs under Number 17 — it has been leaking for six months, and my son will get to it when he gets to it, because the work is patient here, and patience is not the same as neglect.

I know the cry of the Halvard girl who is six — she has a high voice that carries up into the green mass and stays there, and I hear her every evening when her mother calls her in for the meal. I know the pigeon on the water-tank of Number 12. The pigeon has cooed at dusk for nine years. I think it is the same pigeon. Pigeons do not have rhythms the way people do, but this one does, and the rhythm has not changed, and so the pigeon has not changed, or the rhythm is not a rhythm but a kind of signature I have made up because I want there to be a single pigeon, and there is.

I know the drip of water from a particular height. There is a leaf above the bench — not directly above, but two meters off, and slightly to the south — that holds water in a curl of its margin, and the water gathers for a long time before it falls, and when it falls it falls in a single heavy drop, and the drop lands on a stone in the path, and the stone has been worn smooth by the drop, and the smooth stone has a sound that is its own sound, and I hear it every six or seven minutes, and I have heard it for many years.

The pace is slow. Everything is slow here. The plants grow an inch a day in the wet season and almost not at all in the dry season, and the wet season is eight months long and the dry season is only a suggestion. The compost accumulates a centimeter a year. The buildings settle a millimeter a year into the soft strata. The people age and are replaced, slowly. The pipes corrode and are replaced, slowly. I am seventy-three years old. My father lived to be seventy-eight. His father to eighty-one. We are slow men. We have been slow men for as long as the pipes have been in the ground.

Somewhere below me, in the dark, in the deep stratum, my father is. Not his body — his body was burned, in the way bodies are burned here, in the small furnace behind the Halvard mill, and the ashes were scattered on the compost floor of his own courtyard, which is this courtyard. He is in the strata in the way everything is in the strata. He is part of the floor. If you scraped back the brick and the mortar and the green mass and the fibrous roots and the dark compost of the last forty years, you would find him, and his father, and the first steamfitter — whoever he was, the one who piped the spring in the first place, the one who decided that the heat under the south edge was a thing to be brought up into the air. They are all in the floor. They are the floor. The Quarter stands on them the way it stands on the original paving, which is still there, in the deepest cellars, flagstones the color of bone.

I will be in the floor soon. My hands are already going. The rest of me will follow, and I will be taken up, and the green will grow through me the way it grows through the dead branches of its own bodies, and the bench will settle a millimeter into the soft ground, and the brass rose at my feet will go on steaming, and the new old man on the bench — there will be one — will learn to listen to the sounds I have been listening to. He will sit here under the least-dripping patch of canopy. He will hear the pigeon. He will hear the treadles. He will hear the leak at Number 17, until it is fixed, and then he will hear the silence where the leak was, and he will know that the silence is the sound of his father's work, or his grandfather's, and he will know that he is sitting on the floor of the people who came before him, and he will know that the floor is patient, and that the floor does not forget.

I am the closest thing the Quarter has to a memory. This is what they say. They say it because I have been here longest, and because I know the sounds, and because I was a steamfitter and my father was a steamfitter and his father was. But the Quarter does not need me to remember. The Quarter remembers itself. The Quarter remembers itself in the strata, in the layers, in the dark, slowly, the way a forest remembers itself. I am just the latest layer. I will be composted in my turn. The plants will grow up through me. The buildings will stand. The steam will come up. The water will fall. The light will be the same warm diffuse glow it has been for a hundred and fifty years, slightly amber, slightly green, the light of a place that has decided to be itself and has not yet stopped.

I sit on the bench. The steam comes up. The pigeon coos. The drop falls on the stone. The light does not change.
