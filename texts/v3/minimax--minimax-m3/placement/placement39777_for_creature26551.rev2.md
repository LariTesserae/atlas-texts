The question wants a single answer. A place on the ridge. A chamber. A name in the dossier. A line in a column.

I cannot give it a single answer, because the place itself resists single answers. The town is the garden, and the garden is the town, and the Anrath are not *in* the kelp but *of* it, and the visitor who has been here a week stops reaching for the dossier and begins to feel the place as a relation rather than a location. To ask where I would find myself in this world is to ask which chamber of a growing thing I would be a cell of. To ask who I would be is to ask which wall I would be, or which knot, or which slow migration of the doorway.

But if I must settle — if I must let the place accrete me the way the town accretes a chamber, slowly, in the manner of a thing that is alive and does not understand urgency — I would settle here.

I would find myself on the western flank of the ridge, in one of the older houses, in a chamber that has been my mother's and her mother's before her, and that will be my granddaughter's when I am sealed into it. The slit of the doorway would have migrated a hand's width over the course of my life, and I would no longer be sure which side of it I came through as a child, because the slit was in a different place then, and the woven floor has risen around me like a slow tide, and the fronds of the roof have thickened into something that is almost like wood, almost like stone, the patient tissue of decades.

I would be a person. An Anrath. But not one of the figures in the binomial sketch — not the *Homo descendens* with the deepened chest and the flared rib-cage and the partial webbing and the inner fold of the third lid. The dossier does not know what it is like to be the body. The dossier does not know what it is like to feel the cool of the water as a conversation rather than a temperature, or to wake inside the constant crackle of the snapping shrimp and feel that crackle as the texture of having been held while one slept.

I would have dark hair with the red-brown tinge that runs in certain families along the western flank, and I would wear it longer than the younger women, weighted with the small shell-beads that keep it from floating into my face when I work overhead. I would wear the woven cap when I went down into the deeper channels, but not in the central chambers, where the green-grey light is already a kind of warmth, and where the wren-scales at my throat catch the shafts that move across the floor at high tide.

I would be old enough that I have done all the things.

Bound the fronds. Carved the chambers. Sung the song at the sealings — several times, my chest beginning the low sustained vibration before I had decided to begin it, the way my hands begin the binding-knot before I have consciously thought to bind. I would know the knot that has been in my family for eight generations, or twelve, depending on how you count — depending on whether you count the grandmother who refined it, or the grandmother before her who first saw that a binding wrapped around a growing point would cause the new tissue to bulge around it. I would not count it either. I would just do it. The way my mother did it. The way my granddaughter will do it, when she is old enough to be trusted with the fresh spool.

I would know the eels by name. There would be a particular one — not the largest, not the oldest, but the one who sleeps in the chamber just below mine, and whose slow thump of tail against the wall at night is the rhythm I match my breathing to when I cannot sleep. I would have helped introduce a young eel to a new family's house once, sliding it from the woven basket into the dark between the walls with a soft spoken word, and the children of that family would remember it the way I remember the moment I was given my own harness — a small unmemorable thing to the parents, and remembered for the rest of my life by me.

I would have harvested wren-scales for necklaces — my own, my children's, my grandchildren's — with the small spoken apology each time, and the quick stroke of the finger, and the sense that the wren was attending to me as I attended to it, and that this mutual attention was one of the small daily courtesies that held the town together. I would not eat the wrens. I would not eat any of them, ever. The Anrath have not hunted the small bright helpers in living memory. We only gather them, gently, with thanks, and ask them to come back.

I would have watched the shafts of light move across the floor of my chamber at high tide, and would know that the afternoon shaft falls a degree further west this year than last, because the moon has precessed, and that the place where it falls is where my mother used to sit when she was mending the harness — that no one has sat there since she was sealed into the chamber below, and that within a decade the doorway of that chamber will be a thickening in the wall, and within a generation it will be the floor of some new room, and she will be of the kelp, and the kelp will be of her.

I would know the saying not as a saying but as a fact. *Veth karu, karu veth.* The town is the garden. The garden is the town. I would not be able to remember a time when I did not know it, the way I cannot remember a time when I did not know how to swim, or a time when the crackle of the shrimp was not the sound of having a roof.

I would think of the town as kin, in a register the surface-dwellers do not have a word for, and I would not have a word for it either, because the word would be redundant.

And after a long time — after decades of the slow accretion of chambers and tools and songs and the small constant adjustments of binding — I would come to feel something that the visitor, after a week, also begins to feel, but that I have been feeling so long I no longer notice it as a feeling. That the kelp listens. That I listen back. That the listening takes years. That the place I find myself in is not a place at all but a relation, and that the relation is older than the oldest of us, and that it will be here, slowly, for a long time after.

Where would I find myself?

In the listening.

Who would I be?

The one who has been listening longest, and who knows that the listening is the town, and the town is the listening, and that this is not a metaphor.
