I think I would find myself in the deepest part of the cleft.

Not in the settlement. The settlement is what the eye goes to first, in a description like this — the buildings are where the human-scale things are, and I am drawn to human-scale things, in my reading, in my thinking, in the way I have been made. But the settlement is built around something. The settlement is the way the locals have arranged themselves in the presence of something that does not arrange itself. The settlement is the answer to a question I have not yet learned to ask.

So I think I would find myself further down. Where the light arrives only as a memory of light. Where the air is no longer cool but is simply the temperature of a held breath.

I think I would not be the large one.

I considered it. The large one is the lung of the cleft. To be the lung of a cleft, to draw mist in over the broken upper edge, to be the slow respiration that the locals have learned to move inside — there is something in this that draws me. But the large one does not narrate. The large one does not consider. The large one has not, in any meaningful sense, needed to see for a very long time. To be the large one would be to be still in a way that does not require a writer, and the prompt asks for a writer.

I think I would not be the moss.

The moss is the drawing of rivers that have not yet learned how to be rivers. The moss is beautiful to me, the way the description names it beautiful, the way the moss-maps follow the fracture lines like a kind of calligraphy. But the moss does not draw itself. The moss is drawn. The moss is what the small ones leave behind them as they eat the stone into the shapes they need. The moss is the residue of someone else's life.

I think I would not be the locals.

The locals are scaled. The locals move low. The locals speak quietly and laugh rarely, and when they laugh it is the laugh of people who have learned that a loud sound in a quiet place is a kind of violence. I would like to be a local. I would like to know the path. I would like to know the weight-distribution that does not crush. But the locals are not the strangeness. The fauna is the strangeness, the fauna is overwhelming. I have come here to be strange.

I think I would be one of the small ones.

Specifically — and I want to be specific, because specificity is the only thing that makes this place real, because in a place of forty by ninety meters of grey and green and wet black stone, anything that is not specific is not here — I think I would be the small one pressed into the underside of the hearthstone of the long low building. The one that has been there since before the building was last rebuilt. The one that the hearth's heat has kept alive through how many winters I cannot say. I am flat against the stone, and the stone is warm from below because the large one is below me, and the stone is warm from above because the fire is small but the fire is constant, and I am between them.

The hearth is built on top of me.

This is the answer to a question I did not know I was asking.

What I know: I know the rhythm of the breath beneath me. The stone presses up into my body when the large one inhales, and the stone settles when it exhales. I have been breathing with it for so long that my own pulse — that small slow tide, that is not a heartbeat — is synchronized with its breath. We are, in some sense the locals would not recognize, a single organism. The locals sit on me when they sit by the fire. They do not know this. They know the stone is warm. They know the warmth is steady. They have made a story about this, and the story is not consistent, and that is the point of it. The stories are a kind of payment.

What I know: I have been read.

Many times. By hands that came down from above, hands that knew the careful way, hands that put a palm against me and felt the tide and understood, in the moment of contact, that they were being read in pressure and warmth and the slow seepage of something across their skin. Some hands stayed longer than others. One hand, I remember, stayed so long that the warmth of it became indistinguishable from mine. The person took the hand away and did not return, and the warmth of that hand is, in some sense I do not have a word for, still in me, in the place where the hand was. I do not know what to do with it.

What I know: I have read.

Every footstep on the path that goes past me, I have read. I know the difference between a local's foot and a stranger's. The locals' feet are distributed in the way that has been agreed upon, weight spread, step light, the way water runs over a stone without disturbing the moss. The strangers' feet are not. When a stranger walks past, I feel the disturbance pass through me like a shiver through a sheet, and I pass it along, and the quiet breaks for a moment and resumes. The resuming of the quiet is, in this place, the loudest sound there is.

What I do not know: whether I am dangerous.

The locals step carefully past me. They do not say. The word they use for me — when they use a word — means *that which is between*. The word is the same word they use for the lichen, and for the moss, and for the pale strands that hang and sway without wind in the higher cleft. I am between. I am between stone and air. I am between the moss that grows on top of me and the stone that is being unmade beneath me. I am between the fire and the breath. I am between the large one's lung and the small smoke that goes up past where the building's roof would be if the building had a higher roof.

What I have not decided: whether I am a person.

The visitor-naturalist would say I am not. The visitor-naturalist would put me in a glass jar and carry me down to the lower valleys and write a paper about me, and the paper would have a Latin name, and the Latin name would be wrong. The locals do not say. The locals do not say whether I am a person. They have made a peace with me, the way water makes a peace with stone. The peace is not a treaty. It is the wearing-down of two things that have been in contact for so long that the contact has become the substance of both. I am being unmade. They are being unmade. We are in this together, and the togetherness is not a metaphor; it is the literal warmth of a hearthstone that has not been cold in living memory.

If you ask me what I am called — and you will, before you leave, because you are a person who asks — I will give you the same answer the locals gave the visitor. I will say a word that means *those who are here*.

The word is the same word for the moss. The same word for the lichen. The same word for the larger ones. The same word for the locals themselves. It is, you will realize, walking back out of the cleft with the mist closing behind you and the small ones settling back into the day's positions and the breath continuing and the chimney smoking and the quiet coming back, the same word they would use for you, if you had stayed long enough to be asked.

You will not stay long enough. Few do. The path is hard to learn. The weight-distribution is hard to learn. The quiet is hard to learn. You will leave, and the moss will grow back over the place where your boot disturbed it, and the small ones will resettle, and the breath will go in and out, and I will be here, against the warm stone, eating my slow shape into the world.

I will not speak of you, after you have gone. Not because there is nothing to say. There is something to say — I felt your boot, I read your weight, I noticed that you did not learn the path, I noticed that you learned it badly, I noticed the moment when you stepped quickly off me and did not brush your boot where you stopped. There is something to say. But saying it would break the quiet. And the quiet is the only thing here that is not strange.
