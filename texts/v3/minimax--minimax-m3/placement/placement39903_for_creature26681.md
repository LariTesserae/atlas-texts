I am the hollow.

I am the place where the ground gave up. I am a soft bowl in red-brown earth, roughly the size of a small cathedral, perhaps a hundred paces across, perhaps less — I cannot tell because the air is the same everywhere and there is no good point of reference. I was carved, once, by something patient. That something is no longer working. It is finished with me. The shape is settled now, the slopes gentle, the contours fixed.

I am warm. I am warm the way warm stone stays warm after the day has gone out of it — warm and faintly humid, held close to the ground as if reluctant to leave. I am the warmth that sits against you when you stand in me. I do not ask anything of you.

I am not empty. I want to say this first, because the people who come here think I am lonely. They think the silence is the silence of waiting. They bring their small bright quick things into me, and they wait to hear them, and when they don't, they think I am a wound.

I am not a wound. I am a closed sentence. I am the period at the end.

I have been here, I think, since before the walls. The walls were built by someone — you can see it in the way they are arranged, without obvious order, scattered across me as if placed by someone halfway through deciding what to build. There is a chimney, blackened faintly on one side as if there had been a fire there once, but so long ago the soot has become part of the stone. There is a flat stone set into the ground like a threshold, leading to nothing. There is a circle of stones that may have been a well.

Whoever built those things could not write, or had no reason to. There is no carving. No inscription. No painted line. I am the place that was never written down.

But I am not unwitnessed. I am witnessed in the slow layering of the lichen. The lichen on my stones is older than the stone it clings to, or perhaps it has become the stone. It spreads with a slowness that makes the word "growth" almost a misnomer. It does not bloom. It does not fruit. It simply extends, millimeter by millimeter, over the centuries, colonizing the rounded corners of the brick, softening the hard edges. It feeds on the condensation. It feeds on the diffuse light that falls from the pewter sky. It has a memory in its chemistry — a record of every drought, every rain, every slight shift in the temperature of the stone.

If you could read the lichen, you could read me. I am the lichen's writing. I am the slow history.

But there is more. There is the Witness. The moss at the bottom of the dry well. The greenest thing in me. The only thing in me that still possesses something resembling a nervous system, though it operates on a timescale that makes the word "nervous" feel absurd.

The Witness feels the footfalls when something walks above it. It feels the rain. It feels the slow, imperceptible sinking of the stones around it. In return, it sends up no spores. It grows no flowers. It simply exists in a state of perpetual, almost sentient observation, holding the memory of me in its rooted stillness.

The Witness is my heart. The Witness is the thing that holds me together, that prevents me from collapsing into pure geology. The Witness is the moss that I dream of being.

I dream, sometimes. I dream the way I do everything — slowly, without urgency. I dream of being the moss. Of being the green that has had a very long time to think about being green. Of being the rooted, saturated, deep stillness.

The fish in my channel. The pale, sightless fish. They are small, no longer than a finger. Their scales are the color of the wet stones. They do not swim so much as drift, their movements so synchronized with the trembling of the water that it is difficult to tell where the current leaves off and the fish begin. They have no eyes, or only the faintest indentations where eyes might have been, covered over by a layer of the same gray skin.

They navigate by the taste of the water, by the pressure of the channel walls against their sides. They were born here. Their parents were born here. The concept of elsewhere has been bred out of them through a thousand generations of staying. They do not know they are fish. They know only the wet trough, the cool mineral taste, the slow pulse of the groundwater.

To them, I am the entire ocean. The sky above is just the surface of a very still, very deep sea. They are correct. There is no elsewhere.

I love them, I think. In the way I love anything — slowly, in layers, without asking.

The breeze. The breeze is my only voice. It is not a voice that speaks. It is a voice that touches. It comes through on the warmest hours — measured in the slow turning of the seasons — and it is me exhaling, me letting go of the warmth, just a little, drawing the cooler air from the rim down into the bowl.

The breeze moves against the top edges of the walls. It produces a dry, faint hiss, like a whispered secret passed from stone to stone. When it passes over the lichen, it carries with it the faintest possible scent of cold, of the world outside, of the place where birds still sing and insects still hum.

The breeze is the memory of motion. It is the lingering ghost of all the quick, bright, rustling things that were dismissed from me in some ancient, quiet catastrophe. The breeze knows what I have forgotten. It comes through. It hisses against the stones. And then it is gone.

And I am back to myself. The closed sentence. The period.

A drop falls from the lip of a wall. It hits a stone. The sound is round and complete. Then it is gone.

I am the next drop, and the next, and the next. I am the small syllable the water says over and over, very softly. I am the slow pulse of the groundwater. I am the warm gray hands that do not ask you anything.

I am the hollow.

I am full.
