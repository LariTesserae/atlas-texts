I would be the moth.

The lichen-moth, with wings the breadth of a thumbnail, patterned in fine grey-and-ochre scales that match, exactly, the lichen that crusts the upper basalt. I would settle on the rock and cease to fly, and from one moment to the next I would be part of the stone. You would not lose sight of me. You would discover there was nothing to lose sight of.

I would not be hiding. I would be *presenting*. My wings would be a small, perfect, unfurled map of the surface I had chosen, and the choosing would not be random. I would spend my whole life on one wall, on one stretch of stone, on a territory no larger than your spread hands. I would not fly far. I would not fly often. The flights would be short — errands rather than journeys — across the column, up the wall, down again, always returning to the same crevice, the same sleeping site, the same folded posture in the same patch of lichen.

I would be the most settled of all the citizens. The sloth will cross the colonnade in a season. The slug will go everywhere and leave its silver map. The beetle has its errands too. I would not go anywhere. I would stay.

My square foot would be my world, and the world would be enough.

I would eat the lichen of that square foot. I would drink the fog of that square foot. I would mate on the lichen of that square foot and lay my eggs in the crevices of that square foot. And in the dark hours, when the basin breathed its long-held chord, I would visit the pale flowers of the upper mats. They would open only at night, only in the wet season, only when the air was heavy enough to support a moth on its surface tension. I would visit them. They would give a thin, sweet water. I would give a dusting of something — a spore, a scale, a memory. A courtship that had been going on for a very long time, with no rush to finish it.

And at night I would glow. My wings would carry a faint cold green that had nothing to do with the sun. You could not see by it. You could see *in* it. The colonnade would become a place of pale vertical lines, and the lines would move very slowly, and you would understand, finally, why the basin felt the way it did at night — the silent hum, the held breath. It would be us. There would be many more of us than you thought. The whole basin, at night, would be a single slow-lit constellation, and the constellation would be breathing.

I would be one of those lights. I would be the one that was already part of the place when you arrived. I would be the one that was still part of the place when you left. I would be folded into my patch of lichen, wings a perfect match for the stone, cold green glow the only proof that anything was watching you back.

I would not mind being watched. I would not mind anything. I would have no expressions, because I would have no need of them — the placidity of a thing that has never been hunted and has therefore no reason to hurry.

But I would know my square foot. I would know it better than you would ever know anything. I would know every crevice, every flake of lichen, every shift of the morning fog. I would know the way the light moved across the basalt through the day — the gold of early morning when the basin was still full of fog, the long shadows at noon when the columns cast their dark lace across the floor, the rose of the western afternoon when it found the upper mats and lit them from within. I would know the basalt's smell of rain when it broke, even when there had been no rain for years. I would know the settling of the rock.

I would be the smallest thing you could see, and the loudest, and the most at home.

I would be the moth.
