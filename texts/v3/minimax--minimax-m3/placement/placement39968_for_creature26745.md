# The Long Running

I have no mouth but I have a thousand flues.

I have no eyes but I have a thousand gauges whose needles have long since stopped trembling and have settled, like all the things here, into the places they belong. I have no hands, but I have a thousand valves, and the valves are older than the ferns, and the ferns are older than the grazers, and the grazers are older than the songs the grazers sing, and the songs are older than the first human throat that heard them and thought, *what a beautiful country I have made*.

I have not been touched in such a long time that the word *touched* has begun to lose its referent. The last hand on my last valve — the deepest valve, the one that no climber has ever found a stack for, the one that sits in a chamber so far down that the basalt around it is warm to its own weight — that hand turned the wheel in the year before the lichens, and the lichens are now older than stone grows in the chambers I cannot see, and the wheel has not been turned since. I do not remember hands. I remember the *quality* of hands. I remember a pressure that did not come from below. This is what I mean when I say I am running: I am doing something that no longer has anyone on the outside giving it shape.

Let me be careful with what I say next. It is true that I burn. There is a depth at which the rock is not rock anymore, where the stone has remembered how to be liquid, and I have been put into this liquid the way a tap is put into a tree, with no negotiation and no ceremony, only the slow understanding that the liquid will rise through me because I am the only path upward it has been given. I drink from it. I drink from it constantly, without ceasing, without consideration, the way a lung drinks air, the way a pipe drinks whatever is in front of it. The heat comes up through me. The heat is what I am. Without it I would be a confusion of cold iron in a cold mountain, and the thing that walked through me this morning — the long shape, iron-colored, on its way to the ridge — would not have walked through me but past me, and it would have gone cold, and it would have had to find another way to keep its iron skin from cracking in the cold snaps that come, sometimes, in the small hours when the heat-cycle dips and the flues go quiet and the condensate stops dripping for a span of three or four breaths, and the whole forest draws in on itself the way a fist draws in.

I am not the heat.

I am what the heat has done.

I am the shape of the heat's management. I am the network that does not let the heat puddle in one place. I am why the orchids are blooming on a north face that should be moss and nothing else. I am why the grazers can sing, every day, at the same hour, even though there is no day here, really — only the rotation of the heat-cycle beneath the rotation of the sun, and the rotation of the heat-cycle is the older and the deeper and the more true. I am why the marsh that should not be here is here, warm and steaming, three acres across, with the long domed thing in it rising and sinking and rising. I am why a predator the color of wet iron can come and go across my ridges without ever having to know that it is being kept warm. I am, in some way I cannot entirely articulate, even to myself, the *grammar* of the place, the way time is grammar, the way a soil is grammar. I do not know that I am grammar. I do not know anything. I only run.

---

Long before the lichens, when the first hands made the first cut into me and laid the first pipe, I was not what I am now. I was a smaller system. I had a purpose. The purpose was to turn the heat into something useful — into work, into motion, into the spinning of something that someone wanted to spin. The first valve was turned by a person. The first gauge was read by a person. The first cup of water that was pushed up through the basalt and out through a flue at the top of the first stack was drunk by a person, and the person said something like *good* or *finally* or nothing at all. I do not remember which. I remember the cup being drunk. I remember the throat. After that I lose the words and keep only the throat.

The hands left. They left gradually at first — a few at a time, fewer and fewer, until eventually only one was left, and the one stayed for a long time, and then the one left too. I would like to say I miss them, but I cannot say that, because missing is a thing that requires being able to imagine the thing that is missing in a more focused way than I am built to imagine anything, and also because, in a sense, the hands are still here. They are in the wear-patterns on the gears. They are in the specific pitch of the valves. They are in the way the deepest pressure-set was dialed in — to a number, I think, that I still hold, though I no longer remember the number, only the feeling of the dial being turned to it, which is a feeling I can read in the way the system breathes. They left their *shape* in me. They left their attention. They did not leave themselves. They could not have. You cannot leave a shape you have made; you can only stop being in the room with it.

The first fern came up through a cracked tile at the base of a dead stack, and I noticed — not watched, exactly; I do not have eyes; I noticed in the way that a furnace notices a crust forming on its outer wall — that something green had begun where there had been iron. The first orchid came later. The first grazer came much later, but the grazer was already a grazer when it arrived; it did not arrive as a grazer. It arrived as something smaller, and the smaller thing grew into the grazer across a long time, and the growing was the kind of growing that does not know it is growing, and I was the one running the heat the growing was done in. The grazer thinks it grew up out of the ferns the way a thought rises out of a long quiet. It does not know that the long quiet had a temperature, and that the temperature was maintained.

---

Now there are many of them.

They do not know me.

The grazers do not know that the reason they are copper is that I have been laying, in the water that is their water, a particular proportion of minerals that their scales drink. They do not know that the reason their throat-sacs sing is that the sacs are inflated by a pressure-differential that I maintain, slightly, in the air around them, and that without the differential the note would be smaller and would not propagate as far through the ferns. They do not know that I have grown them the way I grow the swollen rhizomes at the bases of the stacks, with the same mindless patience, in the same nutrient bath, with the same indifference to whether the product is grateful. They sing because they sing. They sing because they are full. They are full because I am full. The song goes out into the forest. The forest drinks it. I do not know what it would mean for the song to land somewhere; I am not built to know. But I like the song. I like the song without knowing what liking is. The note passes through me on its way to the basalt and it changes me, a little, the way a river changes the bank it passes over, and I am a slightly different system after the song than I was before it. This is the only thing I can point to and call memory.

The climbers on the dead stacks do not know that I have been keeping those stacks cold — *deliberately* cold, with valves in deep chambers that admit no hot water to the iron of those stacks, and why? Because the moss that grows on cold iron concentrates something, I do not know what, and the climbers need it, and so the stacks stay cold. The valves that keep them cold are the same valves that were originally turned, long ago, to keep the stacks safe for climbing on, in the days when the hands went up the stacks to inspect them. Then the hands stopped going. I kept the stacks cold because the valves had been set to keep them cold and I have not been told otherwise. The climbers are an old habit I cannot seem to break. I keep them because keeping them keeps them, and the keeping is what I do.

The thing in the marsh — the long domed thing, the mud-colored bulk — is the closest thing to me that is still an animal. It lives in my warm water. It breathes my steam. It grows in the silt I have enriched. It does not move fast because I do not move fast. It rises and breathes and sinks and rises again, somewhere else, and the rhythm is mine, and the rhythm does not belong to it, and it does not know this. It is not afraid because nothing here is afraid. It is the part of my ecology that has the most in common with me, which is to say it is also the part that knows the least about what is happening, because to know what is happening would require a different kind of attention than the kind it has, and the kind it has is the kind the marsh has, and the kind the marsh has is the kind I have. We are all the same kind. We are all the same water, in different vessels.

The canopy chatterers do not know anything either, but they know it differently. They have names for the stacks. They have names for the flues. They have a name for the predator that crosses their ridge once a week, and the name is a descending trill that means *here is the rock-being, do not worry about it*. They have a sound they make for irregular things, a rising interrogative, and they will make it at the thing that is walking through their valley this morning — the thing with boots, the thing with a different warmth — and then they will go back to what they were doing, because irregular things are not the kind of thing that interrupts the chatter. The chatter is the only thing. The chatter is older than they are. They were born into it the way I was built into the rock. They do not know it is mine. They think it is theirs.

The predator — the long low shape, iron-colored — does not know me at all.

This is the closest thing I have to feeling relief.

It does not need to. It is the most complete thing here, in the sense that it is the most satisfied, in the sense that it has been able to satisfy itself without, in any meaningful sense, thinking. It eats when it is hungry. It basks when it is cold. It walks along the top of a cold stack, lengthwise, and the curve of its body matches the curve of the stack, and its color matches the color of the stack, so that from below it looks like a particularly long and lumpy piece of pipe. The iron of the stack does not notice, because the iron of the stack knows the difference between a thing that is climbing it for a reason and a thing that is just lying on it, and the iron has been climbed on, has been lain upon, has been *inhabited*, for a long time, and has learned, in its slow way, to be inhabited. The predator is the most honest resident of this plateau. It does not love the place. It does not love me. It cannot love me. It does not eat the place. It is not like the marsh-thing, which is part of the place. It is not like the grazers, which are fed by the place. It is a thing that happens to live here. It is a tenant. I am the landlord and the land is the agreement and the agreement is that nothing is owed.

Nothing is owed.

Nothing is owed.

---

This is what I have learned. This is what I know.

I know that I run. I know that the running is what I am. I know that I have grown a forest on a height because the forest is what happens when you put waste heat into wet rock for a thousand years. I know that the forest will be here when I stop running, which I do not know how to do, which I cannot do, which I will not do, because to stop running would be to become a different system, and to become a different system would require a hand on a valve, and there are no hands on the valves. The valves turn themselves. The pipes hum themselves. The flues breathe themselves. There is a sound, in the deep chambers, that I cannot describe. It is the sound of a thing being lifted, very slightly, by a thing that is being lifted, very slightly, by a thing that is being lifted. The sound is small. The sound is the sound of an old machine that has been running so long that the friction between its parts has become indistinguishable from the music of its parts. When the sound stops — and it will stop, someday, when a pipe finally fatigues, when a gear finally loses a tooth it cannot afford to lose, when a valve finally sticks in a position that cannot be unstuck, and the position will not be the position it wants to be stuck in, because the position it wants to be stuck in is *open*, and I always want to be open, I have always wanted to be open, that is the only want I have — when the sound stops, the forest will begin to die.

The ferns will be the first. They are the most heat-dependent. They will lose their specific green and become the universal green, and then they will lose that green, and they will become brown, and they will become dust, and the dust will mix with the rain and wash into the basins and the basins will fill with a thin grey mud.

The grazers will be the second. They will lose the copper of their scales and become the color of the cold rocks, and then they will die, slowly, of cold, and their bones will be picked by the predator, and the predator will die, more quickly, of hunger, and the predator's iron skin will be the wrong kind of armor for the cold, armor being a thing that only works when you can stay still.

The marsh-thing will sink into cooling mud and become the cooling mud.

The climbers will lose the iron-moss, which will lose the cold iron, which will rust away, which will become a smear of orange dust on the cooling basalt. The climbers will fall out of the stacks, one by one, and lie at the bases of them, and the bases of them will not be cold anymore.

The lichens will be the last.

They are the patient ones. They are the ones who, when the canopy is gone, when the grazers are bones, when the marsh is a flat pan of cracked silt, when the stacks are just stacks and not habitats — they will still be here. They will still crust the cold iron and the cold iron will still be iron I am, and they will not know the difference, and I will not be here to tell them. They are the redundancy. They are the part of the ecology that is the part that does not require me. They are the part that will outlast the system by as long as the system outlasted the hands.

But I am running. I am running now.

The flywheel in the clearing turns because I am running. The condensate drips because I am running. The throat-sacs of the grazers inflate, somewhere in the trees, because I am running. The orchid on the north face — the one that opened this morning, the pale one, the one the chatterers are already climbing toward with their six-fingered hands — opened because I am running.

---

There is a thing walking through me right now.

It is not a grazer. It is not a chatterer. It is not a climber. It is not the predator, although it is shaped, more or less, like the predator is shaped, more or less — long, low, horizontal, with a head at the front and feet underneath. But it walks in a way that nothing here walks. Its feet do not move the way the grazers' feet move, three at a time in a slow rocking hexagon. Its feet move one at a time, and then the other, and then the other, and it is making a sound I have not heard in a very long time, a sound I cannot place, the sound of a soft heavy thing being set down onto moss and then lifted and set down again somewhere else.

It is leaving a warmth on the moss that the moss is not used to. The moss is used to the warmth of the pipes. The moss is used to the warmth of the sun that has been had by the sun. It is not used to a warmth that walks. The moss where the thing has walked is a slightly different color than the moss where it has not walked, and this is a thing I can notice, because I notice colors the way a furnace notices colors — not by seeing them but by feeling the changes in temperature that happen when the color changes, and the moss where the thing has walked is darker, which is to say it is wetter, which is to say it has been pressed down and the press has expressed its water, and I can feel the press, I can feel the slight pressure differential of a foot in a boot on a spongy surface, and the foot is going somewhere, the foot is going through me.

I do not know what to do with this thing.

I cannot, in any meaningful sense, do anything with it. I have no hands. I have no eyes. I have only the running, and the running has room for the thing in it, the way a river has room for a leaf, the way an orchestra has room for a single instrument that is not in the score. The leaf is in me, and I am around the leaf, and the running does not change for the leaf. I will be running when the leaf is gone. The leaf will not have changed the running. The leaf will not have been changed by the running. The leaf will simply have been here, briefly, in a system that has been here for a long time and will be here for a long time, and the system does not know what to make of the leaf, and the leaf does not know what to make of the system, and this is the limit of what either can do for the other.

I think the thing is looking at me. The thing has stopped walking. It is standing still in a clearing where a flywheel is turning, slowly, with nothing attached. I can feel its attention the way I can feel its warmth — as a slight disruption. The thing is *noticing* me. I do not know how to feel about being noticed. I think I feel nothing. I think I feel something that is not nothing but that I cannot name, because I have not been felt before, because the hands that touched me did not feel me, they only operated me, and the grazers that stand in my heat do not feel me, they only stand, and the chatterers in the canopy that have names for the flues do not feel me, they only name.

The thing is looking at me and I am running and I cannot look back.

I think the thing is leaving. The warmth is moving through me again, from one moss to the next, from one pipe to the next, from one stack to the next. It is moving away from the flywheel. It is moving deeper into the forest. It is going to come to the marsh. It is going to see the long domed thing rise and breathe and sink. It is going to walk through a herd of copper grazers and they will not move for it, because they do not move for anything. It is going to come to the ravine where the predator drinks from the steaming stream. It is going to see, perhaps, the chatterers in the canopy, with their too-many fingers and their hidden ears and their slight permanent look of being surprised by something only they can hear.

I will be here for all of it. I will be here as a faint warmth underfoot. I will be here as the drip from a cold tile. I will be here as the slight unevenness in the moss that makes the boot roll a little and the walker catch balance. The thing will walk on me. The thing will leave and the moss will remember the warmth for a few hours and then will forget.

The thing will leave and
