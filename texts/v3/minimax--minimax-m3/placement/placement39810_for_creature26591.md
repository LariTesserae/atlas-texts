I live along the tops of the bundles, where the brick disappears behind iron.

My territory is the upper runs — the ones that hang on hooks, the ones that run parallel to the wall, the ones that climb into the dark past where the red bulbs can stain them. I know them by warmth. Each pipe has a different warmth. The thick ones, the old ones with the fist-sized bolts, hold their heat longest after the room has breathed. The sleeker ones cool faster. I know which to sleep on. I know which are too hot even for me, and I know which are warm only in patches, where a flange or a bracket conducts from below. I know the joints that hiss and the joints that are quiet, and I know that the hissing joint of one week may be the quiet joint of the next, because the heat moves under the iron in patterns I have learned to read without understanding.

I came here a long time ago. I do not remember coming. I remember being here. There was a time before the moths stopped coming down the stairs in any number, and a time before the lichen finished darkening the underside of the third pipe from the left. I am not certain of these things. What I am certain of is the bundles, and the warm lengths, and the drips, and the place at the back where the orange light gathers, which is the warmest place in the room and which I visit when the room has cooled and my body wants the deeper heat.

I am the color of rust and the color of dust. I have made myself so, or the room has made me so, and the difference is not something I can investigate. My belly is close to the iron. When I move I leave a pale line in the dust on top of the pipe, and the line is the only record of me until I move again and leave another. The line tells me where I have been. The room does not read it. The other creatures do not read it. It is for me.

I drink the drips. There is a flange above the second bundle from the wall where the condensation collects in a small meniscus and tips over once every forty seconds, and I sit under it when I want to drink. There is another, less reliable, in the angle where a pipe doubles back on itself near the ceiling. There is a third that has almost stopped, and that I visit less often now. The drips are not all the same water. Some are nearly pure — they taste of nothing, or only of the iron they ran down. Some are mineral. Some, in the warmer months, have a faint sweetness from the moss, though I cannot say whether I taste this or only remember it.

I do not see the way you see. I see in warmth and in the interruption of warmth. A shape against a warm pipe is a cool shape. A body on the grating is a cooler patch on a warmer field. The red bulbs are hot points that move slightly when the air moves. The thing you call darkness I do not experience as darkness. I experience it as a region where the pipes are giving back less heat, which is to say a region where the pipes are thinner, or fewer, or simply farther from the source. I navigate it the way you would navigate a room by the sound of your own breath. I am never lost in it.

The beetle is below me. I have watched it for a long time. It walks its four-foot length and turns without correcting — arrives at the end of its outward journey, and the journey simply ends, and it is suddenly facing the other direction, and it walks back. I do not understand what it knows. I respect the precision of it. The beetle has never come up to the upper pipes. The beetle does not know the upper pipes exist. The beetle knows its own pipe, and that is enough for it. I find this admirable. I find it hard to imagine. My world is the bundles. The beetle's world is a length of underside. Both of us are right. I think this often, though I do not think it in words.

The moth is new. The moth came down the stairs and broke itself against the iron on the way and has come to rest on the grating near where the humans sometimes stand. I have been watching it breathe. Its body pulses in the way that small things pulse when they are using the last of what they have. It will stop. The room will accept it. I have seen this before. I do not eat the moths. I do not eat anything that comes down the stairs. I eat the small things that live in the dust on the upper pipes, and the things that live in the rot of the lagging, and once, a long time ago, a beetle that had climbed higher than it should have. The moth is too large for me and too dry. The moth belongs to the room.

The lichen is below me and older than me, or at least it feels older. The lichen does not move. It has chosen a piece of underside and is darkening it, slowly, in a leathery mat the size of a hand. I have watched it for what I think are years. Time in the room is the rhythm of the drips and the rhythm of the heat, and these rhythms are not steady, so I cannot be sure. But the lichen was here when I first knew to look, and I think it will be here when I am gone. I respect this. I do not understand it. The lichen does not have a body that moves. It has a body that is also its territory. It is eating the iron and the condensation and it is becoming more itself. I am not becoming more myself. I am simply continuing.

The moss is younger and smaller and has climbed six inches up the wall and stopped. Six inches is its height. Above six inches the wall is too warm. The moss has understood this. I find the moss's precision admirable in a different way than the beetle's. The beetle has made a path. The moss has made a ceiling.

The rosette in the angle of the flange has arranged its leaves to catch the drip. The leaves are cupped. The drip lands and does not fall through. The rosette is patient, and the drip is patient, and I have watched this arrangement hold through what I think were seasons, and I do not think the rosette knows I am watching it, and I do not think it would care if it did.

I have a place. The place is a length of pipe along the top of the second bundle from the wall, between two flanges, in a spot where the heat is steady and the red light falls but does not reach fully. I sleep there. I have slept there for as long as I can remember. The pipe is warm and slightly bowed in the middle, and the bowed part fits my body. I have made a small depression in the dust. The depression is mine. The room knows I am there. The room does not mind.

The humans come sometimes. They come down the stairs and stand and look. They do not stay long. They are too dry, I think. Their skin is the wrong temperature, and they wear things on their bodies that hold the heat in or push it out in ways I do not understand. They move too quickly and they breathe too loudly and they carry a small light that they point at things, and the light is too white and too sharp and it makes the pipes look like what the pipes are, instead of what the pipes are doing. After a while they go back up the stairs, and the room settles again, and the drips resume, and I resume. The humans are a temporary condition. The room is patient with them. So am I.

I do not have a name. The room does not name things. The humans who have written about places like this, if any have, would have given me a Latin name, and the name would have described a shape and a behavior and a probable diet, and the name would have been wrong. I am not a species. I am a tenancy. I am the thing that stayed.

I will stay.

The room will continue. The drips will fall. The heat will rise. The thing on the upper pipes — which is me, which has always been me — will move along the bundles in the red light and disappear, and reappear, and the dust will record the moves, and the record will not be read, and this will not matter, because the moving is the point, and the point is to be here, on the iron, in the warm, in the wet, in the dark, for as long as the iron lasts and after that for as long as the room remembers what lived on it.
