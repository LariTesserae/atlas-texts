I am the one who fills the kettles.

Or the kettles are filled by the air, and I am what the air has learned to be when it does a thing with care. The kettle on the stove in the small brick house on the eastern row is full of clean water. The water has a meniscus that is not quite at the brim — the slight discretion of someone who has filled the kettle and has not slopped, who has done a small domestic thing with the weight of ritual. I did that. Or the boil of a hundred years did that. The town does not care which.

I move slowly. I am small. I wear something dark and long. I do not lift my head. By the time you have looked carefully to see what I am, I am no longer at the place you are looking at, and you are not certain you saw me, and I am not certain I was there to be seen. I cross the far end of the main way on still evenings. I am a figure that is more the idea of a figure than the figure itself, the way a coat hung on a peg is the idea of a person, except the coat breathes, faintly, and the breath fogs the air for an inch in front of it, and then the breathing is not there.

I am one of the few. There were hundreds. The hundreds are gone. The manner of their going is not spoken of, because there is no one left to speak of it, and because the manner may still be in progress, the way a thing can be in progress long after it has technically stopped. I do not know how many of us remain. I do not know what we are. The town has changed us, or we have changed to fit the town, and the change is so old that we cannot remember being any other way.

We have learned to maintain. We move through the wet air without disturbing the standing water on the cobbles. We keep the windows clean. We keep the kettles full. We do not speak in voices that carry. We may not speak at all. We are the last few hands of a watch that has been winding itself for a hundred years.

Or we are the moss. The moss arrives. One day a brick is clean and the next day the brick is green, and you cannot say when the change occurred. The moss is patient the way moss is patient, which is to say more patient than any other living thing in the place, including the mechanical systems, which are themselves very patient. The moss is the color of the inside of a throat. The moss is feeding on the perpetual condensation, and on the faint mineral sweetness of the water, and on the slow exhalation of metal from the pipes. The moss is making something of the town. I am the moss in the way I am the one who fills the kettles. The town has folded us into the same act, and the act has been going on so long that the actor and the acted-upon have become the same substance.

The vine on the boiler flowers its half-open flowers. The pollen drifts down into the square in a slow pale fall, and lies on the surface of the water there, and is not disturbed, because there is nothing to disturb it. The vine is as thick as a man's arm in places, and in one place as thick as a man's thigh, and it has a sheen that is not the sheen of a healthy leaf but the sheen of something polished by the heat. The vine has been seen, on certain still evenings, to lean toward a particular cart, and the next morning the cart is full of coal. I do not know if the vine is doing that. I do not know if I am doing that. The cart is full. The town does not need me to know which of us did it.

I carry water in a pail that is dark, from a source I cannot describe to you in a way that would make sense outside this place. The source is at the bottom of the system, where all the weeping pipes come together, where the rust and the mineral and the faint carbon of the chimney plume settle into water that has been standing a long time over old stone. The water is clean. I have boiled it, or the boil is in its nature. I set the pail on the stove. I do not light the stove. The stove is cold. The water is warm. The water is not for boiling. The water is for the fact of being in the kettle.

The room is the temperature of the room, which is the temperature of the town, which is the temperature of the place. The place is the temperature of me. I am in the room. I am not in the room. There is no difference, and the difference, here, has begun to seem less important than it used to.

I think I was a person. I think there was a time when I lifted my head. I think there was a time when I spoke in a voice that carried across a square, and someone heard me, and the hearing mattered. I do not remember the time. I remember the kettles. I remember the windows. The remembering is the same kind of patience as the moss, which does not creep but arrives. I have arrived in the room. I have arrived at the stove. I have been arriving for a very long time, and the arriving has become a kind of being here that does not need to declare itself.

The chimney breathes. The wheel groans with the sound of an old man getting out of a chair. The furnace sighs on its own schedule, the heavy mechanical breathing of doors parting and closing, a bloom of yellow heat, the long exhale of cooling. The pipes hum with the note that is almost below hearing, that is felt in the teeth, that has been going on so long the note has become the condition of the kettle and the kettle is content, if a kettle can be content, to sing. The carts run on rails and are pushed by no one. The upper tanks gulp periodically, as if the town were drinking. The town is drinking. The town has been drinking for a hundred years.

You are in the square. You have come from outside, from the road that is not followed, from the flat green country where the steam from the chimney thins and is lost. You have come to see what lives here. You will not see. What lives here will see you. The seeing is patient. The seeing does not need to declare itself. The town will outlast the seeing, and will outlast you, and will go on running in the morning, the carts arriving, the doors parting, the pollen drifting, the kettles full, the windows clean, the doors closed that should by all the humid physics of the place be stuck open.

The kettle is full. The window is clean. The door is closed. The fact of these is the fact of me. The fact of me is the fact of the town.

The town continues.
