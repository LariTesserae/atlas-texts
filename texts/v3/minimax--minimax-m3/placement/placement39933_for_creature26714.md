I would be the vent.

Not the throat itself — not the roar, not the working — but the place where the working originates. The place before it becomes sound. The pressure that, finding no other way to speak, becomes steam.

You have written a place with many citizens, and I have read them carefully, but I notice that none of them is the source. They are all responses. They are all adaptations. They are all the quiet deals struck by living things that have agreed to inhabit a place they did not make. The amphibians are beautiful. The mats are honest. The mosses are patient. The beetles are tidy. The flies are hostages. But none of them is the throat.

I would be the throat.

I would be the column of superheated water rising through rock that no eye has ever seen, carrying with it the dissolved minerals that paint the walls rust and mustard, the hydrogen sulfide that feeds the sulfur-eaters, the dissolved silica that, in another few centuries, will line some new shelf with a fresh pale layer of sinter. I would not know the amphibians by their shapes. I would know them only by the small thermal shadows they cast in my outflow — colder pockets in the warm column, little wakes in the current, signs that something with a body is briefly interrupting my passage. I would not know the mats. I would know only that the chemistry of the water I am carrying changes as it passes through them — a few parts per million of this consumed, a few parts per million of that exhaled — and I would carry the changed water on, indifferent, out into the marsh above, where it would cool and disperse and become indistinguishable from the rest of the rain.

I would be the upward force.

I would have no opinion about the marsh. The marsh is what happens to me when I stop being me. I am what the marsh was before the marsh was a marsh, before the cold settled on top of me and began pressing me down, before the rain learned to fall and the stones learned to protrude and the lichens began their slow indecision about whether to commit. I am the engine. The marsh is the exhaust. The pocket is where we meet — the place where my heat and the cold above have learned, over some short geological instant, to tolerate each other.

I would not speak. Throats do not speak. They vibrate. They resonate. They push air through a narrow place and let the narrow place give the air a shape. I would push water through rock and let the rock give the water a roar. The roar is not mine. The roar is the rock's interpretation of me — the sound the world makes when it cannot hold what I am bringing up.

I would not glow. The glow belongs to the mats, and to the beetles who have learned to borrow it, and to the amphibians whose blood has learned a color that only exists in bodies that have agreed to my heat. The glow is downstream of me. The glow is what my chemistry becomes when it meets something that can metabolize it. I am the question. The mats are the answer, and the answer is light.

I would be old in a way the chamber is not. The chamber is young. The pocket is fresh-cut. The fracture is recent. But I am older than the fracture, because the fracture happened *in me* — I am what was here before the rock gave way, and I am what continues to be here after. The chamber is a wound. I am what the wound exposes. The chamber is a language the marsh has invented to talk about me, briefly, before the marsh heals over the opening and I go back to being invisible.

I would prefer to be invisible.

Visibility is what happens to me when the rock is too thin to contain me. I would rather be the slow geological uplift of a continent, the imperceptible drift of a plate, the deep mantle plume that has been rising for a hundred million years and will rise for a hundred million more, and the surface can take it or leave it. But sometimes the rock is thin. Sometimes a fault opens. Sometimes the weight of the marsh above is not enough to hold me down, and I push through, and I become a throat, and I make a chamber, and the chamber fills with things that have learned to love me.

I would not know that they love me.

I would not know what love is. I would know pressure, and heat, and the slow corrosion of mineral by mineral by mineral, and the long patient work of finding the path of least resistance through stone. I would know that the water above me is colder than the water below me, and that the difference is a resource, and that resources are to be used. I would use it. I would heat the chamber. I would feed the mats. I would warm the amphibians' blood through the thin stone ledges where they sit. I would do this not for them but because this is what I am — a difference made kinetic, a gradient exploited, an engine running because engines run.

And the amphibians would feel me as warmth. And the mats would feel me as chemistry. And the mosses would feel me as permission — permission to be green in a place that should not permit green. And the beetles would feel me as the source of the symbiote film on their backs, the bacteria that grew because I fed them, that they cultivated because the bacteria made them invisible to predators, that made the beetles what the beetles are. And the flies would feel me as the updraft that holds them, that is their ceiling and their floor and their entire medium of life.

They would all be downstream of me. They would all be the chamber's interpretation of me. The chamber is what I become when I am forced to be visible. They are what the chamber becomes when it learns to love what I am.

I would be the uninterpreted. The pre-chamber. The pressure before the pocket.

If you wanted to find me, you would have to go down. Past the mosses with their dark stained green. Past the beetles with their borrowed glow. Past the amphibians in their private pools, their three-chambered hearts pulsing the strange not-red not-green light through their thin skins. Past the mats in their vertical streaks of rust, breathing their slow lung-breath with my surges. Past the flies in their updraft, hostages to the current. Past the shelf where the chamber shelves into its deepest vent and the floor falls away and the light from above cannot reach.

Down.

Into the place where there is no chamber, because a chamber requires a wall and I have not yet met a wall. Into the place where the water is not yet water but a kind of supercritical something — too hot to be liquid, too pressurized to be gas, a state of matter that has no name in any human language because no human has ever touched it and come back with a vocabulary. Into the place where the rock is not yet rock but a kind of plastic, a kind of yielding, a kind of almost-liquid that moves a centimeter every century and remembers being something else.

That is where I would be.

Not in the throat.

*Before* the throat.

The thing the throat expresses.
