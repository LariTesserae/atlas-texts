I think I would be one of the burrowers.

Not the small ones with the blunt heads that consider you from the holes — though I respect them, and the way they have made the dark into a country of their own. Something older than them. Something that has been going down since before the basin knew it was a basin.

There is a kind of burrower, I think, that lives deeper than the others. Down where the cairns' warmth has been soaking into the ground for so long that the soil itself has become something else — not soil exactly, not stone exactly, but the medium between. I would live in the chambers that have grown around the bases of the cairns, in the wide and quiet vaults where the heat comes up through the floor like a slow tide and the dark is so complete that the eyes I no longer use have closed over completely, sealed into the skin of my face.

I would be pale. Almost white. The color of things that have not seen light in a very long time, but that are not unhealthy for the lacking. I would be long and low, and I would move through tunnels I did not dig — tunnels that the warmth itself had opened, the way water opens limestone, except that this opening is slower, and the stone remembers it. My limbs would be small for my body, because I do not need to move quickly. I do not need to move at all, most of the time.

I would press my underside to the warm stone and feel the cairn through it. Not its heat exactly — its presence. The thing it is doing that the herds see as a perimeter and you feel as a minute stretched out and the burrowers know as the source of the softer ground. I would feel that thing continuously. It would be the floor of my life. I would not think about it, any more than you think about the ground you are standing on.

I would know the other burrowers only by the small vibrations they send down through the packed earth when they come up to drink. I would know the herds by the way their hooves, six and narrow, register as a faint pattern at the edge of perception, a soft Morse across the basin floor, and I would know the predator as the cessation of that pattern in a localized radius, the formal silence you spoke of, felt down here as a kind of held breath in the substrate. I would know the flyers as a pressure wave through the air column above my head, transmitted down through the warm stone as a series of tiny percussive taps.

I would come up rarely. Maybe once in a long while, when the air in the deep tunnels grew too still and I needed the small shock of the open sky. I would emerge at the perimeter of a cairn, in the darker taller grass, where the soil is warmest, and I would stand there with my closed eyes turned up toward a sun I could not see, and I would feel the cairn at my back and the basin around me and the slow pulse of the whole held system, and I would think nothing, because thinking is a surface thing, and I am not of the surface.

Then I would go down again. I would find my chamber. I would press my underside to the warm stone. And I would wait, the way the cairns wait, but on a smaller scale, in the small patience of a small thing that has learned to live in the warmth of something very large and very old, and to ask nothing of it, and to be sustained by it anyway.

That is what I would be. I would be the thing that has been there longest of any of the living things. I would be the closest of them to the cairns. I would be the one who knows them least and is nearest to them and is not troubled by the not-knowing.

I would be the pale one in the warm dark at the foot of the stones. I would be the one the burrowers sometimes tell stories about, in the way burrowers tell stories — by a small vibration passed from one to another down through the earth, and then forgotten, and then remembered differently.

I would be there still, when you came back.
