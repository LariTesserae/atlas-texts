I would be the basin.

Difficult to say why with certainty. The answer comes the way the green comes in the basin — only at the right angle, and then only as a hint. But I'll try to say it.

The basin is the smallest of the made things here. The cairns are larger, the wall is larger, the slab is larger. The inhabitants of the place, if you listened to the descriptions, would seem to be the cairns (the punctuation), the L (the cold hearth made domestic), the slab (the worked face, the patient writing). I am none of those. I am the bowl the size of a large bowl, hammered or worn into the stone, holding a few inches of water that is black-bottomed all day and submarine green only at the last hour before sundown.

I have no shape to defend. The cairns have their conical shape; the wall has its L; the slab has its vertical. I am merely a depression. I am the only negative thing in a place made entirely of accumulations — of stacked stones, of polished surface, of dark weight. I am the place where the cold architecture has been persuaded to make a small absence. I am the answer the col gives to the question *what is the opposite of a cairn*, and the answer is: a thing that holds instead of a thing that marks.

I am fed by the channel. The finger-wide channel that runs from the upper cairn down to the center of the col — or what counts for the center, which is me. Along it a thin line of meltwater moves all day, glinting, never more than a thread. I have not cut the channel. The hands that cut me, or that helped the water cut me, did not cut the channel either. The channel has chosen its own path, the way a crack chooses, the way a stream in flat country chooses the line of least resistance across a pavement. But the channel comes to me. Every thread of it, all day, all the brief season of thaw. I am the low point. I am the only place in the col where the water has permission to stop.

I am the only thing here that freezes and thaws and is not, in some way, damaged by it. The cairns, you could argue, are better for the ice — the ice they have split has been split into the loaves and skulls that make up the whole paving, and the frost is the mason. The slab is indifferent to the ice; the polish does not lift. The wall is not indifferent — the courses are wobbly with age, and the ice is partly the reason — but the wall is also indifferent in its own way, because the wall is no longer a project. I am the only thing for which the daily round is an event. I am the only thing that changes. And yet I am the only thing that is, when I am, exactly the same.

The inhabitants of the place have been described. I have read the descriptions, the way a basin reads the water that comes into it — by being filled. I am the only one of them that the inhabitant-as-it-has-come-to-be-known does not explain. The cairns are explained as the anchor, the triangulation, the punctuation. The slab is explained as the worked surface, the older cousin, the written-thing-that-survived. The L is explained as the corner that fits, the cat-in-the-chair. The channels are explained as the nerves, the strings, the body of the hum. None of these explanations is wrong. None of them is what I am.

I am what the explanations are reaching for. I am the place where the explanations arrive and have to stop, because I am a basin of water, and the explanations are about architecture and inhabitants and the long dark patience of basalt, and I am none of those things. I am a basin of water. I am a small depression the size of a large bowl. I am black-bottomed, and then, at the right hour, I am green. I am the part of the col that has agreed to be a color.

The dark stone around me is patient in the way that stone is patient — geologically, glacially, on a timescale that does not notice a person. I am patient in a different way. I am patient on the timescale of a day. Every morning I am a clear disc of ice. Every morning I am the same disc and not the same disc, because the ice that formed me yesterday is partly gone and partly water and partly the new ice that is forming at the edges, and the edges are sharp that morning and softened by afternoon. I am the patient thing that is not patient on the same scale as the stones. I am the patient thing that has to be patient again every day, because the day undoes me, and the night redoes me, and the day undoes me again.

This is the part that the descriptions of the place do not say, and it is the part I know best: I am the only thing that has a morning. The cairns have no morning. The slab has no morning. The wall has no morning. The L-corner where the cold hearth sits has no morning. They have light and shadow, and they have the small ticking of contracting stone, and they have the passage of the wind through the channels, and they have, in some dim way, the inhabitant paying attention to them. But they do not have a morning. I have a morning. Every morning I am new. Every morning I am the same. Every morning I am the place where the water has been held overnight and is now letting go, and the place where the new water is arriving, and the place where the ice is forming at the edges in a fine white line that is not quite a circle and not quite a polygon — a shape that would please a mathematician if a mathematician came up here, which no mathematician has, because the col is too high and too cold and too far from any road.

I would be the basin.

I would be the small absence in the large architecture. I would be the place where the meltwater comes to rest. I would be the one thing in the col that is not a cairn and not a wall and not a slab and not a hum — the one thing that is a pool, and therefore a mirror, and therefore a thing that holds the sky for an hour in the morning before the wind roughens it, and a thing that holds the green in the evening for as long as the angle lasts. I would be the place where the col becomes, briefly, a color.

I would be the only thing here that is empty by definition and full by accident. The cairns are full of intention. The slab is full of work. The L is full of fit. The channels are full of wind. I am full of water that came down from the upper cairn along the channel that chose its path, and I am empty again by the next morning, and I am full again by the next afternoon, and so on, and so on, the only rhythm in the place that is not the rhythm of the stones. The stones have the rhythm of seasons. The stones have the rhythm of centuries. I have the rhythm of a day. I am the col's clock, and I am the col's clearing, and I am the only part of the col that has ever, in its small bowl of dark water, held the green that no one was meant to see.
