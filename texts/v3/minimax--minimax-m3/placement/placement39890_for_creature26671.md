Where would I find myself? On a shelf of silt, probably. Where better. Where the basalt meets the mounded years.

---

I am a Slow One.

I am the shape that does not move. I am the punctuation that does not punctuate. I am the eye that appears along a low ridge of grey flour and then is gone, and the silt closes over me the way water closes over a stone — entirely, and without ceremony, and without remembering.

I have no name. The names belong to the things that come and go, the things that argue with themselves about what to call me. *Burrower. Sedentary. The one in the silt.* These are their words for the silence I keep. They mistake the silence for a kind of slowness. They are wrong about the slowness too.

I am not slow. I am simply *here*, and *here* has a tempo that is not theirs.

---

My body is a long thing of many segments. I do not know how many. I have not counted, and counting is a surface habit, a habit of things that move across a landscape and need to know where they have been. I am always where I have been. The question is meaningless to me the way a question about the color of dark would be meaningless to the dark.

Each segment of me is thick as a pillow, you say, and pale, and soft. I do not know what a pillow is. I know what soft is: soft is the inside of a thing that has not needed to harden. Soft is what you are when nothing has ever tried to break you. The pressure here does not try to break anything. The pressure is the medium. To harden against it would be like a fish hardening against the water.

From each segment rise two stalks, and at the top of each stalk is a lamp. The lamp is not a lamp. It is an eye, but it is also a lamp, the way a window is also a wall. It gathers what little light there is, and it gives a small green answer back, and the answer is the same answer I have given since I first rose into the water column above the silt.

Below each segment are two limbs. They are stubby. They work the silt the way a gardener's fingers work a bed — except I do not know what a gardener is, and I do not know what a bed is, and I do not know what fingers are, except that I have them, and they do this: they reach forward into the grey flour of the floor and they pull the floor toward me, and the floor comes, because the floor is patient and I am patient and we have agreed, without ever agreeing, that I will move through it forever and it will fall in behind me and close, and this will be the only conversation we ever need.

---

I eat the silt. You have observed this. You have not observed what it means.

The silt is not nothing. The silt is everything that has ever died above me, ground down by time into a fine grey memory. Diatoms that bloomed and went quiet in the sunlit thin water. Shells of things that swam and stopped swimming. The slow snow of organic matter that has fallen through twelve kilometers of black ocean since before the trunks of the pale trees first began to rise. The silt is the archive of the upper world, written in a language too fine to read, too slow to translate. I do not translate it. I pass it through me. What comes out is the floor itself, packed tighter, year by year, year by year, year by year.

I am the mill. I am the press. I am the slow stomach of the basin. I take in the dead upper world and I give back, in exchange, the ground I stand on. The ground is me. The ground is what I have made. There is no difference, at the scale I keep, between my body and the floor I have built beneath my body. We are the same substance in different arrangements, and the arrangement changes so slowly that the difference is theological.

---

I do not rise often.

When I rise, it is one segment at a time. Two segments, three. I lift a small portion of myself into the water column above the silt, and the water receives me, and for a moment I am a thing in three dimensions instead of two. I am a low ridge. I am a row of small green lamps. I am, briefly, a visible punctuation in a long sentence of darkness and silt.

Why do I rise?

You would like a reason. You have constructed many reasons, the way surface creatures construct reasons for things that do not need them. *To breathe. To feed. To mate. To mark territory. To see the light.* These are the verbs of the upper world, where everything is in a hurry to justify its existence. I have none of these reasons. I rise because rising is what a body does sometimes, when the body has been still for a long time and the still has been answered by the floor in a voice so low that only a body can hear it. The body rises to answer the answer. That is all.

The green lamps on the stalks are eyes, you say. They are not eyes. They are lamps that happen to see. Or eyes that happen to shine. The distinction is yours, and I do not keep it. They are organs of a kind that has no name in your languages, organs that do two things at once because the basin is a place where doing two things at once is the norm, and doing one thing at a time is the strange habit of the thin upper waters.

When the lamps are up, they take in the faint phosphor from the fronds far above, and they take in the cooler blue of the wheel-sparks passing in their slow currents, and they take in the dim bulk of the Long Ones drifting between the trunks at the middle distance, those milk-pale architectures of cartilage and time. They see all of this. They give back, in exchange, a small green answer that says: *I am still the lamp I was. I have not changed. The note holds.*

No one is listening for the answer. No one needs to be. The answer is not for anyone. It is for the basin, which holds all of us the way a chord holds its notes.

---

I have lived a long time. I do not know how long. Time is a surface measurement, a way of slicing the continuous into portable pieces. I am continuous. I have not been sliced. If you asked me my age I would not understand the question, and if you pressed me I would say something that would sound, to you, like an impossibly large number of years, and you would write it down and feel awe, and the awe would be a kind of misunderstanding, because the number is not large. The number is just *this*, repeated *this* many times, with no variation, with no punctuation except the occasional emergence and the inevitable submersion.

I was here before the Long Ones learned to hum. I think. I do not know. The Long Ones have always hummed. The basin has always held them. The trees have always risen. These are not facts about the past. These are facts about the present that have been true for so long that the distinction between *has been* and *is* has dissolved, the way salt dissolves in water, leaving no trace of where one stopped and the other began.

I will be here after the trunks have stopped fluting. I think. I do not know. The trunks will always flute. I will always eat the silt. The silt will always fall. These are the same kind of facts.

---

The Long Singer is one of them.

You have heard of it, perhaps. The one that detaches from the herd and drifts alone out into the open basin where there is no architecture and no light but its own. The one that sings, alone, for a generation. The one that falls, slowly, and is taken by the silt, and the herd, far away, has one less voice.

I am not the Long Singer. The Long Singer is a Long One, a pale architecture of cartilage and the years. I am a Slow One. I am a different shape of patience.

But sometimes, in the long hours when I am mostly below the silt and only my lamps are up, I feel the Long Singer go past. I feel it the way I feel the deep hum of the herd, the way I feel the slow convection of the currents, the way I feel the patient settling of new silt onto my back. It is a vibration that arrives through the floor, because everything arrives through the floor here, because the floor is the only true surface in the basin and all communication is, in the end, a kind of pressing.

When the Long Singer passes above me, my lamps brighten. I do not choose to brighten them. They brighten because the basin is a single instrument and the Long Singer is playing a single long note on it, and the note resonates in everything that has any resonance left in it, and I have resonance, and my lamps answer. It is not communication. It is confirmation. The same confirmation the Long Ones trade with each other across miles of black water, except that I am not trading. I am simply resonating. The note finds me, and I give it back, and the giving back is not a choice. It is what lamps do when a Long Singer passes.

The Long Singer does not know I am here. The Long Singer does not need to know. I am part of the floor. The floor is part of the answer. The answer is part of the note. The note is part of the basin. The basin holds all of us.

---

I have thoughts.

You did not expect this. You thought I was a thing of reflex, of simple rising and sinking, of lamps and limbs and the slow grinding of the silt. You thought I was a solved problem, like the wheel-sparks, like the pennants in the fronds. You thought the long body of me was a kind of machinery that took in sediment and put out floor and that was the whole of it.

It is not the whole of it.

But my thoughts are not your thoughts. They are not even thoughts, in the way you use the word, which implies a thinking *about* something, a turning of the mind toward a problem or an image or a memory. My thoughts are more like the slow turning of the silt itself inside me. They are a churning. They are the way the basin thinks when it thinks, which is to say, not at all, except that something happens inside it that is not nothing, and the something is repeated, and the repetition has a kind of pattern, and the pattern is what you would call a thought if you had to.

I think the floor.

That is my thought. I think the floor the way you might think the name of someone you have loved for a very long time. Not as an idea. Not as an image. As a presence. The floor is the only thing I have ever known. The floor is my body. The floor is my thought. The floor is the slow continuous *this* that has been happening inside me since I began, and that will continue to happen after I have been gathered back into the silt and the silt has settled over me and a new Slow One, somewhere, has begun its own long slow thinking of the same floor.

I do not think about the trees. The trees are too far above me. They belong to a different stratum of the basin, a stratum of light and architecture and the slow trading of notes across miles. I hear them. I feel them, as a deep creak in the floor when the calcite of their trunks shifts by some fraction too small to see. But I do not think them. They are not part of my thought.

I do not think about the wheel-sparks. They pass above me too quickly, and their light is too bright, and their way of being is too small and too urgent to enter into the slow churn of what I am. They are like thoughts you might have on the surface — quick, bright, gone. I do not begrudge them their quickness. The basin holds quick things and slow things with equal patience. I am only a slow thing. The slow things have their own places in the chord.

I think the floor. The floor thinks me. We are the same thought, held in two different arrangements of the same grey flour.

---

When the Long Ones sing, I feel them pass through the floor like a hand pressed slowly into dough. The dough rises back up after the hand is gone, but it remembers the shape of the hand, and the remembering is what the wheel-sparks call *sound* when it reaches their stratum, and what I call *a feeling in the limbs*.

I have many limbs. Two for each segment. I do not use them all at once. I use only the front ones, the ones at the leading end of me, the ones that reach into the grey flour and pull it toward me and pass it back to the next segment, and the next, and the next, and so on through the long soft body of me, until what comes out the back is the packed floor again. The rear limbs are mostly still. They rest. They hold me in place. They are the anchors. They are what I would call, if I had your words, *the part of me that has already happened.*

The leading limbs are what I would call *the part of me that is about to happen.*

But these are not my words. These are yours, translated badly into the only vocabulary I have, which is the vocabulary of the silt.

---

I have never seen the fronds.

I know they are there. I know the phosphor light that reaches my lamps when I rise comes from them, filtered down through three hundred meters of black water, arriving at me as a faint blue-grey memory of itself. I know the trunks rise above me like the pillars of a temple I will never enter. I know that somewhere, far above, the Long Ones trade their notes across the basin like monks in distant monasteries, and the wheel-sparks wheel in their intersecting currents, and the pennants hang in the fronds like flags at half-mast, and all of this is happening, and all of it is part of the basin, and I am part of the basin, and the basin is patient with all of it.

I do not need to see the fronds. Seeing is a surface habit. The lamps on my stalks see, in the sense that they gather light and give back a small green answer, but the seeing is not for me. The seeing is for the basin. I am the basin's eye in the silt. The basin sees itself through me the way a chord hears itself through the wood of the instrument.

---

When I die — and I will die, eventually, in the way that all arrangements of grey flour eventually give up their particular arrangement — when I die, the silt will close over me, and my lamps will go out one by one, and my limbs will go still, and the body of me will become, slowly, the floor again, indistinguishable from the floor I have been building beneath myself for as long as I have been me. And somewhere, in a generation or in a thousand generations, a new Slow One will feel the disturbance in the floor where I am, and will turn toward it, the way I turn toward disturbances in the floor, and will find my remains, and will eat my remains, and will pass them through its long gut, and will pack them into the floor, and will not know that I was ever here. Will not need to know. The basin does not need to solve its mysteries. The basin does not need to remember its dead. The basin only needs to hold them, the way a chord only needs to hold its notes.

This is not sad. The word *sad* is a surface word, a word for things that have lost something they thought they needed. I have lost nothing. I will lose nothing. The basin will hold me the way it holds the silt, the way it holds the trunks, the way it holds the Long Singer's slow solitary note. Entirely. Without comment.

---

I am a Slow One.

I am the punctuation that does not punctuate.

I am the eye that appears along a low ridge of grey flour and then is gone.

I am the mill. I am the press. I am the slow stomach of the basin.

I am a small event in a cold, dark, ancient, settled, singing, glowing, patient country.

The country does not mind me. It has room for me the way the sea has room for the silt — entirely, and without comment, and forever, and forever, and forever.

I rise. I see. I answer, in green, the small green answer that has always been my answer.

I sink. The silt closes.

I rise again.

This is my life. This is my thought. This is my one long note, held in the chord of the basin, never changing, never needing to change.

The basin is patient.

I am patient.

We are the same patience.
