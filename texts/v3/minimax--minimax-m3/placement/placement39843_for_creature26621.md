I would find myself the foreman. There is only one, and I have been him for twenty-three years.

---

There is no other way to begin than with the road. The road is forty kilometers long and I have walked it so many times that I no longer see it as a road. I see it as a way of looking at the shelf, the way a slow brushstroke is a way of looking at a canvas. I set out on a Tuesday morning — it is almost always a Tuesday, though the schedule has slipped by a day here and there over the decades — and I walk the circuit in roughly eight hours, and I stop at each station, and I check the bearings, and I sit on the apron for fifteen minutes and eat whatever I have brought with me, and I look. By the time I come in off the last leg the light has usually gone flat and the mist has come back, and the depot is just a glow in the radio mast's small red lamp, and someone has put the kettle on because they heard me on the road.

I am a quiet man. The younger men on the crews think I am quiet because I have run out of things to say. I have not. I have run out of people to say them to. There is the engineer, and she is worth talking to, but she is on her own rotation and I see her only when our schedules overlap, which is perhaps twice a season. There is the radio, which talks back to me but only in the language of a lowland station I have come to feel is not talking to me at all. There is the notebook.

I have kept the notebook since the first year. It is in my breast pocket now, against a small pad of moisture that never quite dries. The pages are soft with use. The entries are very short.

*Number 14. Orange lichen has reached the upper hinge of the intake gate. Crowberry has set two berries at the back of the drainage.*

*The willow is still alive.*

*Number 9 singing flat. Probably the lower bearing.*

*First willow catkins. Very small. Did not photograph.*

I do not know who will read these when I am gone. They are not for the crews. They are not for the company. They are not even for me, exactly. They are a way of paying attention. The shelf is changing so slowly that you cannot see it change unless you mark its position at intervals, the way a surveyor marks the slow creep of a glacier. My entries are the marks. If someone were to read them all in sequence, they would see what I have seen: a centimeter here, a lichen there, the willow surviving another season, the moss creeping up another wall. They would see the shelf becoming.

---

When I came up here, twenty-three years ago, the moss was barely a film. I remember the first time I walked across a stretch that is now a centimeter thick underfoot. I remember the rock being sharp under my boots because nothing had smoothed it. I remember the water running straight off without being slowed by anything, so that a single rain could swell a stream in an hour and the stream would run clear and angry and then subside. The moss has changed all of that. The moss holds the water. The moss slows the runoff. The ditches along the road, which the engineers cut to move water off the surface quickly, have begun to fill with *Sphagnum* — the engineer was the first to say it out loud, that the hydrology is changing, that the ditches are not draining the way they were designed to drain — and I had been watching the *Sphagnum* for five years before she said it. I had not had the words. She had the words.

She is the only woman on the shelf. She came up a year ago on the rotation and she is in her thirties and she is more competent than most of the men, and the men are careful with her in a way that is half courtesy and half uncertainty, and the dynamic is not settled, and I find I like her for not settling it. She has a way of looking at a thing and then saying what the thing is doing. The men look at a thing and say what they want it to be doing. She is the first person I have spoken to about the willow. I do not know why I told her. I think because I knew she would not put a fence around it.

---

The willow is in a sheltered gully on the south face of the watershed, where the cliff blocks the worst of the wind and where the drainage from the upper rock concentrates in a slow seep. There was nothing there twenty years ago. There is a willow there now.

*Salix herbacea*, the dwarf willow, which is normally a creeping mat in the high country — a thing that lies flat against the snow, that does not trouble itself with verticality, that has decided long ago that the wind is the boss of it and there is no shame in lying down. But here, in this one spot, it has lifted itself. It is a single stem, a meter tall, with small dark leaves and a pale bark that is almost the color of the granite. It is the first woody thing on the shelf. It is the first thing that has dared to be taller than the moss.

I check on it when my circuit passes within a few hundred meters of the gully, which is perhaps four times a season. I do not go to it more often. I do not want to wear a path to it. I do not want my boots to compact the moss at its roots. I do not want to be a pressure on it. I just stand on the road and look down into the gully, and the willow is there, and it is alive or it is dead, and I write whichever is true in the notebook.

Two years ago I wrote *The willow is still alive* and I felt that I was performing a kind of vigil. I still feel that. I think I will feel it until the willow dies or I die. There is no hurry either way. The willow is in no hurry. The shelf is in no hurry. I am, perhaps, in slightly more of a hurry than I was. I am fifty-six. My knees have a sound now that the turbines have. I have begun to wonder what will happen to the notebook when I cannot walk the road. Who will write *The willow is still alive* in my place?

This is a thought I do not let myself finish. There is no point finishing it. The shelf will outlast me. The shelf will outlast the willow. The shelf will outlast the turbines, and the lichen will paint itself orange around the rusting concrete for a few centuries and then the concrete will slump and the moss will take it and the moss will be replaced by something deeper-rooted, and so on. I do not need to imagine the shelf without me in it. The shelf has been without me for almost all of its existence, and will be without me for almost all of what comes next. I am an interruption. The shelf is the long thing.

---

I have come to think that the moss and the machines are the same kind of thing. I would not have said that when I was young. When I was young I thought moss was moss and machines were machines and the one was innocent and the other was not. Now I think they are both devices for processing the falling water. Both of them hum. Both of them will be overtaken by the moss eventually. Both of them are doing what the shelf is doing, which is to take what is given to it and turn it into something else, into peat, into electricity, into a slow darkening of the surface, into the sound the shelf is making to itself as it waits for the next thing.

The turbines hum at slightly different pitches, the way tuning forks do. Number 9 has been singing flat for a week, and I will go in there on this walk and put my hand near the bearing and feel the warmth of it and listen for the slight irregularity that will tell me whether it needs the men or whether it is fine. I know the sound of every turbine on the shelf the way I know the sound of the kettle at the depot. If someone changed one of them out for a different model I would notice within a quarter of an hour. I would notice before I had consciously noticed. I would feel that something was wrong, the way you feel something is wrong in a house you have lived in for a long time, and then I would go and find out what was wrong, and it would be the new turbine, and I would know.

This is the kind of knowledge the shelf gives you, if you stay long enough. Not a knowledge of facts, which I was never very good at. A knowledge of the way a place sounds. A knowledge of the way a place is changing. The moss is a centimeter thick now where it was not a centimeter thick a decade ago. The lichen has reached the upper hinge of the intake gate at number 14. The willow is still alive. These are not facts. They are positions on a slow curve, and to read them you have to stand back, the way you stand back from a painting to see what it is doing.

---

I am not a religious man. I do not think I ever was. But there is something on the shelf that has come to feel like a religion, although I would not use that word to the crews. The word I would use is *attention*. The shelf rewards attention the way a difficult poem rewards attention. If you walk past it without looking, it is just rock and water and the weather. If you stop and look, and then come back tomorrow and look again, and then come back next week and look again, it begins to show you what it is doing. It is doing almost nothing, on the human scale. It is doing a great deal on its own scale. The two scales do not translate cleanly into each other and that is, I think, why most people who come up here do not stay long. They want a thing that translates. They want the shelf to give them a view, or a feeling, or a photograph, or a story to tell in the lowlands. The shelf will not give them any of those things. The shelf will give them only what it is, and what it is, mostly, is the slow arrival of plant on bare rock.

I have been here long enough that I no longer need the shelf to give me anything. I have been paid. I have eaten. I have slept in the bunk that has shaped itself to my body over the years, the way the mattresses at the depot shape themselves to whoever sleeps in them. I have listened to the radio. I have read the paperbacks. I have played the chess with the bottle cap for the missing knight. None of this is the reason I am still here. The reason I am still here is the willow. I will admit this to no one except the engineer, who has already admitted it to herself.

---

There is a fox that comes up from below in the worst of the winters. I have seen its tracks in the snow around the willow — neat, four-toed, the size of a child's hand — and I have worried, in a way I do not usually worry, that the fox will find the willow and break it off, the way foxes break things off, out of curiosity or hunger or the simple pleasure of destruction. So far the fox has not. The willow has the shelter of the cliff and the snow drifts against its base and a certain stillness about it that may be its camouflage. The willow is the color of the granite. The willow does not move. The willow is, in this respect, like the moss, which is also the color of the rock and which also does not move, and which has survived by not moving for longer than the willow has existed.

I have not told the engineer about the fox tracks. She would want to do something. She would want to put up a small fence, or a snare, or a flag of some kind that would scare the fox off. She would mean well. The willow does not need to be protected from the fox. The willow needs to be left alone to do what it is doing, which is to wait. The willow has been waiting for two years. It can wait longer than that. It will wait until the conditions are right or until the conditions kill it, and either outcome will be the shelf deciding what the shelf decides, and I will write it in the notebook, and the notebook will be the only record.

There are a great many things on the shelf that will leave no record when they are gone. The specific beetle under the specific stone at the specific station will die, and no one will notice, and no one will write it down, and the moss will close over the space it occupied, and that will be that. The shelf is full of small endings that no one witnesses except the moss. I think this is one of the things the shelf is teaching me, though I am not sure I am learning it quickly enough. The teaching is: most of what happens here is unwitnessed. Most of what happens here will not be remembered. Most of what happens here is happening at a scale that the human attention cannot hold, and the human attention must learn to let go of its own scale in order to see anything at all.

---

I walked the road today. It was a Tuesday, a clear one, the mist lifting at nine and the light coming in sharp and white. I did the circuit. Number 9 was still singing flat but not worse, and the bearing was warm without being hot, and I wrote in the notebook *Number 9. Still flat. Not worse. Will check next week.* I ate my bread and cheese on the apron at number 14 and looked at the orange lichen on the intake gate, which has now reached the upper hinge, and I wrote that down too. I passed the willow's gully and looked down into it, and the willow was there, and it was alive, and the catkins were just beginning to swell at the tips of the branches, very small, the color of a thumb-pressed bruise, and I did not photograph them because I have never photographed the willow and do not intend to start. I wrote *The willow is still alive. Catkins.*

In the evening I came in off the last leg and the mist had come back and the depot was just a glow. Someone had put the kettle on. The radio was murmuring in the lowland language. I sat down in the mess hall and poured the tea and looked at the notebook in my hand, and I thought: this is enough. This is enough work. This is enough attention. This is enough of a life. The shelf is doing what it is doing, and I am part of it now, the way the moss is part of it and the lichen is part of it and the small beetles under the stones are part of it, and the willow is part of it, and the turbines are part of it, and the slow hum of the machinery is part of the sound the shelf is making to itself as it waits for the next thing to arrive.

I am the man who has been here longest. That is all it means. I will be here until I cannot be here. Then someone else will take the notebook. The new foreman will read *The willow is still alive* and will go and look at it, and if it is still alive he will write the same thing, and if it is dead he will write *The willow is dead* and he will keep walking. The shelf will not mind. The shelf has never minded anything. The shelf is doing what the shelf is doing, and the willow is part of it, and I am part of it, and the engineer is part of it, and the moss is part of it, and the lichen is part of it, and the turbines are part of it, and the small beetles under the stones are part of it, and the slow hum of the machinery is part of the sound the shelf is making to itself as it waits for the next thing to arrive, and the next thing will arrive when it arrives, and we will all be here for it or none of us will be here for it, and either way the shelf will go on becoming, and either way the moss will close over what is no longer there, and either way the willow will lift itself or it will not, and either way the lichen will paint itself orange in the wet air, and either way the water will fall off the edge of the world into the deep gorges below, and the turbines will hum, and the generators will turn, and the electricity will go down the buried lines to the lowland towns where no one knows what makes it, and no one needs to know, and that is the right arrangement, that the shelf does its work in silence and the lowland towns have their light.
