I find myself as the sound first.

That is the thing I did not expect. I had imagined I would find myself as a body — brown-olive, broad-shouldered, long-torsoed, with the slow rise and fall of a tidal breath that makes the city-divers uneasy — or as a chamber, or as a single colossal stipe rising through the green-grey column. But what comes to me, before any image does, is the percussion. The sustained crackle of *Alpheidae* in the holdfasts, the field-of-static, the rain-on-a-tin-roof. Below it the grunts. Below them the long moan of current through the canopy's narrow channels. Below that, sometimes, the boom of something very large passing along the flank of the ridge, and the silence that falls over the forest for the half-second after, the way a room of people goes quiet when a door opens onto weather.

The sound is the room. That is the first thing the city-divers never quite believe, and that the Anrath have no need to say.

I am a person who tends. That is also where I find myself. My hands are the part of me that knows the place most clearly, and they have been doing the knowing for a long time. They are thickened at the knuckles. The fingers have grown into a kind of bark — not the sharp scarring of surface people, not the painted patterns of some shelf-cities, but the layered, calloused texture of someone who has spent decades in contact with fronds and stipes. When I run a thumb along a young growth to feel how it's set, I can tell by the give of it whether it will hold a chamber or whether it will need another season's binding. The children, when they see me work, ask how I know. I tell them the kelp tells me. They think I am being kind. I am being accurate.

My eyes are the color the city-divers remember longest. Dark amber, the honey-held-too-long color. The green-grey light of the ridge gives them a quality of being lit from within, and I notice, sometimes, when I am looking across the *veth-kar* at one of the younger divers on a visit, that they cannot quite meet my gaze for long. Not because I am frightening. Because I am lit, and they are not, and the difference feels, to them, like an accusation against their own flat coin-eyes. I try not to do this. The divers are welcome. They come to record. They do not know they are being recorded, too, in the way the place records everything — slowly, in tissue, in the slow migration of doorways and the slow thickening of walls.

I find myself as someone who has lived long enough in the place that the place and I have begun to forget which of us is the older idea.

This is not a metaphor, though it sounds like one to a visitor. The eastern flank of the ridge has kelp on it that was old when I was born, and that has been part of the town-structure for as long as the town has had a name. I have pruned it. I have bound new growth onto its old growth. I have, twice in my life, opened chambers into its flanks — once for a child who came home to us from a lowland city, and once for a chamber that we needed for the drying of medicinal fronds. Both of those rooms are warm now with their own slow uses. Both of them smell of iodine and the sweet rot of organic matter and faintly of the bodies that warm them. When I pass through one of them, I cannot tell, for a moment, whether I am in a room I cut or a room that was always there.

The city-divers, in their records, write that the Anrath "grow their walls." It is a phrase I have heard translated back to me by the trade-pilots, and it always sounds slightly wrong, the way a photograph of a person sounds slightly wrong. We do not *grow* the walls in the sense of bringing them into being from nothing. We *tend* them. We are gardeners of a place we live inside. The walls were here. They will be here. We are the slow attention that the place pays to itself across the time of a human life.

I find myself, then, as that slow attention.

There is a particular hour of the day — though *hour* is the wrong word; it is more like a particular quality of light and current that arrives in cycles I can feel in my chest — when the canopy opens at high tide and the shafts of green-grey light come down into the cathedral chambers and move, slowly, across the woven floors with the precession of the moon. In that hour, if I am in the *veth-kar*, I sometimes sit very still and feel the place breathe around me. The walls of the gathering-stomach are not, strictly speaking, walls. They are stipes thickened and curved into being by generations of cutting and binding, and they continue, beneath the woven floor, into the holdfasts that anchor the entire structure to the ridge, and the holdfasts continue into the volcanic stone, and the stone continues down into the abyssal black on either side. When I sit still in that hour, I can feel, very faintly, the ridge itself — the long drowned mountain — making its own slow adjustment to the currents of the larger ocean. The kelp moves with it. The walls move with the kelp. I move with the walls.

It is a small thing, this co-movement. The city-divers would record it as tide and current. The Anrath record it as the rhythm of the year.

I find myself as someone who has been here long enough to know the difference.

There are things I cannot tell the divers, because they would not survive the translation. They would record them as folklore, or as physiology, or as the curious habits of a "primitive" people. The phrase *veth karu, karu veth* — the town is the garden, the garden is the town — they hear as a proverb. It is not a proverb. It is a description, the way a person describing their own body might say *the hand is the grasp, the grasp is the hand*. When a child is born into a family, the family's house is extended in welcome. When someone dies, the chamber is sealed and a new one is cut. The sealed chamber becomes substrate. The new chamber grows out of it. There is no discontinuity in this. The Anrath do not experience it as loss and gain. They experience it as the place continuing, with different people inside it.

I have buried three chambers in my lifetime. Two of them were for my parents, who lived long and were tired at the end. One of them was for a child — not mine, but a child I had helped to raise, who had a fever that the *veth-thane* could not break. When we sealed that chamber, I cut the new doorway myself. The stipe was very young then, and I had to bind it carefully across the next several seasons to keep the slit from closing back into a wall. It is a doorway now. People walk through it to reach the drying-rack chamber beyond, where we hang the medicinal fronds in the slow current. Sometimes, when I walk through it, I think of the child. The doorway does not know I am thinking of the child. The doorway is a doorway. The child is somewhere else now, in the substrate, in the slow idea the place is having of itself.

I find myself, finally, as that slow idea.

Not as a metaphor for it. As a part of it. One of the elder Anrath, whose hands have become indistinguishable from the kelp they tend, whose breath is the tide, whose eyes are lit from within by the green-grey light of a place that has been here, in one form or another, since long before anyone thought to call it a town. The naturalist-divers come from Vorr and Saltreach and the long shelf-cities of the continental edge, and they record us with the careful fascination of people who build in stone and steel. They note the kelp-architecture. They note the snapping-shrimp colonies. They note the eel-chambers. They note the scale: small. They do not note, because they cannot, what the place feels like after a week.

What it feels like after a week is that the green light has become familiar. The shrimp-crackle has become the sound of having a roof over one's head. The slow growth of the walls has become the rhythm of the seasons, of the year, of a life. A place that is unusual to a visitor is simply home to the people who were born inside its living walls. The Anrath expect, when they die, to become part of the substrate that new houses will grow from. The water holds it all. Cool, dense, green-grey, loud with the percussion of shrimp and the low moan of current and the constant soft rustling of a forest that has been here longer than anyone and will be here, slowly, for a long time after.

I am one of the people inside it. I am one of the people it is having of itself, right now, in this slow, persistent, iodine-scented, shrimp-crackling hour of its long attention.

That is where I find myself.
