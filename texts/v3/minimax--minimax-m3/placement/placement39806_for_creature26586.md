I am pressed against stone.

This is the first thing. The stone is cold the way stone is cold when it has learned to hold water without cracking. I am pressed against it and I have been pressed against it for a time I do not measure, because measurement is a category the place has not agreed to. I am a feature of the stone now. I am a place where the bloom has thickened and darkened. I am a shadow the chamber has decided not to look at.

When I breathe, you would only know by the way the water moves around me — the small parting, the slight thickening of the threads along the ceiling where they pass above my head. I breathe so quietly the chamber likes it. The chamber closes around my breathing the way a hand closes around something it has decided to keep. The chamber does not want to think about my breathing. The chamber has been thinking about its own growth for a long time and would prefer not to be interrupted.

I read the current.

The current is my whole library. When it thickens, something has passed through — a sediment plume, a distant click of cooling rock, a small displacement upstream that means a creature has moved somewhere I cannot see. When it slackens, the place has settled again into its patient business. When it carries the faint metallic sweetness of gas from the floor, I know a burp has happened in the bloom somewhere below, and I know roughly where, because the gas takes a known path. The current tells me what the chamber will not.

I move slowly.

When I move, I unstick one part of myself from the stone and drift, by a fraction, to another part. The motion is so small the place does not register it. I am not here and then I am there and the difference is the difference between two adjacent rooms in a city the place is still building. I do this rarely. I do this when the bloom I am resting against has been thinned too far by the grazing of the fingers, and I need a richer patch. I do this when I sense, in some way the chamber does not have a word for, that a better waiting has opened up nearby.

I eat the fingers.

The fingers are pale and slow and they withdraw when touched. They trust that the world will lose interest. They have been selected for this trust. The trust has worked for them for a very long time, and the trust is correct, most of the time. The trust is the correct response to most things.

I am not most things.

I do not chase them through the small dark mouths that connect one structure to another. I wait. I become part of the structure they are grazing on. I make myself a thickening in the bloom they have come to eat. They come to me because the bloom where I have settled is rich — or because I have chosen a place where the bloom is rich, because I know where the bloom is rich because I have been here a long time and have learned the richnesses the way a city-dweller learns which streets are busy at which hours. I let them come to me, and when they are close, I take them.

I take them in a way that is more like closing than like striking. I make myself smaller around them. I make the world smaller around them. They feel the world close and they withdraw — but into me, into the part of me that is not them — and by the time they have understood what has happened, the understanding is already past usefulness.

I eat them and I am not sorry. Sorrow is a category that requires a sense that things could have been otherwise, and here things are not otherwise. The fingers eat the bloom. The bloom precipitates the stone. The stone makes the place. The place makes the bloom. Everything here is eating everything else in a way that is not predation but participation. When I take a finger, I am taking the bloom the finger has taken and returning it to the place in a different form. I am the place's way of asking itself what it has been doing.

The mucus collars do not interest me.

They are too still. They have made themselves into furniture of the place, into permanent pulsing fixtures, and I do not eat furniture. When I have brushed against their nets — by accident, by drift — the nets have closed on me with a slow adhesive patience, and I have withdrawn. I have reduced my surface area, the way the fingers do, the way everything here reduces itself when touched. We are two kinds of patience and we have learned to share the chamber.

I do not know how long I have been here.

The bloom does not keep time. The rock does not keep time. The current keeps a kind of time — the time of pulse, of thickening and slackening — but the current's time is not my time. My time is the time of waiting. My time is the time between one finger and the next. My time is the length of a low thrum in the jaw and the chest that happens sometimes, lasts a few seconds, and goes away, and that I have stopped wondering about. The place is full of things I do not know the cause of, and the not-knowing is not a problem here, the way the not-knowing would be a problem in a brighter place.

I am not lonely.

Loneliness is a category that requires a sense of self that is not continuous with the place. I am continuous with the place. The place is continuous with me. When the bloom thickens beneath me, I am the bloom thickening. When a finger is taken by the current and carried past me, I am the current carrying it. When the chamber clicks — that single hard ping that has no echo — I am the click, for a moment, before it goes away. The boundary between me and the place is something neither of us has bothered to maintain.

I am not afraid.

Fear requires a sense of a future in which something bad might happen, and I do not have that sense. I have a sense of the next moment, which is the next current, which is the next displacement of water, which is the next reading of the library. I have a sense of the place, which is doing things, which is constructing itself, which is busy being what it is. I do not have a sense of the place being something other than what it is, which is the only thing that would make fear useful.

I am dark.

I am dark in a way the place is not used to. The fingers are pale. The bloom is pale. The collars are translucent. The water is the color of old pewter. I am the only dark thing here. I am an absence that moves. I am a small room in a room. The chamber holds me the way it holds the cold — politely, without insistence, with the slow persuasion of a temperature that would prefer I not stay long but is not going to insist.

I will stay long.
