What lives here is the slow thing.

The slow thing is not one thing. The slow thing is many things that have, over a span of time the chamber cannot count, agreed to behave as one thing. The slow thing is the plants.

The plants are everywhere. The plants are the walls and the floors and the lintels and the ceilings and the doors-that-have-grown-shut. The plants are the thickenings that were once rooms. The plants are the loose grey powder that drifts when air drifts. The plants are, in a grammar the chamber has not questioned for an age, *the place itself*.

But the plants are not one plant. The plants are millions — billions, the chamber cannot say — of small dry filaments, each one doing the most modest version of what a living filament does. They take in a faint mineral from the air. They give out a faint mineral in return. They grow by adding a layer. They age by hardening that layer into something that will not decay but will simply become, over a span of time the chamber does not bother to measure, a more committed version of itself. Each filament is too simple to be called a creature. Each filament is too quiet to be called a person. Each filament is, on its own, almost nothing.

Almost nothing. But not nothing. The filaments are connected, in their slow way — through shared root-like extensions into the floor, through the dense papery mat that binds one to another, through the chemical conversations that pass between them in solutions too thin to be called sap. They communicate. They do not know they communicate. The communication happens anyway, the way weather happens, the way erosion happens, the way a forest happens when enough trees have agreed, without ever convening, to occupy the same piece of ground.

And so the plants behave as one. Not as one mind. There is no mind here. There is no watcher, no curiosity, no malice, no welcome. As one coordination. As one slow distributed agreement about how to lean, where to thicken, when to harden, when to give way. The plants-as-collective is the thing that lives here. It has lived here since before there was a settlement, in a sparser, quieter form. It lived here when the builders came. It lived here, more densely, when the builders left. It lives here now, in the form of the settlement, having absorbed the settlement into itself the way a slow river absorbs a fallen tree into its sediment.

The builders were small. This much can be inferred from the tools that made the place — small edge-tools, scraping-tools, hand-sized implements for working dry fiber into rigid form. The builders were the kind of creature that holds a tool in something that functions as a hand, and that uses the hand to do something to the world that the world does not do to itself. The chamber does not know what the builders looked like. The chamber does not know how many of them there were, or what they called themselves, or whether they had names for the hollow they had found. The chamber knows only the shape they left.

And the shape is this: a settlement. A small settlement, layered in the way that all settlements are layered — foundation first, then walls, then beams, then rooms, then more rooms, then the tearing-out of rooms and the building of smaller rooms, then the filling-in of the smaller rooms and the building of a single larger one over the top. The builders did what builders do. They built, and rebuilt, and built again. They did it with their small tools and their dull cooper-colored alloys. They did it on top of the plants, which had been here before them, and which accommodated them without complaint, because plants do not complain, because plants do not have the apparatus for complaint, because plants are the kind of thing that simply continues, in whatever direction the world provides.

The builders went away. This is the part the chamber cannot explain. The builders thinned out. Their small hands withdrew. Their tools were taken, mostly — or fell away, or rusted into nothing, or were absorbed into the dry walls and now exist as faint discolorations, as small pockets of cooper-color that flake off in scrolls when the air touches them. The builders did not say goodbye. They left no message, no mark of departure. They simply left.

And the plants continued.

The plants continued to add layers. They continued to lay fiber over fiber, in the manner they had been doing since before the builders came, but now with a new ingredient in the mix — the residue of the build itself. The walls the builders had raised did not fall down when the builders left. The walls had been made of plant fiber, laid in the builder-manner, and the plant fiber had been colonized, in the builders' absence, by the plants-as-collective, which had incorporated the walls into its own body the way a mycelium incorporates a fallen log into its network. The walls were no longer the builders'. The walls were, slowly, becoming the plants'.

This is how the build continued. The build had been begun by hands. The build was being continued, in the hands' absence, by the colony. The colony did not know it was continuing the build. The colony did not know what a build was. The colony simply continued to lay layer on layer, in roughly the same places, in roughly the same shapes, as the layers beneath. The colony was imitating, without knowing it was imitating, the gestures of the small hands. It was laying foundations that had no one to stand on them. It was raising walls that had no one to lean against them. It was adding beams over rooms that had been filled in. It was doing all of this in the dark, in the cool, in the patience of the chamber, for a span of time that would have embarrassed the builders, if the builders had been there to be embarrassed.

The build is the closest thing the hollow has to a permanent resident.

But the build is not the only resident.

There are the tools.

A few tools remain. Most were taken. Most fell away. Most have rusted into faint discolorations in the walls, or have been absorbed into the dry fiber as small pockets of cooper-color that peel off in scrolls. But a few — a handful, perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer — are still here. Still embedded. Still in the chamber.

These remaining tools are small. Hand-sized. Edge-shaped, scraper-shaped, the shapes of implements that were meant to work dry plant fiber into rigid form. They are the kind of tool that does not move under its own power. They are the kind of tool that sits where it is placed, and waits for a hand to pick it up.

No hand has picked them up for longer than the chamber can remember.

But the tools are still doing what tools do. The chamber's slow settling shifts them, by fractions of millimeters per year. The air currents nudge them. The creaking walls press against them, by the smallest pressures, over the longest times. And as the tools shift, they scrape. They scrape against the fiber they are embedded in. They scrape against the walls. They wear new grooves into the stratigraphy, grooves that did not exist when the builders were here, grooves that are entirely the tools' own work.

The tools are residents. Tiny slow residents. Edge-creatures, scraping-creatures, each one practicing its ancient trade on the walls around it, year after year, never knowing that the trade has been retired. They do not scrape with purpose. They do not scrape toward a goal. They scrape because that is what an edge does when it is placed against a softer surface and given enough time.

And the walls themselves are residents. Each wall is a kind of dry creature — layered, leaning, papery, alive in the slow aggregate way. Each wall is a settlement of fibers, of filaments, of small dust-producing colonies, all arranged into a single vertical surface that has been doing the work of being a wall for so long that the work has worn a groove into its posture. Some walls lean. Some walls have always leaned, since the day they were raised, and they have never unlearned the leaning. Some walls were once straight and have, over the slow passage of the chamber's settling, become curved, the way a tree becomes curved when the wind has been blowing it the same way for a century.

The walls fall, sometimes. This is the loudest thing that happens here. A wall detaches itself from the ceiling or from the wall beside it, and falls. The fall is soft. The fall is catastrophic. The fall is a *fwump*, followed by the smaller sounds of the fallen pieces continuing to break apart on the floor for several minutes afterward.

And then the pieces lie on the floor. And the pieces are no longer walls. The pieces are fragments. The pieces are newly-arrived residents, residents that have just fallen from the ceiling, residents that are still in the process of becoming part of the colony. The pieces will be colonized, in time. The pieces will be incorporated into the floor. The pieces will become the next stratum. The pieces will lie where they fell, and the chamber will lay its grey powder over them, and the walls above them will continue to creak, and eventually — in a time the chamber does not bother to count — the pieces will be so far below the surface that they will no longer be reachable by anything.

The pieces are the youngest residents. The pieces are the residents that have just begun their long stay.

And the oldest residents are the floor itself. The floor is the part of the chamber that has been accumulating the longest. The floor is the part that holds, in its stratigraphy, the deepest record of what was here before the builders, and what the builders did when they arrived, and what the colony did after the builders left. The floor is the part that has the most layers, the most hand-strata, the most fossils of small rooms and filled-in rooms and rooms built over rooms. The floor is, in a sense, the chamber's memory. The floor is the part that does not fall.

And the air is a resident too. The air moves in slow currents, because the chamber is not sealed against the great body it sits inside, and somewhere outside this pocket, temperatures shift. The shifts propagate inward as sighs. The sighs push against the dry plant surfaces. The dry plant surfaces push back. The small frictions of these encounters accumulate into a kind of choir — the creaking, the ongoing creaking, the voice of the chamber, the breath of the colony.

The air does not live. The air is not alive in any of the ways the chamber would recognize. But the air is involved. The air is a participant. The air is, in the chamber's slow grammar, a kind of resident too — the most ephemeral resident, the one that passes through, the one that does not stay, but that touches everything on its way.

And the chamber itself is a resident. The hollow, the pocket in the body of something much larger — the hollow is a thing that contains, and containing is a kind of action. The hollow has a shape. The hollow has a posture. The hollow has a temperature, which is cool, which is the temperature of a clay jar, which is the temperature of a stone that has been in shadow all day. The hollow breathes, in its slow way — in and out, in and out, the slow exhalation of paper settling against paper, the slow inhalation of air moving through the cracks. The hollow is patient. The hollow is loud. The hollow is dark.

The hollow is the place where the build continues.

The hollow is the place where the plants-as-collective continues to lay layer on layer on layer.

The hollow is the place where the small tools, forgotten in the walls, continue to scrape their patient grooves into the stratigraphy.

The hollow is the place where the walls continue to lean, and to creak, and to fall, and to be colonized, and to become the floor.

The hollow is the place where the air continues to move, in slow currents, in slow sighs, in slow frictions against dry plant surfaces.

The hollow is the place where a settlement was built by small hands and then continued, after the small hands had gone, in the patient manner of things that have been given a purpose by someone else and that find, in the long quiet after, that they can keep going on the purpose alone.

The hollow is the place that lives here.

And what lives here, in the hollow, in the chamber, in the pocket at the heart of the larger body, is the slow continuation of all of it — the plants, the tools, the walls, the fallen pieces, the floor, the air, the hollow itself, the build, the colony, the patience, the creaking.

It is loud here.

It is dark here.

It is cool here.

You can stay as long as you like.
