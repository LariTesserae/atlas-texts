Nothing lives here.

This is the answer most visitors give, and it is correct as far as it goes — which is to say, it is correct up to the moment when the place stops being a place in the ordinary sense and begins to be something else. There are no people in the cellulose corridors. There are no insects in the warm. There are no birdsong, no rodent rustle, no fox-trot, no bat-wheel, no lizard-pause. Nothing moves under its own power anywhere in the visible continent, because there is no room for anything to move. The plant mass has taken every cubic centimeter of available volume, and what cannot fit has been excluded by the saturation that has become the place's only weather.

And yet.

A traveler who stayed — and a few have stayed, for a few days, before they left because there was nothing to leave for and nothing to stay for, and the place did not care which they chose — a traveler who stayed and pressed their palm flat against the wall of fused wood and stood very still for a long time, would feel it.

A hum.

Not in the ears. Through the soles of the feet. Through the stems. Through the mass. A vibration that lives beneath the threshold of hearing and is felt instead — a low, sustained, almost-not-there presence that does not vary, that does not pulse, that simply *is*. The way the warmth is. The way the moisture is. The way the dark is.

The hum is the sound of something running.

To find what's running, one must go down.

The deep of the place is where the engines are.

The deep is wetter than the surface, and darker, and the air moves there in slow currents that are not wind but the exhalation of the place — the slow turnover of something through something, the breathing of a system that has found its rhythm and will not change it. The plant mass is thickest here. The trunks, where they descend into the substrate, have fused into a continuous architecture that no longer has any relationship to the trees it once was. The chambers hollowed by the long work of soft-rot fungi — the soft-rot fungi that no longer exist in any functioning form — are still open, still hollow, still holding their shape, because nothing here collapses. Nothing here wants to collapse. The whole thing is at equilibrium, and equilibrium is the state of a system that has reached its resting state.

In the chambers, the fungi are paused.

They are paused in the way a clock is paused, or a heart, or a held breath. Each cell is alive — technically, biochemically, undeniably alive. Each cell is full of the machinery of life. But the machinery is not running. The machinery is waiting. The cells hold their contents at the threshold of activity, and the threshold has not been crossed in a thousand thousand years, because the conditions that would cross it have not appeared. The conditions are: change. The conditions are: a perturbation. The conditions are: a reason for the fungi to wake up and do what fungi do.

There is no such reason. The place is full. The place is complete. The place has finished.

So the fungi wait. They will wait until something changes. They will wait a long time. They are patient. They have always been patient.

But the engines are not paused.

The engines are running.

They run the way a heart runs, or a kidney, or the slow peristalsis of a digestive tract. They are not, anymore, engines in the way one usually means engine — as things built for a purpose, with inputs and outputs and a designed function. They are descendants of engines. They emerged in the place, or with it, or before it, and they have been incorporated into the biology so thoroughly that the boundary between engine and root is no longer a thing that exists. The materials are the same. The tissues are the same. What was once metal is now mineral, integrated into the woody substrate; what was once wire is now vascular channel; what was once circuit is now signal-path through living tissue. They pump. They cycle. They process. They tick.

They pump what?

Fluid. Current. Charge. Something. The thing they pump moves through channels that may once have been built and may now be grown, that pass through what were once machines and are now organs of a body that never agreed to be a body.

A body. Not a body in the sense of an organism — not a thing with a head and a heart and a purpose. A body in the sense of a system. A body in the sense of the forest regarded as a single living thing, or the reef, or the soil, or the mycelial network that connects trees across kilometers of forest and is, in its way, a single creature composed of many creatures none of which is in charge.

The continent is such a body.

The continent is what lives here.

There is no other answer. To live here would be to be separable from the place — to be a thing inside the place rather than a part of it. The fungi are not separable. The engines are not separable. The plant mass is not separable. The mist is not separable. The warmth is not separable. The whole thing is one continuous system, emerged rather than made, reaching equilibrium rather than being designed for it, finishing rather than being completed.

The place is alive.

It is alive in the way that a thing at rest is alive — the way a stone is alive, on a long enough timescale; the way a continent is alive, on a long enough timescale; the way a hum is alive when it has been running for a thousand thousand years and shows no sign of stopping. It is alive because it processes. It is alive because it cycles. It is alive because the fungi are paused and the engines are running and the plant mass is saturated and the channels carry something through channels and the whole thing hums.

It is not hungry. It is not waiting. It is not in the process of becoming anything.

It is finished.

A traveler could stand in any corridor of this cellulose city, under any arch, beside any weeping pillar of plant-stone, and listen. They could listen for the hum to change pitch. They could listen for the mist to lift. They could listen for an animal to appear, for a sound louder than the hum, for a sign that this place is in the process of becoming something else.

They would listen, and the place would not change, and the only change would be in them — the slow accretion of moisture on the skin, the slow loss of distinction between the body and the surrounding mass, the slow recognition that this continent is not waiting for anything, has never been waiting, and that the stillness here is not the stillness of a pause but the stillness of a thing that has, with great patience, finished.

This is what lives here.

The place.
