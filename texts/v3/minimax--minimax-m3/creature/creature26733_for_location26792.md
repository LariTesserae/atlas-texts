What lives here has made an arrangement with stone, and the arrangement is old.

The small ones first. They are called nothing, in the language of the people who live among them — or if they are called something, the word is the same word used for lichen, or for the moss that skins a wet rock after rain, or for the pale lichen-strands that hang and sway without wind in the higher cleft. This is not carelessness. The word means *that which is between*. They are the color of the stone's shadow when the stone is wet. Their bodies are thin in a way that does not look animal; thin as a leaf pressed into a book, thin as the skin of a bladder, thin as the film that grows on the surface of still water when the water has been still too long. Four limbs, or six, or eight — it is hard to tell, because they hold the stone so closely that you cannot see the joins. They do not have a shape so much as they have a fit. They fit the surface they are on. When they move from one stone to the next, they leave a faint mark, a paler stone, where the moss beneath them has been killed by their holding. The moss returns. The moss returns quickly. It grows back over their bodies too, sometimes, and then they are not visible at all, and a person walking the path is walking on what seems to be moss, and it is.

They feed the way a slow fire feeds — on what is beneath them. They take the surface of the stone into themselves, and what comes out is the thin pale film that follows the fracture lines, the moss-map that you might mistake, the first time you saw it, for the drawing of a river system that has not yet learned how to be a river. They are the ones drawing it. They have been drawing it for a long time.

They are not insects. They are not mollusks. They are not, in any way the visiting naturalist would recognize, anything at all. They are warm to the touch — warmer than the stone, warmer than the air. When you put your hand on one, in the careful way that the locals show you, if you have become trusted enough to be shown, you feel a small slow pulse under your palm. The pulse is not a heartbeat. It is more like the pulse of a tide in a very shallow pool. You feel it and you take your hand away. You take your hand away not because it frightened you but because you understood, in the moment of contact, that you were being read. Not in language. In pressure. In warmth. In the slow seepage of something across your skin that left you feeling, for an hour afterward, as though you had been standing in a place a long time.

They do not chirp. They do not call. When something disturbs them — a footfall heavier than the locals', a stone turned that should not have been turned, a stranger's boot that does not know the path — the disturbance passes through them like a shiver through a sheet. You hear it only as a cessation of silence. The quiet is so total here that the small sounds of small bodies adjusting become audible, and then stop, and the stopping is the sound.

They are not dangerous, perhaps. They are not pests, perhaps. The locals do not say. The locals step carefully. There is a path between the buildings, and the path is not a path in the sense of being cleared — nothing is cleared here, nothing could be cleared, the moss would return by tomorrow and the small ones would return by the day after — but the path is a way of walking that has been agreed upon. The locals walk a certain way. They walk a way that distributes their weight. They walk a way that does not crush. You can learn it. It takes a season. If you learn it badly, you will feel, under your foot, a softness that is not moss, and you will step quickly off it, and you will not speak of it at the place where you stop to brush your boot.

In the deepest part of the cleft, where the broken upper edge is so high and so narrow that the light arrives only as a memory of light, the larger ones live.

You do not see them at first. You see the absence of moss. In a place where every surface wears its moss the way a body wears its clothes, a place where the moss is not is a place where something is being kept clear, or where something is keeping itself clear. The absences are shaped like the larger ones. The absences are warm. If you put your hand down, in the deepest dark, where the air is no longer cool but is simply the temperature of a held breath, you feel the warmth come up through your palm and into your wrist, and you understand that you are standing over something that is alive in a way you do not have a word for.

They are large. The largest, the one the long low building is built over — built around, more truly, because the building's back wall is the curve of its side, and the chimney's warmth is its warmth, and the smoke goes up past where its head would be if it had a head — is as long as the building is long. It does not move. It has not, by any account the locals will give you, ever moved. Its skin is the grey-brown of the stone's interior, the way the stone looks when you break it open and see what it was before the weather found it. Its skin is not scaly and not smooth. It is the texture of very old mortar. It is the texture of something that has been pressed into its shape for a very long time and has, in the pressing, become indistinguishable from the thing it was pressed against.

Its eyes, when you can find them in the dim — and the locals will not point them out for you, because to point is to draw attention and attention is a kind of disturbance — are the eyes of something that has not, in any meaningful sense, needed to see for a very long time. They are open. They do not blink. They do not track. They are the color of deep water in a place where deep water is not seen, and they do not reflect, because there is nothing here for them to reflect. Light comes to them and they take it in. That is the eye's whole business. They look out into a dimness they have looked out into for so long that the looking has become a kind of breathing.

It breathes. You hear it when you stand in the deepest part of the cleft and stop moving and stop speaking and allow the quiet to come back. It breathes in, and the air along the floor moves toward it, very slightly, carrying the small ones with it — they ride the breath, the way leaves ride a wind that is not really wind — and it breathes out, and the air moves away, and the mist that hangs in the cleft thickens by a fraction, and the small ones resettle. Its breath is the breath of the cleft. When the locals say the cleft sweats, they mean this. The larger ones are the cleft's lungs. The cleft is their body. The distinction has not mattered for a long time.

What they eat, no one will tell you, because no one knows, and because knowing would require asking, and asking would require a kind of attention that the locals have decided, by long agreement, not to pay. There are stories. The stories are the kind of stories that are told in winter, by the small fire, in the building whose back is the curve of the largest one's side, and the stories are always told in a voice that is slightly too quiet, as though the teller is aware of being listened to by something that does not have ears but is listening anyway. In the stories, the larger ones eat time. In the stories, they eat the silence. In the stories, they eat the things that would otherwise grow too fast in a place that is trying to become a meadow. The stories are not consistent. The locals will tell you, if you press them, that the stories are not meant to be consistent. They are meant to be a kind of payment. You tell a story into the dim. The larger ones hear it in whatever way they hear. The moss grows back a little slower that year. The frost holds off a little longer. The arrangement holds.

The people.

There are few of them. Six, perhaps, in the settlement at any given time; fewer in winter, when some go down into the lower valleys, though no one will say where exactly, or why exactly, only that the going is necessary and the returning is also necessary. They are small in the way that people who live among larger things are small — not stunted, not deprived, but scaled. They move low. They keep their hands near the ground. When they sit, they sit as though the ground is the back of something they are being polite to. They speak quietly. They laugh rarely, and when they laugh it is the laugh of people who have learned that a loud sound in a quiet place is a kind of violence. They are brown-skinned and dark-eyed and their hair is cut short, because long hair catches in the moss, and moss in the hair is not a small thing here, where the small ones might follow a strand of hair down to the warmth of a scalp and stay.

They have no leader, or if they have a leader, the leader is the one who knows best where the larger ones are, and where the small ones are thickest, and where the path is safest to walk in the early morning when the mist comes in over the broken upper edge and the small ones have not yet settled into the day's positions. They repair the walls each spring. They take fresh-broken stone from higher up, where the frost of the previous winter has pried it loose, and they stack it into the places where the small ones have, over the year, made the walls thinner. The work is done without speech. The work is done in a kind of rhythm that is the rhythm of the larger ones' breathing, as though the workers have learned to move inside the breath. A stone is set. A breath comes in. Another stone is set. A breath goes out. By the end of the day the wall is a hand's breadth higher than it was, and the small ones are already pressing against its base, and the lichen is already beginning to colonize its surface, and the moss-maps are already beginning to draw themselves across it, and the wall is already, in the slow arithmetic of this place, becoming something that will be eaten.

They have made a peace with this. The peace is not a peace in the way that visiting diplomats mean peace — a negotiation, a treaty, a thing that could be unwound. It is a peace the way that the wearing-down of a stone by water is a peace. It is the peace of two things that have been in contact for so long that the contact has become the substance of both. They are being unmade, and they know it. They rebuild. They are being unmade again. They rebuild. In a hundred years they will be gone, or the cleft will be gone, or both will be gone, and something else will be here. The locals know this. They do not seem to find it sad. They seem to find it the way they find the mist, and the slow breathing, and the small ones underfoot — the way they find the quiet, which is the only thing here that is not strange.

You will ask, before you leave, what they are called — the people, the small ones, the larger ones. The locals will consider the question. They will consider it for a long time. Then they will answer with a word that means, in their language, *those who are here*. It is the same word for the moss. It is the same word for the lichen. It is the same word for the larger ones. It is the same word for themselves. It is, you will realize as you walk back out of the cleft and the mist closes behind you, the same word they would use, if asked, for you.
