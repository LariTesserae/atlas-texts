What lives in the Sunken Pass of Olethrun does not live in any way the word has ever been meant.

There are no animals here. There has never been an animal here. There are no fish, no worms, no creatures with mouths or stomachs or the small hungers that animal existence depends on. The water has no oxygen budget to spare for such metabolisms; it carries only the dissolved traces of stone, the fine silt of distant falls, the cold patience of depth. What lives here has made a different arrangement with that cold and that darkness, and the arrangement does not involve eating, or breathing, or moving in any way a swimmer would recognize as movement.

The growths are the closest thing the pass has to a citizen, and even they are not citizens in the way the word implies. They are the inhabitants the way a forest is the inhabitant of a mountain — not as individuals but as a condition, a slow vegetable fact. A growth is a stalk. It rises from a crack in the basalt, dark as wet slate, segmented and ridged along its length like the vertebrae of something that never had a spine. Its surface has a faint gloss that gives back what little light filters down from the surface, reluctantly, the way a polished river-stone gives back a thrown image of a face that has moved on. It grows upward by a finger-length in a year. It does this without a sun. It is black. It is cold. It grows.

The growth has no mouth. It has no stomach. It has no nerve. It has nothing that could fairly be called a sense. And yet it orients. The current comes past it from somewhere far off, carrying the dissolved mineral trace of every rock it has touched along the way, and the growth turns, by some slow turning of its growth-pattern that takes a season to complete, to face the flow.
