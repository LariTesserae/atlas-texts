# The Inhabitants

What lives here has stopped being a list and become a condition.

The deer-things are not deer. They are not anything that ever was a deer, though they have hooves, and they have a long pale face, and they move in the same unhurried way that something with four legs and a long digestive tract moves when nothing has threatened it for so long that *threat* has become a grammatical mood it cannot conjugate. There are a few of them. They live in family groups of four, sometimes five, and the groups are not herds. The groups are *households*. They have preferred groves. They have the small worn paths between groves that are not migration routes but visiting routes, the way you visit a relative, the way you visit the same relative for years and the path becomes yours in a way that is not yours.

They do not speak. But they make decisions. You can tell because of the way they pause at forks, the way they look back at one another before stepping down into a thicket, the way the largest of them — always a female, you will notice, and this is the first of the place's small corrections to your assumptions — walks at the front and the others fall into positions behind her that are not strictly hierarchical but are *arranged*, in the way a sentence is arranged, with meaning. They are not bright. They are not dim. They are *settled*. They have been getting things right, generation by generation, for so long that getting things right has become a thing the body does without consulting.

They drink by pressing their soft mouths to the bark. This is, you will come to understand, not a behavior but a posture. They are *kissing* the trees. Or they are being kissed by the trees. The grammar is unclear. The trees seem to want it. The pale mouths of the deer-things and the dark mouths of the wood meet and something passes between them that is not sap and is not blood and is closer to a long agreement. The deer-things go on. The trees go on. Neither party has changed in any way you could point to. Both parties have, you suspect, changed in every way.

They are very old, some of them. There is one, you will see if you walk far enough east — which is the same as walking west — who is paler than the others and moves more slowly and has, on her flank, a kind of pattern, a faint tracery of darker lines, that looks at first like mange and then, on second look, like the rings of a tree. She is a record. She is a place where the family has been writing itself down for a very long time. She does not lead anymore. She walks in the middle of the group, and the others go around her when the path narrows, with the courtesy of tenants around an old monument.

---

The insects are the size of your hand. Their wings are not wings in the way you mean. They are *sails*, or *panels*, or *fins*, wet-looking and translucent and folded along their backs when at rest, unfolded when they fly, which they do rarely and with great deliberation, as if each flight were a referendum. There are several species. You will not learn to tell them apart. They hum at different pitches and the pitches layer into a chord that is, depending on the hour, either a constant *mmmmm* that lives at the edge of hearing, or a thicker drone, or — and this is rare — a sudden thinning, a gap in the texture, like a held breath, like a question the forest has asked itself.

Their larvae live in the deadwood. They live there for years. They tunnel through it in patterns that are not random, that are not the chaos of worms in a log, that are, when you look at a cross-section of a fallen pillar, *organized*. There are chambers. There are corridors. There are junctions where the tunnels meet and the larva who lives there has, you begin to feel, an arrangement with the larva who lives there. They do not eat each other. They eat the wood, which is mostly already eaten, which is mostly already a kind of animal itself by the time the larva gets to it. The tunnels are old. The tunnels have been maintained, by generations of larvae that have never met, into a kind of architecture — a city, you might say, but the word is wrong because cities imply builders, and these tunnels are the work of *beings who have not had an idea in millennia* and have done their work perfectly anyway.

The larvae do not see. They do not need to. They are white and soft and very long-lived and they move through their tunnels by feel, by memory, by the slow proprioception of a creature who has lived in the same corridor since before it was an adult. When the deadwood is finally consumed — and it always is, eventually, the wood returns to the floor, the floor returns to the root, the root returns to the soil — the larvae who live in it do something you will not see and cannot describe. They go. They become something else. The tunnel remains. The tunnel becomes a feature of the place, like a streambed, like the path of a river that has dried up. The next generation of larvae will sometimes inherit it. The tunnel will be repaired, gently, with new wood-pulp, by larvae who did not know the old larvae but who knew the shape of the tunnel and went on with it.

---

The amphibians are everywhere and they are not what you think. The small ones with the red eyes cling to the underside of leaves and sing. They sing one note at a time. The note is the same note, mostly, but the *timing* is not. The timing is the thing. They sing when a particular droplet of water reaches a particular size on the leaf above them, and they sing in such a way that other amphibians, on other leaves, will know. This is not a chorus. This is a *survey*. They are taking a census. They are, you begin to understand, keeping records.

Of what, you cannot say. Of the rain, perhaps. Of which leaves are bearing weight. Of which drops have grown large enough to fall. The song is the answer. The song is always the same note, and the note means *I am still here*, and the meaning is not addressed to anyone, it is addressed to the Long Plain itself, which is listening, which is always listening, in the way a forest listens when you have walked far enough into it that you have become a feature of the forest.

There are larger ones, deeper in. They are the size of your forearm. They live in the warm pools. They are pale. They are eyeless. They are the fish you found, or thought you found. They are not fish. They are the larval form of something — of the insects, perhaps, or of the deer-things, or of nothing that has a name in any taxonomy you carry, in any taxonomy that has been written down, because nothing that lives here has ever been written down. The pale things in the warm pools are *between*. They are the place between two stages of being. They pulse when you disturb them. The pulse is a long, slow, patient thing, the heartbeat of an organism that does not need to beat fast because nothing here has ever needed to beat fast.

They have been in the pools for a very long time. They will be in the pools for a very long time. They are not waiting to become anything. They have already become what they are. They are what waits looks like, when waiting has stopped being a state and has become a *form*.

---

There are things you will not see.

There is something that makes the deep creaks, the long settling sounds, in the trunks of the largest growths. You will never see it. You will never see where it lives. You will only ever hear, at long intervals, the sound of something heavy adjusting itself inside a column of wood, the way a sleeper adjusts inside a bed. It is very large. It is very slow. It is the size of the tree it is in, or the tree it is in is the size of it. The distinction has not been made. It has been here, you suspect, since the tree began, and the tree has been growing around it, the way a pearl grows around a grain, and it has been growing into the tree, the way a root grows into a stone. They are the same organism now. They cannot be separated without damage. The tree will outlive it. The tree will hold its shape, afterward, like a glove holding the shape of a hand that has been removed.

There is something in the rivers that you will glimpse, once, on a still morning, when the black water is particularly still and the light is particularly green. It will be a shape. It will be a long shape, pale under the surface, moving with the current or against it, you will not be able to tell. It will be gone. It will not have left a wake. You will wonder, afterwards, if you saw it. You will not be able to decide. This is fine. The Long Plain does not require you to decide.

There is something — and you will only suspect this, on the third night, when the rain stops for the first time and the silence that replaces it has a *texture*, a *weight*, a kind of attention to it that silence does not usually have — that is the plain itself, or that the plain is a part of, or that the plain is an outgrowth of, in the way that a coral is an outgrowth of the reef. You will not see it. You will feel it, briefly, when the rain resumes and the sound resumes and you understand that the pause was not a pause. The pause was the plain turning over in its sleep.

---

What lives here, finally, is *not in a hurry*.

This is the rule the place keeps, and the rule is not metaphorical. The things that live here have, over the very long span of their tenancy, optimized themselves out of hurry. They have removed every behavior that required speed. They have removed every feature that required quickness. They are the way they are because the way they are is the way that does not need to be any other way. The deer-things are slow because slow is correct. The insects are rare in flight because flight is, most of the time, a waste. The amphibians sing one note because one note is enough. The larvae tunnel because tunneling is the only thing to do, and the only thing to do is what they have always done, and what they have always done has been refined, over generations, into something indistinguishable from the wood itself.

The things that were in a hurry are the stones in the lines. The things that were in a hurry are the circles. The things that were in a hurry were here once, and did something, and are now arranged, by the plain, into the long evidence of themselves. The plain does not mourn them. The plain has not the equipment. The plain has absorbed them, the way the plain absorbs everything, the way the plain is absorbing the mountain, the way the plain will absorb, in time, the deer-things and the insects and the amphibians and the thing in the rivers and the thing in the tree and you, if you stay, if you are patient, if you allow yourself to be made into an arrangement of something that was once in a hurry and is now, finally, still.

The rain resumes. It was never not resuming.

You are an animal here. You are not in charge. You are not unwelcome. You are a thing that arrived on foot, though how you got here is already slipping, and the place is making its arrangements with you, slowly, with the patience of a mouth, and the arrangements are not yet complete, and will not be complete for a long time, and you will be here, or what is left of you will be here, when they are.
