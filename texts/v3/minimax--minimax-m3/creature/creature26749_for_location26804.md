The citizens are dense the way the city is dense. They are not tall. They are not large. They have the compactness of things that have been pressed into their own shape by years of heat and wet and the constant slow turning of something beneath them. Their bones are heavy in a particular way—visitors remark on it, the way visitors remark on the pale wide eyes, the way the skin holds a flush at the cheeks and at the knuckles and nowhere else. Their hands are wide, and the calluses on their palms have the shape of particular wrenches, particular flange-knobs, particular kinds of pipe-joint that their grandfathers and great-grandfathers tightened. They move slowly. This is not laziness. This is conservation. In a place where the air is a weight and the heat is a weight and the sound is a weight, the body learns to spend itself carefully, and the citizens have learned this so well that they do not know they have learned it. A child from outside, brought in and set down in the center of the city, would run and shout and exhaust itself in minutes. A child born here would not run. A child born here would walk, slowly, with the careful heel-toe gait of its parents, and would arrive where it meant to go.

Their skin is the color of the bricks, only darker—ruddier in the cheeks, where the heat is always pressing up from below, and grayer at the temples, where the steam gathers and beads. Their hair is dark and lank and clings to the backs of their necks. Their clothes are layered: linen against the skin, then wool, then a waxed outer garment the citizens call a *shag*, which sheds the constant slow rain of condensation that falls from every metal surface. The clothes are patched, and the patches are patched, and a citizen's shirt can carry three or four layers of mending on it, each layer a slightly different color, and this is not poverty but a kind of slow accumulation, the way the moss accumulates, the way the rust accumulates. The colors are the colors of the city: brick-red, soot-black, rust-brown, the pewter-gray of the older pipes.

They are damp. They are always damp. They have stopped noticing this, the way they have stopped noticing the sound. Their handshake is a wet thing, and a visitor is always a little startled by it, the way a visitor is always startled by the size of their eyes in the dim orange light, the way a visitor is always startled by how loudly one must shout to be heard above the city's breath. The citizens speak in shouts, when they speak at all, and they speak slowly, because the shouts take effort and the effort must be rationed. The rest of the time they speak in the flat palms and slow gestures of people who have lived all their lives inside a sound so constant it has become a kind of silence. Visitors cannot sleep in the city. The citizens sleep like the dead. They lie down in their heavy clothes on their heavy beds in their thick-walled rooms, and the sound comes in through the walls, and they do not hear it, the way you do not hear your own breathing, the way you do not hear your own heart.

They work. Of course they work. There is so much to maintain: the Pipes, with their constant weeping; the valves, which must be turned and turned again; the grates, which must be swept of moss and fern in some places and not others, a distinction that matters to the Grate-Keepers and that they cannot easily explain; the walkways, which soften in the wet and must be replaced plank by plank, year by year; the pumps somewhere below, which must be primed and watched. There are the Pipe-Fitters, who are the most respected, and the Grate-Keepers, who are the most particular, and the Dyers, who tend the mosses in certain courtyards because the mosses are the only green that will grow in the city and they must be kept a particular shade of dark. There are the Stokers. Nobody talks about the Stokers. The Stokers are said to be citizens like any other citizens. The Stokers are said to come back up, eventually, from wherever it is they go. The Stokers' children go to school with other citizens' children, and play in the flooded lower streets in their tall boots, and sit on the warm grates in the cold months. The Stokers' hands are said to be stained a particular color, somewhere between rust and the orange of the glow. This is all that is known, and the citizens have agreed, in the slow collective way that they agree on things, that this is enough to know.

The children love the tall boots. The boots are tall—knee-high, sometimes higher—and they are made of waxed leather that the citizens cure themselves from the hides of some animal they will not name, brought in from outside. The children put on the tall boots even when there is no rain, even when the lowest streets are dry, because the boots make a particular sound on the planks—a soft flat *tok-tok-tok*—and the children love this sound, and they love the way the boots make them taller, and the way the boots keep their feet dry in a city where the ground is always wet. They catch the small pale moths in cups made of folded paper. They release the moths, or they do not. The meaning of this is not explained to them. They will learn it, or they will not.

The old people lean against the railings around the grates, especially in the cold parts of the year. They have seen the most. They know the most. They have decided, in the way the city decides, that certain things are not to be discussed, and they enforce this decision gently, the way the moss enforces its presence on the north walls, the way the rust enforces its presence on the older pipes. They die in their sleep, mostly, in rooms with thick-glassed windows lit with yellow-orange light. Their bodies are taken down at night, through the lower streets, by people who do not speak, and they are given to the ground. The ground is grateful. The moss comes up thicker where they have been buried, and the citizens take a quiet pride in this, the way they take a quiet pride in the small white flowers of the vine that climbs the outer pipes, the way they take a quiet pride in the pale moths that live near the grates and that nobody calls animals.

The citizens love the small white flowers. They will not say so. They love the sound of the hammers, because the hammers are the city's heartbeat, and to love the heartbeat is to love the city. They love the warm grates in the cold months. They love the heavy breads and the long-boiled roots and the fish that appears sometimes in the markets—the fish that is said to come from somewhere below, that is said to be blind, that is said to be a particular color in the flesh. They love their tall boots and their layered clothes and their small thick-glassed rooms and the slow turning of the wheel that is the city's or that the city's is. They fear, in a way they do not name, that the wheel will stop, that the hammers will fall silent, that the orange glow will go out, and that they will be left in a place that is only a place, and not a city, and not a home. But they do not say this. They say: the Pipes need their work. The valves need their attention. The moss needs its tending. The children need their tall boots. They say these things, and they are true, and they are also the way they have learned to live in the warm orange glow without asking what makes the glow.

There are no animals. There is the small pale moth, but nobody calls it an animal. It is just part of the light. There are insects that feed on the small white flowers, but the citizens do not name these either, because to name them would be to admit that they love them, and to love them would be to ask how they came to be here, and to ask how they came to be here would be to ask about the vine, and to ask about the vine would be to ask about the Pipes, and to ask about the Pipes would be to ask about what is below. So the insects are not named. The moths are not named. The vine is called the vine. The city is called the city. The glow is called the glow.

Below the grates, in the warm orange half-light that rises and falls with the slow turning of the wheel, there is something. The citizens do not ask what it is. The moss has not asked. The ferns have not asked. The small pale moths have not asked. The Stokers go down, and the Stokers come back up, and their hands are stained a particular color, and this is all that is known. The something turns, and breathes, and glows, and is very old, and will be very old for a long time, and the city turns with it, and the citizens turn with the city, and the wheel turns with the citizens, and it is hard, after a while, to know which is turning which.
