What lives here is almost only plants.

The moss is the most of it. A foot and more in places, a thick green floor that drinks from a water table so high it never dries — that has perhaps not dried since the moss began. It is the green of a wet leaf in shadow at its darkest, an old-bruise green, and where the sun finds it the moss gives back a brighter emerald that seems, in certain slants of light, faintly lit from inside, as if it were running a small cooler process than the world around it. The moss is patient. The moss does not move except by the imperceptible growth of its years. The moss takes what falls. The moss takes what weeps. The moss holds its saturation and gives back, in the shaft of low sun, a glow that almost is its own light.

Then the fern, near the western wall. A single one. Its fronds as long as a person is tall, every pinna edged with droplets that hang and do not fall, that grow, slowly, to a size at which the leaf can no longer hold them and lets one go, and the drop falls the long way down to the moss, and the moss takes it in. The fern is the largest single thing alive in the hollow. It is the only fern. There is no second fern to cross with, no spore-bed, no prothallus generation cycling in the silt at the edge of the pool. The fern persists, somehow, alone. The droplets on its fronds are its own weather.

The small tree at the eastern edge leans. Not much — a few degrees off the vertical of the walls. Its broad soft leaves are never quite dry. They turn, in the slightest way, when the sun moves, so the light grazes them differently through the day. The tree is almost a sapling still. A person could walk around it. It is perhaps the youngest thing here, or perhaps simply the most patient of the slow things. It leans, like everything leans, toward the center, toward the pool, as if the pool were a thing it could drink with its whole body.

The orchids grow low. Several of them, in small clusters, in the moss between the tree and the pool. Thick leaves, fleshy, the color of a thing that has been wet for a very long time. And a pale bloom that never quite opens — a bloom that has the shape and the faint, sweet, almost-meat smell of something that is waiting to be pollinated, and that has been waiting, patiently, in the same half-open posture, for as long as the moss at the base of the wall has been growing. The orchid has not been told, or does not believe. It continues, year after year, in its half-open posture, offering what no one comes to take. The smell is faint and sweet and it is offered to the vapor, and the vapor does not refuse it, because the vapor is not the kind of thing that refuses.

The cups at the foot of the weeping stone. Dark green outside and a paler, iridescent green within, each one holding a finger's worth of water and never spilling it, even when the fern above is touched, even when something passes close. The cups are bait-cups. They are the shape of a thing that would catch a thing. Nothing comes to be caught. They hold their water for visitors that do not visit, in the form of an invitation that has not been refused because it has not been received. The iridescence inside each cup is a faint shifting green, like the inside of a shell, like the wing of something that does not fly here.

And a tiny moss, at the base of the hand-axe on the eastern ledge. A moss so small you would not see it unless you were looking. It has begun at the place where the stone meets the rock. It grows a little in a year.

That is what lives here. Plants. Plants, and probably the thin films of growth in the channels of the weeping walls — algae, perhaps, or the simplest lichen, the kind that is mostly a partnership of water and patience. No animals. No insects skating the surface of the pool. No fish. No frogs. No worms in the silt at the edge. No birds. No things that walk or fly or burrow or sing. The hollow is unbroken by any quick movement except the very slow movement of water and the very slow movement of plants.

The plants have, over a long time, taken up the work of animals. The cups hold water for creatures that do not come. The orchid opens for a pollinator that does not come. The lean toward the pool is the lean of bodies that have been drinking from the air for so long they have become shaped like the drinking. Even the moss — the moss drinks. The moss is mostly water already, mostly the hollow's own weather given the form of a plant. The line between plant and place has worn smooth, and the moss is partly the stone and the stone is partly the moss and the air is mostly water and the water is mostly the air.

And on a ledge of the eastern wall, at about the height of a hand resting on a hip, there is a stone. Dark, almost black. Shaped — not by water, not by weather, but by someone, with patience and a simpler tool — into a wedge, a hand-axe, a thing that fits a closed hand. It is not alive. But it is a sign that something once lived here that was not a plant, that was not a moss or a fern or an orchid. Something with hands. Something that could sit on a ledge and hold a stone and shape it with another stone, and that chose, for reasons we cannot read, to leave the shaped stone in the place where the wall weeps most, and then to go away, and not to come back.

The hand-axe is not alive. It is the only trace of the animal that was ever here. The moss has begun at its base. In a thousand years, perhaps, the moss will meet itself around the stone, and the hand-axe will be inside a small hill of green, and the weeping will continue, and no one will know there was ever a hand.

What lives here, then, is only the green patience of things that take and do not give back, that drink and do not walk, that lean a little toward the center of their own weather and grow. The hollow is unbroken by animal life. The hollow has, over its long running, become a place where the only animal that ever came has left only a hand-axe and a tiny moss at its base, and the moss grows a little in a year, and the wall weeps onto it, and the wall will weep onto it for a long time still.
