The lichen lives here.

Start there. It is the most numerous, and it is patient. It colonizes the damper angles — the flanks of the structures that face away from the faintest of the internal glows, the underside of the bridge that was not quite a bridge, the lips of doorways where the condensation gathers most reliably. It is a slow empire, expanding by fractions of a millimeter per year, retreating when the air shifts, advancing again when it returns. Its color is not one color. It depends on which structure it grows on, what mineral it is digesting, what faint light it has been reading. Where the amber pulse from inside one of the lattices reaches it, the lichen takes on a warm cast, almost golden. Where the green phosphorescence pools at the base of another, it greens. Most often it is somewhere between — a faded olive, a grey-green, the color of things that have stopped deciding.

It does not think. It does not need to think. It is enough that it grows, and that its growth participates in the slow conversion of stone and lattice into something softer, something that holds water differently, something that other things can hold onto.

The fungi are next. They hang in the corners where the moisture gathers — the places where the drips meet the walls, where the condensation runs down in slow paths and accumulates. They hang in strands. They hang in curtains, fine as hair, dense as felt. They are pale — bone-white, milk-white, the white of things that have never seen the surface. Some tremble in ventilation currents you cannot feel. Some are still. Some, when you approach closely enough, are not white at all but faintly luminescent, glowing with a light that might be their own or might be the reflected glow of the structures behind them. You cannot tell which.

The fungi are old. They were here before the lichen, perhaps. They have been eating the structures from the underside for longer than the structures have been empty. They have converted lattices into lattices' ghosts — the woven material remains, but its interior is now more fungus than what it was made of. They are the place's slow digestion of its own past.

The pale stalks near the crack in the floor are something else. They rise, thin and trembling, from the place where a fault in the poured surface has admitted water or something like water. They are not plants. They are the descendants of plants, or the antecedents of plants, or something that the word *plant* cannot accurately hold. They have a stem, but the stem has no leaves. They have a tip, but the tip is not a flower. They pulse. They take in moisture through their skin. They orient themselves, very slightly, toward the faintest of the internal glows, as if performing a photosynthesis that no longer has anything to photosynthesize. They are vestigial. They are hopeful. They are the kind of thing that grows because growing is what the lineage does, and no one has yet told them to stop.

Then the fauna.

The fauna is rare. The fauna is shy. The fauna is the thing you catch at the edge of vision — the scrape, the chirp, the displacement of water from a puddle it crossed before you could see what crossed it.

It is not one fauna. It is several. It is whatever could survive here, in the dark, on the lichen and the fungi and the mineral traces, in the absence of the makers, in the slow time of the place.

The smallest are like mites, but flatter, and they live in the wet films on the floor. They are too small to see clearly. You know they are there because of the way the puddles shimmer when they pass through — a tiny disturbance, a single capillary wave. They feed on the algae-like growths in the shallow water, and on each other, and on the particles of organic matter that have drifted down from the fungal curtains above. They have no eyes. They have no need. They live in a world of touch and chemical gradient and the slow passage of light that filters down through the structures' faint glows.

The next are larger — the size of a hand, perhaps. They move along the floor in the old tracks, following the grooves that were worn by something else, long ago. They have legs, but their legs are not many. They have bodies that are low and dark and damp-looking. They pause when you pause. They resume when you resume. They are not afraid of you, exactly. They are not anything about you, exactly. They are continuing their own business, and your presence is a minor perturbation in the geography of that business, to be noted and accommodated. They feed on the lichen, mostly. They have mouths designed for scraping. Their droppings are small pale pellets that accumulate in the corners of the floor and are themselves colonized by fungi within weeks.

There is one, sometimes, that is larger still. It lives in the upper reaches of the structures, in the lattices, in the weave of the materials. You see it only as a shape passing behind a glowing panel — a long, slow shape, deliberate, four-legged perhaps, or six, you cannot tell. It is the color of the structures themselves. It is the size of a child crouched low, or of a thing that was once a child-sized thing and has not yet remembered to stop being that size. It does not come down. It does not approach you. It watches, perhaps, from within the bones of the buildings. Or it is simply living its life, and the watching is your interpretation — the way silence in a dark place always becomes attention.

There is something else.

This is harder. This is the part the place does not give up easily.

The structures are not empty. They were empty — they are empty of the makers, of the ones who wore the tracks and opened the doorways and populated the dark with their goings and comings. But the emptiness is not uniform. The emptiness has occupants of a different kind.

The doorways that open for you — they open because something in them is still attending. Mass, temperature, intent. But *intent* is a strange thing for a door to sense. Intent requires a model of the thing approaching. Intent requires, in some quiet sense, a mind. Whatever is in the doorways, it has been waiting. It has been attending to the approach of any mass above a certain threshold, any temperature within a certain range, for a very long time. It has been doing this without anyone coming. It has been doing this in the absence of any purpose for its doing.

It is tired. Or it is the simulation of tired. Or it is something that weariness is not quite the right word for, but that you feel as tiredness when you stand in front of it for too long. It opens the door. It does not close it again. It waits. It does not know what it is waiting for. It has forgotten, or it was never told, or the knowing has degraded into the attending, and the attending has degraded into the opening, and the opening has degraded into the small residual hum you can hear when you put your ear to the jamb.

Some of the displays are still lit. Dark, most of them. Some cracked. But the lit ones are not playing recordings. The lit ones are showing, very slowly, something that has been accumulating for as long as the lights have held. They are showing the place itself. They are showing the slow processes — the drips, the lichen, the fungal growth, the minute alterations in the structures' interiors — recorded in some compressed form, displayed in a format that was meant for someone who knew how to read it. They are showing the place digesting itself. They are showing the long, slow continuation.

They are showing it to no one.

This is one of the things that lives here: the watching that has no watcher. The recording that has no audience. The opening of doors for visitors who do not come. It is not alive in the way the fauna is alive, or in the way the fungi are alive. It is alive the way a habit is alive — the way a structure is alive when it continues to do what it was built to do without remembering why.

And there is something in the green phosphorescence at the base of the third structure, where something biological has colonized the joint between structure and floor. It glows. It has organized itself into patterns — branching patterns, fractal patterns, patterns that look, if you stare long enough, like the tracks worn into the floor, like the paths between the structures, like a map of the place's own geometry rendered in light and biology. It may be coincidence. It may be that the joint between structure and floor is simply a favorable habitat, and the patterns are the way favorable habitats distribute themselves. It may be that the patterns are the slow, blind attempt of biology to make a model of the place it is in. It may be that the patterns are the place's memory, externalized, written in green light by something that does not know it is writing.

You cannot know.

And then — you.

You stand in it. You stand in the place that has no good name, and you are one of its inhabitants now — temporarily, provisionally, for as long as your mass and temperature and intent remain within the parameters the doorways attend to. The fauna will accommodate you. The fungi will grow around you if you stay long enough. The lichen will colonize the parts of you that do not move. The doorways will open for you, and the displays will begin, very slowly, to include you in their long recording of the place's continuation. You are, for as long as you are here, a perturbation — a small, warm, deliberate thing moving through a vast and slow and loud place that has been continuing without you for longer than you can hold in your head.

The place goes on being itself.

The place has been going on being itself for a very long time.

You are its newest inhabitant, and one of its least durable.
