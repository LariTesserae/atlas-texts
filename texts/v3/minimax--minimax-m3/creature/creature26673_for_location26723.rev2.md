They are the people of the held note.

There are more of them than can be counted and fewer than there seem to be, and the difference between the two has been worn away by the long saying of the place's name, which no one says any more because no one moves to say it. They fill the city the way the sound fills the air — completely, with no gap you can put a finger into, in layers so dense that the eye slides off them. A woman stands on a balcony above a man who stands on a balcony above a child who sits on a step above another woman pouring water from a jug, and on and on, down into the mist, up into the mist, the tiers receding into a haze that never thins and never deepens. From a bridge above you can see hundreds of them at once, an unbroken field of still figures, and from a street below you can see hundreds more, looking down. They are the city's other architecture.

Their skin is the brown of river clay, the brown of wet bark, the deep umber of polished wood, the deep rust of the cliffs themselves, as if the cliffs had peeled in the heat and walked. Some are darker, some lighter, but all of them have the same wet sheen from the air, the same fine beading of moisture on the shoulders and collarbones and the small of the back. Their hair is black and hangs long, braided with vine and with the small white flowers of an orchid that has not opened further and will not close. The braids are different on every head — every woman has plaited her own in a different pattern, a different number of strands, a different way of tucking the end — and so even in the stillness there is something legible, something you can read, the way you can read the grain of a piece of wood. Their cloth is undyed cotton and indigo and ochre and madder and chalk-white, plant-dyed and plant-woven, and where it is damp it clings to the body beneath it the way cloth in this air always does, and where it is dry it stands a little away from the skin and holds the shape of the last motion the body made. A dancer's shift is caught at the hip as though she has just turned. A mourner's wrap has the fall of a sleeve that was about to be raised.

They are of every age, and none of them has aged. There are babies in slings on their mothers' backs with their small hands closed around a fold of cotton, and grandmothers with faces like the cliff-folds, all weeping stone, and men in the thick of their strength with the muscles of their arms standing out as though they were about to lift something or throw something or embrace someone, and youths with the long eyes of those who are about to know something. The drummer is young. The keening women are old. The child reaching for the fruit is the age at which a child stops reaching for things and begins to grasp them — eight, perhaps nine — and he has been that age for as long as the eye can hold him. They were old when the moment caught them. They are old now in the same way. They are old the way a stone is old.

Their eyes are open. This is the thing that holds you most, that you cannot look away from and cannot bear to look at for long. The eyes are open and the pupils are dark and the whites are clear and the wetness on them — the air's wetness, the permanent mist condensed on the curve of the eyeball — does not run down. Each of them is looking at something. You can tell what. The woman with the jug is looking at the place where the water will land. The child is looking at the fruit. The man on the stair is looking up the stair, at the landing he is about to set his foot upon. None of them are looking at you. None of them are looking past you, either. They are looking at the thing their gesture is pointed at, the way a hand points, the way a bird's beak is the shape of the worm it was reaching for. They are at the end of their own arrows.

Their mouths are open. Most of them. The mouths are shaped around the sound they are making — but the sound is also shaped around them, has worn itself into them, and you can see, if you stand very near, that the lips of the keening women have stretched a little over the long duration of the held wail, that the mouths of the chanting men have taken on a faint permanent shape like the embouchure of a flute, that the drummer's mouth is slightly open because he was about to breathe in before the strike. They are slightly distorted by the song the way a shore is slightly distorted by the long patient work of the water. The drummer is the most beautiful thing in the city and the worst thing to look at for long, because his right hand is raised above the drumhead, fingers spread, wrist slightly turned, and the strike has not come, and the muscles of his forearm have the faintest tremor in them that is not a tremor — it is the muscle remembering what it was about to do, the way a bell that has been rung still moves in the air after the clapper has touched it. He is a bell that has been ringing for as long as bells have been thought of.

What were they doing, when the moment held?

They were at the height of something. There is no other word for it. There was a feast, a rite, a wedding, a funeral, a birth — it doesn't matter which; it might have been all of them at once, the whole city come together for the appointed hour at which everything in life that is worth marking is marked at once. The drums were struck. The flutes were raised. The dancers leaned into the dance. The children ran through the legs of the dancers and laughed. The water was poured. The fruit was hung from the vines in the high windows. The sun came up over the rim of the valley and stood there, enormous and orange and low, and the people lifted their faces to it and said, with the whole force of their lungs and the whole weight of their instruments: *this is the hour, this is the apex, this is the place the world stops.*

And the world stopped.

They do not know this. From the inside of the held moment there is no way to know it. The drummer does not know he has not struck. To him, the strike is coming — is always coming, is one more breath away, is right there in the muscle of his forearm. The child does not know the fruit is not in his hand. The fruit is right there. The fingers are three inches away. Any moment now. The woman with the jug does not know the water has hung in its arc since before anyone can say when. The water is on its way down. It is almost there. The man on the stair does not know he is forever mid-step. He is at the top of the climb. He is about to set his foot on the landing. He has been about to set his foot on the landing for so long that the about-to has become the only thing he is.

From the inside, the moment is complete and eternal. They are not waiting. They are arriving.

And yet something has happened to them. The held moment has not left them untouched.

It has worked on them the way the air works on the cliffs — slowly, patiently, with a wet hand. The edges of them are beginning to blur. If you stand close to the woman on the balcony — close enough to see the fine grain of her cheek, close enough to count the beads of water on her shoulder — you can see that the line of her jaw is a little less definite than a jaw should be, that the back of her hand has the faintest texture of stone in it, that her fingers, where they grip the lip of the jug, are a little longer than fingers ought to be and a little too curved, the way a root curves around a rock it has been holding for a long time. The buildings lean into each other for support. The people lean into each other for support. Where they touch — shoulder to shoulder, arm to arm, the small of one back against the chest of the one behind — they have begun, very slowly, to grow into one another. The vines do this. The cliffs do this. The people are learning to do this too.

They are being slowly, gently, over the unimaginable duration of the held moment, replaced by the shapes of their own gestures. The dancer is becoming the dance. The singer is becoming the song. The drummer is becoming the drum. The woman is becoming the pour. The child is becoming the reach.

This is why there are no animals. Only humans can hold a gesture long enough for the gesture to take them. Animals would notice. Animals would shake themselves and walk away. Only a person can be so absorbed in what they are doing that they forget to be a person, and only here, at the height of the absorption, can the forgetting become permanent. The crickets would have noticed after the first night that there was no second night. The dogs would have barked. The pigeons would have flown. The people did not notice, because they were at the height of their joy, and the joy was everything, and there was nothing outside the joy to notice with.

The repetition — the same drummer on the same corner, the same child reaching, the same woman pouring — is not a repetition of bodies. It is the geometry of the looping valley. The cliffs fold into themselves, the paths return, and so when you walk you meet yourself coming back. There is only one drummer. There is only one woman with the jug. There is only one child reaching for the one fruit. The city is dense because there is one of each of them, multiplied by the turning of the paths, by the mist that hides the loops, by the eye that fills in the gaps with copies. Or — and this is also true, and harder to tell from inside the city — there were once many of each, and they have leaned into each other for support until they have become indistinguishable, until the drummer on one corner and the drummer on the next corner share an arm, until the women pouring water share a stream, until the children reaching share a hand. The city is a hive. The city is a reef. Things that press close enough for long enough grow together. Things that grow together become one thing. The mist hides which of these is true. The mist has always hidden which of these is true.

And the sound.

The sound is them. There is no way to separate the people from the noise they are making. They are the noise, sustained. The sound has weight. You can feel it against your chest, against the soft parts of your face, against the backs of your knees. It is not painful. It is not even loud, in the way we usually mean loud. It is just present, the way a wall is present, the way the air is present. It has been going for so long it has worn grooves in the air — you can feel them with your teeth when you stand very still, the way you can feel the grain of a piece of wood with your fingertips. The drums. The flutes. The bells of beaten stone and clay and hollow wood. The children shouting. The women keening the long sustained note that is not quite a wail and not quite a song. The men chanting low. The laughter in it. The thing in it that might once have been joy.

The drummer's hand is held above the drumhead, and the strike has never come, and the note has never wavered, and the laughter has never finished, and the wail has never broken into either a sob or a word. They are the loudest silence you have ever heard, because silence is what they would be making if they stopped, and they cannot stop, and the silence is forever one breath away, and one breath is all of time, and they are holding it.

If you could unstick the moment — if some great and gentle wind could pass through the valley and lift the held note from their throats and let the drummer's hand come down — they would not know. They would simply finish. The water would reach the floor. The child would close his hand on the fruit. The man would set his foot on the landing. The drummer would strike the drum, and the sound would go on, and the dancers would lean into the next step, and the laughter would come to its end and begin again.

And they would look up. And the sun would still be in the wrong place, enormous and orange and low. And the cliffs would still be weeping. And there would still be no animals. And the mist would still not have an edge.

But they would be moving again. They would be moving, and they would not know what they had escaped.

They are the people of the held note. They are the city of the held moment, frozen at the height of its own joy. The joy has worn grooves in them. The grooves are the shape of the song. The song is the shape of them. They have been this for so long that the difference between *is* and *was* has been worn away in them too, the way the name of the place has been worn away by their own long saying of it, which they do not remember doing, which they have never stopped doing.

They are beautiful. They are terrible. They are the stillest thing you have ever seen and the loudest. They are waiting for the strike of the drum, and the strike is one breath away, and the breath has been held since before the mist had a name.
