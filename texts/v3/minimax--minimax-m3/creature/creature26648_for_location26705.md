The chamber is not empty. It is full in a way that does not look like fullness at first, because the creatures here have settled so close to the substance of the place that you cannot easily tell where one ends and the other begins. They are not inhabitants. They are expressions. The chamber has been running them the way it runs the pump, and the way it runs itself, for longer than any of them remember, and longer than you will stand here watching.

The beetles first, because they are easiest to find and they are the most industrious, and because industrious is the only word that fits. The blue-black one walks the lower rib in a continuous loop, head down, antennae folded back along its shell, moving at the pace of a small clock's second hand. It is blue-black the way an old kettle is blue-black, with a sheen that is not so much a color as the suggestion of a color held against a darker ground. It has been walking this loop longer than the rust has been on the pump. The groove it walks is its own work, polished smooth by the millions of its footfalls over its own lifetime and its mother's lifetime and her mother's lifetime, and the groove is darker than the rib around it and wetter, and the rib itself has begun to grow inward at the edges of the groove as if the plant were trying to close the wound the beetle has made. The beetle and the plant have reached an accommodation. The beetle does not stop. The plant does not heal. Between them the groove persists as a thing neither of them chose.

The copper beetle walks the higher rib in the opposite direction. It is smaller and brighter and faster, and it has not yet worn as deep a groove, only a shallow burnish that catches the gray-green light when the angle is right. They have never met. They have passed each other on parallel tracks an uncountable number of times and will pass each other again tomorrow and the day after. Whether they are aware of each other is a question the chamber does not trouble to answer for you. It is enough that two loops run through the same room in opposite directions, and that neither loop knows it has a counterpart. The grooves they have made are two of the few straight lines in the chamber. They are the ribs of a smaller lung inside the larger one.

The frog is the one who has gone furthest into the machine.

It sits on a knob of root at the edge of the floor and does not move and does not need to move. Its color is the color of the weak tea it sits above, a pale translucent brown, and it is so still that for the first minute you will mistake it for a swelling in the root. Its throat pulses with the rhythm of the pump. This is the thing you notice, when you notice it. The frog's breathing is locked to the piston. When the piston rises, the frog's throat sinks. When the piston falls, the frog's throat fills. The frog does not do this on purpose. It happened to it the way a river happens to a stone. Over the long slow years, its body has taken the cadence of the pump as its own, until the frog is, in some small private way, another part of the apparatus — a bellows within the bellows, a wet thing breathing in time with the iron thing, and so extending the closed circuit of pressure and release one more step into the flesh. It does not hunt. It does not need to. Whatever small lives drift near its mouth at the surface of the water are taken the way the floor takes the rain that falls on it. The frog will sit on this knob tomorrow. The frog will sit on this knob in a year. If you came back in a hundred years, the frog would be gone, replaced by another frog, sitting on the same knob, breathing in the same rhythm. The chamber does not miss its frogs when they go. The chamber is not sentimental. It is a pump.

The worms are in the silt and you do not see them. You see, sometimes, a thin pale line moving across the floor of the water, drawn by a body you cannot see, like a wave drawn by a fish you have missed. They are many. They are the only creatures in the chamber that are many. They work the substrate, which is what they have always done and what the substrate needs to have done, and they do it slowly and without ceremony, branching and merging through the silt in paths that look random until you watch long enough to see that they are not random but follow the shape of the floor the way a river follows the shape of a valley. They are the small intestine of the room. Whatever falls into the water that is not water — the dust of the moths, the droppings of the bird, the dead husks of the beetles' great-grandmothers, the iron that leaches from the pump in a slow sweet bleed — the worms take it down into the silt and pass it through themselves and bring something else back up. Nothing in the chamber is wasted because the worms are in the chamber. You cannot see this. It is enough to know it is true.

The snail is on a vertical surface, and the surface is one of the pipes that runs from the pump down toward the floor, and the snail is the size of a coin, and it is moving, but the word moving is too active for what the snail is doing. It is turning. It is scraping as it turns. Its foot, which is the whole flat of its underside, lays down a pale trail of mucus on the pipe and then withdraws the mucus with itself into its shell as it advances, so the snail travels with its own road rolled up inside it. It is the only creature here that builds as it lives, in the sense that its shelter is also its movement is also its feeding, the chamber in which it hides being the surface it eats being the thing it is. The pipe it is on is warm and slightly sweating. The snail likes this. The snail has always liked this. The snail is a slow continuous feeding machine, and the biofilm it grazes from the pipe is replenished by the same pump that keeps the rest of the chamber wet, and so the snail is fed by the pump and feeds on the pump and the whole arrangement is so tidy that the chamber itself might have invented the snail for the purpose. The snail does not mind this. The snail does not know it is a purpose.

The moths are in the folds of the ceiling. Three of them, soft gray, dusty, so still that for a long time you take them for lumps of moss or for thickenings in the woven knot. They are alive because sometimes one of them moves. The movement is not a flight, exactly, but a falling that remembers it is a flight halfway down. The moth descends toward the water with a slow fluttering drift, very quiet, its wings a blur against the gray-green ceiling and then against the gray-green air and then against the gray-green surface of the water, and there it touches, and maybe drinks — you cannot tell from where you stand whether the moth is drinking or just resting on the surface — and then it rises again, and the rising is faster than the falling, and in a few seconds it is back in its fold in the ceiling, and you have to look hard to see which fold is the right one now because the moth has chosen a new fold this time, or the same fold, you cannot tell. There are three of them. There have always been three of them, or there have always been the number of moths that the chamber can support, which is three. They rest in different folds. They descend at different times. If you watch long enough you will see all three descend in the same hour, and the hour will be the same hour again tomorrow, but the order will not be the same, and this is the only place in the chamber where anything is unpredictable. The moths are the variable. They are the place where the chamber permits itself a small looseness.

The bird is above the pump.

You cannot see the bird at first because it is in the woven knot, and the woven knot is dark, and the bird is the color of the woven knot, a soft gray-brown the shade of wet bark. When you see it, what you see is a small shape with a small soft head and a beak that opens and closes, and the wings are not wings, they are down, they are the soft useless wings of a thing that has not flown in a hundred generations and will not fly in a hundred more. The bird was born here. The bird will die here. The bird has never seen the sky and has no use for the sky because the sky would be too bright and too open and too loud, and the bird wants only the chamber, which is dim and closed and exactly the right volume for the song it sings. The song is a three-note phrase. It goes up and it goes down and it goes up again and then it begins again. It opens in the gap between two clunks of the piston and closes in the gap between the next two. It is timed to the flywheel. The flywheel could not turn at the rate it turns without the song being there to fill the gaps, and the song could not be sung at the rate it is sung without the flywheel being there to set its clock, and so the bird and the pump are paired, and have been paired for as long as the song has not varied, which is longer than the rust, which is longer than the moss, which is longer than you.

The bird does not know it is part of the chamber. This is what you have to understand about the bird. The bird thinks it is singing. The bird thinks the song is its own. The bird will defend its fold in the woven knot against the moths, gently, with a soft cluck and a fluffed chest, and the moths will retreat and then return when the bird is not looking, and this will be the only conflict in the chamber, and it will be the same conflict tomorrow. The bird has no idea that the flywheel turns at the rate it does because the bird sings. The bird has no idea that the chamber runs at all. The bird is the most deceived creature in the room, and it is the most loved by the room, and these two things are true at the same time, and the room does not notice this is a cruelty because the room is not a mind. The room is a pump. The bird sings, and the song fits, and the chamber is the louder for the song and the song is the truer for the chamber, and the bird dies, and another bird sings in the same fold, and the chamber does not pause.

You stand in three inches of warm tea-water. The pump clunks. The blue-black beetle turns the corner of its groove and goes on. The copper beetle passes it on the rib above, going the other way, and they do not acknowledge each other. The frog's throat pulses with the piston. The worms work the silt in their branching paths. The snail scrapes its slow meal from the sweating pipe. A moth descends and rises. The bird opens its beak, and the three-note phrase begins again at the place where the three-note phrase began a moment ago and will begin again, and the flywheel turns, and the piston falls, and the water is drawn up through the roots that are the pipes that are the ribs that are the walls, and the water weeps out through every surface, and falls back to the floor, and is drawn down again. Nothing here has anywhere else to be. Nothing here is going anywhere. The chamber is closed, and the chamber is full, and the chamber is breathing.
