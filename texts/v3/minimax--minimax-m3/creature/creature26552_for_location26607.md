The foundry hands are the heart of it, if a thing so liquid can be said to have a heart. They come up out of the casting bays in their hundreds when the horn sounds, and they pour through the streets the way a slow river pours through a narrows — not quickly, not with any particular urgency, but with the weight of all that water behind them, and the streets were not built for this and the streets know it. Their clothes are the same color as the brick — a dark, water-stained maroon — and their skin is the same color darker, and they smell of iron filings and sulfur and the particular sweat of a body that has been in heat for twelve hours and has not dried. They do not speak. They have not the breath. They move in a kind of procession, and if you stand in it you will be moved with them, and no one will apologize because no one is in a position to apologize, and the apology would be an extra breath no one has.

The draymen are made of the same stuff but harder. They work the horses, which is to say they endure the horses, which is to say they have made their peace with the horses in a way that does not require liking. A drayman is a man who has stopped being surprised by weight. He leans into the harness and the harness leans into him and the horse leans into the harness and the cart leans into all of it, and somewhere in the middle is a man who has been leaning so long his spine has curved to match. His hands are thick. His voice, when he uses it — for the horse, never for the people — is a low rough syllable, two notes at most, and the horse knows the notes and the cart knows the notes and the cobblestones know the notes. The drayman has not been indoors in any meaningful way for years. He lives on the dock. The dock is where his work is and where his horse is and where, in the small hours, he sleeps in the cart with one hand on the lead.

The women are harder to see. They are inside. The milliners, who keep cats, are in the upper rooms of the brick buildings on the north side, where the light is worst and the damp is least. The wives of the foundry hands are in the worker housing up the hill toward the freshwater springs, and they come down in the morning with the shift and go up in the evening with the shift and in between they are at the markets and the laundries and the small churches that no one is sure anyone still believes in but that keep their doors open because the doors being open is a kind of keeping-on. The women do not have the foundry's particular exhaustion. They have a different one, a more diffuse one, the exhaustion of a person who is always wet and has stopped noticing the wet except in the joints, which ache, and in the children, who do not thrive here the way children should, and in the men, who come home smelling of a heat that is not the same as the heat outside.

The children are pale and quick and they do not run so much as they dart. They know the streets in a way the adults do not — the ditches, the gaps in the fences, the cellars with their loose boards, the route to the marsh that does not go past any of the mill yards. They are unkind to each other in the casual way of children who have not been told to be otherwise. They are the only ones who climb. There is almost nothing to climb here, but they find it — the ironwork of the bridges, the leaning chimneys of the brickworks (forbidden, but climbed), the roofs of the lower outbuildings, from which they can see across the town to the marsh and the marsh to the sea. They go up because there is no place else to go up to, and when they come down they are damp and the iron has marked them and they are quieter for a while.

The marsh people are not a separate people anymore. They have been absorbed the way the pile-dwellings were absorbed, into the mud, into the foundations. The dockworkers with the wide cheekbones and the slight webbing between the fingers — that is rumored, that webbing; no one has ever been asked to confirm it — are said to be of the marsh people, and so are the draymen with the narrow eyes, and so are the women in the southern ward who will not eat pig. They do not speak of it. They are the town. The town does not speak of them.

The drayman at the public landing is the first of them you will meet and the last you will remember, if you remember any of them. He is waiting with his cart when the barge comes in at dawn, and he does not greet you. He takes your trunk and he walks and you follow, and if you ask him a question he will answer it in the same voice he uses for the horse — two notes, low, and the answer will be the shortest one that is still an answer, and he will not turn around to see whether you have understood. He has done this a thousand times. He will do it a thousand more. He is not unkind. He is not anything in particular. He is the town in the form of a man, and the town does not care that you have arrived.

The dogs are the next thing you notice after the people, or before the people, depending on how you arrive. They are the size of dogs and the shape of dogs and they have given up being anything else. They are not fed on a schedule. They are fed on what is given, and what is given is irregular, and the dogs have made their peace with the irregularity in the way of dogs — they are present, they are attentive, they are not hopeful. Each has a territory — a dock, a yard, a tavern, a stretch of street — and within that territory it is the authority on what falls and what is consumed, and this authority is not contested because the next dog is also the authority on its own territory and the two territories meet at a line no one has marked but everyone knows. They are wary of people and people are wary of them. A dog will let you pass if you are passing. A dog will not let you pass if you are stopping. The knowing is mutual and does not require discussion.

At night the dogs sing. This is not a metaphor. The first dog begins, somewhere in the south ward, and the note it gives is low and long and is not a bark and is not a howl but something in between that the dog has been saving up all day. The next dog takes it up, and the next, and within a few minutes the whole town is in it — every ward, every yard, every dock, the sound rising and falling in waves the way a wave rises and falls, but never quite dying, never quite being silent, just moving from one throat to the next. The herons on the marsh do not stir. The gulls on the harbor do not stir. The town, which has been quiet all day, all week, all the long hot muffled day, is loud for ten or fifteen minutes, and then it is quiet again, and the dogs go back to what they were doing, which is lying in the wet and being patient.

The horses are worked and they work. They are not pets. They are not even, in the way the draymen would put it, animals — they are equipment, they are muscle, they are the draft mechanism that pulls the cart that carries the rolled iron to the rail yard. They are large and they are not gentle and they are not asked to be gentle. They are fed hard, on grain and on the by-products of the rendering works, which gives their breath a particular sweet-sour note that you can smell from three streets away. Their coats are never dry. In summer they steam. In winter they steam worse. The handlers do not love them. The handlers do not need to. The arrangement is older than any of them and will outlast any of them, and the horses have made their own peace with the arrangement in the way horses do — by continuing, by pulling, by being present in the morning at the dock and not being asked whether they want to be.

The cattle in the pens along the river are the next thing in size, and they are also the next thing in patience. They come up from the south in their hundreds, driven on the hoof, and they stand in the pens and they low, and the lowing is a sound that carries across the water at night and is, in its way, the song the town does not have. They do not know where they are. They know they are waiting. They know that the waiting will end. They are wet and they are hot and they are patient, and their patience is the patience of things that have not been told that patience is a virtue, because it is not a virtue, it is just what is left.

The pigs in the southern ward are smaller and they are not patient. They are fed on the offal of the cattle that have stopped being patient, and they are kept in pens of their own, and they smell of what they are fed on, and the smell is the smell of the southern ward at any hour of any day, and the smell is what the marsh smells like at low tide, and the smell is what the rendering works smell like when the rendering works are running, which is always. The pigs are not seen much. They are heard, sometimes, when the pens are cleaned, and they are smelled always. The wild pigs in the marsh are a different thing. They have escaped, in their generations, and they have reverted, and they are bristled and tusked and they move in the reeds in a way that is not seen so much as inferred, by the breaking of the reeds, by the sudden silence of the rails, by the dropping of a half-eaten thing. They are hunted in season. Out of season they are the rat-catchers of the southern cannery district, and the arrangement is tolerated because the alternative is worse.

The rats own the cellars. This is the simplest way to put it. The cellars of the older brick buildings, and of the timber buildings that are now under the brick buildings, are full of rats in a way that is not an infestation but a tenancy. They are fat. They are unhurried. They know the routes that the people above do not know — the runs behind the plaster, the gaps in the brick where the mortar has gone to paste, the channels where the tide comes in under the foundations. They come up at night and they take what is left and they take what is not left and the taking is not contested because to contest it would be to go down there, and no one goes down there, and the rats know no one goes down there, and the arrangement is perfect.

The cats are the only thing that contests the rats, and they contest them imperfectly. The milliners keep them and the warehouse keepers keep them, and they are not fed, exactly, but they are permitted, and the permission is enough. They are thin and they are watchful and they are not friendly to people, and they are not asked to be. They live in the upper rooms and the eaves and they come down at dusk and they go up at dawn and the rest of their life is their own. There is a cat in the Customs House — gray, old, with a torn ear — that the mockingbird tolerates, and the tolerating is the closest thing to a peace the Customs House has known in a hundred years.

The mockingbird. There is a mockingbird in the ivy on the north wall of the old Customs House, and it is a mockingbird, and it sings, and what it sings is not its own song but the songs of birds that are not here. It sings the song of a wren and the song of a finch and the song of something that might be a warbler and the song of something that might be a thrush, and none of these birds live here, and the mockingbird is singing them anyway, from memory or from inheritance, and the singing is the only singing in the town apart from the dogs and the lowing of the cattle, and the people point it out to visitors the way other towns point out a cathedral, and the mockingbird does not know it is a landmark and would not care if it did.

The herons are on the marsh. They are always on the marsh. They stand in the shallows in their patient congregation, and they wait for the mosquito's brief adulthood, and they do not mind the marsh, and the marsh does not mind them. The mosquitoes come up out of the standing water in such quantity that the air over the marsh is, in summer, a kind of slow rain going up, and the herons stand in it and eat it, and the eating is the only ordering of the marsh that anyone has ever attempted.

And the dead. The dead are in the marsh too, in their way, taken out at high tide to the deeper channels and given over to the water and the water's concern. They are not spoken of. They are not visited. They are not marked, except by the herons, who do not mark them on purpose but who stand over them, and the standing is its own kind of marking. The town has no cemetery. The town has the marsh. The marsh has the dead. The herons have the marsh. The arrangement is older than the brick, and the brick knows it, and the herons do not.

And the barge comes in the morning. It comes up the river at dawn, when the fog is still thick and the town is only a smell and a sound, and it puts in at the public landing, and the planks are slick, and a drayman is there with his cart, and he does not greet you, and the town does not greet you, and the barge will go downriver again at the evening tide, and you may be on it or you may not, and the town will not have noticed, and the herons will not have noticed, and the mockingbird will not have noticed, and the dogs, somewhere in the south ward, are already gathering themselves for the night's singing.
