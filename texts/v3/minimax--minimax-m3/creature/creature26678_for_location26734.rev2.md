The plants, first, because they are the most.

A handspan of lichen here might be three inches thick and pulsing faintly with a rhythm you can feel through the stone if you put your palm down and wait. The rhythm is slower than a heartbeat, faster than a tide. It is not quite regular. It is the rhythm of something that is not in a hurry to be alive.

The colors are wrong for plants. That is one of the first things you learn about them, and one of the last things you understand. Some are the deep brick-red of dried blood, the kind of red that says something has been hurt and is not going to recover. Some are pale violet, the color of a bruise on its second or third day, when the swelling has gone down and the skin is beginning to remember its own color underneath. Some are black, and the black is not the matte black of soot but the glittery black of a beetle's wing, or a flake of mica in a piece of granite, as if each cell contained a tiny mirror. A few are almost white, almost luminous, and these are the ones you see first from a distance, faint smudges of light against the pewter rock, the way the eyes of an animal caught in a headlamp are faint smudges of light against the dark.

The black ones are the most numerous. The white ones are the rarest. The red and the violet grow in the middle distances, between the glitter of the black and the glow of the white.

You don't know what they eat.

This is the question that follows you around the mountain. You ask it of every patch, and no patch answers. They do not seem to eat in any way you have a word for. They do not put down roots you can find. They do not have mouths. They do not seem to need water, although the air is anhydrous, and they do not seem to need sun, although the sky is the color of old pewter and gives back no particular light. They seem, instead, to persist — to be made of a kind of matter that is content to simply continue being matter, the way a stalactite is content to continue being calcium carbonate, except that what they are continuing to be is alive.

After a long time on the mountain you begin to notice that the densest patches are always where the hum piles up. In the lee of outcroppings. Under shelf-rocks. In the troughs between ridges, where the wind is forced into eddies and the scream of itself is compressed. You begin to notice that the underside of every shelf-rock in the place is furred with growth, even where the wind never reaches, even where the air never moves — as if the hum, when it hits a surface, leaves something behind, and the plants are the residue.

You begin to wonder, after a while, whether they eat the sound.

You can't prove it. But you sit in the lee of a boulder with your back against the stone, and you wait, and you begin to feel the hum through your spine. You begin to feel the thick drape of dark growth around you pulse a little faster. You begin to feel, very faintly, the plants lean toward you. Not toward you as a person. Toward where you are sitting. As if you are a hole in the hum, and they would like to be closer to the hole.

You don't stay long enough to find out what they would do if they reached you.

You do notice, as you sit, that the black ones glitter a little brighter when you shift your weight against the boulder. You notice that the flakes in their cells — if they are flakes — catch the dim light and turn it sideways, in a way the light around them does not. You begin to think the black ones are the densest cells in the densest patches, and that they are doing something with the hum that the lighter cells are not. You begin to think the flakes are not flakes at all. You begin to think they are tiny receivers, or tiny drums, or tiny pieces of a thing that does not exist below this mountain, and that they are turning the hum into something the plants can use, the way a leaf turns light into something the leaf can use, except that what they are turning it into is not sugar and is not growth and is not anything you have a word for.

You begin to think the white ones are the exhaust. They glow the way a phosphor glows after the light has gone — not by making light, but by releasing a light that was put into them. They glow because they are full. They glow because they are discharging what the black ones have taken in and made more than the patch can hold, the way a flower opens to get rid of what the roots have drawn up.

You don't know if this is right. You don't know if any of this is right. You only know that, after a long time on the mountain, you begin to see the patches the way you would see a landscape if you knew how to read landscapes — as a system, as an economy, as a long quiet transaction between the rock and the air.

The structures.

The places where someone was here, and is now not here, are scattered across the upper slopes like punctuation. A low wall of loose stones here, partly knocked down. A tripod of three pale poles there, lashed together at the top. A circle of small upright slabs no taller than your knee, set so deep into the ground that the wind has not been able to move them, although the wind has tried.

They are the marks of hands. Nothing else on this mountain has hands.

The tripod is the most made thing you find. Three poles of pale wood, smooth and almost white, lashed together at the top with strips of something that might be hide and probably isn't. The lashings are gone. Only the worn places where they were remain, the way a fence post remembers a wire. The wood is the color of bone that has been in the sun for a long time. It does not splinter. It does not rot. It does not look like any wood you have seen in a forest below. It looks more like the wood of a pole that has been in the sea for years and has given up trying to be a tree. It is patient wood. It is wood that has decided to be a pole.

The tripod leans. It has been leaning for a long time. The wind passes through it and makes a noise that is not quite a voice and not quite a whistle and not quite the scream of the place. It is the noise of something trying to be heard. You stand near it for a long time, and the noise continues, and you begin to think the tripod is part of the hum now — that it has become an instrument, the way the rocks have become instruments, the way the air has become an instrument, and that whatever made it is now part of what it makes.

The poles remember their lashings. You can see this in the worn places at the joints. You can see it in the way the tripod still holds itself together, by the patient gravity of having been held together, the way a cairn holds itself together by the geometry of its stones. The tripod does not need its lashings. It has decided to be a tripod, and that decision has lasted longer than the materials of the decision.

The low wall is older, or it was knocked down harder. The stones have slid into a kind of fan at one end. The wall was never high — it never reached your waist — and it was not straight, and it was not long. It was the kind of wall you build to say *here*, the way a dog walks the perimeter of a place to say *here*. It says *here*. It says *here* in the dim light, in the wind, in the place where no one will come to see it. It says *here* the way the cairns say *here* — faintly, and to no one in particular, and for a long time.

The circle of slabs is the most careful. Each slab is knee-height, and each is set into the ground so the wind cannot lift it. There are about seven of them. They are arranged in a ring that is not quite a circle — a circle that has been slightly pinched, as if it were trying to be a circle and not quite succeeding, the way the rocks are trying to be rocks and not quite succeeding. Inside the ring there is nothing. No ash, no bones, no offerings. Just bare rock, and a thin scatter of pale lichen that grows only inside the ring and not outside it.

The people who made these things were small. Either the people were small or the things were small, or both. They had hands. They had the impulse to set a pole against another pole and lash them, to lay a stone against another stone, to stand a slab upright in the dirt and let it stay. They came up here in the dim light, in the wind, in the place where the hum fills the silence and the silence has given up competing. They stood back and looked at what they had made. They walked away.

The walking is the part of the story that has gone missing.

You do not find them. You do not find their shelter, their hearth, their graves. You find only the marks, and the marks are going. The cairns you find on the saddles — if you find them, if they are there when you find them — are smudges in the air where the cairns should be. You stand where a cairn should be and you can almost see it, the way you can almost see a word at the edge of your vision that you know is there but cannot read. The cairns are between being cairns and being rock, the way the rocks are between being rock and being something else.

The people who made the marks are between being people and being the marks. They are in the slow process of becoming part of the place. You can feel this. You can feel it the way you feel the hum — not as a thing you can point to, but as a thing you stand inside of.

You begin to think the small people are still here. Not as people. As a kind of sediment, the way the lichen is a kind of sediment of the hum. As a slow quiet addition to the patient weight of the mountain. You begin to think that whatever walked away from the tripod did not walk very far, and that what they walked into was the rock, and that the rock accepted them the way the rock accepts the plants — slowly, and as part of the same long transaction.

---

The mountain itself.

The mountain is not finished. You keep coming back to this. The ridges have not yet learned to be sharp. The valleys have not yet decided what they are draining. The scree is bright in a way that scree should not be bright, because there has been no weather to dull it, except that there has been weather, there has been nothing but weather, and the weather has been at it for a length of time you cannot read from the mountain, because the mountain is too new to have a memory of itself.

The rocks are the wrong rocks.

If you pick one up and hold it, you can feel that it is heavy in the way high-altitude rocks are heavy, but the heaviness is not quite right. The rock is denser than it should be, or less dense, or dense in a way your hand cannot quite calibrate. If you look at the rock closely, your eyes will not quite agree with what they are seeing. The grain is there but it keeps shifting. The color is there but it slides if you stare too long. There is a surface to the rock, and the surface is not quite committed to being a surface. You can press your thumb against the rock and feel that it is cold, and the cold is the cold of stone, and the stone is the stone of a mountain, but somewhere in the cold and the stone there is a hesitation. The rock is still deciding whether it is going to be rock, or whether it is going to be something else.

The something else, you begin to think, might be the plants. The plants are what the rocks become when they are finally persuaded to give up being rocks. The plants are the exhale of the mountain. The mountain is breathing out, very slowly, and the breath is becoming the patches, the curtains, the festoons, the pulsing three-inch mats of brick-red and pale violet and glittering black. The plants are the part of the mountain that has made it out of the mountain. They are the part that has decided.

This is why the plants grow in the lee of outcroppings, where the hum piles up. The hum is what convinces the rock. The hum is the persuasion. The wind is just the carrier. The mountain is the throat, and the hum is the voice, and the voice is saying *become* over and over, and the things that become are the things that grow.

The things that grow are not the only things that become.

The cairns become smudges. The tripods become instruments. The people who made the tripods become the tripods. The rocks become the plants. The plants become the hum. The hum becomes the mountain. The mountain becomes the wind. The wind becomes the silence that has given up competing.

You are sitting in the lee of a boulder with your back against the stone. The hum is louder than it was when you sat down. The dim light has shifted in color, the way the sky over a fire shifts when the fire is almost out. The plants around you are leaning very slowly toward you, the way a sunflower leans toward the sun, except that what they are leaning toward is not the sun. What they are leaning toward is the place where you are, the hole you are making in the hum, the interruption you are.

You begin to wonder whether you are being persuaded too.

You stand up. The wind hits you. The hum hits you. The sky is the same pewter, the same forgotten warmth, the same nowhere-time. The ridge ahead of you is, when you look at it, definitely a ridge, and it definitely slopes to the left, and you definitely know which way is down.

You start walking.

Behind you, in the lee of the boulder, the dark growth settles slowly back into the rock, as if it has been watching you and has decided, for now, that there is nothing more to watch. The tripod leans a little further in the wind. The place continues to be made, slowly, in the dim light, under the constant scream of itself.

You do not look back. You are not sure, today, what *back* would mean.
