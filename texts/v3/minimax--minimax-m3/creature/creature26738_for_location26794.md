# The Inhabitants

The smiths' hands are the first thing you learn to read, if you stay long enough in the Reaches to learn anything at all. They are broad across the palm, dark across the back, scarred in patterns that repeat across generations with the faithfulness of a family resemblance. A burn across the knuckle of the index finger where the apprentice first holds the tongs wrong. A thickening of the web between thumb and forefinger where the hammer handle has rubbed for years. A small missing piece of the left ear on those who have worked too close to a forge-fire that breathed outward unexpectedly. The nails are kept short—they would catch and tear—and the cuticles are black with fine iron dust that no amount of washing fully removes. Their hands move with the deliberateness of people who have learned that a slip costs a finger or an eye.

Their eyes are the second thing. They are set deeper in the skull than is common elsewhere, and they are dark, and they are slightly opaque, like the pools in certain light. They are not large in the way of true night-eyes; they do not gleam or catch the forge-glow. But they hold more of the dim light that comes to them, gather it slowly, return it in a careful gaze that visitors from open lands find difficult to meet. The smiths can see across a smoke-hazed courtyard in a way that newcomers cannot, and they do not seem to understand why this is remarkable. Their eyes are simply eyes that have grown to the perpetual dusk.

Their lungs are the third thing, though you cannot see them, only know them by their breathing. The smoke of the forges never quite clears—it hangs among the buildings in grey-blue layers, a stratified atmosphere within the atmosphere—and the smiths have learned to breathe through it as one learns to breathe through water. Newcomers cough for weeks. Children born in the Reaches do not cough at all. When the smiths die, the village physicians—such as they are, and there are not many, for the place has little patience for those who do not also work the metal—find their lungs mottled, black and pink in patches, and they say this is normal, this is the lung of a smith, this is healthy in the only way that matters here. The smiths do not find this troubling. They have always known that the air is what it is.

They are wiry, all of them, lean in the way that comes from heat and heavy work and a diet of iron and iodine. There is almost no fat beneath their skin. The tendons stand out on their forearms; the muscles are dense and ropy; the bodies are low to the ground by nature as well as by habit. They walk as if expecting the floor to shift beneath them, which it does, sometimes, in small ways, as the basalt continues to cool and settle. Their skin is weathered to a uniform brown-red by forge-light and steam, and their hair is worn short or bound up tight against the sparks. They smell, all of them, of smoke. Not unpleasantly. It is the smell of the Reaches. A smith who did not smell of smoke would be cause for comment.

---

The children of the Reaches are born into the hammering and know no other sound. Their first year is a continuous percussion—the ringing of metal, the breathing of bellows, the strike and answer of anvils—and they do not cry at it, because there is nothing quieter to contrast it against. The crying, when it comes, is itself percussive, a small rhythmic sound that fits oddly well with the larger rhythms around it. By the time they are walking, they are catching crawlers in copper pans at the edges of the warm pools. By the time they are five, their feet are tough as a dog's paw, and they run barefoot over the warm basalt without flinching. They have never seen the sun clearly. They know it only as a smudge at noon, a brighter place in the cloud. When they are seven or eight, they begin to learn the hammer, and the apprenticeship is long, and the mistakes are written on the body.

Some of them, as they grow older, leave. They go to other settlements, to open lands, to places where the sky is wide and the air is clear and the night is silent. Most of them come back within a year. They cannot bear the quiet. They find the open sky oppressive, the silence maddening, the absence of the smoke-sweet smell disorienting. They come back and take up the hammer again, and their hands re-callous, and their eyes re-adapt, and they settle into the rhythm as if they had never left. A few stay away. These are spoken of, a little, not with judgment but with a kind of puzzled respect, as one might speak of someone who has chosen to live without a sense.

---

The dogs of the Reaches are small and low and smooth-coated, with broad paws that do not sink into the warm mud at the margins of the pools. They are rust-colored or black, never white—white would show every stain, and the stains are constant. Their eyes are brown and quick, and they see well in the dimness. They hunt the crawlers at the pool edges, plunging their muzzles into the warm water and coming up with a writhing basalt-colored thing that they crush with a sideways bite. They eat them raw, mostly, and the smiths do not feed them; the dogs feed themselves. They are not pets, exactly. They are something more like small colleagues, tolerated and useful, slept beside in the warm spots and consulted in their way about the movements of the stone.

For the dogs feel the warmth of the basalt through their paws in a way the smiths do not, and they will not walk on rock that is too fresh, too newly exposed, too close to a still-cooling fissure. The smiths watch the dogs to know where it is safe to step. When a dog refuses a path, the path is not taken that day. The dogs are also the first to know when a new pool is forming, for they scent the water before the smiths do, and they gather at its edges and yap in a particular way that means *here is something new*. The smiths come and look. Sometimes they find crawlers already in the new pool, drifted in from somewhere, beginning the slow colonization.

---

The crawlers are the protein of the Reaches, and they are abundant. In the warm pools they move in slow constellations—thick-bodied, limbless things the color of wet basalt, a hand's length long, surfacing to breathe with a small popping sound, sinking again with a faint trail of bubbles. They have no eyes that anyone has been able to find, only a slight depression where eyes might be. They sense the world through the warmth of the water, perhaps, or through the dissolved metals in it. They taste of iron and iodine when boiled, and faintly of sulfur when raw, and they are nourishing in a way the smiths' bodies have come to require. A smith who goes without crawlers for long grows pale and shakes in the hands. The bodies here have arranged themselves around this food.

There are other things in the pools, and the smiths have names for them, though the names are not consistent from forge-house to forge-house. There are the many-legged ones, flat and segmented, that skate across the surface film and dive when disturbed. There are the shelled ones, whose shells are of crystallized mineral struck from the water they drink, glinting in the forge-light in pale iridescence. There are the threads, tiny drifting organisms that stain the water rust-orange where they swarm; the smiths collect these swarms and dry them for pigment, and the pigment is traded to other settlements, where it is prized. Each pool is a small city, with its own populations, its own territories, its own drift. The smiths know their home pool intimately. They know which crawlers are resident and which are transients, where the shelled ones breed in the cooler margins, when the threads come up in their rust-orange blooms. The pool is a text they have been reading all their lives.

At evening—though the word is a courtesy, for the light does not really change—they stand at the edges of the larger pools and watch. The hammer is set down or hung at the belt. The smoke drifts. The creatures rise and sink, rise and sink. The smiths' breath and the pools' breath mingle in the humid air, and the watching is a kind of rest, and the rest is shared. They do not speak much during this. They do not need to. The sound of the pools—the small poppings, the faint rustle of things moving—is itself a kind of quiet, the only quiet the Reaches has to offer, and they stand in it together.

---

And there are the pipe-voices.

The smiths have a name for them in the older speech, a word that means *the ones who listen from the steam*, but the younger smiths just call them the pipe-voices, or sometimes the metal-birds, though they do not know if they are birds. They have never been seen clearly. Sometimes, in the grey-blue dim of early evening, a small dark shape moves across the cloud-layer a hundred yards up, and the metallic piping comes from it—long notes and short notes in patterns that almost, but not quite, match the rhythms of the hammering below. The smiths do not know what they are. They do not know if they live in the steam-fissures and rise up to call, or if they live in the cloud itself, or if they are something else entirely—a venting of the rock's own pressure, a sound the basalt makes when it cools, mistaken for a creature. They do not seem to land. They do not seem to drink from the pools. They call, and the call fits with the hammering, and the smiths have made a kind of peace with them. They are part of the sound. They are part of the place.

No one hunts the pipe-voices. No one tries to net them or trap them or study them closely. They are above, and they are strange, and the smiths have enough strangeness in their immediate surroundings without seeking more. The pipe-voices come and go, and their coming and going is not predictable, and the smiths note the times of their absence as carefully as the times of their presence, though they could not say why.

---

The lichen comes last, and the smiths watch it with a particular attention. It is pale orange, no bigger than a hand, crusting the rock in sheltered corners—the lee of forge-house walls, the underside of fallen columns, the edges of pools where the mist collects. It is the first colonization. It grows very slowly, a hand-sized patch in a decade perhaps, but it grows. The smiths do not eat it. They do not use it for anything. But there are those among them who map its advance from year to year, marking new patches on the basalt columns with a small symbol, and the marks accumulate, and the lichen spreads, and the two records—human and not—keep pace with each other in their slow way.

The lichen is a promise, though the smiths do not always frame it that way. It says that the rock is cooling. It says that the steam will lessen, eventually. It says that the place will be different, someday, in a timescale so long that it is meaningless to think about. The smiths will not see the moss carpet. Their great-grandchildren will not see it. But the lichen is the first green thing, and it is here, and it is patient, and the smiths look at it and are comforted in a way they cannot quite articulate. The place is becoming. They are part of the becoming. The hammering is part of the becoming. The crawlers in the pools are part of it. The pipe-voices overhead are part of it. Even the smoke, hanging in its grey-blue layers, is part of it—a temporary atmosphere over a temporary settlement, balanced on the surface of a world that is still, after all this time, cooling toward something else.

The hammer falls. The anvil answers. The crawlers surface in the warm pools. The pipe-voices call from the cloud. The lichen spreads, by a hand's width, in a decade. The sound goes on, and on, and on.
