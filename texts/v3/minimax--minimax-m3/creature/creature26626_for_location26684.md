**The Dwellers**

---

The silver ones are the first thing you register, and the last, and most of what lies between. They are not the rulers of the place. They are the place's own dust given a flicker of intention. They hold themselves in the current in loose, lunging clouds, each one the length of a finger or a little more, each one almost perfectly the color of the bruised light that falls between the towers. They turn, all at once, with the smallest possible motion of the tail, and the whole cloud reshapes — a slow breath outward, a slow breath in. They part around you without seeming to decide to. They do not flee you. They incorporate you, the way a stream incorporates a stone, and then close again behind you as you pass. There is no leader. There is no edge. You cannot tell where one school ends and another begins. They have been doing this in the corridors for so long that the corridors are partly shaped for them — the vaults are exactly the height that lets a drift of them pool and wheel, and the narrow places are exactly the width that lets them stream through in a single bright thread. They are the smallest argument the place makes for itself. When something dark moves through and they erupt outward in that sudden silver panic, you understand that the calm of their holding is not calm at all but a very fast series of small reassurances given to one another, the way a flock turns.

The dark shapes that move through the deeper channels are not like the silver ones. They own the corridors the way a sleeper owns a bed. You see them first as absences — a part of the dim that is dimmer — and then as silhouettes when they cross one of the pale shafts falling from above. They are long. They are heavy with a heaviness that has nothing to do with fat, the heaviness of something that has stopped apologizing for its own mass. Some of them are fish in the way a cathedral is a building. Some of them are not fish. Some of them wear their ancestry on the outside — a plating, a jointed armor, a row of spines along the back that the water hums past as they move. Some of them are soft in a way that suggests they do not need to be defended. They move with the slow, ticking confidence of joints that fit. They patrol. That is the right word. They walk the same corridors at the same hours, in the same direction, and the grooves in the soft stone are their footprints. A groove is not really a footprint — a footprint is the mark of a step — but a groove is the mark of a step taken ten thousand times in the same place, and these grooves are deep, and they are everywhere, and the corners of the chambers are rubbed smooth by the turning of bodies that have been turning there since before you were a thought. When one of them passes close, the water thickens for a moment around it, as if the place itself has leaned in. The silver ones know each of them. They know them by the pressure, by the shadow, by the small change in the hum. When a particular dark shape approaches, the cloud above it tightens and rises, the way birds rise from a field when a certain hawk comes over. When a different dark shape approaches, the cloud parts and lets it through with a deference that has been learned in blood.

In the holes of the towers, in the cracks of the walls, in the soft seam where one shell meets another, there is a different kind of life. These are the clingers. They do not move. They are the place's own hesitation made visible. Some of them are frilled — soft fans of tissue, the color of a wound, spread out into the current and waiting. The frill moves but the thing at the center of it does not move. The frill is what eats. The frill closes, slowly, the way a hand closes, and what was inside it is no longer inside it, and the frill opens again, and the waiting resumes. Some of them are crusted — irregular lumps fused to the wall so completely that you cannot tell where the wall ends and the thing begins. They are the color of the wall, mostly, or the color of older walls below — yellowed, browned, gone the color of a thing that has been looked at for a long time. They breathe in the slowest possible way, a small inflation of the surface every several seconds, and they are patient in a way that makes patience seem like a new invention. Some of them are spiked, knobbed, ridged in patterns that have nothing to do with the patterns of the stone and yet look, after a while, as if they were always meant to be there. Among the clingers there are the soft pulsing things, tucked into the corners of the vaulted chambers, beating in their own rhythm. The rhythm is not the rhythm of the water. It is not the rhythm of the hum. It is a private rhythm, set down long ago by some ancestor and kept ever since, and it goes on whether anything is listening or not. A whole chamber will fill with these small beats, layered, slightly out of phase with one another, and the chamber will feel, for a moment, like a heart you have stepped inside.

On the sills, moving with the careful deliberation of something that does not want to be noticed, there are the crawlers. They are low. They are many-legged, or many-finned, or many-something — the limbs blur into one another in the dim, and what you see is mostly the motion of them, a slow ripple along the underside of the body. They have antennae, or something that does the work of antennae — long pale threads held out ahead of them, sweeping the water in slow arcs, reading the current the way a hand reads a page. They walk along the sills and across the flats of the merged tower bases and down into the lower corridors, and they leave faint trails in the sediment. The trails are not made by dragging. They are made by the slight weight of passage — a furrow, a faint compression of the soft powder, that takes a long time to fill itself in again. You can read the age of a trail by how sharp its edges still are. A fresh trail has crisp walls. An old trail has softened. The oldest trails are almost gone, just a faint ghost of a path, the memory of a passage. The crawlers are everywhere in the lower levels. They are the place's own punctuation, the small definite marks made in a long sentence. When one of them meets another, they touch antennae — a brief, careful, almost courtly contact — and then each goes on its way, and the trails continue.

And there are things you cannot see. You feel them in the way the silver ones behave, in the way the dark shapes alter their courses, in the way the hum changes pitch for a moment and then returns. Sometimes a pressure shifts beside you — not a current, not a wake, but an absence, a small cold spot in the warm water, as if something has just decided not to be there. Sometimes, at the very edge of vision, a flicker — a paleness moving where no paleness should be, or a darkness shaped like a purpose. You do not see the things that live in the deepest corridors, the ones in the worn older stone below. You only know they are there because the hum has a depth to it that would not exist without them, and because the sediment in the lower chambers is disturbed in patterns that no current makes. Once in a long while one of them surfaces, briefly, into the visible corridors — a long pale shape, slow, almost boneless, moving through a chamber the way a thought moves through a sleep — and the silver ones rise up to the ceilings in a single shimmering wave, and the dark shapes turn and give it the middle of the corridor without being asked, and the soft pulsing things in the corners go still for a moment, and then resume. It passes. The place exhales. The silver ones drift back down. The dark shapes resume their grooves. The pulsing resumes its rhythm. The hum settles back into its long tone. The thing is already below again, already a memory, already a pressure felt in the teeth.

But the deepest dwellers, the ones that are most truly at home, are the ones you do not see as dwellers at all. They are the builders. They are the polyps, each one a mouth and a stomach and a little ring of tentacles, no bigger than the head of a pin, set into the surface of the towers in their millions, their millions of millions, each one a small soft animal inside a small hard cup of its own making, the cup fused to the cups of all the others, the whole thing grown together into a single architecture that pretends to be a city and is actually a single organism, or many organisms pretending to be a single organism, or some third thing that the language of one and many was never built to describe. They feed with their tentacles when the current brings them something to be fed with, and the rest of the time they simply add another thin layer to the cup they are sitting in, and the cup grows by a hair's width in a day, and the tower grows, and the wall grows, and the place grows, and the place has been growing for so long that some of the towers are older than the memory of the water that surrounds them. Beside them, in the lime and the sediment, the worms — long pale worms, frilly worms, worms that have no face you would recognize, worms that build tubes around themselves out of the same calcium the polyps use, tubes that fuse with the tubes of other worms, so that the wall is partly a city of worm-castles too, each castle the size of a straw. And the cement-secreters — the small crustaceans, each no longer than a thumbnail, that work the loose sediment into the cracks with a glue they make in their own bodies, packing the seams, reinforcing the joints, doing the patient masonry of a place that is always being unmade by the water and always being remade by the small soft hands of its own inhabitants. These are the ones who live here most truly. They do not move through the place. They make the place. They are the towers, and the walls, and the vaulted ceilings, and the worn rounded sills. The dark shapes patrol through them. The silver ones drift above them. The clingers cling to them. The crawlers walk on them. The unseen things pass through them. And the builders, who are also the built, continue, in their own slow time, to add another hair's width to the world.
