The wind is the first inhabitant. It was here before anything else — before the filaments, before the moths, before whatever it was that built the structures. Other worlds have water; this one has moving air, and everything else has had to learn what that means.

To call the wind alive is to misuse the word, and to come close, in this place, to something true. The wind has no organs, no metabolism, no boundary you could point to and say *here, the wind ends and the not-wind begins.* But it has rhythm. Every few minutes it shifts — not gradually but in a single breath, as if something vast above the lip is exhaling, or below the floor is inhaling, and the bowl is the chamber that catches the breath. The wind lurches. The lurching is what organizes every other life here.

The filaments know this.

They are not plants. They are not animals. They are not fungi. They are the third thing — the thing that evolved in air rather than in water or soil, and they have no name you will recognize. Hair-fine. Cobweb-thin. Capillary structures that span rock to rock in long sagging catenaries. Pale — bone white, lichen green, a violet that only commits at certain angles. They do not root. They hang. They are suspended by the wind the way kelp is suspended by tide. They shudder, align, knot, break, re-knot. To look at them is to look at something that is mostly not there.

But they are alive. They metabolize the thin air, the trace moisture, the minerals that arrive on the wind from somewhere. They grow. They produce drag at the edges of the bowl — just enough to slow the wind there. And the wind, in turn, breaks and lifts and redistributes them just enough to keep them from accumulating into something that would stop the wind entirely. It is a truce. It has held, by the look of it, for a very long time.

The moths are the bowl's breath made visible.

Translucent — wings that are mostly air and a little membrane. A faint internal glow that pulses in time with nothing you can identify, though if you watch long enough you begin to suspect the pulse is not with each other but with the wind, with each gust, with the slow tidal shifts that re-arrange the concavity. They do not land often. They ride. They let the gusts carry them and make small corrections — a tilt, a flutter — and the bowl is full of them, a slow snow of pale shapes turning and dimming and brightening as if breathing.

Thousands. Tens of thousands, possibly. They do not cluster. They layer their keening into the roar at the upper edge of hearing — a thin high sound that enters through the skull rather than the ear. Whether this is a population or an individual distributed across many bodies, the bowl does not say. They are a phenomenon. They are how the bowl breathes out, in some sense you cannot quite articulate.

The solitary creatures are harder to see and harder to know.

A flat disc — ten centimeters across, perhaps, though it is hard to tell at an angle and in motion — that skims the rock face on a trailing thread. The thread is anchored somewhere you cannot see. The disc rides the boundary between stone and air. It is dark, against the pale rock, and it leaves a faint track behind it, a smear of something that might be erosion and might be secretion and might be both. Its life, as far as you can tell, is a continuous tracing. It is mapping the rock it never quite touches. It does not stop.

A long pale ribbon that lives in one of the crevices near the floor. You see it unspool, taken by the wind, and disappear into the moving air. Hours later it returns — having looped the bowl in a great curve you were not there to witness — and folds itself back into the crevice. It has done this, presumably, thousands of times. Its life is a continuous journey that has no destination, because it is the destination. It is a closed curve drawn on the air, by a thing that draws it.

A small angular thing that walks the vibrating bar without noticing the vibration. It has too many legs, or too few, or the legs are arranged in a way you have no word for. It feeds on the filaments that have colonized the bar. It eats. It moves on. It is the most animal of the creatures here — which is to say, the most recognizable as having a self. But it is small and it does not cluster, and you would not notice it if you were not watching, and you have to wonder what its inner life is, or whether to eat the filaments that grow on a vibrating bar and to walk that bar forever is sufficient.

The structures were made. They had purpose. They were, once, louder than the wind.

The vertical pylon at the bowl's edge, ten meters, stippled now with the same filaments as everything else, no longer emitting anything visible. You can see where the emission ports were — circular marks fused into the rock, colonized. The pylon is silent now. It stands in the wind and it does not move and it does not produce. It is the kind of object that has given up on being what it was and has accepted being what it is: a thing the wind moves around.

The horizontal bar strung between two rocks, vibrating at a frequency that makes the air shimmer, by no visible means. Its vibration contributes a thin overtone to the roar. It was, perhaps, once a sensor. Now it is a chime. Now it is weather.

The dish half-buried in the slope, pointing nowhere in particular, its inner face a mosaic of corrosion and lichen and the line between them not clear. It was, perhaps, once a receiver. Now it gathers whatever arrives and lets the filaments colonize it. It does not distinguish. It is in retirement. It has stopped trying to do a job. It is the shape of a job, in the wind, and the wind uses it as a windbreak, and the filaments use it as a substrate, and the small angular thing uses it as a place to hunt.

These structures have made their peace. They are no longer what they were. They are now part of what is here. They hum. They contribute. They have become weather.

And the bowl.

The bowl is the inhabitant you cannot see because it is what holds the seeing.

It is in constant rearrangement. Nothing settles. Nothing accumulates. Whatever grows is taken before it can root. Whatever dies is lifted before it can decay. The whole concavity is a digestion. It is processing itself.

And yet — this is the strangeness that lives underneath the strangeness — the bowl is in balance. Erosion is finished. The peaks have found their final lean. The growths and the wind have reached an unspoken agreement: the growths produce just enough drag to slow the wind at the edges; the wind breaks and lifts and redistributes just enough growth to keep it from accumulating. The fauna eat and are eaten at rates that do not change from year to year, or century to century, or whatever longer unit applies. Even the structures have given up on purpose.

The bowl is alive in the way a planet is alive — not as an organism but as a system. A system in equilibrium with itself. A system that has found its own weather and its own metabolism and its own timescale and is content to continue.

And you.

You stand, if you stand, on the slope. The wind takes your hair sideways. The cold takes the heat from your ears. The sound takes your ability to think in straight lines. Below you the bowl shudders and rearranges. Above you the sky is very blue and very indifferent. The moths brush past you and do not pause.

You are, briefly, the only solid thing in a cathedral made of moving air. The filaments part around you and re-form behind you. The disc on the rock face below you traces its endless path and does not look up. The ribbon unspools from its crevice and goes.

You are not part of the bowl. You are not in its balance. You are a passing weather of a different kind — a temporary solidity, a brief interruption in a system that will continue without you. The wind does not know you are here. The bowl does not know you are here. The moths, perhaps, register you — a sudden obstacle in their long slow turns — and adjust, and pass.

You are, for the duration of your standing, the most temporary thing in the place.
