They live here the way a coral lives in its reef, or the way a thought lives in the mind that holds it — as a continuous density of small presences, none of which is alone, all of which together compose something that would not exist if any of them were removed. You have to learn to see them as that, because at first, at the lip, all you register is *movement*. Then texture. Then, slowly, kinds.

The things that coat the walls are not, despite the impression they give, a single carpet. They are many species layered on one another like the pages of a book that has been left open and rained on. The lowest stratum, pressed closest to the stone, is the smallest: mites, almost, or something mite-sized, white and slow, with bodies so pale they seem to be made of the calcite they are eating. They do not crawl so much as graze. A patch of them moves across the wall at a pace you can only detect by returning your gaze after a quarter hour and finding that what was under your thumb's-nail of focus has now slid to under your fingertip. They leave the stone faintly cleaner behind them, polished to a higher shine than the water alone could manage, and they leave behind them, too, a thin dust of their own casting-off, which is the food of the things that live one layer up.

The layer above is the crickets, or the cricket-shaped things — long-horned, pale, with legs made for clinging and antennae longer than their bodies, antennae that move constantly, tasting the air, the mist, the warmth. They are louder than they look. Each one, when it is not actually moving, is producing a faint clicking, a kind of private metronome, and the thousands of them together produce that thin high whine you heard from above — not a song, not a call, but the sound of a great many small nervous systems affirming to themselves that they are still alive. The crickets do not eat the stone. They eat the mites. They eat each other's dead. They eat the droppings of the things that fly. They are the middle managers of this economy, and they have the look of middle managers — busy, slightly frantic, slightly dull, entirely necessary.

Above the crickets, hanging from the ceiling in clusters the size of bunches of grapes, are the folded ones. These are larger. Their wings are the color of weak tea and lie along their backs in pleated fans, and their bodies are soft and grey-pink and show the segmentation of something that began, perhaps, as a larva and never fully committed to being anything else. They cling with the hooked feet of their hindmost segment and hang downward, and they do not move for days at a time, and then for a few minutes they move very much, dropping from their cluster, opening those papery wings, and pouring out into the chamber in slow loops. They are the ones whose passage you feel against your face. A wing-tip, or the soft flank of an abdomen, brushes near you in the dark and the air moves and your skin registers it before your mind does. They are not flying *at* you. They are flying past you because you are warm, and warmth, in this place, is a kind of smell.

You cannot see them clearly, but you can come to know them by what they do. They eat the mist. Not the water in it — they cannot drink that, the way a cricket drinks — but the living things suspended in it: spores, single-celled things, the smallest larvae. The mist is, to them, a kind of broth, and they sieve it from the air with the fine combs along their mouthparts. They also eat one another, when one falls weakly from its cluster to a lower one, and the eating is quiet and thorough. They do not live long. A folded one that has been raised in the warmth of the lower chamber will live a season; one raised higher, where the cold reaches, will stiffen within weeks. Their bodies, when they fall, are consumed by the crickets below, and the crickets' castings drift further down, and somewhere below that the mites graze the stones again, and the cycle is closed.

Below the crickets, in the parts of the chamber the light never reaches, the air thickens and the temperature climbs and the things get larger. You cannot see them without a light, and you should not bring a light, because a light in that part of the chamber is a small sun, and they have not seen a sun in longer than your species has had a word for sun. But you hear them. You hear the displacement of water when one of them moves through the standing pools down there — a long slow wake, the way a fish moves, except that this is not a fish. You hear the breathing: not the click of an insect spiracle, but the bellows of a lung, taking in warm wet air and letting it out in a low rush that carries, faintly, the sweet-rotting smell of the place concentrated. And you hear, at intervals that are not quite regular, that utterance — the one between a croak and a sigh — which seems to come from deep in the chest of the creature making it, and which is not addressed to anything, and which is not a song, and which is not quite an alarm, but which has the quality of *being heard*, as if the creature making it is checking, the way a person calls out in a dark house to be sure someone is there.

You can come to know them by inference, as you can come to know a country by its sounds at night. They are pale. They must be pale, because color is a thing made by light, and they have no light. Their skins, where you might catch a glimpse if a folded one flew too low and startled one upward into the gray half-light, are the color of bone that has been soaked. They are long, longer than a man is tall when they stretch out, and when they are not stretched out they coil in the warm pools and on the warm shelves. They have legs, but fewer than a thing should have, and the legs are placed in a way that suggests they were once for climbing. Their forelimbs, the ones you sometimes see dimly in the mist, end in a soft pad of digits, four or five of them, spread slightly apart, as if still feeling for the next hold. Their heads are blunt and their eyes are large and useless, wet and silver in the gloom, and they are not looking at anything. They are listening with their skin.

Their young are born in the standing water. The young are small — a finger's length, maybe less — and they are born breathing, and they are born knowing which way is down. They do not learn the dark. They are darker than the dark, in some essential way, before they have any experience of it. They grow slowly. A young one that you might find near the edge of the deepest pool, where the gradient of warmth is most gentle, could be a decade old or a century old; there is no way to tell. Their parents, if you can use the word for creatures whose interest in their young is more thermal than emotional, lie near them in the warm water, and the young learn the shape of the chamber by the warmth of the walls against their flanks, and they learn the other creatures by the sounds they make, and they learn the place itself by the way the mist moves. By the time one of them is fully grown it knows this chamber the way a tongue knows a mouth.

And these are the ones who arranged the sticks.

You might not have believed it at first, because the traces are so faint — a few twigs laid across a ledge in a pattern that could be accidental, a smear of white on the basalt that could be mineral, a stone set on edge in a place where stones do not naturally stand on edge. But the more you look, the more you find, and the more you find, the more you have to revise your sense of what the larger creatures are. The smoothed places on the walls, at a height that matches the reach of one of them rearing up out of the water; the worn places on certain floor stones, where the same path has been taken over and over until the basalt has taken the shape of the foot; the smear, which is not mineral, which is a secretion, which is laid down at the edges of the territory of one of them and renewed. The sticks are not random. The sticks are gathered. The sticks are gathered and laid across the approach to the standing pool in which a particular one of them sleeps, and they are renewed when they rot, and they are not laid anywhere else in the chamber, and no other creature in the chamber arranges anything.

This is not, by any measure you would recognize, *thought*. It is not tool use. It is not culture. It is something older than any of those words, something that might be the ancestor of them — a feeling, perhaps, that this place is *mine*, and that *mine* is expressed not by aggression but by tidying. The creature does not know it is arranging. The creature knows only that the sticks want to be here, and that the smear wants to be renewed, and that the worn path is easier if it is followed. If you were to take the sticks away, the creature would, in time, gather them again, the way a person who has been moved in their sleep will, in the morning, move the furniture back. The behavior is the memory. The memory is in the body.

And here is the thing you cannot decide: are these the descendants of the original climbers, or are they something the climbers became, or were the climbers ever here at all? The white smear could be the residue of an old climbing chalk. The sticks could have been carried down by water. The worn places could be the work of a thousand seasons of crickets landing and taking off. The chamber is patient. The chamber would let you believe any of these stories, and would not confirm any of them, because the chamber is not interested in your certainty. The chamber has been here longer than the question, and will continue to be here after the question has been answered in every possible way.

But the smoothed grip on a particular stone at a particular height is the right height for a hand. Not a paw. Not a flipper. A hand, with fingers, with a thumb. You stand in the mist with your own hand against it and it fits. It fits the way a glove fits a hand that was made for it. And the white smear at the base of that stone is fresh.

The mist coils. The crickets click. A folded one drops from its cluster and opens its wings and pours past you in a long slow loop, and the air moves against your face, and somewhere down in the warm dark a creature that may or may not be the descendant of whoever left the smear calls out once, a single low note that is not quite a word, and the chamber folds the note into itself, and you understand that you have been heard, and that being heard does not mean being understood, and that the place does not care which it is, because to the place you are just another warmth moving through it, briefly, on your way back to the lip.
