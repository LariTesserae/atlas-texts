The marmots live here most fully. They are the residents most at home in this wind — which is to say, they have made an accommodation with it rather than escaped it. They dig their burrows under the lee-side of boulders where the wind cannot reach the entrance directly; the entrance itself is a small crescent of packed earth, indistinguishable from the slope above unless you know to look. They emerge in the morning when the wind is at its lowest, and they stand on the flat stones that ring their territories, upright, their forepaws hanging, their heads tilted as if listening to something just below the threshold of hearing. They are the color of weathered granite. Their pelage has a slight silvering where it catches the raking light. From a distance they look like stones that have grown ears.

When they whistle — and they do whistle, one to another across the valley floor — the sound is thin and rising and gone almost before it forms. The wind takes it. You hear it once and then it isn't there, and you only know it happened because another marmot, somewhere, has flattened itself against its rock and is no longer visible in the way it was a moment before. Their warning calls are a single syllable, repeated. They are not loud. They are meant to be sufficient for the distance between two marmots, which is small, but the wind has to be considered, and the wind makes everything harder.

By afternoon they have retreated. Their burrows are cool and damp and dark, and the wind does not reach them there, and the temperature is nearly constant — somewhere in the low single digits, summer and winter, with only the seasonal dampness varying. In the burrow a marmot sleeps, mostly. It eats what it stored in the brief green weeks of early summer, and it loses weight gradually, and by spring it is lean enough to emerge again.

They have been here longer than anyone can say. Their burrows go back into the scree in a way that suggests they have been here since the rock was first exposed, since the wind first began its slow polishing. There are old burrows, abandoned now, with their entrances collapsed, marked only by a slight depression in the slope. There are active burrows with fresh earth at the lip. The marmots have not changed in a long time. They are exactly the right shape for this place, and they know it.

The birds are different. The birds do not live here so much as pass through, though some of them nest in the cliff faces that border the upper valley — the ones you cannot see from below. You see the birds most clearly when they ride the thermals: the warm air that lifts off the dark stone in late morning and runs up along the spine of harder rock in a column, and that gives the raptors something to work against. They hang there. They do not flap. They have tilted their wings at the precise angle and they are motionless in a moving medium, which is a different kind of stillness than the marmot's, which is the stillness of something hiding. The bird's stillness is the stillness of something that has found the current and is letting it do the work.

Their cries are infrequent and high — a single repeated note, thin, almost not a sound. The wind takes these too. You see the birds, mostly. They are dark against the pale stone — dark brown, almost black in the raking light — and they are shaped like pointed leaves, and they turn in slow circles, and they descend along the spine and rise again along it, and they have been doing this for hours and will continue to do it for hours, and the eye loses them eventually against the rock, because the rock is also dark in the raking light, and the eye gives up.

There are smaller birds too, in the gully vegetation — pipits, perhaps, or wheatears, ground-nesting species that live where the meltwater lingers and the moss grows thickest. They are quick and low. They run along the sand in the lee of the rocks. They have the same color as the sand. They are almost not there. They are most visible when they fly — a brief flash of pale underwing, then gone.

The foxes come out at the edges of the day — the hour before sunrise, the hour after sunset — and they cross the open flats with a particular gait, low and fast and slightly rolling, as if they had been poured across the surface. Their fur is the same color as the rock. They are visible only when they move. When they stop, they are part of the stone, and you have to remind yourself that a fox was just there.

They drink at the pools. They are cautious at the pools. They approach from downwind — though the wind is always from one direction here, so *downwind* means a particular compass bearing — and they pause at the lip and listen, and then they drink quickly, and then they are gone. They hunt the marmots. They hunt the small birds. They eat insects when insects are available. They are lean and quick and they leave almost no trace. Their scats are tiny and dry and they crumble into the dust within a week.

They den in the scree at the base of the cliffs, where the boulders are large enough to provide shade and where the gaps between them go deep enough to be cool. You don't find the dens. The foxes are careful. They use multiple entrances. They move the pups if anything approaches.

The insects are seasonal and brief. They come in the summer — the few weeks when the snow has melted and the gullies hold water and the sun is high enough to warm the dark stone. They hatch from eggs that have waited through the winter in the damp vegetation. They are the loudest creatures here for their size — a steady hum from any patch of moss, layered beneath the wind. There are flies. There are small beetles that walk across the sand in patterns that look almost too regular, as if they had been placed. There are mosquitoes near the standing pools, brief and vicious, with a hum that rises and falls as they approach the skin.

They live for their season. Most of them do not see the snow. They lay their eggs in the moss and die, and the eggs wait, and the next summer the cycle begins again.

The herds are the largest inhabitants, though they are small against the scale. They are mountain animals — ibex, perhaps, or a kind of sheep, the kind with the great curved horns that sweep back over the spine. They live along the upper ridges, the places where the rock has weathered into ledges they can walk along with their particular sure-footedness. They are brown against the grey stone. They are visible at dawn when the light is low and raking and they are moving along the skyline, and then the sun rises and they are not visible against the same stone, and by noon they have retreated to the upper cliffs where the wind is too strong for the eye to follow.

They move in small groups. They have a matriarch — an old female who knows the ledges, who has walked them for years, who has a memory of where the snow lies longest in spring, where the grass is greenest in the brief summer. The young males are pushed out at two years and they live alone for a while, or in bachelor groups, on the less desirable ridges. They eat the sparse vegetation — the bent grass, the occasional shrub, whatever the wind has not killed. They are patient. They wait.

Their horns are large. The old males carry them curved back like question marks, polished at the tips by years of walking along the stone. The females carry smaller horns, slimmer, more practical. The horn core the dry streambeds sometimes yield — half-buried, polished, the color of old bone — is from one of these. From a male who died on the upper ridge and whose body was partly carried down by meltwater, and whose skull has come apart over the years, and whose horn core remains, smoothed by sand and wind into something that looks as if it had been made.

The humans are the faintest presence here. They come in seasons when the passes are open — late summer, perhaps, or early autumn, when the snow has retreated enough that the approaches are walkable but the weather has not yet turned. They come for the chert. They come because the chert here is good — translucent, sharp-edged, the same color as the stone it came from, but with a fracture that gives long clean flakes. They sit in the lee of the large boulders and they strike the cores and they take what comes off, and they leave the flakes behind, and they carry the worked pieces down to wherever they are going.

The flakes are still there. They are sharp and translucent and they lie in the dry streambeds where you would not notice them unless you knew to look. Some of them have been there for a long time — the chert weathers slowly, and the edges are still keen, and they look as if they had been struck yesterday. They look as if they had been struck today. This is part of the wrongness of the place — the way the worked stone looks too recent, too available, as if someone were here just now and had stepped away for a moment.

The fire circles are darker. They are on the lee side of the boulders, where the wind cannot reach them, and the rock has a slight blackening from long-ago heat. They are circular. They are the right size for a small fire, the kind a person could sit beside. There is no ash. There is no charcoal. The rock itself was changed — the patina altered by the heat, and the new patina is darker and has not yet been worn away by the wind and the lichen.

There are a few of these places. You can find them if you walk the valley for a day. They are not many. The people who came here did not stay. They came and they did what they came to do and they left and they did not come back, or they came back and left again, and the traces are so faint they almost aren't there.

The lichen is the slowest inhabitant. It grows on the bare rock in places where the wind does not scour it constantly — under the slight overhangs, in the lee of larger stones, on the undersides of the flat slabs where moisture lingers. It is a different color from the rock — a darker grey, sometimes a faint rust, sometimes a pale green that you only see when you are close. It grows at a rate that can be measured in millimeters per decade. It is the first colonizer of fresh stone. When the wind exposes a new surface, the lichen arrives within a few years, and begins its slow alteration, and is still altering it a thousand years later.

It is not aware of itself. It does not adjust to the wind. It simply grows, in the places where it can grow, at the rate that it grows, and it accumulates, and it is the color that it is, and it is patient in a way that the wind is not patient, and the marmots are not patient, and the humans, briefly, were not patient.

The water is the last inhabitant I will name, though it is not alive. But it comes and it goes and it comes again, and it is the reason for everything else — the moss, the insects, the foxes at the pools, the herds at the gullies, the humans at the springs. It is in the gullies in the morning, when the night's melt has run down from the high places, and it is gone by afternoon, evaporated or sunk into the stone, and the gullies are dry and the moss is dry and the foxes go elsewhere, and the next morning the water is back.

The pools in the rock bowls are the exception. They hold water for longer — for weeks, sometimes, when the rain has been regular. They have algae at the bottom. They have insects. They are mirrors in a place otherwise without mirrors. They are the most populated things here.

Everything is in equilibrium. The gullies erode a little each year and the rock weathers a little each year, and the amounts balance. The water comes and goes and comes and goes in the same measure. The animals live and die and the population stays roughly the same. The lichen grows. The wind blows. The light arrives. Nothing is changing. Nothing has changed in a long time. Nothing will change for a long time.

What is strange about this place is that nothing seems to be struggling. The marmots are too well-suited to the wind. The birds ride the thermals too precisely. The foxes vanish into the stone too completely. The lichen grows at too steady a pace. The humans left flakes that look too fresh. It isn't that anything is wrong, exactly; it is that everything is slightly too right, slightly too consistent, as though some hand had smoothed the rough edges without quite finishing the job. The land has a quality of being inhabited by things that have already solved the problem of being here. They have settled in. They have stopped making noise about it. They are content.

And you, walking through, are the only creature that notices this — that finds the fitting too exact, the silence too arranged, the worked stone too available — and that notices it once and then forgets it for an hour and notices it again a day later, and cannot quite say what is wrong, because nothing is wrong, because everything is exactly right, because the place has arrived at its final shape and is content to stay there.
