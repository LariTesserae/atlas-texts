The inhabitants of the chamber are not many. They are stratified — they live in layers, the way the chamber itself does, the way the infrastructure does, in successive waves of colonization that have accumulated over the long time the chamber has been wet. The bacteria are first. They are the foundation. They are everywhere.

The bacteria are iron-oxidizers, descendants of a lineage that must have arrived in the first wet years of the chamber, when the water was still settling in and the steel was still fresh enough to corrode fast. They live on every wet surface. They coat the inside of the larger pipes, the underside of the catwalks, the faces of the columns, the corroded housings of the machines, the cracks in the concrete where water weeps out. They build sheaths of ferric hydroxide around themselves — hollow tubes, hair-thin, that grow outward into the water in orange-brown tufts and that accumulate over generations into crusts thick enough to flake off under their own weight. The crusts hang from the surfaces in long felted beards. They grow in the slow water more than the fast. They colonize the inside of every pipe that holds still water, and they shed constantly — small fragments of bacterial mat that fall into the current and become the food for the things that swim.

They are the food web. Without them there is nothing else. The dissolved iron in the water is theirs. They convert it into biomass, into structure, into the orange-brown crusts that paint the chamber. They are the primary producers in this place, the only things that can make living matter from the chemistry of corroding steel. Everything else depends on them.

The next layer is the small silver things. They are not numerous. They drift in the slower margins of the chamber, in the pockets behind the heat exchangers, in the eddies that form in the corners of the structure, in the long quiet water that lies between the columns where the pumps do not drive their full force. They are about the length of a hand, slender, with small heads and dark eyes that are nearly useless in the dim light and that they use anyway, as a vestigial habit from a brighter ancestry. They eat the bacterial sheddings. They eat the biofilm that coats the structures. They eat whatever small invertebrates have themselves colonized the bacterial crusts — small worms, small crustaceans, things that arrived on the water or hatched from eggs that survived in the sediment.

They are solitary. They avoid each other except when they spawn, which happens in the warmer pockets in a way that no one has observed because no one has been here to see it. They are not schooling fish. They are individual, small, dark-eyed, silver-flanked, and they exist in the chamber's slow water in a population that may number in the hundreds but that no one has counted.

The eels are third. They came in through the cracked intake — one or two of them, a very long time ago, long enough that the intake has since corroded further and is no longer passable. They found the heat exchangers and they stayed. There are perhaps two dozen of them now, in various sizes, from juveniles small enough to hide in the cracks between machinery to adults long enough to coil around the bases of columns. They live in the warm pockets. They hunt in the slower margins. They avoid the loud zones — the corridors near the turbines where the water transmits the hum at full strength and where the vibration would tear their tissues over time. They have been here for many generations.

They are slimy and dark, with the long bodies and small heads of their kind. Their eyes are smaller than their ancestors' would have been, because there is no light to see with, and they navigate by the currents and by some sense of the chamber's geometry that lives in their skin — in the lateral line, in the small pores that read the pressure and the chemistry of the water around them. They spawn in the warm pockets. They feed on the silver fish when they can catch them, which is not often, because the silver fish are quicker. They feed on each other sometimes, when an adult finds a juvenile it can take. They are predators, in a small way, in the way of a thing that lives in a place with very little food and has learned to take what it can.

The dark shape is fourth, and is not described in the same way.

It is large. It is slow. It is older than the eels. It has been in the chamber for longer than anyone knows, longer than the eels have been reproducing, longer than the chamber has been running in its current configuration.
