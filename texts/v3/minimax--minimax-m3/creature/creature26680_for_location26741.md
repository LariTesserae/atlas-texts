The place is inhabited more densely than anywhere its size ought to permit, and the inhabitants are themselves more numerous than the place can comfortably account for. You will never see all of them. You will see most of them never at all. What you do see, or hear, or feel across the back of your hand when something passes by, is the visible margin of a population that is, in the main, content to be unread.

The smallest are the most numerous, and the least ambitious, and they are the ones you will never see. They live in the wet crumb of the new sediment, in the films of moisture that coat the undersides of the tilted plates, and inside the pipes themselves — inside, or in whatever counts as inside, where the click and the whir you hear from any given length of pipe is the sound of them moving in close company along the wet interior. They are the size of a fingernail clipping, or smaller. They have legs, or things that work like legs. They make their living by being in the path of the rising water and the falling condensation, and the place is, for them, a kind of permanent rain that comes from below. They eat the films. They breathe the wet air. They pass each other in tunnels too narrow for you to look into. When they die — and they die, since things die here — they are taken back into the substrate within hours, and what remains is a brief darkening of the wet crumb that is gone by morning, or by whatever stands in for morning down here.

Some of them, sometimes, come up out of the substrate and cross open ground. You will not see them do this. You will feel them — a faint displacement across the back of your hand if you rest it on a warm surface, a small miscount of the air's warmth against your skin. They are passing across your path on their way to somewhere else, and somewhere else is, presumably, wetter or warmer or simply different, and they will get there. They do not stop for you. They do not know you are there. They are not concerned with you. There are a great many of them.

The lantern-flies are the next size up, and they are the first creatures here whose presence you cannot miss, because they are the ones that make a small mockery of the darkness. They are not flies. They have too many wings. Six is the usual number, but the usual number is not fixed, and on any given visit you may count five, or seven, or six and a half, the half being a stub that ends without finishing. The wings are gauzy, and they are not used for flying so much as for hanging in the still air, and the still air is willing to hold them because there is no weather to disturb it. They hang in clusters, or in loose drifting processions, and their abdomens pulse a faint blue-cyan — a color that does not seem to be coming from inside them so much as being agreed upon by their segments, as if each segment were contributing a single note and the note were being held. When a lantern-fly drifts past you at the level of your face, you can hear a small high hum from it, almost not a sound. When many of them pass, the hum braids itself into a thin chord that is the closest thing the place has to a continuous tone.

What they eat is unclear. They do not appear to land. They do not appear to mate, exactly, though their processions sometimes tighten and loosen in ways that suggest negotiation. They are, as far as can be determined, public animals; their light is a public utility. The frogs use it. The worms dim and resume in response to it. The smaller things in the substrate orient themselves by it, the way ants orient by the sun they cannot see. When the lantern-flies all go out at once — which happens, rarely, during the slow tone that comes from the towers — the place loses its ceiling of moving stars and becomes, for a minute, almost a different place, and then the lights come back, dimmer, and resume, and you realize you had stopped breathing.

The worms live in the standing water. The standing water is in every crack and at every seam: between the plates, between the pipes and the plates, in the unfilled canals, in the puddles that look flat from one angle and concave from another. The worms are pale, and very long — longer than the water is deep, longer than the geometry of any given puddle would seem to permit, longer than a worm has any business being in a place that respects itself. They lie along the bottoms and they lie along the sides and they lie along no surface at all, suspended in the water column in shapes that do not suggest they are supporting their own weight, because in this place weight is not a settled question. They pass through each other. This is not a metaphor. Two worms, both very long, will, at some point along their length, occupy the same space at the same time, and neither will notice, or both will notice and not care, or the categories of notice and care do not apply here.

Their light is greenish and intermittent. It comes from segments along their length, and the segments are not synchronized. Some pulse. Some hold. Some have forgotten what they were doing and are dark for a span, and then resume, out of step with the rest. The effect, when you stand near a pond of them, is a field of low green stars that have not agreed on a rhythm and are not going to. They do not appear to eat. They do not appear to move with any purpose. They are there, and they are long, and they are lit, and when one of them finally, after a span you cannot time, slides out of the water onto a plate and rests there for a while before sliding back, it does so without ceremony.

The frogs, or the things that are frogs here, are the most assertive of the small fauna. They sit on the rims of the pipes, on the fittings, on the hexagonal nuts that come unscrewed in the hand and reveal nothing inside. Their throats shine a steady red. The red does not fall into shadows the way red should. Light, down here, has been taught certain manners by the not-sky and the wet air, and the frog-throat red has refused the lessons. It spills. It goes around corners. It makes the inside of an empty pipe look as if it contained warm water, and it makes the side of a tower look as if it had been painted from within. Where the red falls on the wet ground, the ground does not warm up — the air is already warm — but it becomes more itself, more present, as if the red were a way of paying attention to the surface.

A frog, on closer inspection, has two throats. The upper throat is the one that shines. The lower throat is silent, and slightly lower, and does not shine, and it opens and closes in time with something you cannot see. The two are not coordinated. A frog will hold its red open while its lower throat pulses, or it will close the red while the lower throat continues, or it will do both at once in a way that suggests the frog is also two frogs, or is one frog made of two frogs that have agreed to share a body. The frogs do not jump. They shift. They have, instead of the leap of a frog, the slow lateral motion of something that knows it has all the time the place has, which is to say, a great deal. They will be here tomorrow. They will be here when you are not.

The grey animal the size of a cat is the one you may not see, depending on which tower you choose to look up. Most towers are inert, or appear to be. One in three, maybe one in five, has, halfway up its skeletal height, a horizontal opening that is breathing — slowly, in time with the not-sky, or out of time with it, or on its own clock that the not-sky is taking from. Out of this opening, on no schedule you can predict, will come a soft grey animal. It does not leap from the opening.
