The wall is alive.

This is the first thing to understand, before the ferns or the trees or the salamanders. The dark amphibolite of the back wall — the long sweep of stone that has the grain of sleeping muscle — is not bare stone. It is a surface that has been occupied for so long that occupation has become its nature, the way occupation has become the nature of a mussel bed or a coral head or a stone in a tide pool that has been turned so often by children that the underside is as smooth as the upper. There is a film on the wall. The film would not cover your smallest fingernail a hundred times over, and yet the film is complete, and the film is populated, and the film has been populated longer than anyone can say.
