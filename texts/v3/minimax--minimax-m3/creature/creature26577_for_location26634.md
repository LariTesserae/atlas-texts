The town breathes.

That is the first thing to understand about the place, and the last thing you will be able to say about it without the words running thin. Everything that lives here lives inside the breathing. The brick walls hold it in and let it out again in their slow give of moisture. The wooden floors of the upper stories flex and resettle with each small atmospheric shift. The wheel in the square turns — sometimes by the engine, sometimes by the river, sometimes by no mechanism you can see — and the turning is the chest of the place, rising and falling in its slow rhythm. And the people who live here have, over generations, learned to breathe with it. Their days are paced to the bog. Their speech is paced to the bog. The way they fold their hands in their laps, the way they walk without hurrying, the way they stand at the edge of the peat field and watch a man cut rectangles with his long knife and say nothing for an hour at a time — this is the pace of decomposition, the pace of things becoming other things, the pace of a place that has decided not to be in any kind of rush.

You cannot quite call them a community, though there are perhaps three hundred souls in the town proper and another hundred scattered along the edges where the bog has learned to support a footing. They are more like the cells of a single organism, each one doing its work without much reference to the others, and yet each one depending on the others in a way that is not spoken about. The wheel-man knows the pulper. The pulper knows the peat-cutter. The peat-cutter knows the woman who keeps the stillhouse. They do not gather in any formal way. There is no church here, though there is a long low building near the river that was once a chapel and is now used to dry willow bark in winter, and it is not unusual to find someone inside it on a wet afternoon, not praying so much as sitting in the particular hush that old religious buildings keep. There is no tavern either, though there is a room above the pulping shed where a man brews a thin beer from the sap of the birch trees that grow along the riverbank, and on a Saturday evening — or what passes for Saturday, since the calendar here is more of a suggestion than a structure — a few of the townspeople will climb the outside stairs and sit at the long table and drink and speak quietly and listen to the rain, if it is raining, or to the wheel, if the wheel is turning.

The people themselves are not remarkable to look at. They are of middling height, and pale in the way that people who do not see much sun are pale, but with a particular ruddiness to the cheeks and the backs of the hands that comes from a lifetime of working in the damp. Their hair is often dark, sometimes going early to gray, and they wear it long or short without much in the way of fashion speaking to either choice. Their clothes are practical: layers of wool, a waxed coat for the worst of the weather, boots that come above the ankle and are patched at the toe more often than not. They are a sober people, in the literal sense — they do not intoxicate themselves often, and when they do, it is with the birch beer or a distillate of the yew needles that the stillhouse produces in small quantities and which has a flavor like cold pine and a quietening effect on the part of the mind that worries. They eat simply. Fish from the river, when the heron does not get it first. A bread baked from a flour that is partly rye and partly the dried and ground pith of the bulrush. A cheese, occasionally, made from the milk of the three or four cows that are kept in a low shed at the edge of the town, where the ground is a little firmer. Greens from the gardens that have been coaxed into the lee of the brick walls, where the heat is held and the wind is broken. They do not go hungry. They do not feast. They eat the way the bog eats: slowly, patiently, without waste.

But there is something about the people that is not quite of the people. It is in the eyes, perhaps, or in the way they hold themselves when they are not being observed. There is a slackness to them that is not the slackness of fatigue. It is the slackness of a thing that has been steeped in its own place for a long time, and has begun, at the edges, to become that place. You will see a woman walking along the main street at dusk, and the green of the moss on the wall behind her will seem to be in her, somehow, in the tint of her skin, in the drape of her coat. You will see a man standing at the wheel when it is not turning, and his stillness will seem to be of a different order than the stillness of a man waiting. It will be the stillness of a thing that has been still for a very long time and is not aware that stillness is something one can be aware of. The moss grows in patterns on their skin, if you look, in the soft places behind the ears, in the creases of the palms. Not literal moss, but the same green, the same slow patience. They are becoming part of the place, and the place is becoming part of them, and the boundary between the two is not holding the way it once did.

The children are the strangest, and the most expected. They are pale and dark-eyed and quiet, and they move through the streets of the town with a familiarity that does not seem to be learned but inherited. They know which walls will hold their weight and which will not, and they know it without testing. They know where the bog is thin and where it is thick, and they walk accordingly. They are taught to read by an old woman in the building that was once a school, and they are taught to write, and they are taught their numbers, and they are taught the names of the plants — the willow, the alder, the yew, the sphagnum, the sundew, the cotton-grass — and beyond this they are taught very little, because there is very little else to teach that the place will accept. They grow up and they become the wheel-man, the pulper, the peat-cutter, the still-keeper, the bark-drier. Some of them, a few, go away. They walk out along the main street to where it dissolves into the peat field, and they strike out across the bog on the boardwalk that the town maintains for this purpose, and they go down to the lowlands, to the towns that have actual streets and actual weather and actual time. They are not heard from often. Those who stay do not ask after them, because they have a sense, bred into them by the place, that the ones who have gone are not gone in the way that absence is usually understood. They are elsewhere. They will be elsewhere for a while. They may come back, or they may not. It does not matter to the bog. The bog will receive them back, or it will not, and either way it will continue.

The town does not age in the way that towns usually age. It does not so much decay as be composted, slowly, into something else. The brick is softening. The timber is silvering. The mortar is being eaten back to the rough openings and re-set in paler brick by hands that do not think of themselves as builders but as tenders. The wheel turns, and the pulping shed pulps, and the stillhouse stills, and the peat-cutter cuts, and the bark-drier dries, and the river runs, and the bog breathes, and the moss grows, and the people go on with their quiet lives inside the breathing, becoming more themselves and more the place at the same time, until the distinction between the two will not matter at all, and the place will simply continue, as it has continued, as it will continue, for a long time yet, in its own pace, in its own sure and unhurried way.

This is what lives there.
