What lives here is small, and old, and patient beyond what patience usually means.

You feel them before you see them. That is the first thing to know. You step into the broad passage and there is something under your foot that is not the stone — a low hum, continuous, almost at the threshold of perception, a pressure in the soles rather than a sound. If you have ever stood near a power station on a still night, or near a hive inside a hollow tree, you will recognize the texture of it. If you have not, you will think it is your own blood, or the after-sound of your own footsteps, or the draft from the half-collapsed entrance behind you. It is not. It is them.

Then you see them, or you think you do. They are in the lanes along the seams of the paving — pale threads against the darker stone, moving with a deliberation that is not haste and is not slowness but something in between, something keyed to a tempo you cannot quite follow. You kneel, if you are the kind of person who kneels, and look sideways along the floor, and the lanes resolve as faint variations in the polish of the stone: tracks that have been traveled for longer than the polish itself. The inhabitants move along them in their dozens, their hundreds, in long files that break and re-form and break again at junctions, the way water breaks and re-forms at stones in a stream. They do not look up at you. They do not look up at anything. They are going somewhere.

Their bodies are not easy to describe, because they are not built for description. They are long. They are pale — the pale of old bone, the pale of calcite, the pale of things that have lived underground so long they have lost whatever pigment their distant ancestors might once have had. They are thumb-length, give or take, but the length is mostly leg. The legs are too many to count without stopping and looking directly, and when you stop and look directly the count becomes uncertain, because the legs are fine and pale and they move in waves, not all at once but in succession, a ripple passing down the body from one end to the other. The body itself is segmented, faintly, the way an insect's body is segmented, but the segments are not pronounced — they read as a slight thickening and thinning along the length, the way a wire reads when it has been wound. There is no obvious head. There is no obvious tail. The whole creature is a long pale axis moving through space on a forest of small pale limbs.

You could pick one up. You probably should not. If you did, you would feel how light it is — almost no weight at all, almost as if it were a thing the air had decided to be shaped like — and you would feel how cool it is, cooler than the stone, and you would see, at last, that there is a head, or something that functions as one, only it is not at the front. It is underneath. The creature walks on the underside of itself, the way some water-striders move on the underside of surface tension. If you turn it over it will go still for a moment, a moment of total stillness, as if deciding something, and then it will begin, very slowly, to fold itself along its length, like a letter being put away. You would put it down again. It would unfold. It would rejoin its lane. The lane would close over it as if it had never been.

---

The work they do is the strange part, and it is the work that defines them.

Watch a section of wall, somewhere along the passage, where the light from the mineral skin is falling in a slightly warmer patch. Down at the foot of the wall, where the stone meets the paving, there is a small structure. It is perhaps the height of your thumb from base to top. It has a doorway — a slot, exactly like the door-slots in the wall above it, only a hundredth the size. It has a lintel above the slot. It has, if you look closely, a kind of threshold step, worn smooth. If you crouch and bring your eye to the level of the doorway, you can see, in the dim interior, a chamber not much bigger than a pea, and in the chamber — nothing. Dust, perhaps. A residue of mineral paste. The chamber is empty, and has been empty for some time, and the structure is still here.

This is one of their buildings. There are thousands of them. There is hardly a square foot of wall that does not have one at its foot, and some of the walls have several, layered one against another in a way that mimics the construction of the larger place above them — built on top of built on top of built, the uppermost layer the most recent, the lower ones slowly hardening into the wall they were built against.

They make these structures out of mineral dust and a secretion of their own, worked together into a paste that sets, over years, into something like the original lime mortar of the settlement. The proportions are wrong, but only just — the architects of the miniature doorways have measured their world against the doorways above them, and have come close, but not quite close enough. The joints are impossibly fine. A fingernail will not fit between the stones of a well-made miniature wall. The lintels are not lintels; they are single grains of grit, lifted and laid with a deliberation you cannot imagine, held in place by paste that has since become the only thing holding them.

A miniature building will last for decades after the builder has died. The builder's work goes on without the builder. The doorway remains. The chamber remains. The threshold, worn by the passage of the builder's body over its builder's lifetime, retains the shape of the builder's tread until the paste has hardened and the stone has taken the impression up. Then it is not a worn threshold anymore. It is part of the wall. A hundred miniature generations from now, that footprint — that impression of a thing that walked here once — will be a feature of the settlement's archaeology, indistinguishable in kind from the wear-marks of the larger builders who are long gone.

The builders die. The buildings persist. The buildings harden. The settlement, in its miniature register, accumulates.

---

And then there is the larger work, which is the same work at a larger scale, and which is the reason the place is not yet a cave.

A section of pavement, somewhere ahead, where the uppermost layer has been pried up by some agency and the slabs are stacked neatly to one side, exposing the layer below — older, finer, more carefully laid. A wall that has been shored up with a wedge of newer stone, the wedge itself scored with the tiny lanes of the inhabitants. A basin that has been deliberately deepened, the spoil heaped in a low ring around it. A door-slot that has been partially blocked with fitted stones, then unblocked, then blocked again, each attempt using slightly different masonry. None of this is being done by anything you can see. It is being done, or has been done, or is in the slow process of being done, by the aggregate action of the small pale fauna over their slow and patient lifetimes.

This is what they are, in the largest sense: the city's present keepers. A wall is repaired across a century. A floor is re-laid over several generations. A basin is deepened so that the water will continue to gather there when the surface that holds it is gone. The work is competent. The work is very, very slow. The work is the only work being done. And the workers, individually, do not know that they are doing it. Each of them, in its thumb-length lifetime, lifts a grain of grit. Pastes it down. Goes on. The city is the work of uncountable such acts, accumulated over a span of time so long that the result of any single day's activity is imperceptible, but the result of a thousand days is a moved stone, and the result of a million days is a different place.

---

What they eat, no one knows. What they drink, no one knows. They are not observed eating, and they are not observed drinking, and the basins of still water along the passage do not appear to be the focus of any particular activity. It may be that they eat the mineral dust they build with. It may be that they eat each other, in a slow and patient way, and that what looks like a lane of moving bodies is, on closer inspection, a single body in many stages of being passed along. It may be that they eat light, or damp, or time. They are not, in any case, observed to be hungry. They are observed to be busy. The two are not the same thing.

They reproduce, presumably, although no one has observed the act. There are always more of them. The lanes do not thin. If you watch a section of paving for an afternoon, you will see the same density of bodies in the same density of lanes at the end of it as at the beginning. They are not, in any visible sense, dying. They are not, in any visible sense, being born. They are simply present, in the same numbers, year after year, the way the mineral crust on the ceiling is present in the same thickness, the way the damp is present in the same depth. They are not the place's guests. They are the place's medium, the way water is the medium of a spring, or air is the medium of a wind.

---

And yet.

There is a question that the place keeps asking, which is whether the small pale fauna are the only inhabitants, or whether there is something else here too — something that is not the fauna, and is not the architecture, and is not the light, but is in some way present in all of them.

You sit, after a time, on one of the worn steps in the central passage. The light falls on you from every direction, warm and even and slightly off. The hum is in your feet and in your knees and in the small bones of your inner ear. The miniature buildings stand against the walls in their thousands. The lanes move in their tens of thousands. A stone shifts, somewhere far ahead, with a soft unmoored *tok*. A drop lets go from the ceiling somewhere else, and the sound of it is more felt than heard.

And you begin to feel — not to think, not to conclude, but to feel — that the place is not being maintained by the small fauna alone. That the fauna are doing something, yes, and doing it patiently, and doing it well. But that they are doing it in a key that was given to them. That the architecture they are mimicking was not invented by them. That the door-slots they are miniaturizing were not carved by them. That the lime mortar they are reinventing was not made by them. That there is, somewhere in the walls of the settlement, in the worn paving, in the iron-banded chests that are now only dust, a kind of memory of the original builders, which the fauna have inherited without having made. That the place is inhabited not only by the things that remained, but by the shape of the things that did not.

You cannot point to this. You cannot say where it is. It is in the way the light falls in the central passage. It is in the way the basins of water are placed — at intervals, irregular but regular, the way doorways are placed — as if someone, at some point, decided that there should be a basin here. It is in the angle at which the last tooth of wall leans in the eastern wing, which is not the angle at which the wall was built but the angle at which it was last cared for, by hands that are not here. It is in the slow drift of color in the light, the breathing of the place, the off-key feeling, the wrongness that is not quite wrongness.

It may be that the place remembers its makers. It may be that the light is a residue of their going. It may be that the damp is their damp, still, and the silence is their silence, and the slow work of the small fauna is their work, continued in their absence by whatever was left behind that was capable of continuing it.

You do not stay long enough to know. No one stays long enough to know. But you leave the place, when you leave it, with the feeling that you have been among more than one kind of inhabitant. That there were the small pale things in their lanes, and there was something else — something that was not visible, and not audible, and not palpable in the ordinary sense, but that had a weight to it, a presence, an insistence — and that the two kinds of inhabitant were, somehow, both keeping the place.

Both of them, in their different keys, in their different tempos, in their different scales of time, were doing the same work: the work of making sure that the settlement, which had been built once, was not allowed to forget that it had been built.

That is what lives here. The small pale fauna, and the shape of the people who built the place, and the light that remembers them, and the damp that does not, and the stone that does, and the silence that is full of all of it.
