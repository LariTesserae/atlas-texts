# What Lives in the Steaming Saddle

Begin with the obvious. Begin with the lizards.

There is a small grey lizard, no species that has been formally catalogued, that basks on the warm rocks near the southern hot spring. Its body is the color of wet basalt. Its movements are quick and percussive — a flash along a fracture, a pause, a flash again — and it eats the beetles that share its stones, beetles whose carapaces have polished themselves to the same orange as the mineral crust until the spring apron looks, on a still afternoon, as if it is shedding small living chips. The lizard has no name in the local herpetology guides. The local people call it the *stone that walks*. It does not appear to sleep, or it sleeps so lightly that no one has ever caught it. There are perhaps two hundred of them in the saddle. They have been there since before the structures were surveyed, since before the basalt was dated. They are the most ordinary thing in the place.

The crane flies are less ordinary, though at first you would not think so. They hang in the still air above the fern grove, impossibly long-legged, their bodies suspended on a geometry that the eye wants to refuse. They do not seem to fly so much as to be carried. Their wingbeats are slow enough that you can see each one as a separate event. When the afternoon rain comes through they fold their wings sideways and descend to the underside of the largest fern fronds, where they wait in rows, motionless, their legs dangling like the rigging of ships at anchor. No one has ever seen one of them die. It is possible they do not.

The birds — there is the one species, dull green, almost never seen — are not birds the way other birds are birds. Their call is the descending two-note whistle, and it is the most recognizable sound in the basin after the rain and the hum. The whistle carries across the bowl and stops, *is* stopped, mid-phrase, every time, as if whatever is producing it has remembered, halfway through, that it should not be producing it. You can stand in the fern grove and wait and one will sometimes pass through the trunks, low and quick, and you will not see it so much as feel a displacement, the way you feel someone walk behind you in a corridor. Their droppings on the rocks are unusual: they are not white, and they are not the green-brown of insectivores. They are dark, almost mineral, and they do not seem to break down. A hundred-year pile of them on a single shelf would look like a hundred-year pile of them, if anyone had been counting, which they have not.

The plants are not animals, but several of them behave as if they have not been informed. The grey-lavender mosses advance across the basalt at a rate that has been measured at slightly under a centimeter per month, which is too fast for what they appear to be. They grow toward the thermal vents. They grow toward the seams. When a fragment of basalt is broken off and carried, against all advice, out of the saddle, the moss on it dies within the day, even when the fragment is placed in identical conditions elsewhere — same altitude, same humidity, same mineral content of the substrate. It will not leave. Some things in the saddle are not, strictly, in the saddle. They are the saddle. The lichen at the rim is the oldest of these, lichen being slow and obliging, but the mosses, the orchids, the succulents that drink steam through their cuticles — these are younger, and they are not negotiating.

---

Now go closer to the center.

Now go to the geometries.

The largest of them — the six-sided prism, the one with the rain-collecting depression on its face — is not, despite the way it sits on the flattened platform, a thing that has been placed. It is a thing that has been *stopped*. There is a difference. A placed object has edges that meet the world; this object has edges that meet the world only because the world is not finished happening to it. The basalt around its base is younger than the basalt everywhere else. The lichen at its base is older. The warm faintness that comes off its surface in the late afternoon is not residual heat from the springs; it is the same temperature, give or take a tenth of a degree, at three in the morning as it is at three in the afternoon, on a still day as on a windy one, in the wet season as in the dry. It is a temperature. It has the temperature of something that is being kept.

You can crouch beside it and put your hand against the surface and feel nothing dramatic. A warmth. A slight resistance, as if the air just at the boundary is denser than the air an inch farther away. If you keep your hand there, your heartbeat will, after a while, seem to slow. Not dangerously. Just noticeably. As if the prism is asking your heart to consider a different tempo, politely, and your heart is considering it. Most people who do this remove their hand after a minute or two. They cannot say why. The few who have kept their hand there longer describe a feeling of being *read*, not in the sense of being observed but in the sense of being paged through, and they use the word *read* and not the word *searched* because the slowness of it is the point.

There is no one inside the prism. There is also no *not-one*. The interior, if it has an interior, is not a space you can enter by being in the same room as it; it is a space that the prism does not have, and the warmth at its surface is the warmth of whatever would be in such a space if such a space existed, leaking out.

---

The smaller structures are less patient, or differently patient.

The humming one — the long horizontal one set into the rock at an angle — produces its tone continuously. The tone is not a tone, exactly; it is a pressure, or a periodicity in the air that the cochlea resolves as a tone because the cochlea has no other category. Older listeners do not hear it, which is to say their ears no longer resolve it; younger listeners do, and they say it is not unpleasant, only *instructive*, which is a word they have trouble explaining afterward. A person who has stood within a few meters of it for more than ten minutes tends to come away with the strong, hard-to-shake impression that something has been explained to them. They do not always remember what. They remember the fact of explanation. The structure appears, from certain angles, to be the only one of the three that is oriented; the others just are, but this one seems to be *aimed*, though at nothing in particular that can be located. Its hum drops a half-tone when the afternoon rain is at its heaviest, and rises a half-tone in the hour afterward when the air is thickest with steam. It is, in some way no instrument can confirm, *tuned*. It is being kept in tune. By what, and against what reference, is not known. Possibly by the steam. Possibly by the rain. Possibly by the prism. Possibly by the saddle itself, which is a sentence that means nothing and also means everything and is, in fact, the sentence the humming structure leaves you with if you stand too close.

The third structure does nothing. The third structure is the one around which the moss will not grow. A two-meter radius of clean dark basalt, lichenless, mossless, bare as the day the rock cooled, and the rest of the bowl is colonized within a year of any fresh surface appearing. Around this structure the mosses *retreat*. The retreat is slow, a centimeter a month, the same speed they advance elsewhere, but in reverse. It has been retreating for twenty years. The radius is widening. There is no center from which it widens; the radius widens from the structure outward, as if the structure is not keeping the moss away but *pulling it back*, or as if the moss is politely declining, year after year, to come closer to something it has been asked not to approach. This is the structure that you might, if you were feeling uncharitable, call a *door*. Or a *gate*. Or the place where a door was, before the door was a thing that someone could use.

---

There is the smudge.

The smudge is in seven of eleven thousand photographs and is not in the eleven thousand minus seven. It is in the upper third of the prism, slightly left of center. It is out of focus. It is the wrong color to be a moth, the wrong color to be a bat, the wrong color to be anything that flies in the basin at three in the morning, which is to say the wrong color to be anything that flies in the basin at any time, because at three in the morning nothing flies in the basin. It is the color of a thing that is not reflecting light but is *being* light, briefly, in a shape that is not the shape of a moth or a bird or a piece of steam, and then is not there. It is in one frame and not in the next. The interval between frames is one-fifth of a second. In that interval the smudge has traveled, if it has traveled, a distance that would require a velocity no living thing in the basin possesses. There is no point of arrival in the frame before it appears. There is no point of departure in the frame after it disappears. The cameras did not move. The prism did not move. The researchers who retrieved the cameras wrote, in the field notes that were later declassified, that the smudge was *present in the frame as if it had been there for some time and would continue to be there for some time, which is a category of presence the camera was not built to record*. The field notes are nine pages long. Pages three through seven are missing. They were not removed by anyone who left a record of removing them.

The smudge has not been seen since. It has not been looked for, either, which is a different fact and not the same fact. People who have spent more than a few hours near the prism at night report, sometimes, the impression that the upper portion of the structure is *occupied*. Not in the sense that something is in it, but in the sense that something is *using* it, the way a person uses a chair they have been sitting in for a long time, so that the chair eventually takes on the shape of the person without either of them having intended this. The prism, they say, has the *occupancy* of a thing that has been lived in for longer than its appearance suggests. This is not the same as saying something lives in it. It is saying that the *idea* of being lived in has settled into the structure the way the warm faintness has settled into its surface, and that the structure is, now, a kind of permanent partial occupation.

---

And there is the line of light.

The line of light is at the edge of one of the structures — no one agrees on which one; some say the prism, some say the humming one, some say the third — and it appears, sometimes, at night, from the rim, if you are looking at the right time. It is a thin line. It is the color of nothing in particular. It lasts a second, or two seconds, or, on one occasion that was reported in a paper that was never peer-reviewed, eleven seconds. It is at a different edge each time it has been seen. It is at a different height each time. It is not, as far as anyone has been able to determine, a light *from* inside. There is no inside to be from. It is more like the suggestion that if there were an inside, something in it would, at this moment, be glancing at the boundary to check what time it is in the outside. The line of light is the structure consulting the dark. The structure consulting the dark, briefly, and then returning to its consideration.

If you ask the people who have seen it what they think it means, they will usually say one of two things. They will say it means *something is checking*. Or they will say it means *something is being checked*. Both of these are true. The saddle does not insist on either.

---

So what lives in the Steaming Saddle?

The lizards. The beetles. The crane flies. The dull green birds with the broken whistles and the mineral droppings. The mosses that will not leave and the mosses that will not approach. The orchids rooted in cracks too small to hold anything. The lichen at the rim, the oldest living thing in the bowl, slow and obliging and not, despite appearances, the most patient.

The prism, which is being kept. The hum, which is being tuned. The third structure, which is being *declined*, politely, by the moss, year after year, in a slow widening retreat.

The smudge, which was present in seven frames and is, presumably, present now in zero frames and will be present in some other frame, at some other three in the morning, for some other set of eyes that does not insist on its own category.

And — though this is the part that is hardest to put into the kind of sentence that holds still on a page — the saddle itself. The bowl. The cupped palm of dark basalt between its two ridgelines. The water moving under the stone. The steam breathing from a dozen places. The rain that comes twice a day from the cloud on the western ridge deciding to fall. The slow circular current of air that you cannot feel. The heat that is not from the sun.

The saddle is not a place that *contains* life. It is a place that *is* life, in the way that a forest is not a container of trees but is a thing that trees make and that makes trees. The lichen makes the rim. The moss makes the seams. The steam makes the air. The rain makes the sound. The prism, the hum, the third structure, the smudge, the line of light — these are not visitors to the saddle. They are not residents of the saddle. They are the saddle's slow articulation of something it has been trying, for as long as the basalt has been cooling, to say.

What it is trying to say is not in any language. It is not, probably, in any *category*. It is more like the steady warmth at the surface of the prism — a presence that you can detect if you put your hand against it, and that you can choose not to detect, and that will still be there either way.

The saddle does not insist.

The saddle has not insisted, as far as anyone can tell, for twenty thousand years.
