# What Lives in the Garden

## The Inhabitants

They are called *the tenders*, though older poems call them *the listening ones* and the very oldest songs call them *the remnant*. There are perhaps three hundred of them now, though the number is uncertain because no census has ever been taken and because some of them spend years at a time inside the deeper trunks, in rooms that have grown closed around them, and emerge thinner and stranger and not always immediately recognizable as the same person who went in.

They are small. They are dark-skinned in the way that the water is dark — not one color but many, layered, the brown of the silt and the near-black of the bone and the deep red-brown of the older heartwood, with the variations of all of these passing across a single face in different lights. Their hair is long and they wear it tied back or braided close, because loose hair catches on the rhizomes. Their eyes are large and dark and slightly gold at the inner rim, and they do not blink as often as you would expect. They are watchful in a way that does not feel like watching you. It feels more like they are watching the Garden through you, using your arrival as a way of taking its measure.

They live in the rooms inside the trunks. Each room is a chamber that the tree grew around some older hollow — a place where a branch had died and dropped away, perhaps, or a place where a bone had once rested and left a shape in the wood. The rooms are small and curved and lined with the rings of the tree's growth, and they are warm, and they smell of sap and the faint sweetness of the water. A tender sleeps on a mat of woven vine in the curve of the wall. A tender keeps a small collection of objects in a niche — a smooth stone, a bone-fragment, a folded leaf with a pattern pressed into it that may or may not be writing. They do not keep many possessions. The Garden does not encourage keeping things.

A tender usually lives alone in a room, though sometimes two will share a chamber for a season or for years. They are affectionate with each other in a quiet way — a hand on a shoulder in passing, a phrase spoken low, the sharing of food from the same bowl. They do not appear to form families in the way you would recognize. Children are raised in common, in a kind of slow collective schooling that consists mostly of being walked through the Garden by an older tender and being told the names of things, which are not the names you would give them. A child learns the name of a particular bone before it learns the name of its own mother. This is considered appropriate.

The tenders eat the frogs, sometimes, when a frog has grown too old or too large, and they eat the small bright canopy-things when they fall, and they eat the rhizomes themselves, which are starchy and faintly sweet and have to be cooked for a long time in the brown water. They do not eat the bones. They do not eat the larger things in the water, and the larger things in the water do not eat them. There is an understanding.

## The Tending

A tender's work is the Garden. The work is not constant; it comes in pulses, in response to what the Garden is doing. On a morning when the rhizome mat has crept a meter overnight into a new clearing, the tenders will go out with long curved blades of sharpened bone and cut it back, slowly, singing as they cut. On a morning when a buttress has begun to sag, a tender will climb it and lash a brace of woven vine across the joint and wait for the buttress to grow into the brace, which it will do, given time. On a morning when a bone has gone cold, the tenders give the clearing around it a wide berth for as long as the bone remains cold, and they do not speak near it, and when they must pass it they pass with their faces turned away.

The singing is the part of the work that is hardest to describe. It is not song exactly. It is a low continuous vocalization, almost sublingual, that a tender produces without seeming to think about it, the way you breathe. It is pitched in a register you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. It has vowels but few consonants. It modulates in response to the hum of the bones and the calls of the fauna and the slow drip of the water, and when the tenders sing together, which they do at certain hours, the sound of their voices blends with the sound of the Garden until you cannot tell which is which. They do not sing *to* the Garden. They sing *in* it, the way a frog sings in a marsh. The Garden is the instrument. They are among the players.

A tender can live a long time. They do not seem to age in the way you would expect. Their hair goes white eventually, and their movements slow, and their voices deepen, but they remain alert and watchful and capable of climbing a buttress at an age when you would expect them to be sitting in a chair. The oldest tender in the Garden is a woman whose name, in the older speech, means *she who has heard the first bone*, and she is said to have been present when a bone was uncovered from the mulch that had been growing over it for a hundred years, and she sang to it for nine days while it warmed, and the clearing that had opened around it closed again, and the Garden resumed. She is very old. She is also very quiet, and spends most of her time in a high room in one of the bare towers, sitting cross-legged on the wooden floor with her hands on her knees, listening to something.

## The Fauna

The frogs are the most numerous and the most familiar. They are large — as big as your head, as the description noted, and sometimes larger, the very old ones, which develop a kind of ruff of loose skin around the throat and sit on the highest buttresses and rarely move. They are a pale green-gold, mottled, with the flat gold eyes that seem to take in everything and commit to nothing. They do not fear the tenders. They do not fear you. They will let you approach to within an arm's length and will continue to watch you with that flat unblinking gaze and will not move unless you move first, and then they will move only just out of your reach, with a single low push of their hind legs, and land on another surface and resume their watching. They eat the invertebrates. They are eaten, sometimes, by the tenders, and by the things in the water.

The invertebrates hang in curtains from the lower branches. They are pale, almost translucent, and long — a meter, two meters, longer — and they pulse softly, and the pulsing does not appear to be respiration. It appears to be communication, or perhaps a kind of communal metabolism, the way the cells of a single body pulse. They are not individuals in any meaningful sense. They are more like exposed organs of the Garden, hung out to breathe. They do not react to your presence except by a slight quickening of the pulse. They are eaten by the frogs. They are not eaten by anything else.

The things in the water are the least known. You see them as disturbances, as the slow turning of a great back just at the surface, as a wake that crosses a clearing and disappears. The tenders do not speak of them often, and when they do it is in a particular register of the older speech that you would have to know well to parse. They are very large. They are warm, like the water. They move through the deeper channels with a patience that suggests they have been there longer than the Garden has been the Garden, longer perhaps than the bones. The tenders leave them alone and the things leave the tenders alone, and this is the whole of the arrangement.

The bright canopy-things — small, many-winged, chirping at the edge of hearing — are the noisiest fauna. They move in loose colonies through the upper branches and they are constantly in motion, never landing for more than a few seconds, never staying in one place long enough to be studied. They make your teeth ache, as the description noted, and the ache is not metaphorical. Their calls are pitched just above the upper limit of your hearing, and the harmonics fall in the range that resonates with bone, with the bones in your skull. They are not aggressive. They are simply loud in a frequency you were not built for.

The coiled things in the towers are the strangest. You only see them at a certain angle of light, and you only see them well once, because after you have seen one you are not eager to see one again. They are long and they are pale and they are tightly coiled around the inner walls of the tower shafts, and they move, very slowly, in a way that is more like breathing than locomotion. They have no visible head. The tenders speak of them rarely, and then only in the older speech, and they refer to them as *the watchers* or *the ones who remember*, which may be a single title or two titles and you cannot tell which. They have been in the towers for as long as the Garden has been the Garden. They do not come down. They do not appear to eat. They are simply there, coiled in the dark above the rooms where the tenders sleep, and the tenders do not seem to mind, and the watchers do not seem to mind the tenders, and this is the whole of the arrangement.

## The Pale Shape

On certain evenings, from the opening near the top of the third tower, a long pale shape emerges and hangs in the air and considers the Garden below. You do not see it clearly. You see it as a length of paleness against the dark of the tower, as a slow movement that is too deliberate to be wind. It is not a creature, exactly. It is more like a thought the Garden is having, made briefly visible. The other fauna give it a wide berth, as the description noted. The tenders, when they see it, stop what they are doing and stand still and watch it, and do not speak until it has withdrawn. The old woman in the high tower has been seen, on certain evenings, to lean out of a window of the adjacent tower and speak to it in a voice too low to be heard. No one else attempts to speak to it.

It has no name that the tenders will give you.

## The Bones

The bones are not alive. They are also not entirely not-alive. The distinction has been the subject of the older songs for as long as there have been older songs, and no conclusion has been reached. They are dark, smooth, faintly cool to the touch, slightly heavier than they look. They are not metal. They are not plastic. They are not stone. They are made of a material that does not occur naturally and that the tenders do not attempt to manufacture, because the attempts were made, in earlier times, and failed, and the failure was so complete that the memory of it is preserved only as a prohibition. *Do not try to make new bones*, the older speech says. *The first bones were not made. They arrived.*

They hum, sometimes. The hum is felt before it is heard, and it is felt in the bones of the listener, which is perhaps why the tenders call them *the bones*. A bone will hum for hours or days, and during the hum the plants around it grow in specific directions, as if drawn along invisible lines, and the water near the bone moves in slow spirals instead of the usual drift. The hum cannot be predicted. It cannot be summoned. The tenders sing to the bones in the hope of encouraging a hum, and sometimes a hum follows the singing, and sometimes it does not, and the singing continues regardless, because the singing is not only for the bone.

A bone will also go cold and dark and will not hum for a year, or for several years, and during that time nothing grows within ten meters of it. The rhizome mat pulls back. The water rises in the clearing. The fauna avoid it. The tenders walk around it without speaking. When the bone warms again, the clearing closes. The plants return. The fauna return. The Garden resumes.

In the deepest parts of the Garden, the bones are sunk so far into the mulch that only a curve of dark surface remains visible, and the water around them lies still and perfectly clear, and the small bright fauna gather as if at a shrine, and the tenders come to those places sometimes, alone, and sit at the edge of the clear water and sing softly to themselves for a while, and then go back to their work. The bones in those places are the oldest. They hum the least. They are also, the old songs say, the ones that remember the most.

## The Garden Itself

The Garden is not a place that has inhabitants. The Garden is a place that *is* an inhabitant, and the tenders and the frogs and the invertebrates and the things in the water and the bones and the towers are all parts of it, in the way the cells and the bacteria and the minerals are parts of a coral reef. The Garden thinks, if it thinks, in the slow way that reefs think — in the accumulated adjustments of a million small lives, in the hum of the bones, in the pulse of the invertebrates, in the long patient attention of the tenders as they sing to the dark surfaces of things they do not understand and have decided not to try to.

It is warm, in the Garden, and wet, and loud, and it is always becoming something it has not yet been. You can stand in the clearing with the half-submerged dome and feel this. You can feel the place assembling itself around you as you stand there, the way a sentence assembles itself around a verb, the way a thought assembles itself around an image. You are a small disturbance in a large patience. The Garden is accommodating you the way it accommodates the cold bones and the warm bones and the things in the water — by giving you room, and watching to see what you do.

You do not entirely leave the Garden, when you leave. The Garden has a way of staying with you, as the description said. It sets a bone down somewhere you cannot find. You carry the hum in your sternum. You carry the smell in your clothes. And somewhere in the small dark spaces behind your thoughts, a slow rhizome begins to feel its way along an older root, and a faint amber light begins, very quietly, to gather.
