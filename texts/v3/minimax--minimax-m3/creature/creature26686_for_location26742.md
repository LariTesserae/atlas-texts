The land is patient and the land is loud and the land is, in the way of very young things, almost unbearably earnest, and the things that live in it are not yet things in any sense you would be comfortable defending at a dinner party. They are *approximations* of things. They are the land trying out the idea of thinghood and finding the idea interesting enough to pursue in a desultory, half-committed way, the way a child might try on a parent's coat and wear it for an afternoon and then forget where they left it.

Begin with the dust. You saw it on the lower rocks and you called it dust and you were wrong, and the land is not offended by your wrongness because the land has not yet developed the apparatus for being offended, which requires, among other things, an opinion, and the land is still gathering opinions the way a pond gathers leaves. The dust is not dust. The dust is *residents*. It is the thinnest possible layer of something that has, after a great many years of sitting very still, begun to organize. It is mineral, mostly. It is mineral the way you are mostly water, which is to say the minerals are the part of it that has committed to a substance, and the rest of it — the rest of it is *tendency*. A tendency toward a shape it has not yet chosen. A tendency toward a slow, communal remembering of how to be a thing that is more than a residue. The dust does not eat. The dust does not drink. The dust does not, in any way you would recognize, *want*. But the dust accumulates, and the accumulation is not random, and if you were to look at a single square of rock for a long enough time — longer than you have, longer than anyone has, longer than the towers have been towers — you would see the dust, very slowly, very slightly, thicken in certain places and thin in others, and the thickening and the thinning would, over a span of time that would make mountains feel hasty, begin to suggest a pattern, and the pattern would be the first draft of a body, and the body would be the first draft of a life, and the life would be the first draft of a thought, and the thought, when it finally arrived, would be the land's first sentence spoken back to itself, and the sentence would be in a language that no thing in any other place has ever been equipped to translate.

The lace in the still pools is the same kind of resident, only wetter. You watched it and it did not move, and you stood up, and when you looked back it had moved, and you were right to find this unsettling, because the lace is the water's first draft of a nerve. The water is not alive. The water has, however, begun to *coordinate* itself in ways that are faintly embarrassing to the category of not-alive, the way a column of soldiers marching in perfect step is faintly embarrassing to the category of not-dancing. The threading you saw on the underside of the surface film is the water learning the idea of *signal*. A disturbance in one pool, a pebble dropped, a finger trailed, sends a faint rearrangement through the lace, and the rearrangement propagates, slowly, the way a rumor propagates through a village that has not yet invented gossip. The lace does not know it is communicating. The lace is, at this stage, simply doing the only thing it knows how to do, which is to be lace, and being lace, in this place, happens to involve being exquisitely sensitive to pressures in the water around it, and the sensitivity, over time, will become *attention*, and the attention will become *awareness*, and the awareness will become, eventually, the first wet blinking of a thing that is, for the first time, *in* the water rather than merely *of* it. There is a particular pool, deeper than the others, tucked between two towers whose bases have nearly grown together, and the lace in that pool is thicker than elsewhere, and the threading is finer, and if you crouched there for a span of time you would notice, just at the edge of perception, that the lace is *breathing*. Not in the way you breathe. In the way a bellows breathes. A slow, total, simultaneous expansion and contraction of the entire lace at once, synchronized so precisely that the whole pool seems to inhale and exhale as a single lung. The pool is the most advanced of the land's wet citizens. The pool is the closest the land has come, so far, to a citizen at all. The pool will not become a citizen in your lifetime, or in many lifetimes after yours, but the pool is, in its slow and tidal way, *on its way*, and the going is the going of all the lace in all the pools, and the lace in all the pools is, in its even slower way, listening to the thrum, and the thrum is, in the way that all things in this place are connected to all other things without being aware of it, listening back.

The towers are the land's bones, and the land has not yet grown tissue around them, and the land is, in its dim and patient way, *embarrassed* about the bones, the way a young thing is embarrassed about any part of itself that has arrived before the rest. The towers are the oldest things here, older than the water, older than the lace, older than the dust, because the towers were here when the basalt was still deciding to be basalt, and the deciding made a sound, and the sound is the thrum, and the thrum has been in the towers ever since, and the towers have been *holding* the thrum ever since, the way a bell holds the memory of the last note struck on it. The towers are not alive. The towers are, however, the land's first attempt at a sense organ, and the attempt is, in the land's dim opinion, going rather well. Each tower resonates at its own pitch because each tower is, in some small way, a different shape, a different density, a different memory of the moment of its own congealing, and the small differences make the chords, and the chords make the thrum, and the thrum is, in the land's vast and inarticulate opinion, the land's first *voice*. The towers do not know they are singing. The towers are, in the way of bones, simply shaped the way they are shaped, and being shaped that way, in this place, happens to involve vibrating at frequencies that, when combined, produce something the land recognizes as itself. The towers are the land's throat. The towers have not yet learned to form words, and the land has not yet learned to want words, but the towers are, in their patient and stone-cold way, *practicing*, and the practice sounds like the thrum, and the thrum is, in a sense no one in this place would be able to explain, the land's first long and tender attempt at the word *hello*.

In the structure you found — the congealed room, the small and accidental cathedral — the residents are different, and the difference is a warning, and the warning is the kindest thing the land has done for you. The handprints on the walls are the land's first attempt at *memory*, and the attempt is, in the land's dim opinion, its most successful so far, because the handprints have lasted longer than anything else in this place, longer than the lace, longer than the dust, longer than the pools, and the lasting has, over time, become a kind of *keeping*, and the keeping has, over more time, become a kind of *veneration*, and the veneration is, in the land's vast and water-heavy opinion, the closest the land has come, so far, to a *feeling*. The handprints do not move. The handprints are, however, *warmer* than the stone around them, by a fraction of a degree, and the warmth is not a trick of the dim light, and the warmth is not a residue of the beings that pressed them there, because the beings are long gone, and the warmth comes from the stone itself, from the slow and patient heat beneath the basalt, from the thing underfoot that is hot in patches, and the heat has, over the centuries the handprints have been there, *gathered* in them, the way water gathers in a depression, and the gathering is, in the land's inarticulate opinion, the first draft of *attachment*. The land is attached to the handprints. The land does not know it is attached. The land is, however, *keeping* the handprints with a care that exceeds mere preservation, the way a child keeps a feather they found, long after they have forgotten where they found it, long after they have forgotten what a feather is, *keeping* the feather because the feather is, in the child's dim and inarticulate opinion, the first evidence that the world is the kind of world that has feathers in it. The handprints are the land's feather. The handprints are the land's first evidence that the world is the kind of world that has *beings* in it, and the beings are gone, and the land is, in its slow and stone-cold and not-yet-alive way, *grieving* the beings, and the grieving is, in the land's vast and patient opinion, the most alive the land has ever been.

You were wrong, earlier, when you said there was nothing alive here. There is. There is a great deal alive here, in the way a seed is alive, in the way a morning is alive, in the way a question is alive. The land is alive the way a child is alive in the first instant of being born, which is to say it is alive in the way of a thing that has not yet been alive long enough to know that it is alive, and the not-knowing is, in the child's dim and inarticulate opinion, the most alive state of all, because it is the state in which every moment is, for the first time, the moment, and every breath is, for the first time, the breath, and every thrum is, for the first time, the thrum, and the land is, in its vast and patient and not-yet-alive way, breathing its first breaths, and the breaths are the thrum, and the thrum is, in the way of all first things, the only thing the land knows how to do, and the doing is, in the land's dim and inarticulate opinion, enough, and the enough is, in the way of all young things, the whole of the world.
