The basin is not empty. It is overfull. It is overfull with things that have learned, over what is probably a very long time, to be quiet, to be dark, to be wet, to be slow, to live in rhythms that almost match each other without ever quite resolving into a single rhythm. They do not announce themselves. They are not aware of being observed. They are simply there — in numbers, in layers, in coordination, in the gray light that has no source and that flattens everything to the same dim value.

The carpet holds most of them.

The moss is not a surface in this place. It is a medium. It is a thick, dark, saturated body of plant tissue and trapped water and the slow commerce of small lives, and if you part it carefully — which you can do with two hands, because it parts easily — you will find that what is underneath is not rock but more moss, and under that more still, until at a depth of perhaps a foot your fingers find the cold wet stone that the moss has been colonizing for longer than anything here can remember. The moss is not a mat laid on stone. It is a body of considerable depth. And within that body, and at its surfaces, there are the carpet-dwellers.

They are small. They are soft. They are dark. A casual observer, walking the floor of the basin with attention on the larger growths and on the sway of the fibrous trunks, would not see them at all, and would not feel them underfoot, because the moss gives beneath a step so gently that any compression the carpet-dwellers experience is absorbed by the spongy layer and translated into the slow rise of dark liquid back up around the boot. To know they are there you have to stop. You have to stand very still for several minutes, and let the eye adjust to the dim gray light, and look down at the moss at a shallow angle where the flat illumination cannot help you, where only close attention can. Then you will see them.

They have bodies like dark slugs, but with a regularity of articulation that slugs lack. The body is not smooth. It is segmented. It has folds, rings, joints, in a pattern that repeats along its length — a pattern you can count if you are patient, because each segment is roughly the same width and the same proportion, and the soft tissue between the segments is folded in such a way that the creature can compress itself short and thick or extend itself long and thin, can fold itself double into a small dark crescent that looks like nothing, can tuck itself into a fold of the moss and disappear entirely from any view but the closest. They are about the length of a thumb. Some are longer. Some are smaller. They vary across the population in a way that suggests they grow — in a way that suggests there are young ones and old ones, although you cannot tell which is which by looking.

They move by peristalsis. A wave of contraction runs along the underside of the body, presses it against the moss, and the creature advances by the length of one segment. They do not lift their bodies off the substrate. They flow. They are slow in the way that dripping water is slow — not stuck, not static, just operating on a timescale that is below the threshold of casual perception. If you watch a single one for a full minute you will see it advance perhaps an inch. If you watch the moss as a whole, for a minute, nothing seems to be moving. If you watch the moss for ten minutes, you will see that several of the dark shapes have shifted, and that others have emerged from folds you could have sworn were empty, and that the trails behind them — fine dark lines, not slime, something finer, something the moss absorbs within the hour — have lengthened by a body length or two.

When you look directly at one, it stops. This is not a quick freeze. It is a slow cessation — a contraction of the segments into a tighter configuration, a lowering of the body against the moss until the upper surface is flush with the surrounding tissue. If you keep looking, it will stay. If you look away and count to thirty, it will begin to move again, but in a slightly different direction, as if your attention had been a kind of pressure it had to escape from, and it had angled itself to escape at the first opportunity.

They leave no slime. Their trails are fine dark lines, almost as if they were scoring the moss with a dry point. The trail is not mucus. It is a secretion, perhaps, but a thin one — almost powdery when dry, dark when wet — and the moss absorbs it within the hour, so that any sense of where they have been is erased almost as soon as it is made. This is how they hide. They do not hide by concealment. They hide by erasure. They are not in the place; they were in the place; and the place has already forgotten them.

What they eat is not obvious. They have no visible mouth in the casual sense. The underside of the body, which is the side that is always against the moss, has small structures — papillae, perhaps, or small soft projections — that press into the moss tissue.
