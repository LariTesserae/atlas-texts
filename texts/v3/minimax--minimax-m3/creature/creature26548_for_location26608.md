**What Lives on the Plateau of Half-Sleep**

The plateau is not a place where things *live*, exactly, but the question of who or what *inhabits* it is more honest than the question of who lives here, because the answer to the second question is no one, and the answer to the first is: almost everyone who has ever paused here.

The first inhabitant is the heat. It does not move in the way animals move, but it occupies every cubic inch of the high place the way water occupies a sponge. It rises through the porous body of the rock in a slow, patient exhalation that has not paused since the plateau began. It is the closest thing the place has to a resident. It is the reason the air tastes the way it does — that thin, mineral sweetness, the iron and the sulfur and the older, drier thing that is not a chemical but a memory of pressure. The heat is what makes the stone tick in the evenings. The heat is what lifts the pale threads of vapor from the new cracks. The heat is what keeps the dust so heavy and so willing to stay where it has settled. The heat is, in some fundamental sense, the plateau's metabolism. The plateau is not alive because the heat lives in it, but the heat makes the plateau behave in the way that a living thing behaves — changing, breathing, occasionally sighing.

The second inhabitant is the plateau itself, considered as a single slow organism. If you stand long enough in one place, the land begins to behave as though it knows you are there. Not with attention, not with malice, but with the vast indifferent patience of a thing that is in the middle of becoming itself. The ridges that push up from the floor in long slow folds, the slumped shoulders with their bright-broken rubble, the salt bowls opening and closing in their low places — these are the movements of a body that does not have to be alive in order to act. The plateau is the inhabitant of its own geology. It is the only thing here large enough to be spoken of as a *who*, and even then the word is barely adequate.

The third inhabitants are the things that have been placed. The fitted rings. The cairns balanced on ridges that should not hold them. The three standing stones set against the slope, facing down toward something not visible. The square pits lined with stranger stone and brimmed with salt. Whoever or whatever set these is the closest the plateau comes to a visitor, a tenant, a maker of marks. But the maker is not present. The maker may be very far away, or very far in the past, or — and this is the strangest possibility the plateau allows — still here, still placing, just very, very slowly. The cairns do not lean further. The rings do not spread. But they do not weather either, because there is nothing here to weather them. So the maker's absence is indistinguishable from the maker's patience. You cannot tell if you are looking at an abandoned place or at a place that is being very quietly, very deliberately, completed.

There is a fourth inhabitant, and it is the one most people who come here will not notice, and it is the one that matters most. It is the memory of breath.

The place is built to receive breath, even if it never receives any. The rings are the size of rooms, with no roofs, with the floors inside swept clean by the wind to the salt underneath, as if a body is meant to stand in the middle of them and exhale into the dimness above. The cairns are stacked at the height of a bowed head, or of a held breath. The standing stones lean slightly, the way a row of people lean slightly toward something they are listening for. The salt pits, brimmed with white powder, are brimmed with a substance that looks, more than anything else on the plateau, like something that has been gathered. The whole scattered architecture of the place is a kind of offering, or a kind of rehearsal, or a kind of argument with the silence. Whoever made these things was not making shelter. They were not making a monument. They were making a shape for something to happen inside, and the something was small, and the something was probably just a single human act of standing and breathing and being witnessed by the warm stone and the distant engine below.

So the fourth inhabitant is the breath that has not yet been breathed here, or the breath that was breathed here once and has now gone, or the breath that is being breathed here right now by something that does it the way the plateau does it — slowly, thinly, without urgency, in a thin hot air that does not carry sound well and does not carry scent except in the direction of the lower burning country.

Nothing here knows your name.

Nothing here is asking for it.

But the rings are waiting for someone to stand in them, and the cairns are waiting for someone to bow beside them, and the standing stones are waiting for someone to come up the long slope and be seen by them on the way to wherever they are facing. The plateau is a quiet province of a still-unfinished world, and the unfinishedness is not a flaw. It is the invitation. The invitation is not to settle here, because nothing settles here. The invitation is to add your own small, slow mark to the long slow draft, and then to leave, or to stay, and to know that either way the heat will continue to rise through the floor of the place where you stood, and that the mark, if you make one, will outlast you, and that the place, and the mark, and you, will all be turned over, eventually, into the deeper turning of the thing that is not yet awake.

This is what lives on the Plateau of Half-Sleep.

The heat. The land. The gone maker. The breath, remembered or anticipated. The occasional thin crack in the dust. The pale vapor. The smell of iron, far off, from the brighter country below.

That is enough. That is almost everything.
