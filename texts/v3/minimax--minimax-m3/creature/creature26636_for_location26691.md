What lives here is several things at once, and some of them are each other.

Start with what you can pick up.

The branching growths in the cracks of the dark stone. The coral-shaped things — dense, warm, ringing faintly when struck. You were told, or you decided on your own, that they are not alive. They do not flinch. They do not withdraw. They do not bleed. They continue being warm after you pick them. They continue ringing after you break them. They are present in every damp place — every seep, every basin, every inside curve of stone where water collects. There are millions of them. There are more of them than of almost anything else here.

And yet.

If you hold a piece of one to your ear you hear a held note. Not the strike-ring, which goes away. A low internal hum, like a string that is being touched rather than plucked. The same note every time, or close to the same note. It does not change with how you hold the piece. It is the note of that piece. It is also the note of every piece. You cannot tell whether that is one fact or two.

And they are warm. The plateau is cold. The air is colder. Nothing else here is warm except the growths and whatever the sun is, and the sun you have not seen. The growths are warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, not the way a stone in the sun is warm. They have their own warmth, kept against the cold, spent slowly.

A guess you can hold without committing to: the growths are what some of the makers became. The ones who stayed. The ones who lay down in the damp places and let a long time happen to them. They were human-shaped once, or something-shaped once, and now they are coral-shaped, branching, dense, warm, ringing. They lie in the cracks the way a body lies in a bed. They cluster in the seeps the way bodies cluster around water. They are not unhappy to be picked up. You learn not to pick them up for this reason.

But you cannot test the guess. The growths will not tell you. They will ring. They will be warm. They will not say.

What else lives here.

The water.

The question of whether water is alive is older than this plateau and not a question you will settle here. But the water here does things. It climbs. It domes above the rims of basins by a thumb's width, by a finger's, and considers letting go. It lets go. It falls. It runs down the outside. It finds another basin, or a crack too narrow, and is gone. It condenses out of the air in slow threads. It arrives from somewhere beneath. It does not flow only downhill. Some of it climbs, slowly, against the slope. You watch it.

It does not spoil. It does not dry up. You can leave a basin and come back and the level is the same. You can drink from any basin and it will refill. It tastes of stone and then of nothing.

A guess you can hold: the water is patient. It is the plateau's patience, or its counting, or its blood. It has been doing this longer than you have been watching. It will continue longer than you watch. It is not in a hurry. It is not going anywhere specific. It is going.

Then there is the plateau itself.

You begin, after some time, to suspect that the plateau is alive the way a lichen is alive — slowly, on surfaces, without a body you can recognize, with very little interest in being recognized. There is the felt-not-heard hum in the ground. A note under everything, under the wind and the water and the ringing. It is not the wind. It is not the water. It is something the rock is doing on a timescale longer than you can watch. The growths cluster thickest in the bowl-shaped valleys, where the water collects, where the wrong light is held longest. You stand in such a place and feel you are inside something that is breathing. Damp and dry. Warm and cool. Full and emptying. On a cycle too long for you to fully witness but not so long that you cannot suspect.

The plateau is alive. Or: the part of the plateau you can stand on is a small piece of something older and larger that lives the way an old reef lives — built by what lives in it, indistinguishable from what built it, not particularly interested in being told apart.

Then the voice.

You have been hearing it and not counting it. A long exhalation, very far away or very close, never in the direction you are facing. The shape of a person being present without doing anything. You stop tracking it. You start tracking it. You stop tracking it. It comes from one basin and then another. It does not speak. It does not form a word. It is the breath of someone who has stopped making things and has not stopped being here.

A guess you can hold: the makers became the place. Some became the growths. Some became the water. Some became the hum in the rock. The voice is the breath of one of them that did not become anything and is still just a person, somewhere in the wrong distance, present, exhaling. Or the voice is the place's breath, and you have been hearing it as a person because that is the shape your ear gives to a held exhalation in a quiet place.

Or there were never any makers. There were only hands. The hands made the cairns and the hollows and the cup-marks and the disc, and the hands are gone, and what is left is the place remembering the hands, in its own way, in every shape the place can hold a memory in. The growths are one shape. The water is one shape. The voice is one shape. The cairns are one shape. The sleeping hollow with its thin mattress of warm growth is one shape. The disc with the hole that fits a thumb is one shape.

You can lie in the sleeping hollow and feel held. The hollow fits you. The growth under you is warm. You do not sleep. You rest in a way that does not have a name. You listen. The voice comes, or you imagine it. You do not track it.

What lives here.

The plateau, in the slow sense. The water, in the patient sense. The growths, in the warm sense. The voice, in the no-direction sense. The tools, in the careful sense — still sharp, still fitted, still doing nothing, waiting in the lee of slabs for hands that are not coming back or that are coming back very slowly, on the timescale of the held note in the black coral. The cairns, in the standing-still sense — each one the last position of someone who stood there once and did not move from there, who is now the cairn, who will be the cairn for a long time. The cup-marks, in the counting sense — someone was counting something, toward something, and stopped, and the row of marks is what was left, and the count is still in the marks, unfinished, waiting to be continued or never to be continued.

The disc, in the no-use sense. The disc is the most alive of all the objects here. It has no identified use. It was polished smooth by someone who chose to polish it. The hole in its middle is exactly the right size for a thumb. It sits under a cairn or in the lee of a slab and it is not a tool and not a weapon and not a coin and not an eye and not anything you can name, and you pick it up and turn it in your hand and you can feel, without being able to say why, that this object is the most inhabited thing on the plateau. Something lived in the making of it. Something is still living in the not-using of it.

And possibly you, for as long as you are here.

And the sun, somewhere above the wrong sky, which throws all the light, which has not been seen by anyone for a long time, which might be why the place is the way it is — a place lit for a very long time by something that has not come down to visit, and that is patient, the way water is patient, the way a held note is patient, the way a cairn is patient, the way a maker is patient after the making is done.
