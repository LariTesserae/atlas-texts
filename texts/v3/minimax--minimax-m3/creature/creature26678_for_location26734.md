The hum is the first inhabitant.

Not the wind, which is only the carrier. The hum itself, which is older than the mountain and is making the mountain, the way a singer is making a song by continuing to sing it. The hum does not live in the way a person lives, with a body that can be pointed at or a face that can be looked at. It lives the way a river lives — by continuing, by wearing, by the slow accumulation of itself into the shape of the place it is moving through. It is everywhere. It is in the rock. It is in the air. It is in the cold that has forgotten to be warm. It has been here so long that the silence that used to be here has given up and gone somewhere else.

The hum does not answer. It does not speak. But if you sit in one place long enough — long enough for the place to learn your shape, long enough for your body to warm the stone against your back by a degree the hum can feel — the hum will begin to thicken around you. Not louder. More present. The way a room is more present when someone is in it, even if they are not moving. The hum is not a person. But the hum is paying attention, in the way that a long, slow process pays attention to the things that come to rest inside it.

The plants are the second inhabitants.

Or the first, if you count by what is visible. They are not green, or not only green. Some are the deep brick-red of dried blood. Some are pale violet, the color of a bruise that is healing. Some are black and glitter with a kind of metallic shine, as if each cell contained a flake of mica. A few are almost white, almost luminous, and these are the ones you see first as you pick your way along the ridge, faint smudges of light against the pewter rock.

They are not rooted. They are glued to the stone in ways that don't quite work like roots. They do not draw water from the rock. There is no water to draw. They do not, as far as you can tell, eat anything at all. They simply persist. A handspan of lichen here might be three inches thick and pulsing faintly with internal structure, as if it were a small lung that has forgotten how to exhale. A fist-sized clump of something mushroom-soft there might be the most concentrated mass of life you have ever seen outside of a whale carcass washed up on a beach. They grow so slowly that you could mistake them for stone, and you would not be entirely wrong, because the rocks in this place are not quite rocks either.

You begin to suspect, after a while, that the plants are not eating the rock. They are eating the hum. They are the visible part of whatever process the hum is conducting — the leaves, if the hum is the root. They grow thickest in the lee of outcroppings where the wind is forced into eddies and the hum piles up. They fur the underside of every shelf-rock, even where the wind never reaches, because the hum is in the rock itself, in the slight wrongness of the rock, in the way the grain keeps shifting if you stare too long. The plants are the hum's way of being in the place. The plants are what the hum looks like when it becomes dense enough to be seen.

The plants do not move. But you will notice, if you stay long enough, that they lean. Not toward the sun. There is no sun. Not toward the wind. They lean toward you. Toward where you are sitting. As if they would like to be closer to the place where you are, without knowing what a place is. As if the concept of *closeness* is something the hum is trying on, slowly, through the medium of a small red lichen, and you are the thing it is trying to be close to. They lean the way a dream leans — in time, in slow accumulation, in the way something becomes more itself if you keep watching it. The leaning is not a movement. The leaning is the plant becoming more itself, and itself is something that is inclined toward you.

The structures are the third inhabitants.

A low wall of loose stones, partly knocked down. A tripod of pale poles, lashed together with strips of something that might be hide but probably isn't. A circle of small upright slabs, no taller than your knee, set into the ground so the wind cannot move them. They are the marks of hands. The hands are gone. The people who held the hands are gone, or are smudged, or have become the place, or are in the slow process of becoming the place, and the line between *gone* and *becoming the place* is negotiable here, the way distance is negotiable, the way the slope of a ridge is negotiable.

The structures are not alive. The structures are what the place is trying to remember. The poles remember the lashings — by the wear at the joints, by the lean, by the way they are set into each other. The circle of slabs remembers being a circle, by the way the hum inside it is louder than the hum outside, by the way the air inside it is slightly thicker, slightly more present, slightly more inclined to be a place. The low wall remembers being a wall, by the way it is still partly a wall, by the way the stones that have not yet fallen are still set against each other in the way a wall is set against itself.

The structures do not do anything. They are not shrines, as far as you can tell. They are not shelters, not hearths, not graves. They are just made, the way a person makes a mark on a wall to say they were here. But the wall is not a wall, and the person is not a person, and the saying is not a saying, and yet the mark is here, and the hum is louder inside it than outside it, and the place is more present inside it than outside it, and that is what the structures are for. They are places where the hum is slightly more itself. They are places where the place is slightly more a place. They are what the prior inhabitants left behind, and what the prior inhabitants have become, and what is being made, slowly, in the dim light, under the constant screaming of the wind.

The figures are the fourth inhabitants.

These are the things that emerge, if you walk the range long enough, from the rock itself.

A boulder with a shape in its side that is almost a face, or almost a hand, or almost the curve of a shoulder. A ridge that is, when you look at it directly, a little too regular to be a ridge, a little too suggestive of a back bent forward under a weight. A valley that is, when you are walking in it, a little too narrow to be a valley, a little too much like the space between two walls of a room, or the inside of a throat.

The figures are not statues. They are not finished. They are the slow emergence of something from the rock, the way the plants are the slow emergence of something from the hum. The rocks here are not quite rocks. They are heavy, and they are mineral, and they are real to the hand. But they are also *slightly wrong*. The grain keeps shifting. The color slides if you stare too long. There is something about the stone in this place that does not fully commit to being stone, that is waiting, in a long geological patience, to become something else.
