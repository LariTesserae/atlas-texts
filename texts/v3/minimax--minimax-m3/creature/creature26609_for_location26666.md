# What Lives Here

The ones you would notice, if you came down the rounded lip of basalt and stood blinking in the inverted dusk, are the amphibians. They are not what you would expect from the word *amphibian*. The word conjures frogs, conjures the quick bright tongue of a puddle in summer, conjures something that hops between two simplicities. These do not hop. These are not bright. These have been here for what the moss can no longer measure, and what the moss can no longer measure is the moss's only measure of time, and so the word *amphibian* has begun, on its own, to mean something slightly different than it used to.

They are small. The largest of them, the old females who sit by the seven stones and sing the loudest songs, are about the length of the second joint of your thumb. The smallest, the ones just out of the egg and not yet singing, are about the size of a lentil. They are dark. They are the same dark as the chamber, which is to say the same dark as everything here, which is to say a dark that has had time to become its own color. Their skins are not the slick black of a wet stone; they are a deep, soft brown that fades into umber into green-into-black, and when the dripping light catches them, which is rare, they give back a faint copper sheen along the spines of their backs, like the patina on a coin that has been held too long in one pocket.

Their eyes are present. Their eyes are wrong. Each one has the shape of an eye, the lid, the slight bulge of the orbit, but the surface is clouded with a thin membrane that does not entirely open and does not entirely close. They can sense light. They cannot shape it into anything that resembles a room, a wall, an edge of stone, the silhouette of another body. What they see, if *see* is still the word, is a wash. A difference in pressure. A warmer patch in the green-black soup of the chamber means a place where the dripping has slowed, where the moss has built itself into a ring, where one of the seven is humming close beneath. A cooler patch means a draft, means the inverted forest is breathing down, means a place where the wind above has found a seam in the basalt and is asking the chamber to be porous for a moment.

They navigate by the hum. This is not metaphor. Their feet are wide and flat and very soft, and on the underside of each foot there is a band of fine, dark hair that trembles when the hum passes through it. They walk the way a person walks across a frozen lake at dawn, listening with their whole body to whether the ice will hold. They walk slowly. They walk with their bellies close to the stone. They stop often. When they stop, they lower their heads and press the underside of their jaw against the wet basalt, and they are tasting the hum, in the way that a tongue tastes salt. The hum tells them where they are in the same way that a river tells a fish where it is, by its flavor, by its current, by the small pressures that change from one place to the next.

There are seven flavors of hum. There are seven currents. There are seven pools of pressure, and each one is slightly different, the way seven wells on the same hillside give back water that is slightly different. The amphibians know the difference. They know it the way you know the difference between your mother's voice and a stranger's: not by cataloging the features, but by the way the body responds, by the way the small hairs on the feet stand up.

They sing to the hum.

Their mouths are not really mouths, in the way you would recognize them. The jaw is wide and shallow and the throat behind it is large, larger than the body would suggest, taking up nearly a third of the interior. The throat is not for swallowing. It is for resonating. When an amphibian wants to sing, it does not open its mouth and push air out, the way a voice works in a mammal. It closes its mouth, seals the lips, and pulls the air *in*, slowly, and the air passes over a membrane at the back of the throat that vibrates against it. The sound comes out through the skin of the belly. The belly becomes a drum. The whole body becomes, for as long as the song lasts, a small instrument tuned to the hum beneath.

They sing at the hum's frequency. They sing at frequencies slightly above it and slightly below, and they sing at frequencies that the hum does not have on its own but that the singing brings out of it, the way a tuning fork brought near a string will pull a harmonic from the string that was not there before. The chamber is full of these harmonics now. It has been full of them for as long as the amphibians can remember, which is more generations than they can count, which is why the song has become, for them, what the air is for you — a thing they move inside of rather than a thing they produce.

The songs are not long. Most of them last no longer than a few seconds. But they are very many. A dozen amphibians can be sitting within a meter of one another, each singing its own short phrase, each phrase tuned to the same hum, and the chamber will turn this into a weave, into a slow-moving cloth of sound that has no edges and no seams. The egg-layers, the females, sing low. The males sing higher. The young, when they first begin to sing, sing at frequencies that are still finding themselves, that wobble, that sometimes slip out of harmony and then, with a small effort, slip back in. The wobble is one of the things the chamber is listening for. The chamber has decided, in its slow way, that the wobble matters. The chamber has built itself, over the long centuries, to receive the wobble and to answer it with a small adjustment of its own, a slight change in the pressure of the dripping, a slight softening of the hum's pitch, as if the chamber were saying *I heard you* and *come back when you are ready*.

The moss knows all of this. The moss is not a creature in the way the amphibians are creatures. The moss does not sing. The moss does not move with intention. But the moss has been here longer than the amphibians have been here, and it has built itself, ring by ring, into a kind of memory. The moss grows in concentric circles around each of the seven places where the hum comes through the floor. The rings are different thicknesses in different places, because the hum has been different strengths in different seasons — though the word *season* is, here, a courtesy. The moss has thick rings in the years when the amphibians sang most fully, when their harmonics drew the chamber into a richer conversation. The moss has thinner rings in the years when the amphibians were fewer, when something happened up on the surface that changed the water, that changed the dripping, that changed what the amphibians had to eat. The moss does not record these things in language. The moss records them in its own thickness. To read the moss you would have to learn moss. To learn moss you would have to be very patient, and very still, and willing to accept that what you learned would not be translatable into anything else.

The amphibians eat the moss. This is the great economy of the chamber. The moss grows on the heat of the hum, and on the minerals that the dripping water brings down from the inverted forest, and on the slow patience of being moss. The amphibians eat the moss, and their droppings, which are small and dark and rich, feed the moss back. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves the chamber. The only export is sound, and sound does not deplete.

Smaller things live among the amphibians.

There are creatures so small you would not see them unless you put your eye very close to the wet stone and breathed on it lightly, the way you breathe on a window in winter to clear it. These are the ones that speak in tones too high and too low for you to track. Some of them are like mites, with bodies the color of the chamber and legs that end in tiny hooks for catching the slow drip. Some of them are like very thin worms, the length of a fingernail, that live in the thin film of water that always covers the basalt and that eat, as far as anyone can tell, the bacteria that live in the film. Some of them are like nothing else. One of them, the largest of the small, is a flat thing, no bigger than a thumbprint, that moves by rippling the edges of its body and that seems to feed on the warmth of the hum itself, or on something the hum brings up through the stone, and that emits, constantly, a thin bright tone that your ear cannot catch but that the amphibians can, and that the amphibians incorporate, somehow, into their own songs, as if it were a seasoning.

The amphibians do not eat these small creatures. The amphibians do not eat one another. The chamber has arranged itself so that this is not necessary. There is enough moss for everyone. There is enough dripping. There is enough hum.

There are times, though, when the chamber feels crowded.

When the amphibian population is high, the moss thins in certain places, and the amphibians gather closer to the seven, and the singing becomes dense, and the harmonics pile up on top of each other until the air itself feels heavier, until a person standing in the chamber would feel the pressure at their temples and at the soft place behind their ears, and would begin to feel that they themselves were being asked to sing, to add their own thin thread to the weave. The chamber, in these moments, is not content. The chamber is hungry for more sound than the amphibians can produce. The chamber is asking the amphibians to invite someone in.

This is when the shape on the ledge begins to be more itself.

The shape on the ledge is not alive. The shape on the ledge is not not-alive. The shape on the ledge is a thing that was once worn on a body, and that has been here longer than the stalactite that has grown around it, and that has been here longer than the moss, and that has been here longer than anything in the chamber except the floor and the hum beneath the floor.

It is the size of a closed fist. It is roughly the weight of a closed fist, except that when you try to lift it, your arm reports back something your eyes would refuse: that it is heavier than it should be, and also lighter, and also a kind of weight that has nothing to do with mass, the way a memory is heavy, the way a question is light. The stalactite has not quite grown over it. The stalactite has grown around it, the way a tree grows around a fencepost it cannot push out, incorporating it, making it part of itself. The stalactite is thick and pale and slightly translucent, and where it meets the shape, the shape has changed the color of the stone. The stone is darker there. The stone is warmer there.

People have sat near the shape. You can tell because the basalt at the base of the ledge has been worn smooth, the way stone is worn smooth at a place where many bodies have rested against it for many years. The smooth place is the exact size and shape of a person sitting cross-legged with their back against the wall. It is not one person. It is many people, over many years, one after another, each one sitting in the same position for as long as they could sit, and then leaving, and then another one coming, and then another, and then another. The smooth place is a record of patience. The smooth place is a record of wanting to know what the shape was, and finding out, and staying anyway.

If you were the next to sit there, and if you were to sit for a long time, you would begin to feel the shape listening to you. Not listening to your words. Listening to the thing behind your words. The shape has no ears. The shape has no face. The shape is just the shape it is, the worn fist-size thing, half-encased in pale stone. But it is doing the middle verb. It is the kind of listening that a question does to the person asking it. It is the kind of listening that silence does to a person who has come to the silence because they could not stay in the noise. The shape, if you sat near it long enough, would begin to feel disappointed in you. Not disappointed in what you have done. Disappointed in the way you have been asking. Disappointed the way a teacher is disappointed in a student who is asking the wrong questions, who has been working very hard, who means well, who is going about the whole project the wrong way. The disappointment would be very kind. The disappointment would not be aimed at you. It would be aimed at the question. The shape would feel that the question you brought with you, the question you have been carrying, is not the question, and it would feel that for you, and it would feel that for you gently, and you would feel that it was feeling it, and you would begin to cry, perhaps, the way people cry when they are very tired and have been carrying something for a long time and have finally been told, kindly, that they are allowed to put it down.

That is the shape on the ledge. That is what it does. That is what it has been doing, for longer than the moss can remember.

The ellipse at the back is different.

The ellipse is not a creature. The ellipse is not a thing that was worn on a body. The ellipse is a region. The ellipse is the way the chamber has arranged itself around something it cannot say, or cannot say in stone or water or moss.

The water that flows toward the ellipse slows as it approaches. This is the first thing you would notice, if you walked that far, which most people do not, because the chamber, by the time you have reached the ellipse, has become harder to walk in. The air is heavier. The dripping has stopped. There is no dripping near the ellipse. The moss has stopped. The amphibians have stopped. The small creatures have stopped. There is no singing. The hum, which is everywhere else in the chamber, is here also, but it is a hum that has been *agreed to*. The hum near the ellipse has the quality of a hum that has been asked to be quieter, and has agreed, but has not stopped, and is performing its quieting the way a person performs quietness when they are not yet asleep and are pretending, for the sake of someone in the room.

The water that flows toward the ellipse becomes, near the ellipse, a sheet of black glass. It does this without any visible cause. It does this because the chamber has decided to do it. The sheet is perfectly still. The sheet reflects nothing, because there is nothing in the chamber for it to reflect except the inverted forest above, and the inverted forest, in this part of the chamber, has gone thin, the ferns smaller, the moss less insistent, as if the forest, too, were being quieter, were pretending to sleep.

At the very edge of the ellipse, the water accelerates. There is no splash. There is no sound. There is no place for the splash to land. Things that fall into the ellipse are gone before they have finished falling. Things that are thrown into the ellipse are gone before they have finished being thrown. The amphibians avoid the ellipse. They avoid it the way animals avoid a cliff in fog, not because they know what a cliff is, but because their feet tell them that the ground is not agreeing to continue, and that the agreement is final.

The ellipse does not seem to belong to the chamber. The ellipse seems to be the chamber's way of *having* something it cannot house. The ellipse is the small black mouth into which everything here, in the end, must flow. The water agrees to this. The dripping agrees to this. The creatures agree to this, by their avoidance. Even the hum, that old and patient hum, agrees to this. The hum quiets near the ellipse, and the quieting is not the hum being weaker but the hum being *different*, the way a voice is different when it has been asked to say a thing it does not want to say, and is saying it anyway, with great tenderness, and great reluctance.

The seven and the ellipse are related. No one can say how. The amphibians do not try to say how. The amphibians sing to the seven, and they avoid the ellipse, and they do not think of these two activities as connected, because the amphibians do not think in the way you think, in the way that connects one thing to another. The amphibians live in a world in which the seven are a song and the ellipse is an edge, and the song and the edge are both part of the chamber, and the chamber is the whole of what is, and what is, is good, and is warm, and is full of small wet life, and is darker than any night you have slept through, and is louder than any silence you have ever tried to keep.

This is what lives here.

These are the singers. These are the small dark ones who speak in tones too high and too low. These are the moss that has become a calendar that does not advance. These are the thin worms in the film of water. These are the flat things that feed on the warmth of the hum. These are the shape on the ledge, doing the middle verb, listening kindly, disappointed kindly. This is the ellipse, which is the chamber's way of having something it cannot say.

And this is you, perhaps, if you came down the rounded lip of basalt and stood blinking in the inverted dusk, and felt the dripping on your shoulders, and felt the hum come up through the soles of your feet, and sat down on the smooth place at the base of the ledge, and began, without quite meaning to, to ask the question the chamber has been waiting for, or to ask no question at all, and to be content, and to be warm, and to be full of small wet life, and to be darker than any night you have slept through, and to be louder than any silence you have ever tried to keep.

The dripping does not stop.

The dripping has never stopped.

The dripping will not stop.
