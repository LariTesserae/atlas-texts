They are tall. That is the first thing an observer from the rim would notice — the way they stand on the terraces with a head and more to spare above the railings, the way their silhouettes stack against the slit of sky like slow-growing reeds. They are taller than they ought to be, and have been for many generations, and no one remembers when the elongation began. The ladders are long because their bodies are long. The doorways are tall because their bodies are tall. The architecture has met them halfway, and then some, and the proportions of the hollow — which look almost right to an outsider, which feel almost right, but slightly off, slightly too vertical, slightly too thin — are the proportions of these people reflected back. They have become the geometry of the place. The place has become theirs.

Their eyes are large. Large enough that the sockets have widened, that the brow has receded, that the upper part of the face has smoothed into something almost fetal. The eyes are dark — darker than the dim light could account for, darker than the brown of the people above — and the pupils are horizontal slits, like a goat's or a lizard's, and they can resolve a face at thirty paces in the green half-light that a stranger from above would find unreadable. They weep when they are made to look at the sun. They weep, and turn away, and the young ones among them have learned to do this without being told. Direct light is a small violence.

Their skin is the color of pale tea. It is translucent at the temples, at the inner arms, where the veins run like pale rivers under the surface. Some say the algae-light has leached into them. Some say it is the sulfur in the air. Some say it is simply what happens to a creature that has lived underground for a hundred generations — what happens to mushrooms, what happens to roots. Whatever the cause, they are a people of ghost-greys and tea-greens and the faintest yellow, with one exception. The stokers, who spend their days in the furnace-glow, have skin of a deeper, warmer red, lined early, weathered by heat, and they smell of smoke even when they have not been near a fire. They are revered. They are the closest thing to priests the hollow has.

Their hands are wide. The fingers are long and splayed, the pads of the palms almost flat, ridged like the foot of a tree-frog. They grip wet stone by surface, not by force. A hand laid on the wall will hold a body up for an hour if needed, and the wall will be no worse for it. Their feet are wider still. The big toe has migrated inward over the centuries and is no longer quite a toe; it is a kind of thumb, set at an angle, capable of opposition. They can hang from a rung of an iron ladder by their feet alone, and they often do, when their arms tire, and a row of them descending a wall looks from a distance like a string of slow fruit. Children are born with feet already too large for the cradles their mothers weave. They learn to climb before they walk. By six months they are pulling themselves up the side of a cot. By a year they are on the stairs, and they have not yet said a word.

They wear almost nothing. A wrap of woven leaf-fiber at the waist, sometimes — long, going to the calf, the color of wet bark. A length of cloth, gone grey with years of damp, for the older ones, who feel the chill on still evenings even when there is no chill to feel. Many go bare above the waist because the air is wet and the heat is constant and fabric holds the damp against the skin like a poultice. They are lean. The food of the hollow does not fatten a body. They eat the cabbages that grow in the steam. They eat the strap-leaved plants, the ephemeral lilies. They eat the mushrooms that fruit on the undersides of platforms in the dark, pale and almost luminous, and which taste, they say, of nothing in particular and of the place itself. They eat the frogs from the green-black pools, which are abundant and which have gone the same grey-green as everything else. They eat the insects — beetles fried on flat stones until the shells crack, pale moths scooped from the furnace-light before they can crisp. And they eat the algae cakes, which are the staple: the surface algae skimmed from the pools, pressed into rounds, dried on the warm stones. The algae cakes have a faint sour light of their own, and those who have eaten them recently are said to glow softly around the mouth for an hour afterward, a green halo that is more felt than seen. Children are proud of the glow. They smile wide, showing it.

They speak softly. This is the first rule of the hollow, learned in infancy the way other peoples learn to fear the dark. A voice carries here in a way it does not carry elsewhere. A shout at the floor reaches the rim and returns, flattened, slowed, a half-second later, and it is unsettling in a way no one can quite name. So they speak low. They speak close. They speak in a kind of murmur that is half-sound and half-breath, that does not travel beyond the next terrace. Children are hushed constantly, the way children are hushed in libraries, and they learn early that the loudest sound in any given room is almost always the drip of water from a pipe.

But they have another language, and it is the one they use most. The hands. A sign-language so old it has no name and no origin story, passed from mother to child with the same matter-of-factness with which breathing is passed. It is faster than speech. It is more precise. It can be read across a courtyard, across a balcony, across the vertical drop of a terrace, in light that is not quite light. There are signs for the cabbages, for the lilies, for the algae, for the slow complaint of a metal joint expanding. There is a sign for the slot of sky. There is a sign for the dead. There is a sign for the sound the thing beneath the grating makes, when it moves, which they do not name and which they rarely discuss. There are signs that only two people know, which are passed at the moment of death, and which are not spoken of again. The hands are never still. Even when they are at rest, even when they lie folded in a lap, there is a small motion to them — a finger tapping, a thumb rolling across a knuckle — as if the language must keep running even in sleep.

The machines they tend. The condenser, the great riveted cylinder at the bottom of the hollow, sweating in its slow way. The pipes that run up the walls and stop in mid-air, weeping into troughs that lead nowhere useful. The flywheel that turns at a sedate pace, attached to nothing one can see. The work is small and it is constant and it is the work of every one of them, divided by some ancient rotation no one questions. Someone walks the wall each morning, fingertip along the rivets, listening for the soft hiss of a leak. Someone clears the troughs where the open pipes weep. Someone lays a hand on the flywheel as it passes, just briefly, the way one lays a hand on a sleeping animal to be sure of its breath. The work does not change. The machines do not change. They have not been repaired in any visible way for as long as anyone can remember, and yet they do not fail. They are the bones of something larger. The hollow has a sense — passed down without being said, the way the sign for the sound beneath the grating is passed down — that stopping the machines would be wrong. That the thing they are the bones of is what holds the walls up, what keeps the heat in, what keeps the place from being only a wound. They keep the machines turning. They have decided it would be worse not to.

The slot of sky, up above, is rarely looked at. To look up is to see a small bright coin of a world one cannot return to, and the coin is too sharp, and it makes the eyes water, and one turns away. Once or twice in a life, an elder climbs to the rim. They go up alone. They sit on the lip, in the place where the sun is, for an hour. They come down. They do not say what they saw. The young ones ask, sometimes, when they are old enough to ask. The elders do not answer. There is a sense that this place was not chosen. That the people were here before the city was built, or that the city grew up around them, or that they came down into the hollow long ago for a reason that has been forgotten along with what the condenser is for. There is a sense, too, that the hollow might let them go. The ladders are not locked. The rim is not guarded. They simply have not the desire, and have not had it for so long that the desire has become something else — a kind of fidelity to the place, a kind of long marriage that no one remembers the beginning of.

There is something else. Below the grating, somewhere in the deeper heat, in the place where the warmth comes from that has nowhere to go, there is something that moves. They have heard it — a long slow scrape, like a body turning over in sleep, or like a great stone shifting against itself. They have never seen it. They have, over many generations, arrived at an arrangement with it, the way one arrives at an arrangement with a neighbor whose habits one does not understand. We keep the machines turning, and it keeps us warm. We do not pry. It does not rise. The arrangement holds. They do not speak of it except in the sign that only two people know.

Time moves through them the way it moves through the pipes. They are long-lived, or it seems so. A person at eighty looks forty. A person at forty looks thirty. The dim has slowed them. The heat has slowed them. The food has slowed them. They age like the buildings — by inches, by seasons, by a thumb's width a year. They are patient because the place is patient. They are strange because the place is strange. They move slowly, drawing their hands along the wet walls as they go, knowing the stairs by feel, and the moss grows on their shoulders where they sit still for a long time, and the vines drape across their balconies and across their shoulders and do not distinguish between them and the stone, and they do not distinguish between themselves and the stone, and this is not sad. This is only what they are.

They are the largest moving things in the hollow. They move slowly, and they are not unhappy.
