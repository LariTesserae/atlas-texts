The people who live here are not numerous. They have never, in living memory, been numerous. Counting is a habit of places where things come and go, and here nothing comes and nothing goes, and so the question of how many there are is answered not with a number but with a shape — the shape of a settlement that has reached its own size and is content with it. You might be told, if you asked persistently enough, that there are enough. You might be told, more usually, that there is no need to ask.

Their bodies are small and dark. Not in any exotic way. The way things become small and dark when they have lived for many generations in low spaces with low light, on wet stone, under a canopy that almost touches the roofs. Their bones are dense. They have the particular weathering of people who have walked on damp stone their whole lives: the soles of their feet are thick, the arches are high, the toes slightly splayed for grip on the worn concavities of the lanes. Their hands are shaped by stone and ropey vine. Their knuckles are larger than they should be. Their fingers curve slightly inward, as though they have spent decades gripping something they were not going to let go of. Their faces have a kind of softness — not the softness of youth but the softness of long exposure, the way river stones have a softness, the way the carved figure at the basin has a softness. They are slightly blurred at the edges. A newcomer might, in poor light, mistake a person for a particular kind of architectural feature, and this is not considered an insult.

They wear their hair close, when they have it. They wear the dense, oily cloth of the place, woven from a local fiber that does not exist in any catalog of trade goods. The cloth is the colors of the place: wet-stone gray, moss-green, the brown of old wood, the black of the deeper channels. The clothes are layered. They do not appear to be changed often. The clothes themselves become, over years, a kind of second moss. They darken. They dampen. They begin to hold the cold the way the walls hold the cold.

They speak rarely, and when they speak they speak at the volume of weather. Their words are short. They have a particular way of acknowledging what has been said — a small sound, almost a hum, that is not quite yes and not quite no but means something like: I have heard you, and I am not going to argue with what you have said. They will answer a question you asked days ago, while doing something else entirely, as if the question has been traveling through the damp air and has only just now arrived at a place where it could be received. They will not lie. They will not volunteer. The information you receive from them will be exactly correct, and exactly as much as they have decided you should have, and no more.

They are not unfriendly. They are simply complete in themselves. The settlement does not need a newcomer. It does not need a newcomer to leave. It does not need anything from outside, and so the question of whether to welcome or to reject does not arise. You are here. You are not here. Either is fine. They will offer you water from the basin without being asked, and the water will be cold and faintly mineral and will taste of the place, and they will watch you drink it without comment, and the watching will be the extent of the welcome.

---

They have lived here so long that they have a particular relationship to their own houses, which is not, in any conventional sense, ownership. A house here is more like a tree. Several families may grow on the same trunk. The walls of the house you are standing in are the walls that were standing when your great-grandmother was a child. The floor above is the floor of someone else's grandmother. The crypt below is the crypt of everyone, and the doors to the lower level open from inside the houses above, and the people here do not think this is strange because the entire settlement is, to them, one continuous dwelling. To enter a neighbor's house is to enter another room of the same house. To go down into the crypt is to go down into the basement of the same house. They are not communal in any political sense. They are communal in the way the fingers of a hand are communal — parts of one thing, not by choice but by anatomy.

The houses share walls because there was never room not to. They share eaves because water must be shed somewhere, and the same eaves shed it for several houses at once. They share, in some cases, floors. And the sharing is so complete that there are houses here that no single family could tell you the full history of, because the full history would require knowing what every previous family did to every wall, and the walls do not remember in any way that can be recited. They remember in the way that moss remembers — by being slightly thicker in some places than in others, by being a slightly different color where a particular beam once rested.

They are patient in the way of fitted stone. They are not slow. They are unhurried. They know that a wall, once built, will be wet for a very long time, and that the moss will come, and that the vines will come, and that none of this is a problem because the wall was built to be wet, and to have moss, and to have vines. They do not repair in the way that other places repair. They adjust. They negotiate with the place. If a stone shifts in a wall, they let it shift until it finds its new seat, and then they leave it. If a water channel becomes blocked, they redirect the flow rather than clear the blockage, because the water knows where it wants to go and will eventually go there anyway, and the path it carves will be better than the path they would have chosen for it.

---

They know the spring. They have always known the spring. The agreement between them and the spring is renewed once a year, at a particular time, which they will not name to you directly but which you might, if you have been here long enough, notice: the pattern of footsteps around the basin changes for a few days. The cup of a particular wood is taken down from wherever it is kept. The small carved figure on the stone post above the basin is, for that brief period, slightly more attended to — a single drop of water is poured over its head, perhaps, or a single green leaf is placed in its hand, or it is wiped of moss along a single line down its back. Then things return to their normal quiet.

The agreement is not a contract. It is not a ceremony in any dramatic sense. It is a maintenance. It is observed. It is not discussed. The pattern of footsteps traces the six lanes that meet at the iron grate, and the grate is older than iron ought to be, and the water finds its way down into whatever is below. The cup is made of a wood that does not grow anywhere else in the hollow. It might be from a tree that is no longer alive. It is kept somewhere — they will not say where. When it is used, water from the spring is poured into it, and from the cup back into the basin. The cup is passed between hands, perhaps across generations. The hands that hold it know, by the weight of it, by the temperature of it, whether the agreement is in good order.

This is not mysticism. They are not mystics. They are very old at being here, and they have learned what works, and what works includes the cup, and the footsteps, and the figure, and they do not need to explain it to you because explaining it would not make it work better. The working is what matters. The working is what holds.

---

The dead are remembered by other means. There are no gravestones. There are no markers in any conventional sense. But certain stones in certain walls are a slightly different color, and if you know the place well enough, you can read the differences the way you might read a written record — slowly, in a particular light, with patience. Certain vines are permitted to grow in patterns that would otherwise be cleared. Certain mosses are protected on certain surfaces and not on others, and the protected mosses have shapes that are not natural, that have been guided, over decades, into the shape of a particular person or a particular event. The carved figure at the basin has no face because, perhaps, it has had many faces, and they have all weathered away, and this is acceptable, because the figure is not a portrait. It is a posture. A listening. The moss creeps up its back because the moss creeps up everything, and no one cleans it off, and no one says why, and the not-saying-why is part of the remembering.

If you ask about the dead, you will be answered, eventually, in a way. You will be told about a stone. About a vine. About a small change in the path of a water channel that was made the year a particular person died. About the placement of a particular moss-wick lamp in a particular window of a particular house at a particular time of year. These are the names of the dead. They are not less precise than names would be. They are differently precise. They are precise in the way that the path of a root through a wall is precise — slow, organic, irreversible.

The crypt below the houses is not a tomb in the usual sense. It is cooler than the houses. The air in it moves, and is always the same temperature, and no one thinks this is remarkable. They go down sometimes, with the stone lamps and the moss-wicks, and the small yellow light goes down before them. They come back up. They do not speak of what they went down for. It might be the dead. It might be storage of things that must be kept cool. It might be both. It might be something else. They do not say. The shafts that ventilate it are part of the architecture of the place and were never, in living memory, designed — they are simply there, the way the spring is simply there, the way the small green channels in the lanes are simply there.

The animals are everywhere and quiet. The small deer, the size of dogs, move through the understory of the lanes and the edges of the settlement in loose groups of two or three or four. Their hooves make no sound on the moss. They will watch you from twenty feet away and not move. They have the quality of creatures that have made their peace with everything, including themselves. They are not tame. They are simply unafraid, in the way that things are unafraid in places where nothing has hunted them in a very long time. They pass through the settlement because the settlement is, to them, a particular kind of forest — a forest with walls and floors and eaves, but a forest nonetheless, with its own mosses and its own small waters. If you are quiet enough, they will come close enough that you could touch them. They will not let you touch them. They will simply not mind you being near. They go where they go. They do not explain themselves.

The birds are many kinds and none of them brightly colored. The colors here are damp colors. The birds work the bark of the trees and the eaves of the houses, finding what they find.
