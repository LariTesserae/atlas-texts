The people of Mesaq'in are, before anything else, layered.

That is the first thing to understand about them — before their trades, before their three or four languages, before the particular smell of their cooking or the particular way they speak to each other across the alleys. They are layered. The dead are below. The living are above. The smallest of the living are higher still, in the eaves, where the heat gathers and the bells hang from their frames.

A house in Mesaq'in is, then, a small archaeology turned on its head. Walk into the doorway of any of them and you are entering the oldest floor first. The niches are in the lower walls, sealed with plaster and lath, and behind each seal there is a bundle — wrapped in undyed wool, weighted at the corners with small smooth stones, marked with the curled script the town uses for everything. The bundles are kept upright, the way the living are kept upright. Some of them have been in their niches for three generations. The plaster over them has been patched, and patched again, until the seams are the color of the wall itself and only a practiced hand can find them. The oldest of the bundles — and there are very old ones, in certain houses, in certain corners of the southern quarter — have begun to settle into the floor the way the houses have begun to settle into the plateau. They are part of the house now. The house is built around them.

Up from the dead, on the next floor, the living of the house begin. They are crowded. Three families to a house is the norm, and in some of the larger houses near the springs it is four, and in at least one house in the eastern quarter it is, by the count of the bell-keepers, six — though the families in question have argued about this for forty years and no one has gone in to count. The families live one above the other in the same vertical column, and the stair that joins them is shared, and the kitchen, if there is a kitchen, is shared, and the courtyard, if there is a courtyard, is shared, and the disputes are shared, and the children, when they come, belong in some sense to all of the families at once. The town calls this *qatara* — the layered kin — and a person will say, when asked where they are from, not the name of a house but the name of the stair they grew up on.

The smallest children are kept in the eaves. This is not cruelty, and it is not neglect. The eaves are warm. The eaves are where the bells are, and the bells ring often, and the children learn early that the bells are part of the household and not something to be feared. The eaves are also where the smoke from the braziers rises and lingers, and the children, breathing it, are said to develop a particular cast to their eyes that the healers can read from across a room. A child raised in the eaves is a child who has known, before they could walk, the sound of bronze being beaten three streets away and the smell of figs ripening above their cradle and the slow movement of water on the roof of the house. They grow into the town the way roots grow into a wall.

---

And the people are old.

Not all of them, not in their bodies — though many do live long, the slow water and the warm air doing whatever it is they do — but in their habits, in their memory, in the way they hold a thought. The very old of Mesaq'in go down to the springs in the small hours of the morning, before the bathing hours begin for anyone else, and sit in the warm water up to their chests. They speak to each other in voices that have grown so soft over the years that the sound barely carries across the pool. They speak of the people who used to come down at this hour, who are now sealed into the lower floors of their houses. They speak of the storms on the plain, which come only twice in a generation but which they all remember. They speak of the bells, and of who rang them, and of what was rung for. They are a kind of library. They are consulted the way the books in other towns are consulted, only the consultation is done in the warm water and the answers come back in a voice that sounds like pages turning.

The very old are also the ones who do not leave. Some of the people of Mesaq'in have lived in the same house for ninety years and have descended the same staircase ten thousand times. Some have not gone down to the plain below since they were carried up as infants. This is not, in the town, considered strange. It is considered the natural shape of a life. The plain below is the place you come from. The town is the place you stay. To leave the plateau is, for an old person, to step off the edge of the world. They do not do it.

---

The town is multilingual.

Mesaq'in sits above the only road across the flats that does not vanish in summer, and it has always been a crossing, and the people who stay have always been people whose grandparents, or whose great-grandparents, came up from somewhere else. The languages of the plain are spoken in the alleys, and the language of the plateau — a softer, slower thing, with the vowels rounded as if to keep the warmth in — is the common tongue, and there are two or three others besides, brought by traders who married in and never left. A person will switch from one to another in the middle of a sentence, sometimes, when the right word is in the other language, and no one finds this strange. The children learn all of them at once, in the eaves, the way they learn the bells.

This is why the town is loud in three or four languages at once. It is also why the children throw fruit at each other from balconies in any of them, with the same shrieks, and why the disputes on the shared stairs are conducted in whatever language the disputants share, and why the songs sung at the festivals — which are many, and which last for days — mix the words together as if they were all the same.

---

The coppersmiths are the wealthiest guild.

They work in a lane near the western edge of the plateau, where the light is best, because copper wants light the way a fig wants water. Their forges are small and banked with the same scoria as the roads, and the smoke from them rises into the eaves and hangs there until the wind takes it. They beat the metal with hammers that have been in the same families for a long time, and the hammers have worn the shape of the hands that hold them. The coppersmiths own the water-rights. The springs are held in common, by long custom and by the geologists' reading of the stone, but the water that flows from them — once it has left the central pools — is sold. The coppersmiths, who need the most water for quenching and for the small baths their apprentices take after the day's work, have bought up a great deal of it. They are not disliked for this. They are respected. They keep the metals honest. They are the ones who make the bells.

The bells are their pride. Every bell in Mesaq'in was cast by a coppersmith, and the name of the caster is engraved on the inside of the bell in the curled script, and the bells are rung with a rope that hangs down through a hole in the roof. The bells ring for hours, and festivals, and deaths, and the opening of the morning market, and for no reason at all, when a child is born or a stranger is recognized or someone has simply decided the morning requires a bell. The coppersmiths do not decide when the bells ring. The bell-keepers do.

---

The bell-keepers live in the highest house on the plateau, which is also the highest point of the plateau, and which is itself only as high as a tall tree. From their roof you can see the plain. They are a family, the bell-keepers, and the family has held the roof for as long as anyone can remember — perhaps longer, perhaps since the bells were first hung. They are a quiet family. They do not mix much with the rest of the town. They keep the bells. They are the ones who decide, in their quiet way, which of the many occasions the day offers is the right one for a bell.

A stranger is recognized by the bell-keepers within an hour of arriving on the plateau. The bell-keepers do this by some method that the town does not question. A bell rings when a stranger is recognized, and the bell has a particular sound — lower, slower, with a longer silence at the end of it — and when the people of the town hear that sound they go about their business, but they know there is someone new among them. The stranger, if they are listening, will hear the bell and know that they have been heard.

---

The geologists are seven.

They were eleven a generation ago. The oldest of them lives in a house near the springs, in a room that has been added to over the years so that it now extends partly into the cellar of the house next door, which is empty, and partly under the street, which the town permits because the street is already partly under the house. She is the one who has been down into the chamber beneath the southern quarter, and she is the one whose cellar holds the stair that goes down and is said to come up somewhere else. She has not been asked to prove this in many years.

The geologists are, in their way, also a library. They read the stone the way other towns read books. They have a guild-house — a tile-walled room near the springs, with a low ceiling and a floor that is slightly warmer than the floors of other rooms in town. The map is kept there. It is drawn on paper made from the inner bark of the fig, and it is old, and it has been redrawn so many times that the layers of ink are visible at the edges of the map like the layers of basalt in the scarp. On it, certain places are marked. Certain fissures are marked. Certain alignments are marked. The chamber is marked. The stones in the paving of the central square are marked. The stair is marked.

The geologists do not speak of these things often. They speak of them when they are asked, and they answer in the way that old women in every town answer when they are asked about the thing they know best — slowly, and with a long pause before the first sentence, as if they are deciding how much of the stone to translate into the air. They are not afraid of the things they have marked. They have lived with them for a long time. They have a way of standing near the map and looking at it the way a person looks at a relative they have not seen in a while — with affection, and with a kind of patience, and with the understanding that whatever the relative has been doing in the meantime is none of their business.

The town has a way of not asking. The geologists have a way of not telling. These are the same way.

---

The healers are not a guild, but they are known.

They are the ones who use the red-flowered mat that grows on the upper roofs — the mat that makes the noise like pages turning, and that no one is quite sure is a plant or several plants or a single very old plant that has spread across half the northern quarter. The healers cut the flowers and dry them and steep them in oil, and the oil is rubbed into the joints of the very old, and into the chests of the children who have been coughing in the eaves, and into the feet of the *khazne* when the *khazne* have walked too far on the stony roads. The healers are mostly women, though not all of them. They learn their craft from the mat itself, in a way that the town does not try to explain. A healer will sit on a roof, in the morning, with the mat around her, and she will listen to it, and she will decide what to do.

The healers are also the ones who attend the births, of which there are many. The town is warm and the customs are what they are, and there are many children, and the births happen in the small hours, in the warm water of the springs, because the water is there and the springs are warm and the healers are already awake. The bell-keepers hear the births. The bells ring. The town knows.

---

The children are everywhere.

They are in the alleys, and in the shallower canals, and on the lower roofs where the *sireh* foals are kept, and in the eaves where they sleep, and on the stairs that climb outside the walls. They are loud. They throw fruit. They learn the bells before they learn the words for the bells. They learn the languages at the same time they learn the stairs. By the time a child of Mesaq'in is five, they know which stair belongs to which house, and which bell rings for which occasion, and which of the geologists is the one who will answer a question and which is the one who will not, and which of the coppersmiths will give them a piece of copper if they ask politely, and which of the healers will let them sit on the roof and listen to the mat.

The children bury figurines in the gardens. The figurines are made of clay, and they are small, and they are of figures the children have seen — sometimes the bell-keepers, sometimes the geologists, sometimes a *khazne* with a long face. The children bury them in the soft earth of the gardens and forget where they have buried them, and the roots grow around them, and years later someone digging in the garden will turn one up and bring it to a geologist, who will look at it for a long time and say nothing.

---

The beasts are part of the town the way the bells are part of the town.

The *khazne* are kept in byres at the edges of the plateau, where the ground is stoniest and there is the least garden. They are large — the size of ponies — and they have six legs, and thick wool, and a mouth that is placid even when they are chewing. Their lowing is not a sound so much as a vibration, a long felt thing that travels through the stone and reaches you in your heels before it reaches you in your ears. They are what the town uses for wool, and for milk, and for the winter fat that burns in the oil lamps. They are what the town uses for the slow, patient work of dragging cut wood up from the lower slopes. They are not hurried. They are not hurried by anything.

The *sireh* are smaller, and they bray at first light whether or not they have cause. The *sireh* are ridden by the traders who come up the road, and by the coppersmiths when they go down to the plain to buy bronze, and by the children, secretly, when no one is looking. The *sireh* are kept in pens on the lower roofs when they are young, and the foals make a piping noise like wet reeds, and the piping is one of the sounds the town is made of.

The goats are goats. The dogs are dogs. The flightless bird is the strangest of the beasts kept in the town. It is large, and it does not fly, and it has a long red tail, and the tail-feathers are the town's principal export — taken down to the plain in small bundles and traded for the bronze that the coppersmiths work. The bird makes a noise that one of the travelers' guides described, with some puzzlement, as a trombone falling down a staircase. The town hears this noise several times a day and does not find it puzzling. The bird is kept for eggs and for feathers and for the noise, which is part of the town the way the bells are part of the town.

---

And the things beneath.

The town has been built over something it does not entirely understand. The geologists know more about it than anyone, and what they know is marked on the map, and what is marked on the map is not, by the customs of the town, spoken of at length. There are stones in the paving of the central square that are not scoria and not pumice and not anything the geologists can name. They are heavy. They are warm. They ring when struck. No one knows how they came to be there. There is a chamber, somewhere beneath the southern quarter, that the oldest of the geologists insists she has been down into. There is a stair, in her cellar, that goes down and is said by her to come up somewhere else.

The town has built itself over these things the way the moss has built itself over the stone. The houses are above them. The canals run above them. The gardens grow above them. The bells ring above them. The children play above them and bury figurines above them and do not know they are there. The dead are sealed into the lower floors of the houses, and the dead are above them, too, and the living are above the dead, and the smallest children are above the living, in the eaves, where the bells hang.

Whatever is down there has been down there longer than the town. It is patient, the way the wind is patient, the way the water is patient. It does not hurry. It does not ask to be understood. It waits the way the geologist waits in her cellar, with her stair that goes down, and the way the bells wait in the eaves, and the way the people of the town wait in their houses, in their layers, in their warm dim dim, with their bells and their water and their green.

---

The people of Mesaq'in are at the end of the world.

They have made it a garden. They have made it a forest. They have made it a town. They have made it all of these things at once, and they have done it slowly, and they have done it in layers, and they have done it over something they do not entirely understand, and they have done it with bells.

They are quiet, even when they are loud. They speak softly because the sound carries. They speak in three or four languages because the languages are theirs. They are old, and they are patient, and they are warm, and they live in close quarters with their dead and their living and their children and their beasts and their green and their water and their bells, and they have been here, by their own count, for six hundred and forty years, and by the count of the stones in the central square, perhaps longer.

They have nowhere else to be.

They are the inhabitants of the warm high place.
