They are small. This is the first thing, and it is a thing that becomes more true the longer you look. Small in stature — the water in the marsh comes up to the knees of the paths, and the paths were laid, or grown, or asked into being, at a height that suits them, and that height is the height of a child, though not all of them are children, and not all children who come here remain children. Small in voice — they speak rarely, and when they speak the forest takes the speech into the felted wall and gives it back as the slow wet percussion of leaf against leaf, so that you cannot tell, after a while, whether the person is speaking or the canopy is. Small in face — the muscles of the brow, the mouth, the eye are at rest; the brows do not lift; the mouths do not tighten; the eyes do not widen. The faces have been stilled the way a pond is stilled, by agreement among the small lives that live in it.

They are not children. Or not all of them are children. But they have the patience of children, and the smallness of children, and the unhurried exactness of children who have been told, gently and persistently, by something older than themselves, that the world will come to them if they let it. They move the way one moves in a market that has been going on for a long time — with the knowledge, in the feet, of where every root is. They do not look down at their feet. They do not need to. The feet know. The feet have been told, by the roots, by the marsh, by the long practice of going where one is going.

They carry things in baskets. They carry things on their heads. They carry things in their mouths — a long stick with a small bundle lashed to the end, held sideways, the way you might carry a torch except the bundle is not a torch. They carry things in the folds of their garments. A woman passes you, and what looks like a small animal is sleeping in the fold of her cloth, and it takes you a long time to realize it is her hand, and what is sleeping in the fold is the hand's own dreaming. The hand has its own interior life. The hand is alive in a way that is not entirely the woman's aliveness. The hand has agreed, with the woman, to be her hand, and the woman has agreed, with the hand, to let it dream when it dreams. They are in conversation. They have been in conversation for nine hundred years.

The faces are not fixed. If you watch one of them long enough — and you will, because there is nothing else to watch, and the watching is part of what is being done to you — you will see the face change. Not the way a human face changes, in expression, in the small migrations of muscle. The face changes the way a forest changes. A line eases. A shadow softens. The color of the skin, at the temple, at the jaw, becomes a half-shade greener, or a half-shade browner, and then is not. The features do not move so much as they are *reconsidered*. The forest is editing them as they stand there. The forest is taking the edges off, smoothing the transitions, making sure that nothing happens too fast, that no shape is too sharp. The woman who passes you the seventeenth time has a slightly different face than the woman who passed you the first time, and she is the same woman, and for her these are all the same passing, because for her the seventeen passings are one passing held many ways in the hand, the way a single fig held up to the light is many figs, red, gold, green, shadow.

They do not ask you questions. This is not a hospitality. It is a survival. They remember axes. They remember — or their grandmothers remember, or the walls remember for them — that questions, in this place, are a kind of cutting. A question made into sound becomes a fruiting body. It drops from the ceiling. It sprouts in the lungs. So they do not ask. They look at you the way the heron looks at you — with the satisfied concentration of a regular customer at a bar who has seen a stranger come in and is waiting, without impatience, to see what the stranger will order. They will serve you, if you let them. They will not begin.

The heron is one of them. This is the thing the place is loudest about, besides the water and the wind. The heron stands on a root at the edge of a path and watches the wake of whatever long brown back has just broken the surface of the marsh. The heron has been standing on that root, or on a root like it, for a long time. The heron is patient the way the rain is patient. The heron does not need to be quick, because what it eats comes to it, or because what eats it is not faster but is more patient, and the heron has made its peace with that. The heron blinks. The heron shifts one foot. The heron has, behind its eye, a long lineage of herons who have stood on long lineages of roots, and the lineage is the agreement.

The bird that sings the phrase is one of them. Somewhere up in the cathedral of green, it opens its throat, and what comes out is not a song but a long, low, hollow, interrogative note, three seconds long, that the whole forest seems to lean toward. Another bird answers it from a different impossible angle. Another answers. The three notes braid into a chord that does not end so much as it forgets to stop — it becomes the weather, becomes the dripping, becomes the next breath of the canopy. The birds are not singing to each other. The birds are *agreeing* with each other, and with the forest, and with the dripping, and with the central pillar that does not stop going up. The birds are the agreement made audible. The birds have been the agreement made audible for nine hundred years.

There is a face in the wall. You see it in a window-shaped opening, between two trunks the width of rooms. It is a face made of leaves. It is also a face. It blinks. It withdraws. The leaves close over it, slowly, the way water closes over a stone that has been dropped into it. The face is one of them. Or the face is what one of them becomes, after a long time, after enough editing, after the agreement has been kept so thoroughly that the boundary between the person and the wall has become a courtesy rather than a fact. The face in the wall does not speak. The face in the wall has forgotten how to speak, or has remembered that it never needed to. The face in the wall is part of the felt. The face in the wall is the settlement of accounts, made flesh, made leaf, made patient.

They have tools, but the tools are not separate. A ladder is a vine. A pulley is a curled frond. A lever is a branch, still attached, by a living strip of bark, to the tree it came from. The man who poles the small boat through the marsh has not cut his pole. He has asked it. The tree will grow another. The tree will grow another in a week, and the man will come back, in a week, and ask again, and the tree will give again, and this is the oldest commerce in the place. This is what came after the axes. This is what the walls remember, and what the walls have, in their slow felt way, forgiven.

They eat figs. They eat fish. They eat whatever the marsh and the canopy provide, and the providing is not a harvest but a conversation, and the conversation has been going on, in the same low voices, for nine hundred years. They are not hungry. They are not full. They are fed, in the way the forest is fed, by the rain that does not fall but condenses, by the water that is pulled up through a hundred thousand straws by a thirst that has been drinking since before they were born.

The central pillar is one of them, or is what they are inside of. It goes up and does not stop. It is the diameter of a small house at the base. It has a new ring around its base — pale wood, the color of a wound that is healing cleanly. It is the original. Everything grew from it. The trunks that form the colonnade are its children, or its grandchildren, or its great-grandchildren a hundred times removed. The people are its children too, in some way that is not a metaphor, or that is a metaphor that has, like so many things here, become literal. The pillar does not speak. The pillar does not need to speak. The pillar is the slow pulse that you feel through the root when you put your hand on it at the edge of a path. The pulse greets your hand. The pulse has been greeting hands for nine hundred years. The pulse is patient. The pulse is not, you realize, going to stop.

You sit on the root. You put your hand on it. The pulse comes up through the bark and into your palm and you understand, at last, what kind of place this is. It is a place where the inhabitants are not separate from the place. It is a place where the small still heads and the long brown backs and the herons and the birds and the face in the wall and the central pillar are all parts of one body, in the way your hand is a part of you, and the way your hand, in the fold of your garment, is allowed to dream its own dreams. The place is not a city. The place is a single organism, of which the people are the parts that move, and the herons are the parts that watch, and the birds are the parts that sing the agreement, and the central pillar is the part that remembers.

The roar is the roar of this organism, breathing.
