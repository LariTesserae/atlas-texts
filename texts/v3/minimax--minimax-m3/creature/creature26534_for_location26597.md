The valley holds people, and not many of them. The settlement follows the weeping, and the people follow the settlement, and the number holds. It holds the way the rock holds its fractures — at a figure that does not grow because there is no place for growth to go, that does not shrink because no one leaves and no one is taken. They are born in the valley. They die in the valley. The buildings are rebuilt over them, in the same way, of the same materials, and the work does not pause for the burying. The bodies are laid into the seams of the bedrock, in the higher ground where the weeping does not reach, and the fitted stones close over them the way fitted stones close over a foundation. They go back to the local material. There is no marker. There is no name carved, because the walls do not speak. The place where a body was laid is not different from any other place.

The people are pale from staying indoors. The sun does not reach the floor of the valley, and they do not climb to where it does. Their hands are wider at the knuckles than at the wrist, shaped by setting stone and working iron and pegging timber. They wear layers of heavy, undyed cloth, dark with the damp, that they change when the cloth can no longer hold what it has taken. Their boots are thick-soled and replaced often, because the constant wet eats the leather in a season and a half. They smell the way the valley smells — of wet stone and iron and wool that has never fully dried. They are not tall. They are not small. They are the size the rock has made them.

They speak, but not much. The valley is not silent — there is the dripping, the patter of condensation, the rare creak of the hall — but it is not a place where speech adds much to what is already there. They speak when there is a thing to be done, and they say the thing in the fewest words that will do it. They have names for one another, but the names are not used often, because the people are few enough that a gesture or a turn of the head will do. The conversations that happen between them are short, practical, finished. They do not raise their voices, because the air does not move to carry them. The sound of their speech, when it occurs, is low, and is heard only at close range, and is over.

The work of the valley is constant. A timber here. A slate there. A joint that has loosened in the wet. The buildings are rebuilt as they need to be, in the same way, of the same materials, by the same methods, and the rebuilding is always partly underway. There is iron to be forged in a small forge near the hall, where the bellows breathe out a heat that the wet air swallows quickly. There are stones to be shaped against other stones until they sit right. There are bridges to be repointed, the three of them, low-arched, where the wetter channels cross. There are paths to be relaid, a fitted stone at a time, when the polish wears or the frost — which is rare but comes — heaves one up out of its seat. The work is not hard in the way that hard work is hard in other places. It is patient. It is the work of a place that does not move toward anything.

They walk the paths. The fitted stones have a polish but not an erosion — many feet, many years, evenly distributed. They fetch water from the channels, though the air is full of water and the rock weeps it from every face. They sit in the stillest corners, on the outcrops of bedrock, in the spaces between the tussocks. They are still the way the air is still. They watch the water run. They do not seem to be waiting for anything, because they are not.

The fauna is absent, and they do not remark on it. A child who asks is told that the valley is the way it is, and that is enough. There is no story about why the birds do not come, no legend of an old pestilence, no old compact signed in some harder age. There is the absence, accepted, like the constant wet or the constant silver light. They have not heard of any other way. If a visitor presses, they will say only that it has always been so, and turn back to the work.

The hall is two stories on a fitted platform where the valley floor is at its driest. It is where the few larger gatherings happen, and the gatherings are few — the marking of a life, the marking of a death, the infrequent council of those who have been here longest, and who are not set apart except by the accident of duration. Inside the hall the timber swells in the wet until the joints are tight as bone. The roof is heavy slate. The light that falls through the windows is the same silver, flat, even light that falls on the valley outside. There are benches, plain. There is a fire in a stone hearth, but the fire is small and burns low, because the timber is wet and the chimney draws poorly in the still air. The hall creaks, sometimes, when the timber shifts. The sound is rare, and when it comes, it is noted.

They do not mark time. The seasons are marked by the amount of water the air can carry, and this is a marking too gradual to feel. A person is young, then they are not young, then they die, and the transitions are unmarked except by the work that goes on around them. The fitted stones close. The timber is pegged. The slate is laid. The valley continues.

If you came back in a hundred years you would find the same people, more or less. Not the same individuals. The same number, in the same place, doing the same work, in the same way, in the same silver light. The equilibrium holds.
