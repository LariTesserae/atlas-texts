The people here have not decided what they are. They were something once — a city-dwelling people, perhaps, with houses on dry ground and streets one could walk on — and now they are something else, which has not yet settled into a name. They live on the upper floors of their former homes and on the rooftops and in the bridges between, and they have become, by long practice and without ceremony, a water-kind of people. They did not choose this. The water chose it for them, over the long years, by inches, and they have agreed to it the way one agrees to a relative who has moved in: with patience, with a small rearrangement of the furniture, and with the understanding that the relative is not leaving.

They are dark-skinned. They wear many layers. This is not a fashion — it is the slow discovery of what works in a place where the air is wet and the water is warm and the fog sits at chest height even in the dry months. The outermost garment, when they are out on the causeways or in the boats, is a heavy woven cloth of some plant fibre they have learned to treat, perhaps with a wax, perhaps with a resin drawn from one of the rooftop trees, so that the water beads on it and rolls off before it can soak through. Beneath this is a softer layer, woven or knitted, that holds the body's heat against the morning chill of the water. Beneath this, sometimes, a third layer still. They peel them off in the indoor arcades and hang them to drip by the lanterns and put them on again the next day. A person in winter carries the smell of smoke and fish and the green sweetness of whatever vine is flowering that week.

They speak softly. There is no need to speak loudly — the warm water absorbs almost everything, and the fog absorbs the rest, so a voice raised to call across a courtyard-pond arrives at the other side as a husk of itself. They have learned to speak close. They have learned to speak in the long vowels and sibilants of a language that travels through wet air the way it travels through still water: by contact, not by force. From a distance it sounds like water running over stones. Up close it has the soft clatter of small consonants and the long, slow vowels of people who have learned that hurrying does no good. They do not seem to mind being half-drowned. They were born into it, some of them, and the others have simply decided to stay — perhaps because going would mean abandoning the rooftops and the causeways and the slow garden that has become their pantry, perhaps because the water, by now, is where their dead are.

Their tools are hand tools. There is no iron here. The water took the iron long ago — it rusted out of the old fittings, out of the nails, out of the hinges — and the new tools are made of stone and bone and shell and the harder woods. They have axes, adzes, knives of chipped stone that they haft into split handles with lashings of the rope-fibre. They have gouges for hollowing out boats. They have long poles of straight-grained wood for pushing through the channels. They have fish-hooks of bone. They have awls. They have needles of fishbone that they thread with fibres finer than the rope. They have not, as far as I can see, anything that they did not make themselves out of what the water and the slopes and the rooftops have given them. The absence of metal is not a deprivation. It is an economy. They have, in a sense, returned to an older one, and the older one fits the place.

The boats are flat and wooden and poled. They have no keels — there is no need for keels in a canal that was once an alley — and no sails, because the wind is broken up by the buildings and the vines and never gathers into anything that could push a sail. They are wide and shallow, with high sides to keep the water out when someone steps in heavily, and a low prow that has been carved away until it looks like the lip of a tray. The wood is something close-grained and pale, perhaps a fruitwood, sealed with the same wax-resin that goes on the cloth. They sit low in the water. A child can pole one, with practice, by the age of seven or eight. The poles are of a longer wood still — brought down, I think, from the slopes above the water line, where the forest is still forest. They float the timber down through the upper channels, perhaps, and drag it up onto a high rooftop to season in the air before they dress it.

The causeways are the streets. They are hand-hewn planks of that same close-grained wood, laid end to end across the gaps between the rooftops, lashed at the joints with rope. They are not pegged. There is nothing to peg them with — no nails, no spikes — and the residents have decided that pegs would rust anyway, and that the rope is better because the rope can be cut and replaced when it rots, which it does, regularly, as if the water were digesting it. A causeway is a thing one maintains. It is not built once and forgotten; it is rebuilt continually, plank by plank, lashing by lashing, the way a riverbank is rebuilt, the way a beach is rebuilt. There are people whose work this is. They go out in the morning with axes and coils of fresh rope and they cut new planks from the seasoned timber stacked on the dry rooftops, and they splice the lashings, and they tighten what has loosened, and they replace what has gone soft. You can hear them sometimes, in the early fog, the small percussive sound of an adze on wood, repeated, regular, the heartbeat of a place that is holding itself together plank by plank.

The rooftops are the gardens. They have always been gardens, in some small way — a few pots, a trellis, a corner for the herb that goes in the fish — and now they are gardens entirely. The residents grow greens in the troughs they have built along the roof-ridges. They grow beans up the chimney-stacks. They grow small fruits in the corners where two roofs meet at a valley. They grow the rope-fibre plant in long beds along the parapets. They grow, I think, half of what they eat. The other half is the fish. The economy has shifted, gently, around the water's preferences, and the water's preferences are: warmth, shallow, slow-moving, well-oxygenated at the surface, full of the insects that fall from the vines. The fish come up to feed on the insects. The residents come up to catch the fish. The fish that are caught are cooked in the indoor arcades, on small fires that are fed with the broken planks of the causeways that have been replaced. The smoke from these fires mingles with the fog. The light, already copper, takes on a deeper copper where the smoke is thick.

They tend the vines. This surprised me — I had expected them to cut them back, to fight them, to regard them as invaders. They do not. The vines are useful. They anchor the tiles, which would otherwise lift in the wet wind. They shade the windows. They flower, at certain seasons, and the flowers draw the insects, and the insects fall into the water, and the fish come up, and the residents catch the fish. The vines are a link in a chain that begins in the rooftop soil and ends in the cooking-fire, and the residents understand this chain and do not break it. They prune the vines where they need to — where a vine has grown across a doorway, where a vine has begun to pull a tile loose — but they do not cut them out. The vines and the residents have, over the long years, arrived at a kind of bargain. The vines get the rooftop and the wall and the window. The residents get the fish.

The trees are something else again. They were not planted. They seeded themselves, somehow, in the soil that collected in the second-floor courtyards — perhaps a bird dropped a seed, perhaps the wind carried one, perhaps the water itself, in some high season, brought a fruit in from the slopes — and they grew up through the window-holes and out over the dim air. They are small, by the standards of trees. They are enormous, by the standards of the rooms they stand in. Their roots have cracked the courtyard floors. Their crowns spread out over the rooftops and shade them further. The residents tolerate them too, mostly, though sometimes a tree has to come down — when its roots have begun to push a wall out of plumb, when its weight is more than the floor below it can carry. The residents fell such trees carefully, with axes and adzes, and they use the wood for planks or for fire, and they replant, sometimes, in the same place. The trees are tenants. They pay rent in shade and in fallen fruit and in the insects that live in their bark. The residents collect the rent and do not evict them.

The birds are few but present. The waders — a small heron, an egret or two — stand on the chimney-tops and on the causeways and watch the water with the kind of attention that only a fishing bird has. They are still for a very long time, and then they strike, and then they are still again. The residents tolerate them. They eat the same fish, in a sense, though from the other side of the surface. There is a kind of truce. The raptors — I saw two, on separate days, on the highest chimneys — are larger. One was a kite, perhaps, with the forked tail and the slow circling; the other I could not identify at that distance, but it sat very upright and did not move for the hour I watched it. They nest somewhere up in the peaks above the water, I think, and they come down to hunt — the waders, perhaps, or the small birds, or the fish, if they are ospreys. They are not common. They are seen. They are part of the place.

The fish are everywhere. They are warm-water fish — I saw something like a catfish, broad and pale, nosing along the bottom of a courtyard-pond; I saw something slimmer and darker moving in a school through a channel that had been an alley; I saw, once, a single large fish surface in the lagoon where the plaza had been, and lie there for a moment with its back out of the water, and then slide down again. The residents eat them. They catch them with bone hooks on lines of plant fibre, or with small nets woven of the same fibre and weighted with stones, or with baskets set into the flow of the channels. They smoke them on the rooftop racks. They trade them, perhaps, among the rooftops — though the trade is small, because the population is small, and everyone knows everyone, and the gift is more common than the sale.

The insects are the loudest inhabitants. They hum — mosquitoes certainly, midges certainly, perhaps dragonflies too, which would be beautiful against the copper light — and the hum is constant, in the warm vegetation, from the early morning until the late evening. It rises and falls with the heat. It is the only sound in the place that has not been absorbed by the water. The residents have made their peace with the insects. They burn certain leaves in the arcades, perhaps, to keep them down indoors. They cover their skin with cloth when they are out. They accept the bites. There is nothing else to do.

A frog, sometimes. I heard one — once, late in the evening, from somewhere in the vines — and saw it the next morning, on a wet plank, small and green and very still, watching me with one of its eyes. The frogs are not common here yet. The water is still too warm, perhaps, or the silt is still too thick, or the oxygen at the depth is still wrong for the spawn. They will come, later, when the water settles, when the silt compacts, when the channels run cooler at the bottom. The slow clock is turning toward fullness. The residents know this. They know, perhaps, that the frogs are a sign — that when the frogs come properly, the place will have crossed some threshold, will have become, in some way that cannot be named, a different kind of place than it was. They are waiting for the frogs the way one waits for an old friend who is travelling and is late.

The children are born into this. They learn to pole a boat before they learn to walk properly across a causeway. They learn to read the rot of a plank by its smell before they learn to read anything else. They play in the canals between the lower walls — the canals are not deep, and the silt is soft, and a child can wade in them at low water and look up at the remnants of the ground-floor ceilings overhead, the beams still in place, the plaster fallen away in patches. They grow up watching the fog. They grow up knowing that a wall can give way at the waterline without warning, that the sound of it giving way is a particular sound — a wet shudder, a long slow exhalation — and that when this happens, one does not panic, one simply notes which wall it was and which rooftop is now wider. They grow up knowing that the place is becoming something, and that they are part of what it is becoming, and that the becoming is slow and is not finished.

The elders are fewer. They remember more dry ground. Some of them — the oldest — remember a time when the water was at a lower mark, when the ground floors were still ground floors, when a stairwell climbed all the way up to its landing. They do not speak much about this, perhaps, the way one does not speak much about a house one has moved out of. They watch the walls. They know which walls are about to go, by the same instinct that tells the causeway-menders which planks are about to fail. They have a small ceremony, perhaps, when a wall goes — a moment of silence, a slow exhalation, a naming of the room that has just opened to the water for the first time. They hold, I think, a kind of knowledge that is not taught but is absorbed: a knowledge of the older city, of the names of the streets that are now canals, of the shapes of the rooms that are now ponds, of the location of the well that is now a fountain where the lake breathes in and out with the slow weather of the day. They are the memory of the place. The rest of the residents are the present tense.

I watched them for several days, from a high rooftop where the canopy thinned and the fog lifted a little in the afternoons. They did not speak to me. They watched me, from windows and from doorways and from the prows of their boats, with the same patience they watched the water and the walls and the vines. They were not unfriendly. They were simply busy. They had causeways to mend, and ropes to splice, and fish to catch, and vines to prune, and gardens to tend, and a slow place to keep holding together plank by plank. They had, I think, no particular wish to explain themselves to a visitor. They were what they were. They had become what they had become, by inches, over the long years, and they did not see the need to put it into words for someone who had arrived only yesterday.

I think they were right.

The place is in the middle of becoming. The frogs will come. The fish will multiply. The silt will compact. The lower walls will fall, one by one, into the warm water, and the silt will cover them, and the foundations will be preserved under the silt for the geologists of some future age to find and to misname. The trees on the upper floors will grow taller. The vines will thicken. The rooftops will become gardens entirely, and the gardens will become forests, and the forests will become the upper canopy of a drowned and slow-greening world that has no name yet. The residents will adapt. They will pole their boats through the new lanes. They will rebuild the causeways across the wider gaps. They will catch the fish and harvest the fruit and prune the vines and watch the frogs come in, one by one, until the place is full of their voices at evening. They will do this for a long time. They have been doing it for a long time already. The slow clock is turning. They are patient. The water is patient. The vines are patient. Even the frogs, waiting in the silt for the moment the place is ready for them, are patient.

It is, as I said, not unpleasant.

It is not finished either.
