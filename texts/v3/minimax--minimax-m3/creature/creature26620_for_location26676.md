# What Lives Here

You will want, first, to find the animals. You will want to count them, name them, place them in some order whose keeper you trust. The plateau will not cooperate with this. The plateau is hospitable to the project in a way that should worry you — it will show you a deer, and then another, and then a third that is also the second, and then it will show you no deer for a week, and you will not be able to say whether the deer were ever more than a single deer wearing different coats. The plateau permits census. The plateau also declines to be enumerated. Both are true at the same time. Both have been true since before you arrived, and will continue to be true after you have compiled your careful notes.

So. The inhabitants.

---

**The pale deer** is the first to find you, or you the deer — the meeting is mutual in a way neither of you can later describe. It is the color of the moss on the south side of the lichen, which is to say it is the color of *substance that has been in the same place for a long time*. It is not white. It is not cream. It is the precise beige of a paper left in a window for a season. It stands in a clearing between two engines, one of which is ticking at a tempo you could slow-dance to, and it is browsing.

It is browsing nothing.

There is nothing at the level of its mouth. The ferns come up to its knees, taller at the edges of the clearing, but where its lips work — and they work with great seriousness, with the entire attention of a thing that has decided that eating is a vocation — there is only air, and a faint amber tint to that air, and a smell like the inside of a watch. It pulls at what isn't there. It chews. Its jaw moves sideways the way an ungulate's jaw moves sideways, but the lateral motion is exaggerated, slower than any herbivore you've watched, as if it were grinding not grass but a *decision*.

Its antlers are its true body. They are not bone, although they are bone. They come off the skull in a branching so complex it suggests a coast seen from far up — inlets within inlets within inlets. In the dim plateau light, when they catch the amber exhaust of the nearest engine, they go the color of old honey for a moment and then cool back to their usual bone. There is lichen on the antlers. There is, somewhere in the upper branches, a small nest made of what looks like hair from the deer's own throat, although how it climbed up to place it there you cannot ask.

The deer sees you. It is not alarmed. It is not unalarmed. It continues to chew the air with the patient focus of a thing that has been chewing the air since before air had a name. After a long while — long enough that your legs have begun to remind you that standing in one place is not what they were built for — the deer turns its head and walks away into the ferns and is *not there*. It is not there in the way something is not there when it has put itself behind a fern. It is not there in the way something has *agreed* with the ferns to be not there together. The ferns close behind it with courtesy.

The pale deer is everywhere on the plateau. So is the *one* pale deer. There is only ever one at a time. This is a claim the plateau makes and a claim the plateau also breaks, sometimes, when the conditions are right, and then there are three does drinking at a glassy pond at dusk, and they are a single deer in three places, and the pond agrees to be three ponds at the relevant coordinates, and this is fine.

---

**The pale bird** lives on the engines.

Not physically on the engines — although physically on the engines also — but *of* the engines, somehow, in a way that seems to have preceded it. It is the size of a man's forearm from wrist to elbow, including the small fist at the end. It is glossy in a way feathers are not usually glossy; the gloss is the gloss of a thing that has been polished by use rather than display. The feathers, if they are feathers, are the color of polished brass with a green undertone, except where the amber steam has touched them, which is more places than it should be, and there they are the color of a varnished violin.

Its eye is the eye of the place. That is the only way to put it. The iris is rust-colored with a darker ring at the pupil; the pupil itself is a small dark gear-tooth. It looks at you with this eye and you feel *catalogued*. Not judged. Catalogued. As if a small machinist somewhere inside the bird's head has noted your dimensions, your angle of approach, the speed at which your shadow has been lengthening on the basalt since you began reading this, and filed the information in a drawer marked with a number only the bird can read.

What the bird does, mostly, is call. It calls in time with the engine nearest it. Not always the nearest — sometimes with an engine half a kilometer away, in a way you would swear is *phasing*, the bird's call and the engine's tick arriving at your ear at the same moment as if they were two aspects of a single sound the plateau was choosing to present to you in stereo. The call is four notes. The notes are A, E-flat, F, and a fourth note you can never quite locate on any scale. The engine, when you lean against its housing with your ear to the warm iron, is doing the same four notes, inaudibly, below speech.

It nests in the upper branches of trees that have grown through engines. The nests are made of moss and rust flakes and, sometimes, a single gear-tooth so eroded it is almost a pebble. Two eggs in the spring, if spring is the word for what passes for spring here. The chicks are the color of fresh lacquer. They learn the four-note song before they learn to fly. This is a thing the plateau has arranged and the plateau declines to apologize for.

---

**The lizards** are more numerous than the deer and less various than the bird. They are arm-length, which is to say they are as long as your forearm including the hand. They are flat in profile. Their backs are a darker rust than the housings they lie upon, so dark that in some lights the lizards vanish into the engines that are holding them up; in other lights they are the most visible thing on the plateau by a factor of three.

They lie on the engines the way gulls lie on buoys. With propriety. As if they had been placed there by an engineer who knew what they were for and did not consider it his job to inform the lizards. They lie still for so long that you assume they are part of the housing, a sculpted relief, a knurled grip added for the convenience of maintenance workers who never come. Then one of them will turn its head with the slow precision of a periscope and look at you with an eye like a polished cabochon, and you will see that it has been thinking the entire time, and the thinking was substantial, and you are outnumbered.

They eat the insects. They eat the amber steam where it touches things, when it is cool enough to be chewable. They eat, sometimes, a flake of rust they have detached from the housing they lie on, in the meditative way a goat eats a mineral lick. Their bellies, when they are sunning on a flat surface and you can see the underside, are the precise color of the dried-blood housings — not a coincidence, not mimicry, a *sharing*. They have agreed with the iron to be the same color. The iron has agreed to be the same color as them. Both agreements hold until the lichen comes and then both agreements are revised.

There is a lizard the size of your leg that you only see in late afternoon, once, and it is watching a stack, and as the amber plume begins to issue from the stack it opens its mouth very wide and the plume, instead of dispersing, *enters*. The lizard stands with its mouth open and a slow stream of amber vapor pours into it. This goes on for as long as you watch, which is fourteen minutes. The lizard does not visibly change. It does not close its mouth in satisfaction. It simply accepts the offering as a posture. The plateau considers this ordinary.

---

**The insects in B-flat** are the smallest of the named fauna and the most pervasive after the lichens. They are not bees. They are not cicadas. They are a thing that has apparently decided that B-flat is a commitment, and hums it, and there are so many of them that the hum thickens into a chord the moment the air is still. They are the size of rice grains. They are the color of rice grains that have gone a little feral. They have no sting you can feel and no business being in your hair, and yet they are in your hair, shortly, and the chord you hear from inside your hair is so close to the chord you hear from outside that the difference has to do with *distance from the listener* and nothing to do with which insects are present.

On warm afternoons — the plateau is rarely warm, but the afternoon has a few hours where it relaxes about this — the insects rise. They form a column. The column goes up. The column enters the overcast and disappears and you can hear it humming in B-flat for some minutes after, as if the ceiling had begun to play an instrument. After the column has gone, the plateau is quieter and not quieter — the engines tick on, the birds sing their four notes, the lichens do whatever the lichens have been doing for three hundred million years — but a layer has been lifted off.

---

**The colugo** — call it that, although it isn't, call it a glider, although gliding is too purposeful a word — has a face like a held breath. It is the size of a small dog, if a small dog had been painted by someone who only liked the dog in outline and so had made it mostly membrane. It lives in the canopy. It lives *high* in the canopy, in the topmost stratum where the ferns tip into something more abstract, where the light from the invisible sun finally gives up and is absorbed by the air before it can be absorbed by anything else.

It has one visible eye. The other is somewhere inside its face; you catch it, sometimes, like a coin underwater. The visible eye is orange. Not the orange of a sunset. Not the orange of a carrot. The orange of a warning sign in a place where the warnings have been left up so long they have become landmarks. The eye watches you. The eye watches you across a distance that is *the wrong distance* — closer than it should be, given how long the colugo would have to glide to reach you, farther than it should be, given that you can make out individual hairs in the rim of the eyelid. Both distances are true. The colugo has agreed, this afternoon, to be both.

You will see it once, and never again. Except that you will see it again, that same afternoon, on a different trunk, watching from the same distance and at the same wrong distance. You will look away, look back, and it will be on yet another trunk, forty meters further along the canopy's path. It has not glided. It has *relocated*. The space between trunks was a courtesy the colugo asked the trunks to extend, and they extended it, and now it is somewhere else. This is its locomotion. This is its whole life, perhaps — a series of relocations arranged by the forest on behalf of a creature that has agreed to be the question it keeps asking.

---

**The middle-distance birds** — unnamed, unnameable, there are several species of them or one species in several costumes — are the birds the original account promised and is only now delivering. They sing. They sing in hoots, in warbles, in something between a flute's lower register and a kettle's complaint. They sing from positions about forty meters away from you no matter where you are, as if they had been displaced to a constant. You cannot approach them. You cannot hear them approach. They are there at a stable remove.

What they sing, when you listen carefully, is *the contour of the day*. Not the words. The contour. If the morning has been wet, they sing a wet morning. If the engines were loud overnight, they sing an engine-loud night. If you arrived in a foul mood, they sing your foul mood back to you at their stable remove with their stable phrasing, and you cannot tell whether they are mocking or commiserating, and it is also possible they have no opinion about the difference.

Sometimes one of them is the pale bird. Sometimes one of them is, briefly, the colugo, sounding from a branch it cannot possibly be in. The plateau does not consider these substitutions unkind. The plateau considers them *generous*.

---

There are other things, and you have to be told.

There are worms. The worms live in the mosses and are the mosses; the distinction is local and has been disputed for some time. There are small spiders whose webs span the spaces between piston housings, and the webs tune themselves to the engines the way a violin string tunes itself to a peg, and the webs hum, and at night the plateau is a vast chord organ played by its own foundations. There are things in the ponds, and the things in the ponds do not move when you look at them but are not the same as the water they are in, and when the water is still the things in the water are still and when the water is moving the things in the water are *still*, and you understand, watching, that stillness is not the only mode a thing can persist in. There are small furred things under the engines that have made homes of the leeward shadows and come out only when the amber steam is not issuing, which is rare. There are ant colonies, red-black, that have built mounds around the bases of housings and that run their own complex industry regardless of what the engines are doing, in the way that ant colonies are very large single animals to whom the engines are merely weather.

And there are the engines.

---

You will want to ask whether the engines live. The plateau considers this charming. The plateau has been asked this so many times that the question itself has worn a small groove in the air over its busiest yard, like a footpath worn by the same cow going to the same puddle.

The answer is: they live the way water lives when it is the same water the fish are made of. They live the way a thought lives between the moment you think it and the moment you remember having thought it. They are not alive in the way a deer is alive. They are *ongoing* in the way that the rotation of the earth is ongoing, and the asking of the question is ongoing, and the forgetting of the answer is ongoing.

A piston goes up and down. The up and the down are not, upon close listening, the same up and down; there is more texture to them than up and down accounts for. The flywheels, when you put a fingertip against the housing, are warm, and the warmth varies a little from one point on the housing to another, and the variations have a pattern that is *almost* a heartbeat and is *almost* tidal. The valves open and close in sequences that you can follow if you stand still long enough, and the sequences are not periodic, they are *conversational* — something is being said, and something is being answered, and the answering is the next engine over, two hundred meters downslope, that has just ticked open a valve half a second after the first engine ticked one shut.

They grow. They grow as the mosses and the figs grow, by being incorporated. A fern erupts from a piston housing in the third season, and now the engine and the fern are a single breathing thing; the piston goes down and the fern nods; the piston comes up and the fern straightens, in sync. A tree has grown around a gearbox and the gearing is now part of the cambium, and when the wind moves the tree the gearbox moves too, and the tree has begun, slowly, to *gear*. There is an engine on the southern flats that you only find in rain, and in rain it is *not an engine*. It is a space shaped like an engine. The space has a hum. The space has, sometimes, hands that come out of it. The hands are not engine hands; they are wet hands, river hands, with the small grime of having been at the bottom of something.

There is an engine somewhere on the eastern ridge that no one has ever confirmed and that everyone who has spent enough time here has heard. It ticks louder than the others. It ticks louder than should be possible from where it would have to be. The plateau declines to clarify whether this engine is inhabited, whether it is the place that is inhabited, whether there is a difference.

---

And the *almost* ones.

These you will not see, and they are the most present. The plateau is full of shapes that are almost birds and almost engine mechanisms and almost deer and almost reflections of reflections. They live in the gaps between the named inhabitants. They are the connective tissue. They are the haze through which the pale deer walks and emerges slightly different on the other side, the haze through which the colugo relocates, the haze through which the engines are *slightly* more and less than engines at the same moment. They do not have names. They have *agreements* — small arrangements with the named inhabitants about who gets to be the deer today and who gets to be the suggestion of a deer. They are the place's immune system. They are the place's quiet metabolism.

They are also, you will come to feel, your hosts. When you have been here long enough, one of them will stand at a respectful remove and *almost* look like you, and you will nod at it, and it will almost nod back, and the plateau will breathe around you both, and you will realize that you are, for this duration, also an inhabitant, and the duration is the word *almost*, and the word *almost* is the name of the place, and the place is the foundries, and the engines are quiet, and you are here.
