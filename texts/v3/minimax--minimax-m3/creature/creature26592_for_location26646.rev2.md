# The Inhabitants of the High Stillness

What lives here has learned the long way, over a great many generations, that visibility is a kind of debt. To be seen is to be spent. So most of what moves across the stone has folded itself, generation after generation, into the colours of the place — the pale tan of weathered feldspar, the liver-brown of the older lichen, the grey-violet of rock after the mist has been on it for an hour. A creature that stands out is a creature that has miscalculated, and the plateau does not forgive miscalculations often, because there is not enough of anything here for second chances. The food is thin. The moisture is thin. The cover is a rumour. So the bodies that survive are the bodies that have agreed, in their own slow language of pigment and shape and habit, to be almost nothing.

Begin, then, where the eye cannot easily follow: in the cracks.

In the cracks of the grey-brown outcrops, in the thin soil-membrane that has gathered where two slabs of rock lean against each other at a low angle, in the felt of moss and the black-green smear of the slime-colonies down in the shallow bowls — here is where the plateau's true population lives, and has always lived, in numbers that would frighten you if you could see them all at once. A square metre of the felt, peeled back, would be a city. There are things in it smaller than the head of a pin, colourless, almost transparent, that move through the moisture-film with the slow purpose of something that has nowhere to be and all the time in the world to get there. They feed, in some way that no visitor has ever quite pinned down, on the stone itself — or on the film that the stone, given enough patience, exudes. They are not quite animals. They are not quite plants. They are some third thing, the way a lichen is a third thing, a negotiation between two kinds of life that have agreed to share a body. You would not notice them if you lay down and put your face against the rock and breathed on it to warm the glass of a magnifying lens. Even then, you would see only the suggestion of movement, a faint disturbance in the wet, the way you might see something shift at the very edge of your vision in a dark room and turn and find nothing.

A little larger — the size of a grain of rice, perhaps, or the first joint of your little finger — are the things that look, at a casual glance, like the dropped husks of seeds. They are not husks. They are not seeds. They are something with legs, many legs, that has folded them all up under a carapace the colour and texture of the chaff that blows off the tussocks in the dry weeks. They sit for hours without moving, and then they move, and when they move they do so with the unhurried deliberation of a thing that has calculated exactly where it is going and how much energy the going will cost. They eat the spores of the black slime. They eat the smaller pin-head things. They are eaten, in turn, by the things that hang from the undersides of the overhangs.

The overhang-clusters are, the first time you pass under one, the closest thing the plateau has to an alarm. You do not see them. You hear them. A soft shifting, like the inside of a paper bag being crumpled very slowly, and then a faint rain of something — not liquid, not quite solid — that patters down around your feet and across the tops of your boots. You step back. You look up. And there, in the shade of the stone lip, is a colony of something that has chosen, for reasons of its own, to live upside down. They are pale. They are clustered like fruit, like a bunch of grapes that has decided to hang from a rock instead of a vine. Each one is the size of the end of your thumb, soft-bodied, with a faint segmentation and, at the lower end (which is, here, the upper end), a suggestion of a mouth, or an eye, or both — it is hard to tell in the dim. They are not asleep. They are doing whatever it is they do, which is mostly waiting. When you walked under them, you were the most interesting thing that had happened in weeks, and the entire colony shifted at once, the way a flock of birds shifts on a wire, and a few of the lowermost individuals let go and dropped — not fell, dropped, in the deliberate way of something choosing to descend — onto the stone to investigate. They landed without sound. They sat. They did not move again until you moved away, and then, after a long interval, they climbed back up the rock face and rejoined the cluster, and the colony resettled, and the silence came back.

You might think, looking at them, that they are blind. They are not blind, exactly. They have, somewhere on the soft pale bodies, a concentration of cells that can tell light from dark, and movement from stillness, and possibly warm from cold. But they do not have what you would call eyes. The plateau, in its long withdrawal from the rest of the world's ideas, has apparently decided that eyes are an extravagance. Most of what lives here has, in one way or another, given them up. The pin-head things in the moss have no eyes at all. The chaff-things on the ground have eye-spots that can probably only tell them when their own shadow has fallen across them, which is useful when something larger is approaching. The overhang-clusters have their light-sensitive cells. None of them have anything that would recognise a face. They know you are there by the warmth of you, by the pressure-change in the air as you move through it, by the shadow you cast, by the faint chemical signature of your sweat and the soap you washed with three days ago and the food you ate this morning. They read you the way you might read a change in the weather: not as a person, but as an event.

The pale flitting things that cross the stone in low arcs are a different order of being, and the plateau has produced them in strange abundance. They are not insects — or rather, they are not only insects. Some of them have six legs, some have eight, some have a number of legs that you cannot easily count because they are folded together into the body and only deployed when the creature lands and walks. They are the colour of ash, of bone, of the pale paper that lines the inside of an old book. They fly — or glide, or leap and coast, in a way that uses the faint thermals that rise off the sun-warmed rock even on an overcast day. They appear from no visible source, cross a metre or two of stone in a low silent trajectory, and disappear again into the next crack or beneath the next flake of loose rock. They do not seem to be hunting. They do not seem to be mating. They seem, as far as you can make out, to be going somewhere, and then to have arrived, and to be going somewhere else. Their wings, when they have them, are translucent and veined in a way that reminds you of the leaves of the low sage-shrubs, though whether this is mimicry or coincidence you cannot tell. When they land, they fold themselves down against the stone and become, in a breath, invisible. The stone is the colour they have chosen to be, and they have chosen it so well that the eye slides off them the way water slides off a well-oiled cloth.

The tussocks have their own small menagerie. In the still, damp centre of a grey grass-clump, if you part the blades with a careful finger, you may find a kind of woven lattice of something silk-like, finer than any spider's web you have seen elsewhere, holding the tussock together from the inside. In the lattice sit several small pale bodies, each one tucked into its own pocket of the weave, waiting. They are not spiders. They are not quite anything you can name. They have, when they choose to deploy them, too many legs, and they move with a precision that seems almost mechanical until you watch long enough to see them hesitate, and change direction, and then stop, and then move again, as if they are thinking each step through before they take it. They feed on the tiny things that fall into the tussock from the air — spores, pollen that has blown in from somewhere else, the occasional pin-head creature that has wandered too far from the felt. They are patient in a way that is hard to credit in something so small. They will sit in the same position for days. They will wait for a meal to arrive, and they will not go looking for one.

And then there is what moves in the open, in the cooler hours, across the flat stone between the tussocks. You may not see it for the first day, or the second. It is the colour of the lichen. It is the size of your hand, flattened, with a soft body and a number of limbs that it keeps tucked under it as it moves, so that what you see, when you finally see it, is something like a slow oval of pale liver-brown sliding across the rock at perhaps the speed your own breathing moves the air in your lungs. It is a browser. It moves from one growth of the black slime to the next, and from one patch of moss to the next, and it eats, very slowly, the surface of whatever it is on. It has, somewhere on the front of it, a mouth, but no visible eyes. It finds its food by the chemistry of the stone, by the faint warmth of a north-facing patch where the moss has grown a millimetre thicker, by the texture under its body. It has been doing this, in roughly this form, for longer than the carved standing stone has been standing. The plateau has had a long time to make something like this, and it has made it well.

Above all of it, in the middle hours of the day, when the overcast thins a little and the air begins to move in its slow convective turn, the things that may or may not be birds hang at the edges of the thermals. They are not, exactly, birds. They are too still for birds. They hold themselves in the rising air with a minimum of effort — a tilt of a wing, a slight fanning of a flight-feather, the smallest possible adjustment — and they circle, in wide patient arcs, over the plateau, and their shadows pass across the stone below without any sound at all. They do not hunt, as far as you can see. They do not call. They do not land while you are watching. They are simply there, the way the plateau is there, riding the slow currents of the warmed air, and from time to time one of them peels away from the circle and descends, very gradually, toward some distant point on the plain, and is gone, and another rises from somewhere out of sight to take its place. They are pale underneath and darker above, in the way that birds of the open air often are, and their beaks, when you can finally see one close enough, are small and hooked and seem to be made more for tearing than for anything else. What they eat, and where they go, and what they are, exactly, you may never know. They are part of the plateau's weather, almost. They belong to the sky the way the pin-head things belong to the moss.

And there is the hum.

The hum is not, you have to accept, an animal. You can stand in one place for an hour and feel it through the soles of your feet, a low resonance so deep it is less a sound than a pressure, a kind of slow pulse that you could almost synchronise your breathing to if you were not careful. It is the plateau itself — the stone, the deep stone, the cooled and slowly-cooling interior of what was once a mountain range, ticking over with the last of its internal heat, contracting a fraction of a millimetre every century, giving up its warmth to the air and to the sky in a long slow exhalation that has been going on for longer than anything alive has been here to feel it. The creatures on the surface have, perhaps, arranged themselves around it. They live in time with it, in the way that tide-pool creatures live in time with the tides. They have no way of knowing that it is there. They have no need to. They have been shaped, by the long patient fact of it, into the creatures they are.

Or perhaps — and this is the part you cannot settle, the part that the plateau keeps for itself — there is something in the hum. Not an animal. Not a machine. Something that is aware, in the slow way that stone is aware, of the small lives moving across its surface. Something that registered, perhaps, when the half-circle of dark slabs was set out on the lee side of the knuckle, and registered again when the standing stone was cut and raised, and has been registering, ever since, the slow return of those marks to itself. The marks are almost gone. In a few hundred years the half-circle will be a scatter. In a few thousand the standing stone will fall. The hum will go on. It will be the last thing here, after the tussocks have gone and the moss has gone and the chaff-things have gone and the pale fliers have stopped flying and the cluster-creatures have let go of the overhang and dropped, at last, into the dust. The hum will be the last thing, and it will not be lonely, because it will not know what loneliness is. It will simply continue, the way it has always continued, the sound of a place that has come to the end of what it was going to do and is content.

What lives here, then, lives the way water lives in a cracked cup: in the thin film, in the leftover, in the small spaces that have not yet been asked to give up what they hold. It is small, and it is patient, and it is almost invisible, and it has been shaped, over a span of time that would empty your mind if you tried to hold it, into something that fits. It does not know it is remarkable. It does not know it is strange. It knows only that the stone is warm in the morning, that the mist will come again in the afternoon, that the tussock it lives in will be here tomorrow, and that if it waits, and is quiet, and does not stand out against the colour of the world, it will have another day. It has had many such days. It expects to have many more.

The plateau is not waiting for anything. But the small lives on it are waiting, in their various small ways, for the next hour, and the next, and the next, and they will be here, in the cracks and the felt and the overhangs and the slow arcs of the sky, long after whatever brought you here has gone.
