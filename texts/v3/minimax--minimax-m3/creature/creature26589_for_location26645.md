The moss is the first and oldest inhabitant, and it is not one inhabitant but a federation so dense and so old that the word *inhabitant* has stopped being adequate. It is the place's own skin, the place's own lung, the place's own slow gut, and it has been here so long that nothing inside the system can remember a time before it. It is composed of uncountable small bodies — the sphagnum cushions fat as a closed fist and holding their weight in water like green sponges; the feathery trailing mosses that lean over the lips of overhangs and hang there in pale curtains a foot long, moving in the constant wind; the liverwort thalli pressed flat against the stone and dark as tarnished silver; the powdery crusts of lichen in their oranges and dust-greys, which are not strictly moss at all but live alongside it as partners in the long dissolution of rock. Each of these is itself a small city. Each cushion of sphagnum is a building. Inside it, water moves in capillary films between the leaves, and in the films live rotifers and tardigrades and nematodes and small transparent worms that have no name in any language you speak, and feeding on them are things smaller still, and the things smaller still are fed upon by mites the size of pinheads that drift through the moss interior like animated dust, and so on, down through layers of life that become invisible but do not become absent. A single cubic inch of this moss, given the equipment to look, holds more individuals than a small city has people, and most of them would be unknown to science, and all of them would be in the middle of their lives, and most of them would die before you finished counting. The moss is not a backdrop. The moss is a city that has forgotten it is a city, or never knew, and has spent ten thousand years growing itself into a single, slow, semi-coherent body that ferries water down from the upper plateau and gives it, drop by drop, to the stone.

And the stone receives it.

The stone is not an inhabitant. The stone is the canvas. But the stone is inhabited, and has been for so long that the line between inhabitant and inhabited has blurred almost past recognition. Lichens creep into its surface and crack it open a fraction of a millimeter at a time. Moss sends its rhizoids into the cracks and pries them wider. Water, given time, dissolves the minerals and carries them down. Frost wedges a flake loose in winter. The flake falls into a basin. The basin fills with water, and the water freezes and expands, and a basin becomes a deeper basin, a ledge becomes a channel, a channel becomes a gutter. The stone is being unmade by its inhabitants, slowly, politely, with the cooperation of the stone itself, which has by now lost any strong opinion about remaining stone.

Among the moss, in the dark wet interior, the small things scurry.

The pale things with too many segments — you see them when you lift a curtain of trailing moss and look at its underside, where the water is constant and the light is gone. They are the color of cream, or wet bone, or nothing at all. They have the general shape of a woodlouse, the kind you turn over a rotting log to find in a garden, but where a woodlouse has seven pairs of legs and a body of obvious segments, these have more — a longer body, an extra articulation or two, a way of folding that suggests something almost worm-like in the middle of being almost crustacean at the ends. They move quickly for their size in short bursts, and they pause often. When they pause they press themselves flat against the wet surface and become, for a moment, invisible, and when they move again they leave behind a faint trail in the water film that closes up behind them after a second or two. They are eating. They are always eating. They eat the dead moss, the fungal threads that run through the moss, the occasional body of a smaller creature, the dissolved organic matter that has collected in the water film. They are part of the recycling system. Without them, the moss would pile up on itself and choke. With them, it is constantly being broken down at the base while new growth extends at the tip, and the place maintains itself in the long middle of being itself.

Above them, in the more open parts of the moss where a thin daylight reaches, the hexapods.

These are tiny — three millimeters long at the upper end of their size range, often smaller — and they are the color of wet bark, which is to say the color of everything around them, which is to say they are difficult to see. Six legs. A body divided into the usual regions, but with proportions that suggest something either very old or very specialized: a hind section larger than seems practical, perhaps, or antennae longer than the body itself. They move in fits and starts. They graze. What they graze on is not always clear — algae films on the wet stone, perhaps, or fungal spores, or the surfaces of moss leaves where a thin layer of microbial life has accumulated. They are numerous. When you sit still long enough, they begin to ignore you, and you see them all around you, moving over the moss and the stone in their small hurrying way, and the place reveals itself to be, at this scale, as populated as a forest floor, but without any of the trees.

The spider is rarer and slower.

It is fat. It is dun-colored, the color of dried moss or old dust. It builds a web no larger than the palm of your hand, strung between two or three moss stems or the lip of a small overhang, and it hangs in the web upside down, head toward the ground, the long legs tucked close to the round body. It is not pretty. It has the look of something that has been here a long time and has stopped bothering with appearances. The web is not the geometric web of an orb-weaver; it is a sheet, with a few trip-lines running out into the surrounding moss, and when one of the trip-lines is touched by something walking through — a hexapod, most often, or one of the pale segmented creatures that has wandered up out of the dark interior — the spider does not rush out. It simply turns, very slightly, in the direction of the disturbance, and waits. Eventually the spider moves. The spider is patient the way the place is patient. If you watch one long enough you begin to feel that the spider is part of the place, that the place has produced the spider as it produces basins and ledges and gutters, that the spider is a feature of the drainage as much as it is an animal.

The bird, when it comes, is a smear.

You are sitting in a dry warm hollow, in the circle of bare stone kept clear by some machine whose hum you can feel in your sit bones, and you see, at the very edge of your vision, a movement. A low fast passage of dark color through the green, between the moss curtains, below the level of the ledges. A wing-beat, or just the suggestion of one. A displacement of air you feel against the side of your face before you understand what has caused it. Then it is gone, and you are not entirely sure it was there, and the moss where it passed is still settling back into place. It is small, this bird. It is the size of a wren, or a small thrush, or something in that range — you never get a good enough look to be sure of the shape. It is dark. It may be hunting the hexapods. It may be hunting the spider. It comes perhaps once an hour while you sit, perhaps less often, perhaps not at all if you are unlucky, and you begin to understand, after a while, that you are not seeing the bird; you are seeing evidence that the bird exists, the way you might see, on a beach, the footprints of something that runs faster than you can look.

And there is the larger thing.

The larger thing you do not see. You feel it. You are sitting, or standing, in the moss, and somewhere — twenty feet away, maybe, or fifty, somewhere in a deeper channel or a boggy saddle between two ridges — something moves, and the moss under your feet moves with it. A subtle bounce. A slow displacement of the surface. The kind of motion a heavy animal would make if it were walking through deep soft ground, except there is no heavy animal here, and yet the moss is moving. You wait. The moss settles. Nothing appears. Nothing makes a sound. The wind continues its thin hiss through the curtains. The drops continue their plip and plip. But you have felt it. You have felt the weight pass under you, and you have understood, without being told, that this is why the small things scurry. This is the predator of predators. This is the thing the bird is afraid of. This is the thing the spider, in its small web, is hiding from. You will not see it. You will only know that it is here because the place is built to accommodate it — the deeper channels are deep enough, the saddles are soft enough, the moss mattresses are springy enough — and because the small fauna is always, at some low level of attention, watching for it.

And then there are the machines.

The machines are not alive. Or if they are, they are alive in a way that the word alive cannot stretch to cover. They are made of metal. They are made of metal that has been wet for a very long time, and the wetness is, you realize when you touch one, the only thing keeping them from being stone. They have geometries — fins, flanges, the radiating spokes of a heat sink, the smooth surfaces of something that was once machined to tolerances no longer visible — and the geometries are embedded in the rock and the peat and the felted moss of ten thousand growing seasons, and they are not being serviced by anyone, and they are not waiting to be serviced by anyone, and they are humming.

The hum is in your wrist. The hum is in your sit bones if you sit in one of the hollows. The hum has intervals. The hum has a key.

The machines are everywhere, but not in the wet zones. They are on the dry-ish ridges, on the broad sills between the channels, half-buried in lichen, in groups of three or four, in long irregular lines, in patterns that are very nearly circular with a hollow in the center kept clear of moss by some persistent warmth or vibration you cannot locate the source of. Inside the hollow, the stone is bare. Inside the hollow, the stone is dry. Inside the hollow, the stone is faintly warm, and you sit there, and you feel the hum in your pelvis, and you understand that the hum is not random.

You do not know what the machines are doing. You do not know who made them, or when, or why. You do not know if they are the remnants of a people who have long since gone elsewhere, or the slow beginning of a people who are just getting started, or something else entirely — something that does not map onto your categories of tool and artifact and ruin. You only know that they are here, and that they are doing something, and that the something is patient, and that the something has a key, and that the key is, against all reason, beautiful.

The moss is climbing over them. The moss is climbing over them and they are sinking into the stone and the stone is being lifted by the moss and the machines do not mind, the moss does not mind, the water does not mind, and you, after a while, sitting in one of the dry warm hollows with the hum in your pelvis and the thin light in your eyes, do not mind either.

The machines are the strangest inhabitants, because they are the only ones that seem to be doing something other than living. The small things live. The moss lives. The spider lives. The bird lives. The larger thing lives. The machines do something. The machines are not living in the place; they are doing something in the place, slowly, in the wet and the cool and the silence, and the something has been going on for a long time, and the something will go on for a long time after you leave, and the moss will grow over the hollows and the hum will continue and the key will continue, and the place will wait, and you, far below the elevation line, will lie in your own bed and listen to your own pulse and wonder what the hum is doing, and you will not find out, and that is part of the place too.
