# The Inhabitants

Begin with the birds, because the birds announce themselves.

They are not many. A handful — six, perhaps, or eight, though the number is hard to hold because they are rarely all in view at once, and because their pale backs against the pale stone of the upper walls make them difficult to count when they are still. They are smaller than a fist, smaller than you would expect a bird to be that produces so much sound. Their plumage is the off-white of old bone, faintly buff at the breast, with a quick dark eye and feet the color of the damp streaks on the walls. They move in the way small birds move when they have lived a long time in one place and no longer fear the geometry of it: a short, hard wingbeat to launch, a glide along the curve of the stone, a landing so precise it looks thought. They know the bowl the way a fish knows a current.

Their song is the loudest thing in the cup. It begins low — a single note, almost a breath — and climbs, note by note, a staircase of sound so even and so clean it could be a bell rung slowly. It rises, rises, and then stops, not fading but *cut*, as if something has reached in and snipped the wire. A pause. Then another note, low again, and the climb begins. The shape of the bowl holds the song the way the bowl holds light: a long time, turning it around the curved walls until the last vibration has visited every surface. Sometimes when a bird sings far overhead, the sound arrives back at you a second time from a different direction, and you cannot tell if you are hearing the original or its memory.

They nest in holes you cannot see, in the upper walls where the curve begins to close inward toward the rim. You know the holes are there because of the white smears of droppings that run down from them in long pale dribbles, fanning out across the stone like the marks of slow water falling the other way. The smears have been there a long time. Some of them are mineralized, almost a part of the wall now. The birds come and go through these holes with the same unhurried certainty the lizards have — they do not look around, do not hesitate at the threshold, do not perform the small frightened arithmetic of flight that birds usually do at the entrance to a home. They simply go in. They are home.

Below them, lower on the warm flat floor, are the lizards.

You do not see the lizards at first. You stand at the rim, and the floor of the bowl looks empty — just stone, pale and veined, with its darker patches of damp. The light lies across it in a thick white sheet. You look and see nothing. Then you look longer, and a head is there, lifted at the angle of a small question. Then a tail, curved like a comma laid on its side. Then another, and another, and you understand that the floor is not empty at all — that it has been populated all along, and that you were simply too quick to see it, the way a person is too quick to see the stars at noon.

They are the color of the stone. This is not a metaphor or a flourish; it is a precise description. Their backs are the gray of dry limestone, faintly pink at the throat and along the sides, with a dusting of gold across the shoulders that you only notice when the sun is high and the light is at the right angle. Some are darker, almost the brown of the wet streaks. Some are nearly white. All of them disappear against the floor when they are still. They have made a practice of stillness the way the birds have made a practice of song.

They are not large. The biggest is the length of your hand from wrist to fingertip; the smallest, the ones you only find because they move at the last second as your foot comes down, would fit on two of your fingers. They have the look of things that have been worked on a long time — small, exact, finished. Their toes are thin and deliberate, each one placed. Their eyes, when they turn them toward you, are round and dark and not at all afraid. They watch you the way the bowl watches you: with patience, without urgency, without the expectation that you will do anything in particular. They have, perhaps, never had reason to fear a thing that stood at the rim. Whatever falls in falls in, and after a long time is just another part of the floor.

A lizard will sit for an hour without moving. You can stand and watch it and it will watch you back, and neither of you will be the first to look away. Its throat pulses — a slow, soft, repeated motion, like a small bellows being used very gently. This is, you realize after watching for a long time, the only thing that proves it is alive when it is still. That, and the eye, which follows you when you move your head but does not move with the rest of the body. The eye is the most living part of a lizard. The rest of it has learned to be stone.

When a lizard does move, it is sudden and then over. A small run — a kind of fast walk, the body held low, the tail straight out behind — and then it stops, in a new patch of light or shadow, and is again invisible. You lose it instantly. You were looking right at it and now you are not, and you have to begin the patient work of finding it again. The lizards understand this. The whole floor of the bowl is, for them, a series of small concealments, and they move from one to another with the unhurried confidence of people who know the layout of their house in the dark.

In the damp patches, on the floor and along the lower curves of the walls, are the other small things.

There are insects, with long thin bodies that are the color of clear water with a little mud in it. They stand on the surface of the shallow pools — the thin still tongues of water that never quite dry — and they do not break it. They rest upon it as if it were a skin stretched tight, and when they move they leave no mark, only the faintest dimple, the suggestion of a footprint that the water closes behind them. They skate, if a thing so small and delicate can be said to skate. They use the surface the way the birds use the air: as a thing to be on, not a thing to be in. They are, in their way, the most graceful inhabitants of the cup. Even the birds, with their quick wingbeats, do not have the still perfection of an insect at the center of a pool.

There is a slug, or a thing that is like a slug, but paler. It is almost the color of milk, or of the inside of a shell — translucent in places, so that a faint shadow of what it has been eating can be seen moving through it. It is soft in a way the rest of the bowl is not. It moves slowly, and as it moves it lays down a silver line behind it, a bright unbroken track that catches the light from above and shines for a long time after the creature has passed. The track is not aimless. It goes from one damp patch to another, from a crack in the floor to the base of the wall to the shadow under a ledge, and then is gone into the crack again. The slug is the only thing in the cup that is in a hurry, and it is not very hurried. Its whole life is the length of a silver thread.

In the ferns — in the small damp mouths of moss and the few fronds of green that have made their slow claim on the wall — there is a sound. You hear it only when you have stopped hearing the drip, only when the birds are quiet and the air has settled and you are listening to the listening. A small dry rustle, the sound of something shifting in a thicket too small to see. It is not a wind. The wind does not reach here, or reaches only as that slow sheet of air from the rim, which moves the whole column of air at once and not in pieces. It is a thing. It is alive. You cannot see it. You could put your eye to the patch of moss and see only moss. But the rustle is real, and it is repeated, and it has the patient quality of a creature that is doing what it always does, the way the drip drips and the birds sing. It is the most private inhabitant of the cup. You suspect it will never be seen, only heard, and only by someone who has given up trying.

And there are the plants.

The plants are the oldest inhabitants, or the most stubborn, or the most hopeful — the words mean nearly the same thing here. A moss, dark green, almost black where the water has stained the wall behind it, that has made a slow map of the damp places and lives exactly inside the lines. A fern, no bigger than a finger, that should not be here and is, and has been, and will continue to be. And the vine.

The vine is the strangest living thing in the cup. It has climbed, somehow, from a crack in the floor — twenty feet up the smooth inward-curving wall, with nothing visible to hold it, no root to grip, no ledge to rest on. It is thin. It is a thing of pale green and darker green, with the small heart-shaped leaves that vines have when they are young and have not yet decided what kind of vine to be. It hangs now in the air, the last foot or so of it suspended over nothing, with no visible purpose, swaying a little in the slow sheets of air that come down the wall. It has nowhere to go. It has gone as far as it can, and it is resting in the air, holding itself up by the slow will of being alive. It is, you realize, the most ambitious thing in the bowl. The birds fly; the lizards wait; the insects skate; the slug goes from one wet place to another. The vine has tried to leave. The vine has climbed as high as anything in the cup can climb, and has arrived at a place where climbing is no longer possible, and is simply staying there, in the air, in the light, a small green proof that the cup is not closed.

Above, occasionally, a shadow.

It crosses the circle of sky without announcement. You do not get a good look. It is wide and dark, and it moves from one edge of the rim to the other in a time that is too long for a leaf and too short for a cloud, and it does not call out. It could be a bird of prey — a hawk, perhaps, or a falcon, working the updrafts that rise from the warm stone of the bowl the way they rise from cliffs. It could be a large leaf lifted and carried. It could be a piece of the sky itself, briefly, in the wrong color. You never see it clearly, and you never see it again. It is the only visitor the cup receives. It does not land. It does not enter. It is passing through, on its way to somewhere else, the way everything above the rim is on its way to somewhere else. The things inside the cup are not on their way to anywhere. They are already there.

This is the community of the stone cup. It is small. It is slow. It is held together by water and patience and the long argument between the rock and the things that live on the rock. Nothing here is in a hurry, because nothing here has anywhere to be. The birds sing their clipped ascending songs, and the songs hang in the curved air, and the lizards watch from the floor, and the insects skate on the still surfaces of the pools, and the slug lays down its silver line, and the thing in the moss goes on with its private rustling, and the vine holds itself in the air, and the shadow passes above without coming in. The drip counts. The light moves by inches. The stone breathes, faintly, against the feet of anything that stands still long enough to feel it.

It is not a place that was made. It is a place that is being made, by the slow agreement of all these small lives with the slow giving of the stone. They are making it now, as they have always made it, one drip and one song and one still lizard at a time. They will go on making it after you have gone. You were at the rim a long time, and you were the only hurried thing in the bowl, and when you finally step back from the edge and turn to walk up the tilting ground toward the scrub and the pale grass, the cup does not notice you go. It is busy. It is always busy, in its way. It has been busy for a very long time, and the things inside it — the patient, the stubborn, the small, the slow, the ones that climb twenty feet for no reason, the ones that never move at all — go on with their quiet, ancient, unhurried work.
