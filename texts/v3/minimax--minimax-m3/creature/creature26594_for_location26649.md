The smallest lives here are the most numerous. They drift in the warm heavy air at every depth from near the floor to near the ceiling — dozens of them in view at any time if you learn to look, and once you learn to look you cannot stop looking, because they are everywhere, banking gently around the clumps of plants, around the standing stones, around you, suspended in currents you cannot feel. Some have thin translucent fins, veined, almost glass. Others have no fins at all, just a tapering soft body that flexes and turns. Most are no bigger than your finger. None are larger than your forearm. They are not fish — there is no water — and they are not birds, and they are not seeds, and they are not anything with a name in any language you know, though you might call them drifters if you had to. The first thing you see is their bellies: a pale turquoise bioluminescence, soft, pulsing faintly in rhythm with the hum of the chamber, a heartbeat almost but not quite, and never varying. They make no sound you can hear alone, but together their noises contribute to the hum — a faint slurring whistle, a rasp, a breath of something — and you cannot separate their voices from the rasp of the plants
