The water is alive with things that have no names. They are single-celled, mostly; some form chains; some have tiny whips of flagellum that beat in patterns no larger than the wavelength of light. They are photosynthetic, mostly, or they are not. They bloom and die in cycles that have not yet settled into seasons — when one dies it sinks as a faint smear of dust; when millions bloom together they tint the water toward green or rust or, in one observed cycle, a faintly purple bloom that lasted three weeks and then collapsed. They are the actual fabric of life here — the air, the climate, the weather of this drowned place. A swimmer moving slowly through a trough would feel the water alive against the skin without being able to see any particular reason for it.

Above the microbial weather, in the upper ten or fifteen meters where light still has some clarity, the larger plants have taken hold. They are called kelp here, though they are not quite kelp. Their holdfasts grip the sharp ridges of half-finished mountains; their stipes trail downward in curtains that anchor near the surface and reach toward the troughs, bending in currents so weak the movement is barely a property of them at all. The smaller plants grow on the leaves of the larger plants, and smaller still grow on those, in a kind of inverted forest whose canopy hangs downward. The slow creak of stipes bending under their own weight is one of the only sounds the place makes. On the peaks that pierce the surface — the basalt teeth, the granite spines — things that look like terrestrial shrubs cluster in the cracks, their leaves thick and dark, sometimes flowering in colors that do not quite match the spectrum any local pollinator would prefer. There are no pollinators here yet. The flowers will have to wait for someone to come admire them.

The plants live here with the confidence of an organism that knows it is the dominant life. They are not tentative. They do not behave like early colonizers. Their confidence is, perhaps, a property of being plant — the patience of a thing that does not have to move, and that has therefore not yet noticed that it arrived too recently to be where it is.

The animals are sparse, and the ones that exist are small.

What look like shrimp flick through the curtains and are gone. Their bodies are mostly transparent; the dark thread of their gut is visible through their sides; on the larger ones the eyes reflect green when the light catches them at certain angles. They are probably feeding — on the smaller plants, on the microorganisms, on whatever passes within reach of their appendages. A few of them make the small clicks that the water absorbs: not language, not echolocation, just the sound of a small hard body meeting another small hard body in a place that is otherwise almost silent. They leave briefly visible trails in the silt when they disturb a ledge. A swimmer moving slowly through a trough might see four or five in an hour, and conclude, incorrectly, that the place is empty. They are sparse in absolute terms, but in the terms of this place they are the most visible life it has produced.

There are translucent fish no longer than a finger. They school, sometimes, in groups of six or eight, drifting through the troughs at depths that put them below most of the curtain. Their fins are like tissue paper. Their eyes, when you can see them, are disproportionately large for their heads, suggesting they live in a world where vision matters more than speed. They feed on the flickers, probably, or on the smaller plants, or on the microorganisms. No one has catalogued their diet, because no one has catalogued anything here, yet. They are not numerous. A passing shadow might flush a school from a ledge; you would see them go, briefly, in a coordinated pulse, and then they would settle again into the dim.

And then there is the small fish.

The one on the ledge no one will ever name.

It has been hovering in the same spot, by the look of it, since morning. Its body is the length of a child's finger, perhaps a little longer. It is opaque rather than translucent, which is unusual for a fish this size in water this clear; its color is a brownish gold that takes the green-gilt light of the surface and renders it warmer than the surroundings — a small warm thing in a place that is mostly green and grey and the dark color of wet rock. Its eye is dark. Its mouth opens and closes in a rhythm that has nothing to do with breathing; it is tasting the water, perhaps, or it is doing what fish do when they are not doing anything else.

Its fins work — not continuously, but in small adjustments, tiny flicks that keep it positioned against the current. The current is weak. It would not need to do this if it were patient enough to drift. But it does it anyway. It is, in some way, claiming the ledge; or it is simply the kind of animal that holds position; or it is doing both, because the two are not mutually exclusive.

Nothing here would eat it. Nothing here is large enough to. It might, if it lives long enough, become large enough to eat the flickers that pass through the curtains — a kind of ecological arrival, a thing finally filling a niche that the geology opened. Or it might not. The conditions are slightly off for animals: too warm in some seasons, the oxygen stratified in ways that do not quite match what a fish would prefer. It is here against the odds.

It has no name. No one has ever watched it long enough to give it one.

It is just one of the few things here that has decided, against the odds, to live in this place; and it does so without ceremony, in the green-gilt water, between the sharp ridges of a mountain range that has not finished becoming a mountain range before the water closed over it.
