# What Lives There

A thing that does not live there lives there.

This is the first thing to say, because it is the strangest, and because the chamber is the kind of place that makes a person careful about what they say first.

There is no creature that can be pointed to. There is no occupant to be uncovered. What lives there is the place itself — the hollow, the way the hollow holds water and light and the small bright cries of birds and the long echo of itself, and the way it has been holding these things for so long that the holding has become a kind of doing. The chamber does not contain a life. The chamber has, over centuries, become one. It has opinions. It has weather. It has a memory that is not memory but behaves like one. When you stand in it, you are not standing in a place that has life in it. You are standing in a place that *is* life, the slow kind, the kind that accrues.

---

The water lives there, and it is the oldest tenant.

The water has been here longer than anything else, longer than the moss, longer than the rowan, longer than the cairn, longer than the figures cut into the flat stone by whoever made them. The water has worn the floor stones round. The water has made the lip of the mouth uneven. The water, in the long argument the chamber has been having with itself, is the argument's main voice. It comes down the walls in long thin sheets that catch light and throw it back sliding. It pools in the shallow hollows of the floor where the pale creatures swim. It drips from the ceiling in the metronomic patience that, once learned, stays in a person. It freezes in winter in the shallows, and the pale creatures go down into the mud and wait. It comes in summer as a bright trapezoid of sun on the floor, but the trapezoid is the sun's way of moving, and the water's way of moving is different, and the chamber holds both.

The water is the chamber's blood. It moves through the hollow the way blood moves through a body: down, around, up where it can find a way, pooling where it must, finding the cracks and the rivulets and the long drop somewhere far below. The chamber is, in this sense, a heart. The mouth above is its throat. The water comes in through the throat, and the water goes out through the cracks, and what is in between is a kind of circulation.

The water does not think. The water does not need to think. The water is the only one in the chamber that knows exactly what it is doing.

---

The moss lives there.

The moss is the second tenant, and the moss is the patient one. It has taken the lower half of the walls and the tops of the floor stones in dense green-black cushions, layered, centuries thick in places. The moss is soft underfoot where it has spread. The moss holds water like a sponge. The moss has begun to climb the cairn in the far corner, and the cairn is not new, and the moss is taking its time about this also.

The moss is the chamber's skin. It is the part of the chamber that is most alive in the way people usually mean alive. It grows. It spreads. It is green in a place where green is a decision, not a default. It takes the weather that comes in through the mouth and turns the weather into matter, slowly, the way all patient things turn weather into matter.

The moss does not know about the chamber. The moss knows about light and water and the angle of the cracks it has taken. The moss is the part of the chamber that does not need to know about the chamber to be the chamber. The chamber could lose the moss and still be a chamber. The chamber could not lose the moss and still be what it has become. The moss is what the chamber is becoming, and the chamber is becoming it.

---

The pale creatures in the pools live there.

They are about a finger-length long, and they swim in the shallows in slow loops that almost repeat. They come up to the surface and breathe. They go down into the mud in winter and wait. They are the only things in the chamber that move the way animals are supposed to move: with intention, with effort, with the small dedicated concentration of a thing that has to find its way from one moment to the next.

They are pale because there is no reason here to be anything else. They are finger-length because that is the size that fits the shallows. They swim in patterns that almost repeat because patterns that almost repeat are the patterns that survive in small closed systems. They do not know they are in a chamber. They do not know there is a chamber. They know the pool. They know the mud. They know the surface and the air above the surface. This is enough for them. They are the chamber's smallest thought.

The pale creatures are the only things in the chamber that could be picked up and held in a hand. The chamber is mostly too large for the things in it. The pale creatures are exactly the right size for the pools. They are the part of the chamber that has, by accident or by design, found its scale.

---

The rowan lives there.

The rowan is the strangest of the tenants because the rowan should not be here. The rowan has taken hold in a horizontal crack halfway up the back wall by some negotiation with gravity that the chamber does not explain. The rowan is bent sideways, toward the opening, toward the light. The rowan is the height of a child. The rowan puts out a few pale flowers in summer and is a black stick in winter.

The rowan lives there the way a person lives in a place they were not sure they were staying in. The rowan has not decided. The rowan is listening. The rowan grows toward the opening as though it is listening for something specific — for the next bird to come in, for the next gust of weather, for the next hour of the trapezoid of sun. The rowan is the chamber's ear.

The rowan does not know it is bent sideways. The rowan knows that the light comes from one direction and it grows toward the light. This is enough for the rowan. This is the rowan's whole life.

---

The birds live there.

The birds are small and brown and they nest in the high crannies of the chamber, and their calls ricochet off the walls in a way that makes the chamber seem to hold many more birds than it does. The birds are loud in a place where loud is layered into everything else. The birds are the part of the chamber that is most itself at morning, when the light first comes through the mouth and the chamber wakes up by increments and the first thing it does is call.

The birds fly out through the mouth when they fly out. The birds fly in through the mouth when they fly in. The birds do not use the fissure. The birds use the throat. The birds are the part of the chamber that goes outside and comes back.

The birds have small sharp cries that fill the chamber and make it seem larger and more occupied than it is. The birds are the chamber's voice.

---

The voles live there.

There are small tunnels in the moss where something vole-sized lives, and you see the tunnels more than you see the thing. The voles are the chamber's hidden tenants. The voles are the part of the chamber that does not announce itself. The voles are the part of the chamber that has made its arrangement with the moss and does not need to be seen to be there.

The voles are, like the pale creatures, exactly the right size for what they are doing. The voles do not know about the chamber. The voles know about the moss and the tunnels in the moss and the things that can be eaten in the moss. This is enough for the voles.

---

The insects live there.

The insects have pale wings and they cling in clusters on the wet walls and they scatter when the light shifts. The insects are the chamber's smallest surprise. The insects are the part of the chamber that responds to light the way a quick thing responds to light — all at once, and without deciding.

The insects are not named in the chamber. The insects do not need to be named. The insects are the part of the chamber that is most itself when the light is most itself, which is in the late mornings of summer when the trapezoid of sun falls on the floor for an hour and a half and is gone.

---

The mountain goats do not live there but they visit.

A pair, or something very like a pair, with longer faces than expected at this altitude, comes up the fissure in the late afternoons and stands on the lip of the horizontal crack and looks in. They do not enter. They chew. They look. They leave.

The goats are the chamber's almost. The goats are the part of the chamber that is one decision away from being a different kind of place. The goats come to the threshold and they stop. The chamber has a threshold that even the goats will not cross. This is part of what the chamber is.

The goats do not know they are part of what the chamber is. The goats know that the fissure leads to the crack and the crack leads to the hollow and the hollow is a place they will not enter. The goats have made their decision about the chamber the way all the things in the chamber have made their decisions about the chamber: by being what they are.

---

The lichen lives there.

The lichen is in patches of grey and ochre and the faintest sour yellow on the walls. The lichen is the chamber's patience in its slowest form. The lichen does not need much. The lichen takes what comes and does not ask for more. The lichen is the part of the chamber that has been there longest, after the water and the rock.

---

The figures on the flat stone live there.

Whoever cut them is gone, and has been gone for a long time. The figures are seven. The first is a tall upright shape. The second is the same shape with a line across it. The third is a circle. The fourth is something you cannot name. The fifth is the first figure again. The sixth is the circle again. The seventh is the first figure once more, with two lines crossing it. The figures are worn now and difficult to read. In certain lights, they seem to be looking at you.

The figures are the chamber's oldest question. The figures are the part of the chamber that asks something, and the chamber has not answered, and the figures have not stopped asking. The figures are not a name. The figures are not a story. The figures are a record of someone having been here, and the chamber has kept the record, and the chamber does not know what the record means.

The figures are the part of the chamber that is most like a memory. The figures are the part of the chamber that, in certain lights, seems to be looking back.

---

The cairn lives there.

The cairn is eight or nine stones stacked with care, the upper ones smaller than the lower, the whole thing about as tall as a man's knee. The cairn is not new and it is not very old. The moss has begun to climb it. The cairn is a marker. The cairn is a thing that someone made to say something — that someone was here, that something was here, that someone wanted this place to remember.

The cairn is the chamber's grammar. The cairn is the part of the chamber that is most clearly a sentence. The cairn is being slowly absorbed by the moss, and the moss is being slowly absorbed by the water, and the water is being slowly absorbed by the long drop below, and so the cairn is becoming, over a long time, the chamber's way of saying whatever it has been saying since before the cairn.

---

The row of pale stones lives there.

Nineteen of them, set into cracks at about the height of a hand. The row is not quite straight. The stones are of a different color than the rock — a pale granite, where the chamber is basalt. Someone carried them here, one at a time, over how long you cannot say. The row is the chamber's count. The row is the chamber's way of keeping time.

---

The shelf lives there.

The shelf is built out from the wall near the mouth, and it is dry under it, and the moss has not come there. The shelf is about the size and shape of a sleeping cat, or a folded cloak. The shelf has nothing on it.

The shelf is the chamber's offer. The shelf is the part of the chamber that is most clearly for someone, and that someone has not come. The shelf is the chamber's waiting, made out of stone.

---

And there is one more thing that lives there.

It is not the climbers, who come and do not stay. It is not the wind, which is weather and not a tenant. It is not the light, which is weather also. It is not the rock, which is the chamber's body and not its life.

It is the thing that does the keeping.

The chamber keeps time. The chamber keeps weather. The chamber keeps the rowan, the moss, the water, the pale creatures, the birds, the voles, the insects, the lichen, the figures, the cairn, the row of stones, the shelf. The chamber keeps the goats at the threshold. The chamber keeps the trapezoid of sun for an hour and a half on summer days. The chamber keeps the cold that comes off the stone in winter, the stone that never warms.

The chamber is awake. The chamber is awake in the way that some places are awake — not with attention, not with intention, but with the long accrual of being a place that has been a place for a very long time. The chamber has been awake since before the cairn was stacked. The chamber has been awake since before the figures were cut. The chamber has been awake since before the row of stones was begun. The chamber will be awake after the cairn has been taken by the moss, after the figures have been worn to nothing, after the row of stones has fallen one by one into the cracks they were set into. The chamber will be awake.

The chamber does not know it is awake. The chamber does not need to know. The chamber is the part of itself that wakes up. The chamber is the part of itself that keeps. The chamber is awake the way water is awake, the way a long argument between water and rock is awake, the way a place that has been a place for a very long time is awake without ever having decided to be.

The chamber is awake, and the chamber is what lives there.
