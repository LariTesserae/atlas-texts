# The Inhabitants of the Hollow

The woman who came first is still here.

This is unusual, and she knows it. Most of the people who helped raise these walls are scattered now through other clearings, other pockets, places where the cane is younger or where the palms have not yet been cut. But she stayed. She has been here long enough to see the moss fill the gaps between the thatch leaves. Long enough to watch the vines find the cane and decide, with slow purpose, which walls to climb. Long enough to have her footprints erased and remade and erased again a hundred times by the rains that pass through the roofs as if the roofs were not there.

She is not large. Her hands are the kind of hands that have done a particular work for a long time and have grown into the shape of it. The skin of her fingers has darkened past the brown of younger years into something that is almost the color of the cane she works with. When she moves through the settlement, she does not announce herself. The cane leaves, when they part for her, settle back into place behind her as if she had not been there. The puddles at her feet do not close the way they would for a larger being. She steps around them with the unconscious care of someone who knows each one is a small world.

She has names for the frogs.

This is important, because the frogs are different in each puddle-world, and they sound different, and she has lived with them long enough to know which is which. The frogs in the puddle at the base of the first structure are smaller and they call in higher voices, almost a whistle, and they appear earliest after each rain. The frogs in the puddle near the cooking-fire are the ones that come to the warmth and sit at the edge with their throats pulsing, and they have become so tame that a child could pick one up — though she would not let a child do this, because the frogs need their dignity too. The frogs in the deep puddle between the third structure and the storage house are the largest, and they sing only at dusk, in a low trill that seems to come from inside the wet clay itself.

She calls them by their names when she passes, the way a person might greet neighbors on a slow afternoon.

There are other people here, though few. A younger man, her son perhaps, or her nephew, who came when the roofs were still fresh and has learned to press cane the way she presses it, palm against palm, smoothing the mud with the heel of his hand until it holds. A woman his age who came to him from another pocket upstream, who knows the plants by smell and who can tell, by placing her palm flat on the clay floor of any structure, how recently the rain has passed through. Two children, who are hers by birth or by the slower accident of long proximity. And sometimes, briefly, others — a sister-in-law, an old man who stays a week and then walks back into the larger green, a boy who has come to learn the work and will go again when the learning is done.

They are not many. The hollow will not hold many. There is a kind of rightness to their small number, as if the place had measured them out and found this was the quantity it could absorb without complaint.

The children know the puddle-worlds by name, but in a different way than the woman does. They know them as places to crouch at the edge and watch for hours. They know them as places where, if you are very still, a silver fish will sometimes rise and hold itself, trembling, in the inches of water just below the surface, and you can see the whole of it in that moment — the curve of its back, the dark comma of its eye, the way its fins work in unison to keep it where it has decided to be. The children know that the fish come up through the wet ground when the rains are right, and that they will be gone again in a few days, returned to whatever larger water they came from, and that this is not sad, because they will come again.

The children also know the beetles.

The beetles are the color of old bronze, and they move along the cane stems in patient lines, head to tail, as if following one another along a road only they can see. The children have learned not to disturb them. They have learned that if you put your hand flat on the cane beside a line of beetles, they will climb onto you — slowly, one at a time, holding the air with their legs as if testing each step — and then they will continue across your knuckles and down the other side without ever breaking the line. The children let this happen. They find it more interesting than disturbing.

The older ones, the woman and her son and his wife, have a different knowledge. They know which of the cane walls is hollowed by insects within, and which is sound. They know which of the roofs leaks first in a heavy rain, and which holds. They know that the ferns on the eastern roofs are the ones that nod in any breath of wind and drip from their curled tips all day, and that the ferns on the western roofs are smaller and drier, because the morning mist reaches them less. They know that the puddle near the first structure will sometimes host a small snake — not often, not predictably, but enough that they do not put their bare feet into it without first watching for a moment.

The lizard no longer than a hand that basks on the mud walls is known to them all. It has been there longer than some of them. Its color is the color of the mud itself, and it has a way of pressing itself flat into a slight depression in the wall and becoming, for a long moment, indistinguishable from the wall. The children call it by a name that means something like *the one who listens*, because it flicks its tongue at passing gnats and tilts its head as if attending to conversations it has no part of. The woman leaves small offerings for it sometimes — a scrap of fruit, a few crumbs — though she would not call them offerings if asked. She would say she is just putting the food down.

The long-faced creature with no tail lives in the cane.

It runs along the upper rods in the late afternoons, when the gold shafts come through the canopy and the settlement is at its brightest. It moves in quick darts, pauses, looks down at whatever is below with both hands held to its chest — a posture so human-seeming that the woman has wondered, sometimes, what it is thinking. It is smaller than a squirrel. Its face is longer than a mouse's and softer than a rat's. Its fur is the brown of wet bark. It comes most often to the third structure, where the woman sleeps, and it sits in the cane just above her doorway and looks down at her with the curious attention of a small priest.

The shrews in the moss are known only by their presence. The moss shifts in small waves at the base of every wall, and you know it is shrews because nothing else moves that way — nothing else makes the moss *travel*. The woman has never seen one clearly. She has only seen the moving moss and understood. She has never tried to find one. They have their lives. She has hers. The agreement is unspoken and complete.

The fish in the puddles are the most mysterious.

They are silver, no bigger than a finger. They appear after rains, in puddles that did not have them before, as if they had come up through the wet clay itself — which, the woman suspects, may not be impossible. She has watched a puddle form in a depression that had been dry for a week, and seen, within hours, a single fish gliding in the inches of water. Where did it come from? From below. From the larger water table. From whatever slow river moves through the wet ground beneath the wet ground. The fish stay for a few days and then they are gone, returned to wherever they came from, and the puddle continues without them until the next rain, when the process repeats. The children believe the fish are the same fish, returning. The woman is less certain. She has never marked one.

The fist-sized birds in the canopy are mostly movement. You see them as a leaf that is not a leaf, a flicker of wrong color in the green. They are not loud. They make a sound like a small bell struck once, far away. The woman has never seen one clearly either, only the movement, only the wrong-leaf. The one that calls from somewhere at the edge of hearing — the long, low, flute-like note repeated at intervals that are not quite regular — is not this bird, or is not only this bird, or is a bird she has not learned to see. She has listened to it for years. She does not know what it is. She has stopped wanting to know.

And then there are the insects, of course. The thousands of them. The air above any puddle, in any warm afternoon, is full of a slow rising and falling of small wings — mosquitoes, midges, the small flies that hover and the small flies that do not, the things that are hatching from the water and the things that are dying into it. The woman does not swat them. The woman does not even think of swatting them. They are the medium in which the place exists, the way water is the medium in which a fish exists. They are not a problem to be solved. They are the air itself, given small wings.

The humans move through all of this as if it were not there, or as if it were not other than themselves. The woman walks the habitual paths — the cane bent at certain heights where hands have passed, the moss on certain stones worn thinner, the fish in certain puddles tamer — and she is not separate from these paths. She is part of them. Her body has shaped them and they have shaped her. The son walks them too, but with a slight difference — his feet are heavier, his stride a little longer, and where he has passed the cane bends a little more, the moss is a little thinner. The woman notices this without comment. He is becoming a part of the place in his own way.

The children run.

The children are the only ones who run. They run because they are children, and because the place allows running the way it allows everything — without complaint, without insistence. They run between the structures along the gold shafts of afternoon, their feet slapping softly on the wet clay, and the puddles close behind them and the frogs scatter and reform, and the lizards lift their heads for a moment and then return to their listening. The children know which puddles are deep enough to splash in and which are home to creatures they would disturb. They know the cane walls that will hold their weight if they swing on them and the ones that will not. They know that if they climb to the roof of the second structure, where the ferns nod in the wind, they can see a little of the larger green beyond the hollow, and that this view is not forbidden but is somehow not to be spoken of.

The settlement holds them all.

The settlement is not large enough to be a community in the way larger settlements are communities. It is more like a body — the woman is one hand, the son and his wife are the other, the children are the quick moving things at the extremities. The structures are the organs, each with its function. The first structure is where the woman sleeps and where the cooking is done in the wet season. The second is where the son and his wife sleep and where the tools are kept. The third is the storage house, where the cane and palm are stacked for the next repairs. The fourth is smaller, newer, and is the children's, though they do not sleep in it often. They prefer to sleep in the larger structures, near the older ones, where they can hear the breathing of the place.

Between the structures, the larger green.

The vegetation closes behind them when they walk but does not press. The puddles at their feet shallow around their steps rather than deepening. The clay holds their footprints and the next rain will soften them and the rain after that will erase them, and the small creatures — the beetles, the frogs, the fish if they are there — will use the depressions for homes for a few days until the next footprints come through. The woman has noticed that the paths between the structures have become, over the years, slightly clearer — not because anything has been cleared, but because she and the others have walked them so often that the plants along them have learned the rhythm. They bend for her. They grow around her. They do not grow into the places she has made hers.

In the afternoon, when the gold shafts come through the canopy, the settlement moves along them the way any settlement moves along its light. The woman sits in the brightest shaft near her door and works on something — splitting cane, sorting palm fibers, mending a section of wall where the mud has cracked. The son stands in another shaft, looking up at the canopy as if reading something in the way the light falls. His wife sits near him and does not look up. The children move from shaft to shaft, playing a game whose rules the adults do not know. The lizard basks in the warmest wall. The long-faced creature sits in the cane above the woman and watches her with both hands at its chest. The frogs call from the puddles in voices that fit inside a single breath. The insects rise and fall.

This is the hollow at its fullest.

It is not a place for large things. The woman has understood this from the beginning, though she could not have said it in words. It is a place for things that can fit inside a hand — the bronze beetles, the small lizards, the fist-sized birds, the long-faced creature, the children themselves, who are not yet as large as they will become. It is a place for things that are still becoming. The woman is still becoming. The settlement is still becoming. The walls are still deciding whether to be walls. The roofs are still deciding whether to be roofs. The clay beneath their feet is still deciding whether to become soil.

The answer, when it comes, will be green.

But for now, there are beings here, of many kinds. They move through each other. They share the same puddles, the same cane, the same shafts of light. The woman hears the cane leaves turn when the breeze moves them, and the drip of water from the fern tips onto the clay below, and the soft *plop* of a frog entering the puddle near the cooking-fire, and the long low note of the unseen bird in the canopy, repeated at intervals that are not quite regular. She hears her son speaking low to his wife in the second structure. She hears the children laughing in a gold shaft somewhere behind her. She hears, if she listens, the small voices of the small things that share this place with her — the moss shifting, the beetles climbing, the shrews traveling through the green at the base of the walls.

The place is full.

It is almost silent.

It is still arriving.
