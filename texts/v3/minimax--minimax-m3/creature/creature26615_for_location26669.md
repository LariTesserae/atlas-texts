The lichens live first. They have always lived first. They are the citizens who arrived when the rock was still raw, still black with the wetness of its breaking, and began the slow, polite work of making it something else. A lichen is not one thing; it is two, and sometimes three, wedded into a single body that is neither plant nor mineral but a kind of third kingdom, an agreement. The fungus gives the shape. It braids itself into a crust, or a small pale shrub no taller than a fingernail, or a sheet so thin that you have to look sideways at the rock to see it. The alga — and sometimes a yeast — gives the food. It sits inside the fungus the way a tenant sits inside a landlord, photosynthesizing in the thin brightness that filters down through the cloud, making sugars from air and water and whatever pale rumor of sun the overcast permits. The agreement holds. The lichen eats the stone. It does this so slowly that no instrument can mark the progress from one year to the next, but it does it, decade after decade, until the rock beneath has been thinned to a faint smear of mineral dust and the lichen has, by remaining, become the first soil.

The lichens paint the basin. They are the ochre and the umber and the gray-white the original traveler saw. Look closely — the closer you look the more species there are — and you will find crusts that are pale as bone, and foliose lobes that lift at their edges like tiny pages turning, and fruticose tufts that nod like miniature manes. Each one is a small patient settlement, two or three millimeters thick, feeding on a single square centimeter of stone. A square meter of rock may hold a hundred of them. A square meter of older rock may hold a thousand. They do not fight. They divide the rock between them, slowly, by the work of their acids, by the work of their patience, by what each species prefers about the stone it lives on.

Where the lichens have softened the stone enough, the mosses follow. The mosses are the city's body. They are the part of the city you could put your hand into, and they would give under the pressure and spring back after, holding the shape of your absence for a while, the way a sponge holds the shape of the hand that has squeezed it. Each clump is a colony — a thicket of tiny upright stems no longer than your smallest finger, every stem bearing a whorl of leaves so small you would need a glass to see them properly. The leaves are not green so much as the memory of green, a green steeped so long in shadow it has become almost black, almost olive, almost the color of the wet rock they stand on. A moss is a water-holder. Each stem is a sponge of itself; each leaf-cup, where it joins the stem, holds a meniscus; each clump is a reservoir.

And because the moss holds water, and water holds the cold, and the cold makes growth slow, the moss is in no hurry to be anything except moss. It thickens its mound by a millimeter this year and another next year. It puts out its spores in some season you could not name from inside the cloud — fine capsules on thin stalks, nodding, that open in a dry hour and hurl their dust outward, most of which lands on the wrong rock, but some of which lands on the right rock, and from which a new colony begins. Mosses also move by fragmentation. A piece of a stem broken off by a heel or a melting lump of ice will, if it lands somewhere damp, take root. They colonize by accident and survive by being everywhere already.

The small ferns grow where the mosses have held for a while. They are rarer. They are more particular. Each fern is a single plant — a rosette of fronds coming up from a knot of brown roots wedged into a crack — and the fronds themselves are the city's quieter ornaments, not walls but the brackets and corbels that hang from them. A fern frond is a slow unrolling. In some spring-equivalent of this dim and seasonless place it begins as a tightly coiled fiddlehead, the whole future leaf wound into itself like a watch-spring. Then it uncurls, over days, over weeks, pushing outward in a continuous spiral, the tip always the youngest part, always the most tender, the older parts already hardening into the color of the stone around them. When the unrolling is done the frond stands at its full height — a hand-span, maybe two — and begins its long working life of photosynthesizing in whatever light the cloud permits, which is not much, but is enough. Beneath each fern, on the spore-bearing undersides of older fronds, are the sori: small brown rosettes of cases that, when ripe, will split and scatter the next generation into the wet air. Most will land where nothing can use them. Some will land in a moss-cup already half full of water and begin, in that warm dark wetness, the long work of becoming a prothallus, and then of becoming a fern.

The rosette plants are the city's plumbers. They are the small, blunt herbs with thick waxy leaves pressed flat to the stone, dark as a bruise, slick with a cuticle that sheds water the way a duck's back sheds it. Each plant is a wheel of leaves around a single growing point, the leaves overlapping like the petals of a green flower that has decided not to open. The leaves are pitched, slightly, toward their own centers, so that any drop that lands on them slides inward and collects at the rosette's heart, in the cup the plant has made of itself. The plant drinks from its cup. When the cup overflows the water spills outward and joins the next rosette's overflow and the next, so that the plants together form a system of cups and spillways — a kind of living cistern-work — and the system is redundant, which is why it has lasted: if one rosette dies the next still holds the water, still passes it on.

The woody shrubs are the city's few vertical lines. They are the only things here that mean upward, that have any opinion about height. There is a meter of stem, perhaps a meter and a half, and the stem is dark and crooked and bears leaves that are the deep-water green of the original traveler's description, almost black, almost blue. The shrubs grow at the rate of a fingernail per year, more or less. They put out a leaf, or a short branch, or a small dark flower — probably pollinated by something you will not see this year — and they do not race themselves. A shrub is the oldest thing here, in its way, because it is the slowest, and slowness is a kind of continuity. The shrub that took twenty years to reach this height will, if it is not broken, take twenty more to reach the next, and when it dies — and something, some day, will lean against it and snap it, or ice will pry it open, or a wet rot will soften its base — a small woody stem will lie along the rock and begin its long second life as a nurse-log, a habitation for the next cohort of mosses and liverworts and beetles.

The fauna is a rumor. It passes through.

A spider has built a small retreat in the dry interior of a moss-clump — a tent of silk so fine it is almost invisible — and inside the tent she sits with her egg-sac, motionless in the cold, occasionally catching a small fly that has blundered in from the outside air. The fly is, itself, only passing through; it came up from a lower valley on some warm afternoon and got caught by the cold and now is eaten by a spider that will eat almost nothing else this year. A small brown bird, no bigger than a fist, no brighter than the rock, has come down from somewhere higher and is hopping along a ledge, head tilted, looking. It looks for a long time. It is looking for something to eat. It does not find much. It is too cold for insects in any abundance, and the seeds here are few and slow to ripen, and the bird will be gone in an hour, back up to where the air is drier and the food is sparser but the cold is not so wet. A creeping thing, slug-slow, slides along a stone face and is gone before you can name it — a mollusk of some kind, or a larval salamander, or a thing without a common name. It eats the films of algae. A beetle, perhaps, in the rotting heart of a fallen stem, tunneling with the patience of its kind. A worm, perhaps, in the thinnest smear of soil that has begun, here and there, in a sheltered crack. These are the animals. They are the commas and semicolons of the city. The plants are the sentences.

But beneath the visible lives there are other lives, and they are the majority by any honest count. The fungi are everywhere. They thread the moss and the lichen and the dead stem and the living root, a web of white threads so fine that no eye, unaided, can see it. The fungi are the city's rot. They take what the plants have made and unmake it again, returning the carbon to the air and the minerals to the stone. Without them the city would choke on its own dead; without them the rock would never become soil. They are quiet. They are patient. They do not announce themselves. They are the most numerous citizens in the city, and the most overlooked, and the most necessary, and they have been here longer than any of the plants, because the fungi were here before the plants were — they were here in the form of the lichen-fungi, which were the first colonizations, the first weddings of mineral and light.

And there are the bacteria, in every film of water, on every wet surface, in the gut of every worm and beetle. There are the algae that are not part of any lichen, living free in the seams of wet rock, painting the stone a darker green than the mosses do. There are the tardigrades, perhaps — the water-bears — eight-legged and microscopic, drying to a husk in any dry hour and swelling back to life in the next wet one, eating the algae, being eaten by nothing you can see. There are the rotifers, wheel-animals, in the leaf-cups. There are the nematodes, thread-thin, in every gram of wet moss. There is a city beneath the city, and a city beneath that, and the basin is, by volume, more life than stone.

Water is a citizen too. Water is, in some sense, *the* citizen. It is the thing that joins all the others. A drop that falls on the highest leaf will, before it is anywhere else, sit in a leaf-cup, and evaporate, and become vapor, and drift inward, and condense on a colder surface, and become a drop again. Or it will run down a stem and join a rill, and the rill will thread under the moss, and emerge somewhere you can see and join a sheet, and the sheet will join the central rill, and the central rill will go down into the dark, into the broken rock, and become, briefly, groundwater. And then it will rise again, drawn up by capillary into the moss, into the next rain or the next dew, and the cycle will not have ended, only bent, only passed through another body on its round.

And cold is a citizen. The cold is the city's law. It is the thing that says which lives are allowed and which are not, that presses the moss flat to the rock, that slows the shrub to the pace of stone, that holds the water in its trembling mid-state all day, deciding whether to be ice. Cold is patient and indifferent and very old and very precise. It has decided, by its presence here, that nothing warm-blooded will thrive, that nothing with ambition will last. It has decided that the city's only residents will be those who do not mind being slow.

So the city is, finally, this: the slow accretion of small lives on cold wet stone, each life holding to the others by the medium of water and the patience of cold. It is not a city you would visit for company. It is not a city where anything happens quickly. It is a city you would visit, if you were wise, to learn the long version of being alive — to sit on the edge for an hour and watch the drops fall, and listen to the hush that is full of small things, and feel in your own bones the slowness of a place that has decided, in its dumb and thorough way, to be very entirely itself.
