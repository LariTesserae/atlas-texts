The residents are few, and the counting of them is itself a careful practice. Most agree on nine — nine figures who can be spoken to and who will, after a small delay, turn their heads. Nine bodies that still carry the weight of being bodies in the conventional way, that still remember, however distantly, what it was to eat and to grow warmer in a sun that did not exist here. Beyond these nine the population frays. There are the doorframe leaners, who rest their shoulders in the angle of stone and watch the street with the patience of long looking. There are the stair sitters, who have found, on one of the city's more reliable flights, a step they return to night after night and which returns to them. There are the almost-people — shapes seen against a far wall in the deeper hours that, approached, are only the pale backs of the fauna, or the long shadows of nothing in particular, or the city's own slow drift given momentary form. The residents do not dispute the count. They have learned not to. They have learned, instead, to greet whatever shape presents itself with the same small tilt of the shoulders, and to wait and see whether the tilt is returned, and to take the return, when it comes, as confirmation enough.

Their thinness is the first thing. They are very thin, in the way that the dressed stone is thin in places, as if the city and its people had been pared by the same patient hand. Their faces have lengthened. The skin has drawn close to the architecture beneath it, and in the older ones — and most of them are older, though no one marks the years here — the cheekbones and brow ridges have become a kind of topography one could read, the way one reads the city's own walls. They are pale in the way the stone is pale, in the way the fauna are pale, in the way the films are pale. This is not the pallor of illness. It is the pallor of having had only certain things to eat, and only the cool air to drink, and only the dim pewter light to grow by, for so long that the body has come to match its surroundings as a kind of courtesy.

They move slowly. Not from weakness — though they are weak — but because slow movement is a kind of negotiation with the geometry. A step taken too quickly is a step that may not arrive where it was aimed. A pace measured against the walls is a pace that finds its way home. They have developed a gait the visitors, when there are visitors, mistake for reverence. It is not reverence. It is the careful pacing of someone who has learned, by long correction, that the floor cannot always be trusted to be where it was a moment ago. To walk here is to ask the city, with every step, where it would prefer you to be. The city, sometimes, answers.

Their eyes are pale. They have the quality of looking at something slightly to the side of what they are seeing. This is not dishonesty; it is adaptation. To look directly at a thing here is to invite the thing to slip. A resident looking at a wall may, on the periphery, be tracking a door that wasn't there a moment ago. A resident addressing another resident will rest their gaze on a point just past the shoulder, in the air, in the small neutral space where the geometry can settle. Children, had there been any in living memory, would have learned this first. None have been born here for a long time. The youngest of the residents arrived from below, and even they cannot say how long ago that was with any precision. There is, perhaps, one resident who was a child here once, before the third stratum stopped being built, who remembers, distantly, what it was to run. They do not run now. They have, perhaps, run their last.

Their clothing, when they have it, is mostly a matter of fabric that has been repaired so many times it has become a kind of geological layer itself — multiple strata of patch over patch, the colors long gone to a uniform pale grey, the stitching done in fine mineral grit when no thread is to be had. Some of them wear the films. They peel the films carefully from the upper walls and drape them across their shoulders, where they adhere of their own volition and continue to grow there, slowly, in patterns that the wearer can feel as a faint tightening of the skin. The films do not seem to mind being worn. They may, in fact, prefer it. Several residents go wrapped in them like thin pale cloaks, and in the deeper hours, when the phosphorescence climbs the unfinished walls of the third stratum, these residents glow faintly themselves, as though they had been writing themselves in their own slow handwriting for a long time.

Their days are not days. The light here does not rise or set; it merely thickens and thins. They have, instead, the slow routines of the body, which they have learned to honor because honoring them is all that stands between themselves and the unreliability. They wake, eventually, when the air has the right quality of dim. They do not eat first — there is rarely enough to make eating the first act — but they sit, in the reliable corners, and take small bites of whatever has been gathered: a crumb of pale fauna residue, a flake of dried film, a fragment of the city's own pale powder that gathers in the angles of the rooms and that, held on the tongue for long enough, yields a faint mineral sweetness. They chew for a long time. They swallow carefully. The body's extraction is patient. It is a craft they have all learned, and it is the only craft, and it is enough.

Then there are the routes. The residents maintain, between them, an informal map of the angles of trust — a network of paths that have, by long use, become dependable. These routes avoid the corners that fail to meet. They avoid the doorways that admit to more than they should. They use the streets that, however much the geometry wanders, have learned to return. The map is held in common. It is updated, sometimes overnight, when a building has rotated or a wall has unspooled and reassembled itself elsewhere. The residents compare notes in the morning, in voices that the walls swallow almost before they finish. They speak anyway. The speaking is the point.

The pale fauna are the only other visible life in the city, and they are abundant in the way that small and shy things are abundant when nothing larger has eaten them in a very long time. They are, as has been noted, small — no larger than a handspan, with their long limbs folded. They are pale. They are almost translucent, in the sense that the light passes through them in a way it does not pass through stone, though it does not quite illuminate them either. They have too many joints. The joints are quiet. The fauna do not click or scrape as they move, even when their many-jointed limbs fold through angles that should require sound. They live in the cracks of the city, in the spaces between fitted stones, in the lee of fallen lintels, in the dark undersides of things that have shifted overnight. They are seen, mostly, in the deeper hours.

They do not look directly at anything. This is the first thing one learns about them, and the last. To approach a fauna with direct attention is to watch it fold itself into the nearest shadow and not return. They move, instead, in the periphery. They are at the edge of the vision even when they are in the center of it. Their lines — those faint traces they leave along the stone with the tips of their forelimbs — are visible mainly to those who have agreed not to look at them directly. The residents have learned to glance. The visitors, when there are visitors, have not, and so the visitors see very few of the fauna, and the residents see many, and the city has both populations, and they do not quite intersect.

The lines themselves are not markings anyone can read with confidence. They suggest writing in the same way the films suggest writing. They branch, in places. They repeat, but never quite. There are residents who have spent years trying to learn them, and there are residents who have spent years trying to forget them, and neither group has produced a translation. The lines may not be for reading. They may be the fauna's slow way of keeping the city honest — of tracing, each night, the edges of things, so that when the geometry shifts, something has been noted. Or they may be something else. The fauna do not say. They do not look at anything directly, and they do not say.

The films are a third population, if population is the word. They are not alive in any sense the residents have words for, and yet they spread with what can only be called patience. They grow on the upper walls of the dressed stratum, in the unfinished hollows of the third, in certain chambers of the second that no one enters except by mistake. They are pale. They dry into the stone and emerge from it again, in seasons that have nothing to do with weather, in cycles that have nothing to do with the moon. They are peeled, sometimes, and worn. They are studied, sometimes, by residents who have decided that patience is the only tool left to them. They organize, in the deeper chambers, into something almost resembling writing — characters that hover at the threshold of legibility, repeated but never quite, shifting between visits. The residents do not read them. They have come, over the years, to sit with them. To sit, and wait, and watch the films shift, and to feel, perhaps, that they are being addressed in a language the body might learn before the mind does.
