# What Lives in the Wettrot Reach

They are called, by the few who have a word for them, wetfrots — though the name is a guess that caught on, the way names do for things no one wants to spend too long looking at. They are not frogs. They are not quite anything. They are what the bowl made.

A wetfrot is the length of a forearm and the weight of a loaf of bread. Its body is long in the way that a sausage is long, low to the ground, with no visible neck to speak of — the head simply thickens out of the shoulders the way a knot thickens out of a rope. The legs are short and set wide, the way a lizard's are set, so that when a wetfrot walks its belly nearly touches the stone between its feet. It has four legs. It uses all four. It does not run. It does not need to. There is nothing here it is running from, and nothing here it is running toward.

The coat is what you notice first, and then what you cannot stop noticing. It is the color of wet basalt, the color of the inside of a well, the color of the bowl itself at the hour the bowl is darkest. There is no pattern to it. It is not mottled for camouflage; it is the color of *here*, and here is what they are. The fur is long and coarse and lies in loose ropes along the body, and it holds water the way a sponge holds water — drips for an hour after a rain, beads for half a day, never fully dries. When a wetfrot lies down on a rise and the light is low, you cannot see the wetfrot. You see the rise. You see the wet stone. The wetfrot is the same thing.

The face is blunt and patient. The eyes are small, dark, wet, set forward but not far apart, and they have a quality that is hard to describe without sounding foolish: they have the look of things that have been watching the same view for a very long time and have stopped needing to interpret it. The ears are low and round and lie almost flat against the skull; they swivel, but slowly, the way a weather vane swivels. The nose is the most-used part of the animal. It is broad and always damp and it works constantly, even when the rest of the wetfrot is still. The mouth is a thin line that becomes, when open, a surprising width, and the teeth behind it are small and flat and grind rather than cut. Wetfrots do not eat meat. They do not appear to eat anything you would recognize as food. They crop the coarse grasses down to the nub and they lick the wet stone where the mineral films form, and they swallow gravel, the way birds swallow grit, and that is the whole of what a wetfrot does with its mouth.

They are social in a way that is not quite social. They are *gregarious*, in the older sense of the word — they live together because they live together, not because they have chosen each other. There is no leader among them. There is no structure. There is only the fact of the press of them, the density of them, and within that press, small families — a mother, a father, three or four young of various sizes, pressed flank to flank along a rise like books on a shelf, each one in its own space but each space touching the next. They recognize each other by smell, by the particular warmth of the body beside them, and possibly by sound, though the only sound a wetfrot makes individually is the slow click of its teeth as it grinds, and this is so quiet and so constant that you cannot tell one click from another.

A wetfrot pup — if pup is the word, and there is no better one — is born looking like a wet stone that has somehow come alive. It is the size of a man's fist and it does not open its eyes for the first day, and when it does open them they are the same dark and patient as its parents', and it does not seem surprised by what it sees. Within a week it is moving with the press. Within a month it is indistinguishable from the adults except by size. There is no play. There is no learning period you would recognize as such. There is only the bowl, and the press, and the slow routine of being a wetfrot in a place that has made the wetfrot in its own image.

The routine is this:

They lie along the swells through the long gray hours, packed shoulder to shoulder, in rows that are not rows but that have a row-like quality, as though each animal is following the curve of the rise in a way that was not decided but just is. They grind. They breathe. Their eyes are open and they are watching, and what they are watching is not anything in particular, but the act of watching is constant and unbroken. Sometimes one will stand — slowly, as though the standing is an event it is undertaking for the first time — and step over its neighbors with a care that is not care but simply the geometry of small bodies in close quarters — and walk a few paces to a new spot, and lie down again, and resume. When many of them do this at once, the press shifts, and a slow wave passes across the bowl, and the sound of it is the scrape-and-click the bowl makes when it is moving.

At certain hours they rise and move. The smaller ones go first — always the smaller ones — and they move in the grooves, the shining single-file tracks along the ridgelines, toward the central water. They go down to drink. They go down to lick the stone. They go down because it is the hour for going down, the way the tide is the hour for the tide. They move in a continuous stream, one after another, and the stream is so even and so patient that it looks less like a migration than like a fluid being poured. They reach the water. They drink. They do not linger. The stream reverses, and they go back to their swells, and the bowl resumes its held-breath quiet.

There is no courtship you can see. There are no fights. There is no territory. When a wetfrot dies — and they die, though rarely, and always in the center of the bowl — the others close around the body and the press absorbs it, and within a day there is nothing left to see, only the slight darkening of the stone where the body was, and that fades too. There is no grief because there is no expectation. A wetfrot is born into the press and the press receives it. A wetfrot dies out of the press and the press closes over the place. The bowl is the same bowl before and after. This is not cruel. It is the only way the bowl can be.

In the deep hours, when the twilight has gone as dark as it goes, and the eyes of the wetfrots are showing as the small wet points across the ground that the description has already told you about, and the air has gone cool in the way that goes past cool and into something almost mineral, the wetfrots make the only other sound they make. It is not a call. It is not a song. It is a low continuous vocalization, a hum so low it is felt more than heard, that rises from the press all at once and holds for a span and then fades, and then rises again. The bowl is full of it. The stone vibrates with it. The water in the shallow ribbons trembles in sympathy with it. The grasses stop leaning. It goes on for hours. It is not a sound that means anything. It is the sound the bowl makes when the bowl is being most itself, the way a hive hums when the hive is whole, the way a sea sounds when the sea is being the sea. The wetfrots make it because they are here, and here is a place that makes this sound, and they are the place's instrument.

They have no predators. Nothing comes into the bowl. Nothing has ever come into the bowl. The ridges are too sharp and the footing too uncertain and the air too wet and the press of wetfrots too dense to invite any animal with the instinct for self-preservation. Hawks pass over. Hawks do not land. The wetfrots know this. They do not look up. There is nothing to look up for.

They have no fear. This is the strangest thing about them, and the thing that the few who have stood among them find hardest to bear. They will not move for you. You can stand among them and they will look at you with their small dark patient eyes and they will not move. The press will flow around you as it flows around a stone, parting at your ankles and closing again behind you, and if you stand very still the press will simply incorporate you, the way water incorporates a post driven into a streambed, and within minutes there will be wetfrots lying against your boots, breathing their slow breath, grinding their small teeth, watching you with the same unhurried attention they give the stone. They are not tame. They are not curious. They have no opinion of you at all. You are a feature of the bowl. The bowl has decided what you are, and they are not going to argue it out of you.

This is what lives in the Wettrot Reach. These are the wetfrots. They are the color of the place. They are the temperature of the place. They are the patience of the place. They were not put here. They grew here, the way the ridges grew, the way the water collected, the way the mineral films form on the stone after a rain — slowly, and as a consequence of the conditions, and without ever deciding to. They are the bowl's answer to the bowl's question, and the question was never asked in words, and the answer will not be given in words, and the bowl does not require either from them. It only requires that they be here, and be many, and be quiet, and press together in the long gray hours, and make the low continuous sound that is less a sound than a condition, and so be the Wettrot Reach, which is the name for a place that is not a place but a way of being full.
