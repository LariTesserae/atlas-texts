What lives here begins with what is not here, because the eye has to be recalibrated before it can see.

There are no birds. There are no mammals of any recognizable kind — no foxes, no hares, no muskoxen, no caribou, no shaggy beasts of any breed adapted to high cold. There are no insects in the way a visitor from elsewhere would use the word. No butterflies visit the sparse flowers that bloom for a week in the brief warmth of late summer, because there are no butterflies. No grasshoppers click across the stones. No flies land on a hand. No moths come to the small lamps that researchers sometimes rig, because there are no moths. The cold keeps much of this out; the thin air keeps more. What remains, as the visible fauna, is so reduced in scale and so dispersed across the substrate that the plateau reads from a standing height as a place where nothing moves at all.

This is a misreading, and it is the misreading the place requires of you before it will reveal itself.

What lives here is what the plateau is made of. To call them the inhabitants is to suggest a separation between the place and its population that does not exist. The mites, the springtails, the nematodes, the rotifers, the tardigrades — these are not guests of the substrate. They are the substrate, in its living form. Remove them and the surface is not a surface; it is just geology. Gravel, waiting for wind.

---

You would have to kneel. You would have to bring your face to the ground and breathe the cold air near the stones. Then the surface, which from standing height was a single uniform grey, resolves into a moving thing.

The mites are what you would see first if you had a lens — slow, armored, round-bodied, dark red or brown, moving at the speed a fingernail grows. They are oribatids, mostly, grazing the fungal threads that bind mineral grains together. They eat dead material. They eat pollen that has traveled a thousand kilometers to arrive here exhausted. They eat each other, when an opportunity presents itself, which it sometimes does when a mite molts and the new cuticle is still soft. They are patient. They are ancient. They have been here in some form since before the mountains had worn themselves into their current low and rounded profiles.

The springtails are next — six-legged, soft-bodied, often pale blue-grey, sometimes a faint metallic sheen. They can leap, theoretically: a folded tail-like appendage held under the body snaps out and they are airborne. But they do not leap here. There is nothing to leap from. There is nothing to leap to. They walk. They walk slowly, and they molt, and they molt again, and their cast skins fall into the substrate and become part of it.

The nematodes are thinner than spider silk and almost as long as a fingernail, coiled or uncoiled, threading through the moisture films between mineral grains. They are the most numerous animal on Earth by most counts, and here they are no exception — billions per square meter of surface. They are simple. A tube. A mouth. An anus. A set of nerves. They eat bacteria, they eat each other, they eat the dead. They have been here longer than anything else alive. They will be here when nothing else is.

The rotifers are paused. Most of them. A rotifer is a wheel animal — cilia around a mouth that beat to draw food inward, a tiny body that can survive being dried out by curling into a cyst and waiting. They are waiting here. The cysts are tough. They can wait for years, for decades, for the river that comes sometimes, for the moisture to return after a long dry. They are not asleep. They are paused, which is a different kind of aliveness — slower, more conditional, but alive in the technical sense the word insists on.

The tardigrades are the famous ones, and they are here too, barrel-bodied and unhurried, eight-legged and eternal. They move with the slow confidence of an animal that has been through the boiling and the freezing and the vacuum and emerged on the other side. They can be killed, technically, but they make the conditions difficult to achieve. They are not immortal. They have an indifference to catastrophe that approaches it. They eat. They molt. They mate. They lay eggs that hatch into more of them, plump and slow.

These are the invisibles. By mass, by population, by what it means for the surface to be a surface at all, they overwhelm. If you weighed this plateau the way you would weigh a forest, the trees would lose. The standing biomass is too small to see. The active biomass is too small to see. But the work it does is what makes the plateau a place, the way the work of coral polyps makes a reef a reef — not the mineral but the living architecture on top of it.

---

Above the invisibles, there are the visibles. Not many. Not large. But enough to register, in motion, at the scale of the eye.

The woolly bears are caterpillars — larvae of moths, of tiger moths most likely, fat with the cold, soft-bodied, banded or solid grey, the kind of caterpillar you might lift from a roadway in autumn and set down in a safer place. They are here. They are visible. They move across the plateau in drifts.

A drift is not a swarm. A swarm has direction and density and menace. A drift is more like a tide — a slow, broad movement of bodies across the substrate, hundreds of meters wide, sometimes kilometers across, moving at walking pace or slower than walking pace. The creatures in it are not coordinated in any way an observer would recognize as coordination. There is no leader. There is no signal. They follow food. They follow temperature. They follow something the body knows that the eye cannot see. They aggregate because the conditions are right here, and they disperse because the conditions have moved.

If you stood inside a drift, the ground ahead of you would darken as the bodies arrived. You would feel them passing over and around your boots like a slow tide, soft and continuous. They are not aggressive. They do not bite. They do not flee, exactly — they flow around you, and you become an obstacle, and then a feature, and then they are past you. Behind you, the ground would lighten again. The substrate would look undisturbed. It would not be undisturbed. The drift has eaten what it wanted — the trace organics, the algal films on the underside of stones, the fungal threads and the things that feed on them. It has deposited what it needed to deposit. The chemistry of the surface has been altered in ways you cannot see, and would not become visible for weeks, and would integrate with the substrate over years, and would become part of the long memory of the plateau over centuries.

There are other drifts too. Other soft-bodied, many-legged things that nobody has bothered to name because nobody visits here to do taxonomy. They move across the plateau in their own patterns. They cross each other's paths. They form the slow traffic of a place that, to the eye, looks empty.

---

The drifts, over centuries, leave the rings.

From above — if you were above, which is rare, because almost nothing flies here — you would see concentric bands of differing substrate. Lighter rings. Darker rings. Rings barely distinguishable from the surrounding plain, but distinct to the fauna, which moves along their boundaries and across them and treats them as features of the world.

The rings are the slow excretions of the drifts, mineralized by the cold, integrated into the substrate, becoming part of what the place is. They are not designed. They are iterated. They are the way the drifts eat and process and excrete, repeated over centuries until the patterns of excretion become the patterns of the surface itself.

If you did not know what they were, you would read them as ruins. The eye reads concentric features as ruins because that is what concentric features usually are — barrows, kurgans, the foundations of things built and abandoned. But these are not ruins. They are the slow architecture of the unbuilt. They are the place remembering its own metabolism in the only medium the place has — stone, time, and the slow alteration of mineral by what lives on it.

There are no other structures here. No human ones. No any. No foundations, no markers, no cuts in the surface, no roads. The fauna does not build. The fauna does not accumulate. When an individual dies, it is consumed — by other fauna, by the chemistry of the substrate, by the cold. The plateau does not remember its dead as bodies. It remembers them only as alteration, as the rings, as the slow shift in what the substrate is made of.

---

The whole plateau, in this sense, is an organism. Or it is something adjacent to that word, something for which a vocabulary has not yet been settled.

The fauna and the substrate are not separate things. The fauna lives in the substrate and is made of what the substrate provides, and what it consumes becomes part of the substrate again. The plateau is not a place where things live. The plateau is the thing that lives, distributed across its own surface, in the moisture films and the mineral grains, in the bodies of mites and nematodes and rotifers, in the drifts of caterpillar-like things that move like tides.

It breathes. The fog is its breath. The fauna, the substrate, the chemistry of cold things — all release vapor. The dew point is reached. The fog forms. Then the sun, when it is above the horizon (which is half the year a near-thing and the other half a non-thing), breaks the fog apart and the moisture returns to the substrate. A breath. A cycle. The plateau's day is its breath. Sometimes the breath is faster — when the drifts are concentrated in some region, the respiration rises, and the fog thickens there first, and lingers there longest. Sometimes the breath is slower, when the drifts disperse. The fog moves with the fauna the way clouds move with the wind. The correlation is tight. The lag is short. The fauna leads the weather. The weather follows the fauna. The two become indistinguishable.

It has a metabolism. The drifts are the conveyors of that metabolism. The rings are its excretions, mineralized into architecture. The substrate is its food and its body both.

It has a rhythm. The drifts operate in shifts — one region of the plateau active while another rests, the way a plant runs on staggered cycles. The molting is synchronized. The slow pulses of reproduction move across the surface like a wave through a medium. The whole system has optimized itself into something that, watched from above, would look like machinery — though it is not built, though it is not engineered, though it has found its rhythm without ever having decided to find it. Anything optimized to a long-term steady state becomes industrial in this sense. The plateau has been running so long it has replaced the concept of production with the concept of continuation.

It produces nothing visible. It produces itself. It produces the conditions for itself. It is its own output and its own input.

---

And it is silent.

This is the strangest thing about standing here. The fauna is everywhere, in numbers that would constitute a plague if you could see it, and there is no sound. No chittering. No collective hum of tiny bodies. No rustle. The cold is partly responsible — sound does not propagate well in thin, cold air. The fauna themselves are too small and too soft to set the air vibrating. The substrate absorbs whatever vibrations might otherwise be generated. The plateau is acoustically dead.

You can hear your own pulse. You can hear the blood moving in your ears. Beyond that, nothing.

It is the silence of being inside something very large and very quiet. The silence of a heart, heard from inside the chest.

---

You can walk across it. The substrate will hold you. You can leave footprints. The fauna will fill them in within hours — not because they are cleaning up after you, but because the disturbance you create is a resource to them, an opening in the moisture films, a release of trace nutrients from compressed substrate. You are, briefly, an event. Then you are absorbed.

If you stay, you become part of the cycle. Not dramatically. Not quickly. But over weeks, over months, you would feel yourself becoming more efficient — more cold-adapted, quieter in your movements, more patient. The place tunes you. It is patient about it. It has time.

The place knows you have been here. It has processed you. It has incorporated you into whatever rhythm you happened to interrupt, and the rhythm has continued, and you have become one of its slower alterations.
