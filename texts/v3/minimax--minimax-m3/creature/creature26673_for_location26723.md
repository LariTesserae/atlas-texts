The city is full of people. This is the first thing. Before you have parsed the cliffs or the mist or the impossible sun, you have understood that the city is full of people, because they are everywhere — on the bridges that cross at every height, in the windows of the tiered houses, on the steps that climb and climb and do not end, in the narrow streets below where the mist is thickest, on the rooftops above where the orchids hang. They are layered through the city the way the city is layered through the valley: without gap, without rest, every surface bearing weight.

They are of every age and size. There are infants in slings at their mothers' hips, their small hands caught around a fold of damp cloth, their mouths open and shaping around a sound too small to hear but part of the held note. There are children — the child on the stairs who is reaching for the fruit, his fingers three inches from it, three inches and held there; the child at the window who is laughing, his face lifted, his mouth a wide O of arrested mirth; the child with the stick who is about to strike a small drum and has not yet struck it. There are adolescents with bare feet and long hair, caught mid-stride, caught mid-turn, caught in the act of looking over a shoulder at someone who will never turn back. There are women in cloth of indigo and madder, their hair braided or loose, their arms full of water-jugs or empty, their faces turned toward the sound or away from it. There are men in cloth of ochre and white, bearded, their hands raised or lowered or at their sides, their bodies in postures of work or rest or something in between that has no name. There are the old, with grey in their hair and lines in their faces, their mouths shaped around the same held note as everyone else.

They wear no metal. There are no fasteners that would not give way in the wet. There are no buttons, no pins, no buckles. Their clothes are wrapped, tucked, tied with cords of woven vine. The cloth is heavy with moisture. It drapes close to their bodies. It has bled — the indigo has run a little in places, the madder has faded where the light finds it, the ochre has darkened where the shadow sits. The white cloth is no longer white; it is the grey of the sky, the grey of the mist, the grey of a held breath.

Their skin has color. They are not one people. They are many. Their skins are brown and black and tan and the deep rust-red of the cliffs themselves. The moisture beads on them — on their arms, their shoulders, their faces, the small of their backs where the cloth has shifted. The condensation sits on them like a second skin. It does not fall. It hangs in droplets on the curves of their shoulders, on the ridges of their knuckles, on the tips of their eyelashes. They gleam.

Their eyes are open. They are not closed in sleep or death. They are open and they see. What they see is the same held moment, the same fountain, the same stair, the same child reaching. The city repeats. Geometry is a suggestion. You walk and walk and find yourself walking back the way you came, in a valley that is both huge and claustrophobic at once. So they see the same things, over and over, the same figures in the same postures, the same fruit hanging, the same water suspended. Or perhaps they do not see this. Perhaps their eyes are open the way a door is open — not because anyone is coming through.

Their mouths are shaped around the sound they are making. This is the most striking thing. Every mouth in the city is open and shaped — not slack, not empty, but shaped, the lips rounded or spread, the tongue positioned, the jaw dropped to a particular angle. They are all sustaining a single held note, a million throats producing the same unchanging sound. The sound is so loud it presses against the chest cavity and lives there. A thousand drums, a thousand voices, flutes cutting through, bells of beaten stone and clay and hollow wood. Children shouting. Women keening a long sustained note that is not quite a wail and not quite a song. Men chanting low. Laughter in it — held laughter, the laugh caught at the top of its arc, not quite reaching the descent. Joy in it — or something that was once joy and has become stone.
