The city breathes in three shifts.

The first shift begins before dawn, which is not, here, much darker than day. The workers move through the streets in groups, their boots on the wet cobbles making a sound like rain on a tin roof — a soft, persistent percussion that goes on and on, one set of feet leaving the next to the next. They have made this walk so often that they do not see the buildings. They see the gutters, the slick spots, the places where the moss has crept over the edge of a step. They know which alleys flood at high water and which doorways stay dry. They have memorized the city in their feet. Among them are machinists, molders, foundrymen, the tenders of the chemical baths, the precision hands. Most carry their lunches in tin pails. The foremen carry nothing; they have someone to carry for them. The apprentices carry their masters' tools as well as their own, and walk a half step behind.

The second shift is the loud shift, the day shift, the shift with the most bodies in the street. School is out, though school is brief and half of it is apprenticeship. The children of the valley learn to read in the morning and to work in the afternoon. The girls learn the terraced gardens and the dye vats and the long bookkeeping of the small shops. The boys learn the foundries and the machine rooms and the rail yards. The brightest among them — and the valley has its bright ones — learn to read the fog. They learn to know by its taste and weight whether the smelting will go well that day, whether the chemical works will hold, whether the river is bringing the kind of silt that will clog the intakes. This is not science. It is older than science. It is the kind of knowing that lives in the body, the way a machinist knows when a bearing is about to fail by the sound it makes ten seconds before.

The third shift is the quiet shift. The city is mostly asleep under it. The workers move alone or in pairs. The street lamps are bright in their absence, each one a small sun in the gauze. A woman walks to her shop. She has done this for thirty-one years. She whistles the same three notes at the corner of Forge Street and the Avenue, though she does not know she does it. Her grandfather worked the same shop; his tools hang in the same places on the wall; the apprenticeship is still seven years, and she has seen many boys through it. She is sixty-three. She will work until she cannot, and then she will be given a room in one of the guild halls and a small pension, and her chair at the bench will go to whichever of her grandchildren wants it. Three of them want it. She has not decided.

She is not sentimental about the shop. It is damp. The roof has leaked in three places for as long as she has worked there, and the buckets catch the drips with a metronomic patience. Her hands are scarred and her knees are bad. She has buried a husband and raised four children and seen the foundry change hands twice. She does not love it the way a tourist loves it. She loves it the way one loves a room one has lived in for a long time — with the familiarity that does not need to be remarked upon, the knowledge of where every stain came from, the sense that one could not be made comfortable anywhere else.

The foremen are a different sort. They are large, mostly. They have put on weight in the middle and kept it there, the way the moss keeps to the north face of a building. They eat well. The foremen's wives are women of a particular kind — capable, plain, running the household and the small shop and the apprenticeship fees with the same steady hand. The foremen themselves come up through the shop; almost all of them. There are no school-trained managers here. There is no class that did not work first. A man who arrives from outside and tries to give an order he has not earned will find, by the end of the week, that nothing in the shop is moving correctly, and by the end of the month he will have gone. The valley does this quietly. It does not need to be loud about it.

The apprentices are another kind again. They are fourteen when they begin, sometimes younger. They are small in their overalls. They have not yet grown into their bodies. They have not yet learned to read the foreman's silence, which is the main thing the foreman communicates by. They carry tools up and down stairs until their arms ache, and they sleep the deep sleep of young men who have worked hard all day. By the third year they begin to understand the shop — the way the machines want to be cared for, the way the master wants to be approached, the way the city wants to be walked through. By the fifth year they are nearly grown into it. By the seventh they are ready, and the master makes a small ceremony of it, and they begin their own benches, with their own apprentices under them.

The terraced gardens are tended mostly by the old, and by the women whose shifts permit it. The fruit trees are very old. The vines have been trained and re-trained until they are architectural — green walls, edible eaves, hedges that have become parapets. A woman pruning a vine on the seventh terrace up from the Avenue is pruning something her grandmother planted. She is fifty-eight. She has bad teeth and bad lungs from the chemical works, where she worked until she was forty. She comes up here every morning in good weather and most mornings in bad. The fruit is small and tart. It goes into pies and into a kind of brandy the valley makes from the windfalls. She gives most of it away. She could not eat it all. She knows the names of her plants. Some of them she has named herself — old names, valley names, not the names in any book.

The river people live lower in the valley, where the brown water runs fastest. They are barge-men, dock hands, the sort who tie up at night and sleep in the boats and rise at first light. They are lean and brown and weather-bitten. They are the only ones in the valley who see the sun — because the sun, when it comes, comes down the river. They bring in news from outside. They bring in the things the valley cannot make for itself — certain foods, certain kinds of cloth, the occasional book. They are regarded with a slight suspicion, as all outsiders are, but they are useful and so are tolerated. Some of them marry valley people and stay. Some of them do not.

The dye-makers have their quarter, low and dark, near the river, where the water is most convenient and the smells are most tolerable to those who do not live there. Their works give the greenish flicker that lights the lower valley at night. They are quiet men, mostly. They are chemists, of a kind — empirical chemists, the kind who learned in the shop and not in a school. They keep their recipes in their heads. They have small notebooks with coded entries. They are very rich and live plainly. They eat the same food as everyone else. They drink the same brandy. They are respected and a little feared, which is how chemists have always lived among their neighbors.

The union temple is on the High Street, near the guild halls. It is a stone building, old, with a bell that rings at six, at noon, and at six again. The religion is not complicated. It honors the work. It says that the worker who does his task well is doing a holy thing. It says that the body is a kind of tool and should be kept in good order. It says that the union is a kind of family and should be honored as one. The priest is a working man who has been chosen by the other working men. He keeps the books of the union and arbitrates the small disputes. He marries people and buries them. He gives a kind of sermon on Sundays, though most of the valley does not come, and the ones who do are mostly old.

The guild halls are larger. There are seven of them, one for each of the major trades. They have meeting rooms and dining rooms and small dormitories for the old. The bells ring at different hours, and the fog muffles them, and a worker in any part of the valley hears, at any given moment, two or three of them at once, blurred together into a single tone. The guilds keep the apprentices' fees. They keep the small pension funds. They keep the records of who has worked where, and for how long, and in whose service. They are the memory of the valley.

The home life of the valley is not separate from the work. The houses are layered, added onto, room after room over the generations, until a single family might occupy four floors and two additions and a converted outbuilding, with the grandmother on the ground floor and the apprentice son in the attic and the kitchen somewhere in the middle. The walls are thin. The children of one household hear the arguments of the next. The smell of one kitchen fills the stairwell of three. There is not much privacy. There is not much loneliness either. A person who lives here is rarely alone for long.

The children play in the alleys. They learn the city young — which alleys flood, which walls can be climbed, where the iron railings are loose enough to swing on. They learn the names of the dogs they don't see. They learn to read the fog. They learn, very early, which adults to avoid, and which ones have biscuits. They are not especially cheerful children, but they are sturdy. They grow up fast. They go to work at fourteen. They marry young. They have children of their own by twenty. They are tired by forty and dead by seventy, mostly, though a few stubborn ones go on longer. The valley does not pity them for this. The valley does not pity itself. The valley is not a place that pities. It is a place that continues.

The dead are buried up the slopes, where the terraces end and the moss takes over. The cemetery is a terraced thing itself, set into the hillside, each grave marked with a small stone and a name and a date and, sometimes, a tool — a hammer, a chisel, a wheel, a small iron gear. The dead are visited on certain days, in the spring mostly, when the weather permits. A woman brings flowers from the garden. A man brings a flask of brandy and pours a little on the grave. The fog covers everything. The cemetery is quiet. The valley, below, goes on working.

The pigeons live in the roof spaces. They are not tame but they are not afraid. A worker on a ladder, replacing a tile, will often find himself looking at a pigeon that is looking back at him with the small bright eye of a pigeon. The pigeons live on what falls. There is always something falling. The rats live in the drains. They are large. They are not afraid of anything, because nothing in the drains is afraid of them, and the cats cannot follow them where they go. The cats live in the alleys and on the rooftops. They are lean and territorial and they have small wars of their own, at night, which a person sleeping in a thin-walled house will hear. The dogs are kept indoors. They are loved. They are the only clean thing in many of the houses. The streets are too wet for them.

Sometimes a heron comes up the river and fishes for a day before the neighborhood dogs are brought to the bank to drive it off. The dogs bark and lunge at the end of their leashes. The heron lifts off, unhurried, with a slow beat of its long wings. It does not come back that season. Once, very rarely, a fox comes down through the upper terraces, through the gardens, into the alleys, and there is a small commotion — a chase, a barking, a coop broken into — and then it is gone, and the valley returns to itself.

The light is always low. The electric lights stay on at all hours, supplemented by the orange glow of forge windows and the greenish flicker of the chemical works. The fog carries each light's color and blurs it into the next. Colors run. A person walking through the valley at night sees, at any given moment, a wash of yellow and orange and green, all of it softened, all of it running into everything else. The eye cannot rest on any single thing. The eye is always moving.

A worker is always up on a ladder re-laying a tile. Another is always in the gardens, pruning. Another is always in the shop, teaching an apprentice. A bargeman is always poling up the brown river. The mist is always forming and re-forming around the chimneys and the bridge cables. The moss is always growing on the next pipe. The valley does not stop. It has not stopped for a hundred and forty years. It will not stop tomorrow. The woman with the bad knees will walk to the shop tomorrow morning and the day after, and the boy carrying his master's tools will grow into his overalls, and the foreman will eat his supper, and the priest will ring the bell at six, and the fog will come in, and the river will run brown, and the moss will grow another millimeter on the north face of the brick, and the city will continue, as it has continued, the way a body continues — not finished, not finished, not finished.
