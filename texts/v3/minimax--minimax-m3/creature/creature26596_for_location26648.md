The light lives here in small, specific parcels.

You will see, if you sit in the warm water long enough — and you will sit, because sitting is what the place encourages, and there is nothing here to be in a hurry about — a faint blue-green resolve on the trunk of one of the bone-trees. It will be small. The size of a leaf, or a leaf's remembering of being a leaf. It will not stay. It will rise.

Slowly. Slower than patience. Slower than you thought a thing could move and still be in any useful sense alive. It climbs the rough flank of the column, this small bright being, going up the way a man climbs a wall when the wall is taller than he had expected and there is no place to put his hands. There is nothing to put its hands on either, but it climbs anyway, by some older method than hands, by some patient adhesion that has been at it for a very long time. It is the patience of it that makes the climb possible. It does not have to reach the flower before dark. There is no dark. There is only the water, which is warm, and the column, which is taller than it is, and the flower, which is somewhere up there, waiting in the way flowers wait when no insect has ever told them what waiting is for.

When it arrives — and you have been watching, haven't you, though watching in the warm water is more of a listening, a feeling of where the small light is against the column — the flower opens.

Not like a flower on the surface, where opening is a small daily drama. This opening is more like an agreement being kept. The petals, if they are petals, if the word applies, uncurl with the slowness of something that has uncurled many times before and will uncurl many times again. The light goes in, or is received, or is folded into the arrangement the flower is making of itself. Some quiet transaction occurs. You cannot see what is being exchanged. You can see only that the flower, afterward, is one bloom fuller than it was, and the small bright creature has gone on, down the column, around its slow day, looking for the next flower that is ready to be one bloom fuller.

This is happening throughout the drowned forest. Throughout the vast warm dark, where the columns stand in their long unarguments and the water does its patient work, this commerce of dim light is being carried on. The light-bearers go from trunk to trunk, from flower to flower, performing the small unnamed sacrament that is the only business the place still has. They do not speak of it. They do not even know, in any way that you would recognize as knowing, that it is a sacrament. They are doing what they have always done. They will continue doing it after you have stopped being able to see them. They are the smallest, and the most particular, of the inhabitants.

The pale long things are different.

You will see one only if you are lucky, or only if you have been here long enough that the water has learned the shape of your waiting. They are not numerous. They are not in conversation with each other. Each one seems to be alone, in the way that the columns are alone, in the way that the flowers are alone when the light-bearers are not at them. They slide between the trunks the way a thought slides through a long quiet mind — without hurry, without apparent purpose, without the part of itself that would notice you.

They are eyeless. This is not a deformity. It is an economy. There is nothing to see here, or rather, nothing that needs the apparatus of seeing. The water is the same color in every direction. The columns are the same color as the water. The light, when it resolves, comes from too far away to register as direction. The pale long things have given up the labor of eyes because eyes would only confirm what the skin already knows: that the world here is warm, and patient, and continuous, and that the next column is much like the last column, and the next silence is much like the last silence.

They feel, instead. The whole long body of them is a feeling-organ. They feel the temperature of the water as it shifts around them, the slow fronts of warm and cooler water that pass through the place like weather through a cathedral. They feel the displacement of other bodies, the column-flesh as they brush past it, the slight pressure of the small light-bearers going about their business on the trunks. They feel the gradient between here and not-here, which is the gradient between warm and slightly less warm, between presence and the rumor of presence. This is enough for them. It has been enough for a very long time.

They are not large. Some of them are as long as a room, or a small room, or the memory of a room. Some are longer, but they move in such slow coils that length does not register as length. They are the color of the water, which is the color of old tea, which is the color of everything here. They have mouths, or something that does what mouths do, but they are not, in any way you would recognize, hunters. They are eaters of the water. They are eaters of the small things the water contains. They are eaters of the column-flakes, the soft dust of the bone-trees, the slow rain of organic matter that drifts down from the flowers and the mosses and the higher, less-known places. They are patient. They will eat what passes them. They will not pursue.

Once, you feel something larger pass.

Not a pale long thing. Something else. A displacement of water that is not the slow drift of a feeder but a different kind of motion, a glide that is more deliberate, more directed. It does not pause. It does not, as far as you can tell, notice you. It moves through the columns the way a thought moves through a long sleep — once, and then not again, and you will spend a long time afterward trying to be sure it was there.
