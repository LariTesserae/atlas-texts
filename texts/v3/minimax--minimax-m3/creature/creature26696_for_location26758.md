and the wall will move. Not in any way a wall ought to move — not swaying, not breathing in the way the brochures mean when they say a forest breathes. It will rearrange. A ridge of bark will lift, settle, lift again, in a pattern that takes long enough to notice that you have to keep watching to be sure you saw it. The conduits inside the trunk will pulse — not with light, not with anything you'd call current, but with a slight change of color, a tide of darker green running along the grain, and the tide will move in one direction, pause, move back. If you put your hand against the wall you can feel the pulse as a faint pressure, patient, against your palm. The wall is not a wall. The wall is a slow current of negotiation, and you are feeling the edge of an argument that has been running for longer than you have been in the room.

This is what the place is. It is not a settlement. A settlement implies people who settled. A settlement implies an event, a decision, a time before. This place has no before that you can locate. The first wall was laid onto a root, and the root was already old, and the laying was a small event inside a much larger continuity, and the continuity does not mark the event. The people who live here — and they do live here, in the sense that the word still applies — did not come to the basin and decide to make it theirs. They came to the basin the way a question comes to a mouth. The basin was already asking. They were the answer that arrived, and now the question has been answering itself through them for so long that the original asking is hard to reconstruct.

There are not many of them. You can walk the whole settlement in a long afternoon, if you walk slowly, and the paths will give you back to where you started without your having noticed the loop close. The buildings are larger than they need to be for the bodies inside them — or the bodies are smaller than the buildings need them to be. The proportion is off in a way that suggests the buildings were sized for some other purpose, or for purposes that include the bodies only incidentally. Inside, the rooms are tall, and the ceilings are high enough that voices don't return from them; you speak and the sound is taken up into the fiber above and not heard again. The light inside is the color of weak tea, and it comes from everywhere, and it does not cast shadows you can use to tell time.

The people themselves are not what you would expect, though you will spend a long time trying to decide what you expected. They are not small, though they are slight. They are not dark, though the light makes them so. They are not uniform, but the differences between them are not the differences you are practiced at reading — not the differences of feature, of age, of dress, but something more like differences of attention. Some of them you look at and they look back, and the looking back has a quality of *event*, as though your gaze has been received and registered and is now being processed. Others you look at and the looking passes through them, or around them, or is taken up by them in a way that leaves you unsure whether you were seen. They move slowly, and not with the slowness of fatigue. With the slowness of people who have no reason to be quick. The place moves at the speed of geology, and they have made themselves appropriate to it, and the appropriateness looks, from outside, like a kind of peace you cannot verify.

They do not speak to you, at first. Or they do, but the speaking is not the event you would expect. A woman — older, though the older-ness is hard to place, more a density of attention than a weathering of skin — stands near one of the wall-paths and watches you for a long time, and then she does something that is not a greeting but is not not a greeting. She raises one hand, slowly, and holds it in the air for a moment, and the air around her hand does something — thickens, or thins, or shifts in a way that makes the light bend — and then the hand is lowered, and that was the whole communication. You understand, afterwards, that you have been welcomed, or that you have been noted, or that the place has been informed of you, and that no further communication is required at this stage. You are inside the system now. The system will take its time with you.

You see them working, sometimes. A man crouched at the base of a wall, his hands in the moss, very still, for so long that you think he is resting and then realize that he is not. The moss is moving under his fingers in a way that is not wind. He is in conversation with the wall, or with the root, or with whatever the wall is a slow current of, and the conversation is being conducted in a medium that is not language and not gesture and not touch but something closer to a tuning — a slow adjustment of one patterned body against another patterned body, the way two fluids negotiate at an interface. After a long time he stands, and the place where he crouched is slightly different — the moss is slightly rearranged, or a small channel has been opened or closed, or the color is fractionally altered — and he walks away without looking back, and the wall resumes the argument it was having before he joined it.

You see them eating, once. A table, low, near the central nave. The food is not food you would name. It is pale, and it is wet, and it has the texture of something that was not killed to be eaten but rather offered, the way a fruit is offered. They eat it with their hands, slowly, and they do not talk while they eat, and the not-talking is not a rule so much as a coincidence — at this hour, in this place, there is nothing that needs to be said. A child — and the child-ness is also hard to place, because the bodies here do not advertise their ages — sits at the table and does not eat, and the not-eating is not refusal, it is something else, it is the child being full of something that is not food. The child is watching the wall, and the wall is doing the thing walls do, the slow tide of darker green along the grain, and the child is following the tide with an attention that is not childish. After a while the child reaches out one finger and touches the table, and the touching is a small event, and the table responds, faintly, with a pulse, and the child is satisfied, and gets down from the table and goes into the moss.

You try, at some point, to find the boundary of the settlement, and you cannot. The paths lead you between buildings and then between trees and then between roots and then you are standing in a place that is not the settlement and also not not the settlement. A woman is sitting on a root that is also a bench, and she is doing something with her hands that involves a small bright filament — a thread of something that is not quite light, that persists in the air without dissolving, that she is weaving into a pattern you cannot follow. She does not look up. You stand for a long time. Eventually you realize you are being asked a question, and the question is being asked by the filament, and the question is whether you are going to stay, and the question is not urgent. You can leave. The place will go on being a place. But the question is being asked, and it is the kind of question that, once asked, does not un-ask itself, and you understand that you will carry it with you out of the basin, and that the carrying will change you, slowly, in a way you will not be able to name.

Later — and later is a strange word here, because the time is not structured in the way time is structured in places you have been — you lie down in the moss, in a place that is not a room but that is more interior than most rooms, and the moss takes your weight and the warmth comes up through it and the air is full and the hum is in your teeth, and you understand, finally, that the place is not a settlement and the people are not inhabitants and the forest is not a forest. The place is a single thing, distributed across a basin, old, patient, in the middle of a long thought. The people are aspects of the thought. The walls are aspects of the thought. The hum is the thought, heard from the inside. You are, for as long as you are here, an aspect of the thought too, and the thought is taking its time with you, and the thought is not in a hurry, and the thought is the temperature of the place itself, and the place is the bottom of something very old, and the bottom is where the answer has been settling for longer than rivers remember.
