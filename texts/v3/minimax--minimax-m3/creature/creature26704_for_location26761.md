The people of the hollow are small in number — three hundred, perhaps four, never more, because the basin will not feed more. They are pale the way cave fish are pale, the way mushrooms are pale: a waxy, slightly translucent pallor that comes from generations of grey light and water-borne minerals. The skin has a faint sheen to it in certain lights, the same petrol-iridescence that moves across the basalt when wet — not always, not on everyone, but on enough of them, in enough angles, that visitors from the southern lowlands have been known to remark on it and then refuse to remark on it again. The children are told that this is the water-table showing through. They are told it is the city's reflection. They do not ask which is true. The question is not considered good form.

They are short by outside standards, though there has been no one to measure against in living memory, and their shortness is the kind that comes of cramped architecture as much as of genetics. They duck through low arches without thinking. They turn sideways in the narrow lanes as a matter of course. They climb stairs that go nowhere because the stairs are there, and because the muscle-memory of climbing is in their bodies even when the destination has been lost. Their hands are broad and calloused, the knuckles scarred with the small white crescents of healed basalt cuts, and the fingers often have a tremor in them that is not age but exposure — a fine, constant vibration, as though the wetness of the place were entering them through the fingertips and trying to shake itself loose. They do not speak of this. Most of them do not even notice it.

Their hair is dark and very thick, and the women and some of the men wear it in tight coils close to the scalp, bound with strips of wool and weighted with small stones — river-rounded pebbles, gathered by children from the deeper channels, polished by years of handling until they gleam like the wet mortar. The effect is not decorative. It keeps the hair out of the way in the wet, and the stones make a faint sound when the wearer moves, a soft clicking that the inhabitants read the way other peoples read footsteps. A woman passes in a lane and you know she is elderly by the particular weight and rhythm of her beads. A child passes and you know the child is calm by the silence of them. The people who do not coil their hair — the smith, some of the goat-keepers who work the upper terraces in wind — let it hang loose, matted into ropes, and these folk are considered slightly wild, slightly suspect, not quite of the lower town.

Their eyes are pale — grey, grey-green, the indeterminate color of the water itself — and large, with lids that fold in a way outsiders find difficult to look at directly, as though the eyes were perpetually on the verge of closing against a brightness that never quite arrives. In the hour or two of true sun, when the cloud breaks and the light comes in sideways across the basin, the inhabitants retire indoors and pull shutters across their windows. The children peek. The adults do not. After two generations of overcast, even the brief light is a kind of weather, and they have learned to wait it out the way they wait out rain.

Their clothing is felt, almost always — the goat underhair that they comb and card and full in the early summer, when the light lasts long enough to work by. The felt is dark, the natural color of the goats, and it is layered, three or four garments deep in the cold months, and it is almost always slightly damp, because nothing here truly dries, and a faint mineral crust forms on the outer surface after a few wearings, giving the felt a pale powdery bloom at the cuffs and hems that the inhabitants brush off but do not remark on. The wool is finer, when it can be had, and reserved for the very young and the very old, who feel the cold worst. Children wear it as a kind of second skin until they are old enough to be put into felt, and the transition is a small rite that the parents mark by burning the wool garment in the floor-pit. The smoke that rises from these small fires is the only smoke in the hollow with any particular scent — animal, sharp, briefly present, and then gone.

They smell, collectively, of the place. Sulfur at the southern wells, sweet stone at the northern ones, wet wool, goat, lichen, the faint metallic tang of the drainage channels where the salamanders live. Visitors from outside carry the smell home with them in their clothes and cannot get it out, and this is sometimes the reason they do not return. The inhabitants do not notice their own smell. It is the smell of water and work.

They work, and their work is mostly stone. The lime-chipping in spring is the largest collective labor and the only one with a fixed date: when the last frost has gone out of the upper terraces, which is to say when the mist lifts for an hour one morning and does not return, the able-bodied go out along every wall in the settlement with flat stones and wooden mallets and chip the pale crusts that have accumulated over the winter. The crusts come off in flakes and sheets, sometimes in long unbroken curls like bark, and they are gathered into baskets and pounded in the central paving-stones — the three polished spots that the foot-traffic has made — until they are a fine white powder. The powder is mixed with ground basalt and water from the northern wells, and the paste that results is the season's mortar. Each spring, the settlement is re-mortared, in part, and each spring it is also in some measure rebuilt, because the chipping reveals joints that have failed and stones that have shifted, and the work of repair begins the moment the chipping ends. The whole process takes six weeks. The inhabitants mark it the way other peoples mark a new year.

Stone-dressing is the daily work, the work that fills the long hours when the lichen is not being harvested and the goats do not need to be moved and the rain is of the kind that permits it. The sound of it — the rhythmic pound, the scrape, the fine dust falling — is one of the loudest human sounds in the hollow, and it carries through the mist in a way that other sounds do not, so that even in the upper terraces, even in the goat-pens, you can hear someone, somewhere, dressing stone. The work is skilled. A good dresser can split a basalt sheet along its grain with three measured blows, and the resulting slab will be plumb and square and ready to set. Bad dressers split stone that cracks in the hand, that crumbles, that wastes. The good ones are respected, and their children are taught young, and the bad ones do other work — goat-keeping, pig-keeping, the harvest of medicinal mosses up on the unfinished tower, the night collections in the lower town.

The night collections are done at first light, before most of the settlement has risen. Two people, sometimes three, move through the lower lanes with a cart and a long-handled scoop, and they gather what the night has left in the channels and the standing water of the permanent thin film that covers the lowest streets. The work is quiet and unpleasant and necessary, and the people who do it are not stigmatized but they are not sought after either. They eat apart from the others, in a small room off the central paving-stone, and they are given the first cut of the boar-pig and the best of the cheese. They are owed a debt by everyone, and the debt is paid in this small way.

---

Their food is what the place permits. The grey porridge — lichen and moss, dried and powdered and simmered with water and a little goat-fat — is the staple, eaten twice a day, sometimes three times in the heavy work seasons. It tastes, by report of the few visitors who have been persuaded to try it, of nothing in particular and of stone after you have finished. The inhabitants eat it without expression. They have eaten it for so long that the texture of it — slightly gritty, slightly slimy, the consistency of wet clay — has become for them the texture of food itself, and visitors who chew with vigor seem to them to be performing an unnecessary act.

The boar-pigs are the principal meat, and they are butchered in late autumn, when the southern slope has been rooted enough to make them thin and the killing is a kindness. The meat is smoked over the floor-pits for a week, then hung in the cold upper rooms, then eaten through the winter as it will keep — cured, dark, strong, with a gamy taste that the inhabitants find bland and that visitors find unforgettable. The goat cheese is good. The inhabitants know this. They are quietly proud of it, in the way that people are proud of things they have done well within strict limits.

Salamanders are eaten, though rarely. The cats catch them in the drainage channels and leave the small grey bodies on doorsteps, and the inhabitants thank the cats and throw most of the catch back into the water, and a few are taken and grilled whole on a flat stone over the floor-pit. The children eat them most often. The adults find them too much work for too little reward. The cats understand this and bring fewer salamanders to houses where they have been refused.

Bread is made from a flour ground from the inner bark of the rowans on the western slope, which is a generational tree and a venerated one, and the bark is taken only when a tree has died of its own accord and only with a small ritual of apology. The flour is coarse and dark and makes a bread that is more like a hard cake than a loaf, and it is eaten on the days of the lime-chipping and on the longest day of summer and at the new year, when the inhabitants mark the turning of the seasons in whatever way the weather permits. There is not enough bark-flour for daily bread. The rowans are not enough.

Their sleep is governed by the horn. The unfinished tower that rises a single story above its neighbors is the highest building in the hollow, and it serves as a watchpost and as a drying-platform for the medicinal mosses, which are spread there on flat stones in the rare clear hours and which the inhabitants credit with properties no one outside would recognize. The horn is sounded at the day's ending by whoever has the watch — a post that rotates among the older children and the adults who have no other pressing work. One note, held. The mist takes it within a few hundred yards. The sound is less a signal than a punctuation, and the inhabitants who hear it pause in what they are doing, briefly, and then continue. The horn is not answered. There is no other note. The day simply ends.

Their speech is quiet. The walls hold sound, and the mist absorbs it, and the inhabitants have learned to pitch their voices to the distance they need to be heard across — low, soft, slightly hoarse from the damp. They speak a language that has many specific words for moisture: ground-mist, roof-talk, knee-water, the half-day, and others the visitors do not learn. Outsiders sometimes mistake this for a regional dialect of something they know; it is not. The language is its own, and has been for as long as the oldest inhabitant can remember, which is not very long, because memory in the hollow is short. People die at sixty, seventy, and they take their reasons with them.

Their griefs are not spoken of constantly. The wetness of the place — its central fact, the thing that defines every hour of every life — is not named directly. They speak of the weather, of the rain, of the channels, of the wells, of the cisterns, of the work, but they do not speak of the wet itself, the way one does not speak of a death that has not yet happened. The children absorb this by example. They learn to live within the wet the way one learns to live within grief — by the slow accumulation of small accommodations, by the building of walls and routines that hold the thing at bay without ever drying it out. They are patient people. They have to be.

---

The dead are burned. The floor-pits are used, and the smoke rises through the roof-holes with the day's smoke, and the bones are gathered after — small, pale, mineral-rich from a life of mineral water — and are placed in the channels, where the water takes them within a day. The inhabitants do not mark graves. They do not have a word for the practice. The dead are returned to the wet, and the wet receives them without ceremony, and the living continue. The children are told that the bones go to join the city below, and they do not entirely disbelieve it.

The smith works one week in four. The rest of the time she keeps goats, because goats need keeping and because the metal is expensive and the work is slow. She is the only person in the hollow who can draw wire, and the only one who can solder, and the only one who can work bronze without cracking it. Her tools are iron, brought up from the lowlands in small consignments, and she maintains them with a care that approaches devotion. She has apprentices, but none of them yet has the skill, and the inhabitants are quietly aware that when she dies, the hollow will lose something it cannot easily replace. They do not speak of this. They have learned to live within the prospect of loss.

The horn-blower on the tower has the longest view in the settlement, and the inhabitants sometimes go up to stand with her in the brief clear hours, when the mist lifts and the basin is visible from above as a single dark shape pressed into the floor of the hollow, like a coin dropped into wet clay. They stand and look at themselves from outside, briefly, and then they go back down. The view is not considered lucky. It is considered accurate.
