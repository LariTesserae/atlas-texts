What lives here is one thing, and many things, and the distinction has stopped mattering.

A single one, if you could pick one up — and you cannot, easily, because they do not like to be separated from the layer, and the layer does not let go — is about the length and thickness of the last segment of your smallest finger. It is built in plates, narrow ones, hard ones, fitted tight along a back that rises in a low ridge and falls away to a softer underside where the legs are. The plates are the color of an iron stain on a porcelain sink: a deep dried-blood red at the shoulders that fades to a dusty rust-orange toward the rear, and along each plate a darker seam where the new shell has not quite finished hardening over the old one. The legs are paler, almost the color of the stone itself, because the legs do the work and the work wears them down, and the dust packs between the joints and grinds them into a kind of pale paste that they drag with them everywhere. There are many legs. I do not know how many. They are too small to count on one that is moving, and they are all always moving.

The head, if that is the word for it, is not a head in any way you would recognize. There is a forward end, and at the forward end there is a small dark mouth that works sideways, a little at a time, like a millstone, and there is a pair of short antennae of a kind that taste the air rather than smell it, and there are no eyes, because there has never been any reason for eyes in here. It finds its way by the feel of dust against the antennae, by the gradient of powder between where the grinders spit and where the separator drops, by the warmth of a shaft that has been turning all morning, by the warmth of the bodies ahead of it in the current. It does not need to see the next one of its kind. It only needs to feel that there is another one close enough to touch, and it always is.

They are not insects. They are not quite anything. They have the look of something that began as something else and has been worn down by what it eats into a shape that fits the food. Their gut, from end to end, is a slow stone mill, lined with something that is not quite chitin and not quite enamel and not quite grit but does the same work as all three. They swallow the powder the grinders make — and the powder is very fine, finer than flour, finer than talc, a powder that hangs — and they pass it through themselves, and what comes out the other end is even finer, and that is what falls onto the belts to be lifted back into the hoppers and ground again. Each one of them is a stage in the grinding. They are part of the machine. They have been part of the machine for so long that you cannot say where the machine ends and the animal begins, and the question has not occurred to anyone because there is no one to whom it could occur.

A single one eats a little dust, and excretes a little finer dust, and lives for a span of time that is hard to guess because nothing here keeps time. It is born from an egg laid in the dust, which is the same as saying it condenses out of the dust, because the eggs are indistinguishable from the dust except under very close looking, and the young that hatch from them are the color of the dust at first and only darken to the dried-blood red as they eat and grow and shed their skins. They shed their skins many times. A shed skin is a pale hollow thing, paper-thin, ridged in the same low ridge as the body, left behind in the current and trampled under and slowly worked back into the powder by the feet of the others. There is no graveyard in the hollow. There is only dust, and the dust is made of everything that has ever been shed or died here, and it is still being made.

They eat each other. This is the part you do not want to think about, and the part that the place does not care whether you think about. The current is not all one speed. There are eddies at the edges, and in the eddies a smaller one, a freshly molted one whose new shell has not hardened, can be held down by six or eight of the others and opened along the soft underside and eaten, slowly, while the rest of the current flows past as though nothing is happening. The shell goes back to dust. The soft parts go back to dust. The dust goes back into the hoppers. The grinders do not pause. The current does not pause. There is no such thing as a body in the hollow for very long. Everything that stops being one of them, very soon, is again what they eat.

They cluster where the dust is finer. You can see the clusters from the rim, if you know to look — darker patches where the carpet thickens and rises, where the bodies are three or four deep instead of one, where the legs of the lower ones are simply touching the legs of the upper ones and the upper ones are being held up by the lower ones and the lower ones are not complaining because complaint is not something they do. They cluster at the pipe-ends where the capped flanges have worked loose and a slow breath of extra-fine powder leaks out. They cluster under the grinder housings where the powder first falls. They cluster in the lee of the separator trough where the torn belt has frayed into a brush of rubber threads that comb the dust out of the air. They cluster, sometimes, in the shed, in the places where the corrugated roof has become more hole than roof and a thin column of daylight comes down; in the column of daylight the dust is visible, hanging, and they climb each other to get into it, and what they are doing in the column of daylight is not clear, because there is nothing in the column of daylight except slightly less dust and a different temperature, but they do it anyway, and when the sun moves they move with it, and the cluster moves across the floor of the shed like the shadow of something that is not there.

They have no names for themselves. They have no signals except the touch of the next body, and the next body, and the next, and through that touch a kind of information moves, the way information moves through a school of fish or a flock of starlings: a shift here is felt there a moment later, and the whole current corrects. The current always turns the same way. The current has been turning the same way for longer than any one of them has been alive, and none of them remembers a time when it turned the other way, because none of them was alive then. The current is older than the current's members and outlives them and is made of them. If you stood in the hollow and waited long enough — a few hours, a day — you would see the carpet renew itself under your feet. The ones you were standing on would be carried past you, and other ones would come up from behind you, and you would never have moved and the place would have moved under you like a slow tide. They do not notice you. You are too big, and you are too still, and you do not taste like dust, and you are not part of the current, and so far as they are concerned you are a piece of machinery that has not yet started turning.

The carpet is the youngest thing in the hollow. The stone is older than anything. The machines are older than the carpet. The dust is older than the carpet, because the machines were making the dust before there was anything to eat it. And the carpet is winning.

This is the part that is hard to sit with, and the part that is true. The grinders lose a little of themselves each day, and the separator loses a little, and the shed loses a little, and the carpet gains the difference. The bowls worn into the floor beneath the machines are not made by the machines. The machines are bolted to the floor. The bowls are made by the carpet, by the constant slow crawl of the bodies in their long circle, by the abrasion of a million small hard feet on a stone that is older than feet, by the patient wearing-down that is faster than the machines are wearing-down and that is happening in every direction at once. The hollow is being deepened by the carpet, by a fraction of a millimeter per year, by a fraction of a fraction, and the deepening is being matched by the deepening of the dust on the floor, so that the floor of the hollow is no longer really stone. It is a deep layer of compacted powder, and beneath the powder, at last, stone, and the powder is the carpet's powder, made of the carpet's eating and the carpet's dead and the carpet's shed skins and the carpet's own grinding-down of itself, and the carpet moves on top of it the way a slow river moves on top of its own silt, and the silt is the river, and the river is the silt, and the hollow is the shape they have agreed on for now.

They will outlast the machines. This is not a guess. The shafts will seize. The belts will finally tear through. The housings will rust through. The corrugated roof will fall in entirely. And the carpet will still be there, eating the dust, excreting the finer dust, wearing the stone down to make more dust, wearing itself down to make more dust, the current still turning, the eddies still eddying, the clusters still clustering in the places where the dust is finest, the smaller ones still being opened along the soft underside in the lee of something that is not paying attention. They will be here when there is nothing in the hollow but the hollow. They will be the hollow, then.

They are red. They are the color of a thing that has been dried in the dark for a long time. They are clicking. They are patient. They are very, very many, and very, very small, and they are winning, and they do not know they are winning, and it does not matter whether they know. They will be here for a long time after the machines have forgotten how to turn, and after that for a long time after the dust has forgotten it was ever stone, and after that for as long as there is anything at all in this cool hidden loud place that can be ground finer by a small hard body that does not need to drink.
