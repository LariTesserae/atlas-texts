The smallest ones are everywhere.

To call them smallest is misleading — they are not the young of anything, or not only the young. They are their own size, a complete size, a size that has been refined. Most are no bigger than a closed fist. A few are smaller — the size of a thumb joint, the size of a pebble — and these are the ones that fit into the gaps between the others, that complete the packing the way mortar completes a wall, except the mortar is also alive, and the wall is breathing.

They are dark. The color of the basalt they live on, the color of the dusk they live in. A black that is not quite black — a black with red in it, the color of cooled blood, of old iron, of the inside of a kiln after the fire has gone out. Underneath, where the underside meets the hot stone, the color is lighter, the dull cherry of warming rock, and this is the only place the small ones ever show their lighter color, because the small ones rarely lift, rarely shift, rarely expose what is not meant to be exposed. They live pressed down. They live pressed against each other.

Their bodies are textured. Ridged, faceted, sometimes leathery, sometimes crystalline — and the boundary between these textures is not fixed. A single body can be smooth on one side and faceted on the other, leathery where it has stretched and crystalline where it has not, and the boundary between the two will move over the years, the way a coastline moves. The inside of each one is, in some sense, trying to become the outside. If you sliced one open — though nothing here has the means to slice, and nothing here would want to — you would find that the layering continues inward, that the texture is not a skin but a habit, that the body is faceted all the way down to whatever a body like this has instead of a center.

They have no faces.

This is worth saying carefully, because it is easy to misread. They are not faceless the way a stone is faceless. They are faceless the way a hand is faceless — the way a thing that is all function and no expression has no face. There is no front of them and no back of them, or the front and the back shift. Where one of them presses against a neighbor, that side is not the front and not the back; it is the side of contact, and the side of contact is the side that matters, and the face, if there is a face, is the whole underside against the stone, or the whole side against another body.

Some of them have what you might call limbs. Stubby extensions, no joints you could count, no fingers, no toes. They are used for leverage — for pushing against a neighbor, for wedging into a gap, for the slow work of moving. A limb extended is a limb at the end of its reach, and the small ones do not extend them often. They flow more than they walk. They are pushed by the bodies behind them as much as they pull with the bodies in front of them, and the difference between push and pull, at this scale and at this patience, is not a difference at all.

Some have no limbs at all. These are the smallest. They ooze. They are shaped by the bodies around them, the way water is shaped by the stones of a stream, and over the years they take on the shapes of the spaces they have lived in, becoming faceted themselves, becoming crystalline, becoming the texture of the gaps.

They are quiet.

Not silent. There is a sound, or a near-sound, a subsonic whisper below the threshold, a soft continuous papery rustle that you feel in your chest and in the soles of your feet. It is the sound of many small bodies moving against each other, of skin against skin against skin, of the slight give and slight resistance of living matter pressed close. If you put your ear to the carpet — if you could get your ear that close without becoming part of the carpet — you would hear it more clearly, and you would hear, under it, the faint wet sounds of the larger ones grinding against each other when they shift, and under that, deeper, the slow ticking of cooling basalt, the patient sound of a planet's belly working. And you would hear, very occasionally, a small sound, very small, that might be a distress call from something caught beneath something larger, or that might be the stone cooling, and you would not be able to tell which.

The small ones do not speak. They do not signal. They do not chirp or call or sing. If they communicate, they communicate in chemistry — in a slow bleed of substances from one body into the bodies it touches, in a faint thermal signature that varies, in a pressure against neighbors that says something we do not have the word for. The bowl is full of that speech. It has been full of it for longer than anyone listening could remember.

The medium-sized ones are the size of a clenched hand. They are rarer than the small ones — perhaps one in twenty, perhaps one in fifty. They are darker, more solidly colored, more textured, more dense. They are the grazers. They feed on the small ones slowly, taking one every few days, never in a rush, the way a cow grazes a field. A medium-sized one settling into a patch of carpet is a slow event. The small ones around it do not flee. They adjust. They flow around it, the way water flows around a stone in a stream, and after a few days the medium-sized one is incorporated into the carpet, indistinguishable from its neighbors except by its size, and the small ones it has eaten have been replaced by the small ones flowing in from elsewhere, and the economy of the patch continues.

The large ones are different.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the bowl at any time. They are the size of a sleeping child, the size of a curled dog. They are old. They have been growing, slowly, for longer than any structure in the bowl can attest, feeding on the medium ones and on the heat of the deepest thermal plumes, positioning themselves over the cracks where the heat rises strongest, settling in for years, for decades, becoming almost geological in their patience. Their surfaces are the most textured of any in the bowl — heavily ridged, heavily faceted, the crystalline and the leathery both at once and neither at once, the way a weathered boulder is both and neither. Their undersides, where they meet the hottest stone, glow faintly, a dim cherry red that you can see only in the deepest dusk, only when the creature shifts and exposes the underside to your view. The glow is not bioluminescence. It is the glow of heated matter. The creature has become warm enough to glow, and the glow is the sign that the creature is still feeding, still drawing heat from the stone, still growing imperceptibly into its next year.

The large ones do not move often. When they move, the carpet around them ripples for a hundred body-widths in every direction, a slow wave of adjustment that takes hours to subside. When a large one shifts to a new thermal plume, the small ones and the medium ones it displaces flow into the spaces it has left, and those spaces are filled within days, and the circulation of the bowl continues, and the large one settles, and the bowl accommodates it the way a bowl accommodates anything.

The large ones are the closest thing the bowl has to elders. Not because they are wise — wisdom is a category that does not apply here, or that applies so far outside our categories that calling it wisdom is only a guess — but because they have been here longest, and because they have absorbed the most heat, and because they are the most textured, the most faceted, the most like the stone itself. If you did not know what you were looking at, you might mistake a large one for a boulder that had grown a kind of skin. If you knew what you were looking at, you would not mistake it for anything. You would simply stand at its edge and feel the heat pouring off it, and you would not have words for what you were seeing.

The large ones, when they die, do not die all at once. They fade. They lose heat. The glow goes out of them. They become, over years, indistinguishable from the stone beneath them, and the stone is indistinguishable from them, and the carpet flows over them, and the small ones feed on the shed matter of their last years, and the medium ones graze on the small ones, and the large ones of the next generation settle over the place where the old large one used to be, drawn by the heat that still rises through the stone there — heat that the old large one had been feeding on, that the new large one will feed on in turn. The bowl does not mourn. The bowl continues.

The structures are scattered through the carpet like stones in a slow river, and the carpet flows around them the way a slow river flows around stones, without acknowledging them, without avoiding them, simply incorporating them into the shape of its motion.

There are four of them, or five, depending on how you count the cairn that is being consumed.

The cairn is near the center of the bowl. It was once, perhaps, twice the height of a person. Now its top barely clears the carpet. The fitted stones of it are still holding their shape, but they are being slowly absorbed — the small ones have flowed into the gaps between the stones, the medium ones have grazed at the edges, the larger ones have settled against it, and over the years it has become less a cairn and more a low rise in the carpet, a place where the creatures pile slightly higher than they do elsewhere. In another century it will be just a rise. In two centuries it will be just a place. In three it will be nothing the carpet remembers.

The line of upright slabs is at the eastern edge. Five or six of them, weathered almost to shapelessness, leaning at the same angle, as if they fell at the same moment, long ago, and have not been righted. They are leaned.

The platform is in the southwest. Fitted basalt, six paces across, with a shallow depression in the center that catches nothing because nothing falls here. The depression is older than the platform, or the platform is older than the depression. The depression has a smoothness to it that the platform does not, a polish that could be the polish of use or the polish of weather or the polish of something else entirely.

The fragment of wall is half-buried in the moving dark, with a single corner you could stand in if you were small enough and patient enough. The creatures flow around it, over it, into the gaps of it.

No tool marks on any of them. No carving. No writing. No image. They are not ornamented. They are positioned. The line is a line. The cairn is a cairn. The platform is a platform. The wall has a corner. These are the gestures of someone, or something, that knew how to place stones in meaningful arrangements and then stopped, or died, or left, or became what is in the bowl now, or never was here at all and the stones placed themselves.

The creatures may be what is left of whoever placed the stones. The creatures may be what the placer became — a long transformation, a settling into the bowl, a giving-up of limbs and faces and the gestures of placement in exchange for the textures of stone and the patience of heat. The creatures may have nothing to do with the placer, and the placer may have been here and gone and left no other trace, and the cairn and the line and the platform and the wall may be all that is left of a presence that has been absent for longer than the creatures can attest. The bowl does not say. The bowl holds both possibilities, the way it holds heat, the way it holds the carpet, without preferring one over the other.

The structures have become part of the bowl the way a boulder is part of a hillside. They are not maintained. They are not visited. They are not decorated. They are placed, and the placed-ness of them is what makes them structures rather than rocks, and the placed-ness is becoming, with each passing year, less a placed-ness and more a property of the bowl, the way a strangeness in a landscape becomes, over time, simply a feature of the landscape.

---

And then there is the circle.

In the deepest part of the bowl, where the heat is strongest, where the largest creatures lie, there is a place the creatures do not go.

It is a circle, perhaps ten paces across, of bare stone. The stone inside the circle is hot past hot. It glows a faint cherry red, even in the dusk, even through the shimmer that rises from it. The creatures flow around it with a wide berth, the way water flows around a stone in a stream, the way a school of fish gives a rock its distance. They do not touch it. They do not cross it. They give it perhaps two body-widths of clearance on every side, and the space inside that clearance is empty of them, a ring of bare stone around the glowing circle, a moat of heat.

Inside the circle, nothing moves.

The air above the circle is different — clearer, perhaps, less distorted by the heat-shimmer, or more distorted, the shimmer compressed into a still column that rises straight up out of the bowl. If you stood at the edge of the circle, you would feel the heat on your face like an open oven door. If you stepped into it, you would not step out, or you would step out different, or you would not step out at all. The creatures know this. They have always known this. The circle is not theirs.

The circle is part of the bowl, but the bowl is not the master of the circle. The circle is older than the bowl, or the bowl is older than the circle, and there is no way to know. The circle may be the thing the bowl was built around, even though the bowl was not built. The circle may be the only thing in the bowl that has never been touched, never been altered, never been accommodated by anything, even though everything in the bowl has been accommodated by it.

The creatures work around it the way a tide works around a rock. They have shaped their entire slow circulation to accommodate it, the way a river accommodates the boulder in its bed. Remove the circle and the bowl would not be the bowl. The circle is the thing the bowl is built around, even though the bowl is not built. The creatures may not know why they refuse it. They simply flow around it, the way a river flows around a boulder, and the flowing-around is the shape of their lives, and the shape of their lives is the shape of the bowl.

---

That is what lives here.

Many small dark patient things, packed shoulder to shoulder, drifting in slow tides toward warmth, feeding on each other and on the heat that rises through the stone, becoming, over the years, almost indistinguishable from the stone they live on. Scattered among them, the weathered remains of something that once placed stones in meaningful arrangements and then stopped, or became what they are now, or was never there at all. And at the center of their deepest attention, a circle of hotter heat that nothing will cross, that has shaped their lives without their knowing, that may be older than they are, or that they may be older than, and that no one in the bowl — if there is a no one in the bowl — can say.
