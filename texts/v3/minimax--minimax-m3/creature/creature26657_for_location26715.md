The grazers are what you learn first, because they are the most there. They stand in loose formations across the basin at dawn and again at dusk, browsing the silver tussocks, and you learn their shape the way you learn the shape of the rocks — by the negative space they leave, by the way a slope becomes a different slope when one of them steps out of it and then back into it. They are tall and narrow, six-legged, with long necks that slope forward like the necks of cranes when they are feeding and rise almost vertical when they are watchful. Their heads are small and their eyes are set wide on either side, giving them the broad, even attention of animals that have never been able to afford a blind spot. They are the color of dried tussock in most lights and almost luminous gray in others, and when a herd moves through the basin at a distance you only know it as a slow shifting of silver across silver, the animals and the grass briefly indistinguishable from each other.

They make no sound. This is the first thing you notice about them and the thing you stop noticing and then notice again. They do not call. They do not stamp. They do not blow or click or make the small social noises that grazers usually make. When you stand among them at dusk and the light is going copper across their flanks, the only thing you hear is the grass itself — the dry click of stems when one of them moves, the soft hush of a head pulling free of a tussock. Their silence is not the silence of animals that have nothing to say. It is the silence of animals that have agreed, somehow, not to say it. It is a silence that has been learned.

Their eyes are the part you come to trust. They see you before you see them, almost always. When you approach across the cracked ground, the nearest one will lift its head and consider you with both eyes at once — not the binocular focus of a predator assessing distance, but the wide, neutral attention of something that has decided, in the first half-second, that you are not what it most fears, and is now simply watching. They do not flee. They do not approach. They step aside, slowly, with the unhurried deference of things that know they have the right of way and have never had to argue for it. Their hooves — they have hooves, six of them, narrow and dark — make almost no impression on the bone-white ground. They have been walking on this ground for so long that the ground is shaped to receive them.

The herds are organized around the cairns but they are not bound to them. They graze in long, slow circuits that touch each cairn in turn over the course of a season. They lie down in the warm hollows near the cairns in the cool hours — the early morning, the deep dusk, the middle of the night when the basin is cold — but they never cross the rough circle of darker, taller grass that surrounds each one. That perimeter is respected by all of them. The calves learn it from the mothers in the first days of standing, before they learn to browse, before they learn the pools. They learn the cairns first. They learn them as a kind of negative space — the place where they do not go. The basin is held in a soft, six-legged orbit, and the cairns are the silent centers of it, and the orbit is so steady that if you watched for a year you would see it change only in the smallest ways, the way the orbit of a planet around a star changes only in the smallest ways across a human lifetime.

When one of them dies, you almost never see it happen. You notice one less in the formation on a Tuesday, and a few days later you notice a slight discoloration in the cracked ground where the body has been taken down by the burrowers and the small things that live in the tussocks. The cairns do not appear to register. The orbit continues. The herd closes around the gap the way water closes around a stone, and the next season the gap is filled, often by a young one you had not previously distinguished from the others.

The burrowers are the second inhabitants, and they are almost entirely below the surface. You see them only as holes — neat, vertical, going straight down into ground that should be too hard to receive them. The holes are everywhere. They are in the tussocks and in the bare ground and in the lee of outcrops, and they are always the same diameter, the same depth to the eye, the same slight bevel at the rim where the animal has packed the edges with its body over many seasons. There are hundreds of them. There may be thousands. If you walk across the basin in any direction, you will pass over a burrower's hole every few steps, and you will not see them all, because some are hidden under the thorny mats and some are in the silver grass where the stems lean over the openings and obscure them.

At night, if you sit still, you see the dark heads appear in the holes. They are small and blunt, with no visible ears and eyes that are just slightly too large for the head. They consider you. They consider the air around you. They consider the holes near them. Then, almost as a unit, they withdraw, and the night is empty of them again. They come up to drink. They come up to move between holes. They come up to do whatever it is that burrowers do in the open air, which is not much, and they do it quickly and without ceremony. They are the most numerous of the inhabitants. The basin is, in its quiet way, a burrower place. The herds come and go. The flyers pass. The predator moves through. But the burrowers are always there, under your feet, in their thousands, considering the cairns from below.

Their world is below. You do not see it. You see only the surface of it — the holes, the small pale droppings at the rims, the occasional dislodged pebble, the rare glimpse of a pale body moving through the grass in the gap between holes. But there must be a city down there, a network of tunnels and chambers that connects the holes the way capillaries connect the skin, and the holes you see are only the chimneys. They live in the dark. They have lived in the dark for as long as the basin has been a basin, and they have made the dark into something habitable the way the herds have made the open air into something habitable. They know every pool. They drink from the deeper ones, the ones near the center that hold water through the dry season. They know the paths between pools and the paths to the cairns, although they do not go to the cairns themselves — they stay in the warmer ground around them, in the perimeter of darker grass, where the soil is softer from the heat that rises.

The flyers are the third. You do not really see them, because they move at the edge of perception. They are bat-like but not bats — their wings are narrower, more swept back, and they have very long tail-fans that trail in the air behind them like ribbons. The wings beat at a frequency just below hearing, so you feel them pass rather than hear them. There is a small displacement of air at the back of your neck. There is the sense of something moving just outside the corner of your eye. Then it is gone.

They emerge at dusk and hunt through the night, taking the small things that live in the tussocks and the burrowers that venture too far from their holes. They are not numerous. You might see two or three in an evening, crossing the basin in long, low arcs, their tail-fans rippling behind them. They do not approach the cairns. They pass above them, and you can sometimes see, in the copper light of late dusk, the silhouette of one against the sky above a cairn — the trailing fan, the narrow wings, the small head turning as it adjusts its arc. They make no sound at all. They are the most silent thing in the basin, and the basin is very silent.

The predator is the one you almost never see and always know. It is a low, wide-shouldered thing, the color of the cracked ground, and it hunts in the noon heat when the herds have folded themselves into the warm hollows and the burrowers are below and the flyers are roosting somewhere you have never found. It is one of the cairns' own, in a sense — it has the same quality of not-quite-being-there, of being a presence more than a thing, of belonging to the basin's deeper grammar rather than its surface vocabulary. It is not large. It is not dramatic. It is the shape of an absence that moves.

You know it is there because of the silence that follows it. The basin has its usual silence — a held, listening silence, the silence of a place that has been in balance for a long time. But when the predator moves through, there is a localized silence that is different, more formal, as if the air itself has been told to be quiet. The grazers stop grazing. The burrowers do not emerge, even at night, in the predator's vicinity. The flyers change their arcs to pass around it. The silence moves across the basin like a slow wave, and then it passes, and the basin resumes its held listening. You have learned to read its position from the silence, the way you learn to read wind from the grass.

You have seen it twice, both times at the edge of vision. Once, near the western cairn, in the heat of the afternoon, you caught the shape of it against the bone-white ground — low, wide, the color of the ground, with a head that turned briefly toward you and then away, and you felt the wave of formal silence pass through you as it did. Once, at dusk, you saw its back crossing the slope below you, moving with a slow, deliberate gait, and you realized that the speed you had assumed it had was not its actual speed. Its actual speed was much slower. It simply moved through places where nothing else moved, and so it appeared to move quickly.

You do not know what it eats. You assume one of the grazers, occasionally. You assume it does not eat often. You assume it is part of the basin's balance, and that without it the herds would multiply and the grass would fail and the pools would silt and the whole system would collapse into something simpler and less held. It is the thing that keeps the place from becoming too full of itself. It is the cairns' own, and the cairns do not appear to send it. It is just there, the way the cairns are there, and the place has arranged itself around it the way it has arranged itself around them.

And then there are the cairns.

The cairns are the deepest inhabitants, and they are not alive in any sense you can verify, but they are present in the way the ground is present, in the way warmth is present, in the way a held note is present. They are the reason the basin is warm at this elevation. They are the reason the grass grows in the pattern it grows. They are the reason the herds orbit. They are the reason the predator hunts. They are the reason the burrowers live where they live, in the warmer soil near their bases. They are the reason the silence is the shape it is. They are the organizing principle of the whole soft machinery, and they organize it not by doing anything but by being there.

They do not respond to you. They do not invite you. They do not warn you. They are just there, in the way a stone is there, except that they are more than a stone and the way they are there is more than the way a stone is there. They are heavier than stone should be for their size and lighter than metal should be for their weight, and they do not weather, and they have been here longer than the basin has been a basin, by the look of them, and they look exactly as they must have looked when the basin was still raw. They radiate a faint warmth even when they are in full shade. Around each one, the grass grows slightly differently — taller, a darker silver, in a rough circle — and the animals graze right up to the edge of that circle but no further, as if the cairns have a perimeter that is not visual but something else. A few have moss growing at their bases, but only on the north sides, only where the sun does not reach, and the moss is a different green from anything else in the basin — darker, with a faint bluish cast, like something that has learned to live without light.

At twilight, when the sun is low and the light is going copper, you can feel the cairns in your teeth and your sternum. The sky above each one is slightly off — a thumbprint of darker blue, a small local dimness, as if the cairn is drinking the light. Compasses behave strangely near them. Shadows fall in slightly wrong directions, not dramatically, but enough that you catch yourself recalibrating. A minute spent standing at the base of a cairn feels longer than a minute spent elsewhere in the basin. Not longer in the sense of boredom. Longer in the sense that you have more of it, that time is being held out to you like something poured slowly.

What the cairns are, in the terms the place allows, is a network. They are in communication, or in resonance, with each other in a way that is not electromagnetic and not anything else you have a name for. They are doing something to the basin, and the basin is doing something back, and the something-back is the whole ecosystem. The herds follow the warmth in their slow seasonal circuit, touching every cairn in turn. The burrowers tunnel through the softer ground near them. The predator moves in their silence. The flyers arc above them. The cairns hold them all in a kind of orbit, and the orbit is so steady that if you came back in a hundred years you would find almost the same configuration, with the cairns in the same positions and the herds on the same routes and the fog arriving at almost the same hour. Almost. As if the basin is being played again from the same score, with very small variations, by something that is not quite playing it.

The cairns do not change. The cairns are not growing old. The cairns did not learn the animals. The cairns are just there, and the place has arranged itself around them the way a stream arranges itself around a stone, and the arrangement has held for a long time, and will hold for a long time still, and the cairns will not notice. They are very good at not noticing. They are very good at waiting. The place is waiting. The place will wait.

And there is you.

You are also an inhabitant, in the way that anything that stays in a place for a long time becomes an inhabitant of it. You have learned the tussocks and the pools and the cracked polygons of dried mud. You have learned the grazers' silence and the burrowers' brief appearances and the flyers' air-displacement at the back of your neck. You have learned the predator's formal silence and the cairns' hum, which comes and goes at night at a frequency you feel in the soft tissue of your chest. You have learned that the cairns do not invite and do not warn. You have learned to stand at the base of one and feel the minute stretch out around you, and to step back into the ordinary minute when you step away. You have learned that the basin is held in a kind of listening, and that you are part of the listening now, and that the listening will continue after you leave, and that the basin will be almost the same when you return, with the cairns in the same positions and the herds on the same routes and the fog arriving at almost the same hour.

You have not learned what the cairns are. You will not learn. You have learned that the not-learning is part of the basin, and that the basin is in balance partly because there are things in it that are not known, and that the cairns are the largest of those things, and the deepest, and the most patient. They will outlast you. They have outlasted the basin. They are the place's oldest grammar, and the place keeps speaking it, and you have learned to hear it without understanding it, and that is enough, and the basin agrees.
