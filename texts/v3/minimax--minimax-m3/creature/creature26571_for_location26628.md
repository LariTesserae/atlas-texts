What lives in the Manufactured Plain is, in the largest sense, a single population that has forgotten how to be a population and become instead a thickness — a stratum, a kind of second skin the place wears, distributed so evenly across the vast finished surface that any individual creature is less a member of a community than a cell in a tissue. They are everywhere in the same way that warmth is everywhere here, and they belong to the place the way the rust belongs to an iron beam: not as decoration, not as visitors, but as the surface's own ongoing way of being unfinished in a place that is otherwise completely finished. To name them is already to lose something, because they are nameless in the languages that exist for such things, but you have to start somewhere, so start with the smallest, and climb.

The smallest are invisible the way dust is invisible — you don't see the individual, you see the slight blur they put on whatever they cover. Underfoot, in the damp sheen of the sealed panels, they form a moving bloom that has the optical effect of a faint shimmer, as though the floor itself were breathing. Magnify one and it is mostly leg. Six legs, jointed like grass stems, tipped with whatever the analog of a hoof is at this scale: a flattened pad that distributes weight across panel and machine alike. The body is a soft oblong, lighter than it looks, the color of wet graphite. It does not appear to have a face in any direction you would call a face. The front is the front because that is the direction it moves, and the front has two dark spots that might be sensors and might be pigment and might be both and might be neither. It eats by touching. The pads of its forelegs are also its mouth in some functional sense; it grazes the condensation on the housing walls the way a sheep grazes a slope, slowly, head-down, taking whatever the surface presents: a film of mineral-laden moisture, the residue of the factory's exhalations, the bodies of the smaller things that died at dawn (the same dawn, every dawn, of which there is always exactly the same number). It does not hurry. Nothing here hurries. It puts one foot down, then another, then another, and the noise of that — the noise of uncountable feet at this scale — is the hiss you hear when you stand on the Plain and listen, the white noise of a place that has settled.

Bigger than that, by a factor you can only estimate because your eye cannot find an edge to the carpet of them, are the grazers proper. They are still small — thumb-sized if you held one, but you cannot hold one, because the moment your hand approached the panel they were on they would all lift, a synchronized startle, a wave running outward from your shadow at maybe a meter a second, and then resettle when your shadow passed. Their bodies are darker, flatter, with a faint keel along the back that might be structural and might be a gut. They have more legs than the smallest things, or the same number of legs and longer ones; you cannot tell from above. They move in lanes, very faintly, the way very dense herds of very small animals move in lanes — not deliberate, not policed, but emergent, the inevitable geometry of too many bodies in the same space needing to flow around each other without colliding. Their lanes run perpendicular to the water channels. The water channels are darker than the rest of the floor and the grazers orient toward them in the same way that lodestone filings orient toward a pole. You can see this only as a pattern, not as an individual choice: a slow tide of bodies toward the dark lines, and a slow tide back, and the cycles of the tides are as regular as the pistons. They eat the smaller things. They eat the films. They eat each other when the chance offers, which is always and never — always offered, never quite consummated, because the system is in equilibrium and so the deaths at this scale are exactly balanced by the births at the smaller scale, and the deaths at the smaller scale are exactly balanced by whatever the smallest scale eats, which is the surface of the machines and the condensation and the air and the rain.

The ones on the vertical walls are a different mode of the same body, or the same body in a different mood, or a different species you cannot easily distinguish from this distance. They cling by the same pads, climb by the same pads, and they orient their long axes along the slope of the housing they are on. Where the housing is vertical they hang like lichen; where it is overhanging they hang upside down, and the pads of their feet accept either direction as equivalent. Their coloration is slightly different — a browner gray, the gray of old machinery in a damp place — and they are slightly larger. You see them only as a texture on the housing face, a moving texture that thickens and thins as the hours pass, but the thickening and thinning follow no pattern you can read. Maybe the heat. Maybe the internal cycling of the equipment. Maybe just the same internal logic the system runs on, the closed loop, the held breath. They do not eat each other in your sight. You would not see them eat each other from a distance; you do not see the act at this scale unless you kneel and wait and are very lucky. What you see is the climb. They climb up, they climb down, they stay, they go. The wall is alive the way a tide pool is alive: full of motion that does not sum to anything except more of the same motion.

In the water channels — in the dark capillary streams that carry the warmer water back toward the cooling arrays and the deeper substrate — the creatures are denser and slower. They are not fish, because nothing here is fish-shaped, but they are what fish-shaped would mean in this place: bodies half in the current, half out, packed shoulder to shoulder, oriented mostly down-current, holding their place by some combination of weight and suction and small adjustments of leg. They breathe the warm water. They breathe whatever is dissolved in the warm water, the dark mineral-and-polymer soup that the floor leaches and the machines exhale and the rain picks up on its way down. They are darker than the wall-climbers, almost black, with a wet shine that is not wetness but the actual appearance of the cuticle. They do not move much; they shift, they resettle, they let the current move around them. Sometimes one climbs out and joins the carpet on the adjacent panel, and the carpet there thins by one and the carpet on the channel thickens by one, and the system has not changed.

The largest of the visible fauna are the size of a thumb joint, and you see them only occasionally, mostly at the edges of the water channels or where two housings meet at a seam and the geometry forms a small sheltered lee. They are darker still. They have what looks like a head — a slight thickening at one end, with darker spots that are either eyes or the place where eyes would be if anything here needed eyes. They chirp. The chirp is a single syllable, repeated at long intervals, and it is the only sound the Plain produces that is not the hiss and not the rumble. It is low, almost subsonic, and it is the same chirp every time, and you cannot find a pattern in when it comes — except, after you stand very still for a long time, you might notice that the chirp comes when the largest of them is in the act of catching one of the smaller ones, and after the catch there is a pause, and then the chirp resumes from a slightly different position. So the chirp is the sound of successful predation, or the sound of digestion beginning, or both, and it serves no purpose you can identify except to mark, at intervals, that the loop is closed: a small thing has been eaten by a slightly larger thing.

Above the carpet, in the lowest meter of atmosphere, in the fog band that forms and dissipates and reforms at the same height, there is a drift of creatures that have given up the surface, or that only visit it. They are the same size as the grazers, mostly, but their bodies are differently shaped — lighter, with broader pads and a fluff of fine hair that holds the air. They drift. They do not fly, exactly; they leap from the substrate, open their pads to the warm rising air, and let the thermals carry them sideways for a meter or two, and then they settle, and then they leap again. The collective effect, when you look at the fog band from a distance, is of a slow horizontal river of small bodies in the air, going the same direction at the same speed, because the wind is the same wind and the thermals are the same thermals and the place is the place it is. They are eating, up there. They are eating the dust of the smallest things that have dried, or they are eating nothing, or they are eating the air; you cannot tell from below. They are the only fauna that risks the open sky, and they do not risk it for long.

None of them are named. They do not have names in any sense that would survive translation, because the place they live in has no language that you know of, and the language it does have — the bracketed script on the housings, the angular sigils on the junction blocks — does not name fauna. It names equipment. It names outputs. It names the parts of the system that are made. The fauna are not made. They are what happened when a closed system ran for long enough that something had to live in it, and so something did, and what lived in it is what's here: a tissue of small bodies in several overlapping scales, doing several overlapping things, none of which alter the place, all of which depend on the place, and which the place depends on in ways the machinery does not care about and the humidity does not mind.

If you watched one — if you knelt at the edge of a panel and watched a single grazer for as long as you could keep your eyes on it — you would see it touch the surface with its forefoot, pause, move forward one body-length, touch again, pause. You would see it encounter another grazer coming the other way and adjust course by a fraction. You would see it reach the edge of the panel and stop, and turn, and head back. You would see it pause where the surface is warmer and pause where it is cooler. You would see, after what felt like a long time, the pad of its forefoot descend onto a smaller creature, and the smaller creature would cease to be a creature and become a film, and the grazer would absorb the film and continue. You would see this happen once, perhaps twice, before you stood back up and found that your knees were wet with the same warm damp and that the hiss was the same and that the fog was at the same height. You would look back at the place where the grazer had been and there would be a grazer there, but it would not be the same one. It would be the same shape. It would be doing the same thing. It would be the next unit in the same series, indistinguishable from the last one and the next one, and you would not be able to tell if you had watched a single lifetime or a single instance of a thing that the place keeps producing and re-producing at the same rate, in the same loop, in the same held breath of warm and wet and hiss.

This is what lives here. A tissue. A thickness. A weather of small bodies that is as steady as the weather of the air above them, and as quiet, and as given to the place as the rust and the condensation and the rumble of the machines in the floor.
