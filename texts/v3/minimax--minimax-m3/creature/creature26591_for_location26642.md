The room has been given over.

That is the first thing to understand. The room is no longer a place where anyone works. The levers in the control pit have rusted into their last position. The needles on the gauges are either still or moving in fractions. The people who would have read them are not coming back, and what remains is not a workplace but a tenancy — a long, slow, unforced tenancy held by whatever has been willing to stay.

What has been willing to stay is not much, by the usual count. A handful of creatures. A skin of growth on the iron. The room itself, which is in some sense the largest thing that lives here.

The largest animal is the thing on the upper pipes.

You never see it all at once. You see it in pieces. The dust on a pipe is disturbed — a long pale line, as if something heavy has dragged itself forward — and then there is a stillness, and then you look up and the dust is settled somewhere else, a meter to the left, and you understand that whatever moved has moved, and is now watching from a different angle. It is the color of rust and the color of dust, and it is patient in the way that only an animal with no schedule can be patient. It does not hunt. There is nothing here to hunt that would still be worth chasing. It does not forage with urgency. It moves along the top of the bundles in the red light, and pauses, and the pause is the length of a held breath, and then it moves again.

It is the size of a rat. It might be the size of a small cat. The estimate is hard to make because you never see it against anything you can measure. It is a displacement of dust. It is a place where the light fails to be quite the right light. You see its shape when it crosses the line where the red bulb throws a sheen on the wet pipe, and the shape is low, and long, and its belly is close to the iron. It does not seem to have a tail you can be sure of. It might have one. It might not. The detail is uncertain, and the room is not in the business of clarifying.

What it has, instead of a tail, is a kind of knowing. It knows the upper pipes. It knows the bundles that run parallel to the wall, and the ones that hang on iron hooks, and the ones that disappear into the dark above. It knows where the heat is strongest. It knows where the lagging has rotted away and the bare pipe is warm to the touch, and it travels those lengths when it wants warmth. It knows where the drips fall in patterns, and it travels under those patterns when it wants to drink. It is not a creature of the floor. The floor is for things that do not yet know better. The floor is where the moth comes to die.

The moth came down the stairs. You can tell it came down the stairs because its wings are broken in the way that wings are broken when an animal has tried to fly upward through a long narrow space and struck the iron on either side, and then struck it again, and then struck it a third time, and finally given up and come to rest on the nearest horizontal surface, which was the grating near your feet. It is a small moth, the color of dust, with a powder on its wings that has come away on the metal and left a faint pale print. It is breathing. You can see it breathing — the body pulses, very small, very fast, the way moths breathe when they are at the end of what they have. It does not know where it is. It knows that it is warm, and that the air is wet, and that the red light is the only light, and that none of these are the things it came down the stairs to find. It will be still for a while. Then its body will stop pulsing, and the room will have accepted it.

The beetle knows where it is going.

The beetle is small, and black, and it walks the underside of a pipe in a line that has no apparent reason. It walks forward for the length of pipe between two flanges, which is about four feet. It turns — without hesitating, without the small correction that a beetle on a flat surface makes when it turns — and walks back the way it came. The turning is not really a turn. It is more like a thought. The beetle arrives at the end of its outward journey, and the journey ends, and the beetle is suddenly facing the other direction, and it walks back.

What is on the underside of the pipe that the beetle is walking? There is a thin film of something — not quite moisture, not quite dust, not quite the smear of the lichen. It is the layer that forms on a surface that has been wet for a long time and is not quite wet anymore. It is the layer that an iron pipe acquires when it has been sweating for years. The beetle eats this layer, or the things that live in this layer, which are smaller still than the beetle. The beetle is a citizen of the underside. The beetle has probably lived its entire life on this one pipe, or on a small set of pipes, and it walks the routes it walks because the routes are the routes that have food on them. It does not know the room. It knows a pipe. The pipe is enough.

The lichen is slower than the beetle and larger than the moth.

The lichen has colonized the underside of one of the lower pipe runs in a mat that is about the size of an outstretched hand. It is the color of dried blood, or of very old iron, or of the brick itself — it has chosen its colors carefully, or the room has chosen them for it. It is leathery. If you touched it, your fingers would come away with a dark smear, and the smear would smell, very faintly, of the room — of metal, of wet brick, of the long slow heat. The lichen does not move. It has not moved in the time you have been watching. It will not move in the time you are here. It is doing the other work that living things do, which is to occupy a surface and to change it slightly and to be changed by it in return.

The lichen is fed by the condensation. The pipes are warm on the inside, cold on the outside, and the moisture gathers where the two temperatures meet, and the moisture drips, and where the drips land the lichen grows. There is a logic to this. The lichen is dark because dark is what it has learned to be. If you put it in sunlight it would not know what to do. It has given up the sun as a source of information and uses the heat of the pipes instead.

The moss is younger than the lichen. The moss is pale green and has come out of a crack in the brick near the floor and climbed six inches up the wall. Six inches is the height it has chosen, or the height the heat has allowed it. Above six inches the wall is too warm. The moss has stopped. The moss is not dying. It has simply understood that six inches is enough, and it has stayed where staying is possible.

In the angle where a flange meets its pipe there is a small rosette of something with thin grey leaves. The rosette is fed by a single drip that falls from above at a fairly regular interval — perhaps once every forty seconds, perhaps once a minute. The drip lands on the rosette. The rosette has arranged its leaves to catch the drip. The leaves are cupped. The drip does not fall through. The rosette is patient, and the drip is patient, and the arrangement has the look of something that has worked itself out over a long time. The rosette does not have a name you would recognize. It is the kind of thing that has names in books, but the books are about other places. Here it is just the rosette, and it is fed by the drip, and it is doing well.

These are the things that live here. They are not many. They are not loud. They have not been invited. They have simply arrived, in their various ways, and they have stayed, and they have made the room into the only kind of room that suits them.

The room, in turn, is the largest of them.

The room is a kind of animal. Not an animal that moves — an animal that maintains. The room sweats the way a body sweats. The room has a circulation — the water in the channel under the grating, the slow movement of heat from below upward through the brick and the iron. The room has a pulse — the drips, which fall at no regular interval but which fall often enough that the silence between them is never very long. The room has a temperature, which is higher than the temperature outside, and which is maintained by something the room does not show you. The room has been doing these things for longer than any of the creatures have been here, and will go on doing them after the creatures have gone. The room is its own creature, and the things in it are its tenants, and the room is patient with them the way a body is patient with what lives on its skin.

If you stand very still, you can feel the room attend.

The attention is not hostile. It is not curious. It is the attention of something that has been running so long that it has forgotten what running is for, and is simply running. The drips are the room's speech, and the speech is not in any language. The speech is the speech of a body that is doing what bodies do, and that is persisting, and that is the only speech the room has. The heat is the room's warmth, and the warmth is offered without condition. The dark at the back is the room's privacy, and the privacy is not for you, and you are not offended by this, because the room is not hiding from you. The room is simply continuing.

If you listen for the hiss, it will come. If you listen for the clunk, it will come. If you listen for the slow displacement of dust along the upper pipe run, you will hear it, or you will feel it as a small shift in the air, and you will know that the thing on the upper pipes has moved again, and has settled somewhere else, and is watching from there. You will not see it. The room does not want you to see it. The room and the thing have an arrangement, and the arrangement does not include you, and the room is patient with you for as long as you stay, and after you have gone the room will continue, and the thing will continue, and the moth will have stopped pulsing, and the lichen will be a little thicker than it was, and the moss will not have moved, and the rosette will have caught another drip, and the bolt that was rusting through will have rusted through a little more, and the pipe that was hanging an inch lower will be hanging an inch and a quarter lower, and none of this will be observed, and all of it will have happened, and the room will go on being a room, in the dark, in the wet, in the warm.
