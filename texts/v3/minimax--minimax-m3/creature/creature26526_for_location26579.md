# Who Lives Here

The question is asked, sometimes, by people who have come down into the bowl for one of the surveys and have looked around at the basalt and the steam and the ferns and the three geometries in the middle, and have noticed, with the part of their mind that is not yet committed to whatever they came down here to measure, that there is an unusual scarcity of animal life and an unusual presence of something else. They ask the question. The saddle does not answer it. The saddle contains, as far as can be told, what it contains. The list is short. The list is also, in places, difficult to read.

This is what is here.

## The Lizard

The lizard is the most visible resident, though *visible* is doing a lot of work. It is a small thing, dark grey, and it lives almost entirely on the warm rocks within a few meters of the southern hot spring, where the mineral apron has made the ground more hospitable than anywhere else in the saddle. It basks. That is the verb you would use. It sits on a flat stone and lets the geothermal warmth move into it from below while the ambient air does its best to cool it from above, and the difference between the two temperatures is the entire content of its day.

It eats what it can catch. Crane flies, mostly, when they land badly. Beetles, when it can outmaneuver them, which is most of the time, because the beetles here are armored for the steam and not for the lizard, and have not evolved an escape strategy faster than a small grey thing with a quick head. The lizard does not eat the moss. Does not eat the lichen. Does not attempt the orchids, which are tougher than they look and contain, by smell, something the lizard prefers not to discover. It has not, in any way that can be observed, noticed the structures.

When disturbed, it does not run. It ceases to be where it was. It is in a crack that you did not see, and then it is not, and then you have looked away. It has been doing this since the basin uplifted. It will be doing this after you leave. It is the most ordinary thing in the saddle, and the only one whose life you can describe without hedging.

## The Insects

The crane flies are too large. They are too large for the air at this altitude, which is thin and slow to give up its oxygen, and they are too large for the steam, which is thick and warm and makes their wings heavy. They do not fly well. They hover with an effort that is visible in the way their long legs trail behind them and in the way they occasionally set down, briefly, on a flat stone, as if to recover. They are ungainly. They look as though they are holding themselves up by main force, and that is not wrong.

But they are here. They are here in numbers that suggest the saddle is good for them, and it may be that the steam, which would be a liability for almost any other flying insect, is a gift to one that needs the humidity around its spiracles. They mate in the cooler air above the ferns. They die on the warm stones. Their bodies, when they die, do not decay in the way you would expect. The mineral crust takes them. The orange precipitate that builds up on the apron of the hot spring includes, if you look closely, the chitin of crane flies that fell onto it weeks or months ago and have been half-absorbed into the stone. They are becoming the place. They are the place, in a small and accumulating way.

The beetles are smaller, harder to see, and the color of the mineral crust itself. Their shells are the orange of the apron's edge, the cream of its older surfaces, and against the basalt they are nearly invisible. They move in the seams between rocks, in the wet cracks where the steam condenses, and they have the quality of things that have been here longer than they look. They are patient. They do not startle. If you put your hand down near one, it does not scurry. It simply continues what it was doing, at the same pace, with the implication that whatever you are, you are not the most important thing in the seam.

## The Bird

There is a bird in the fern grove. There may be more than one. Almost no one has seen them clearly. They are dull green, the green of the mosses that have not yet decided what color to be, and they keep themselves in the interior of the stand of tree ferns on the western shoulder, where the trunks are black with moisture and the light comes through in single shafts that do not quite reach the ground. They are heard more often than seen, and what is heard is a two-note whistle, descending, that carries across the whole bowl and stops — not fades, *stops* — as if the bird that was making the sound had been interrupted by something with a hand over its mouth.

They are not migratory. They do not leave. They are not, as far as anyone has been able to determine, breeding in the conventional sense, or if they are, the nests have not been found. They live in the ferns. They eat what the ferns contain. They make their whistle. They stop. That is the entire visible shape of their existence.

The ornithologist who visited in 1994 spent eleven days in the saddle and saw the bird twice, both times for less than four seconds. She wrote in her notes that it was "as if the bird had been here a long time and had become cautious in a way that was no longer quite caution." She did not return. A second ornithologist, in 2003, recorded the whistle on audio equipment for forty-one minutes before it stopped in mid-phrase, and did not resume during the remaining six days of the visit. The recording was filed. It has not, as far as can be told, been listened to.

## The Mosses

The mosses are not animals. They are also, in the saddle, not quite plants. They advance across the basalt in slow visible pulses, year by year, in a way that is not how moss is supposed to behave. Moss spreads. Moss does not, as a rule, *advance*. But here, in the seams and on the cooler stones, the colonies have a frontier, and the frontier moves. You can stand at the edge of one and watch the boundary between colonized stone and bare stone and see, if you come back in a year, that the colonized stone has grown by a centimeter, or two, or three. They are doing something on a timescale that is faster than geology and slower than biology, and they are doing it with intent.

Their color is not quite right. It is somewhere between grey and lavender, and the orchids rooted into the cracks share it, and the bird in the fern grove shares it, and it is the color of things that are partly camouflaged against a basalt they have decided, for their own reasons, to resemble. The orchid specialist who came through in 2008 noted in passing that the mosses' color "did not match any documented species in the region" and moved on, and this is what most people who come to the saddle do: they note something that does not match, and they move on. There is a lot to note. There is a lot to move on from.

## The Absent Ones

There used to be more.

In the early surveys, before the basin was understood to be what it is, several attempts were made to introduce small mammals to the saddle. Voles, in 1988. A pair of pikas in 1991. The voles lasted eleven weeks. The pikas lasted longer — almost five months — and then one day they were not there, and there was no sign of them having died. The grass had not been eaten down. There were no bodies. They had been, and then they had not been, in the way that the smudges in the photographs were, and then had not been.

A domestic cat was brought up by a graduate student in 1993 and released near the rim to control a rodent population that did not exist. The cat walked to the southern lip, looked down into the bowl for what the student described as "longer than a cat looks at things," and then walked back up the trail and sat down by the field vehicle and would not move for six hours. The student gave it water. It went home the next morning and, according to the student, never again expressed any interest in going outside.

Larger animals do not come up the trail. The trail is not, in any case, a trail. There is no defined way down into the bowl. The approaches are steep, the footing is bad, and the basalt is sharp. People go down with care. The few raptors that hunt the ridges above have been observed circling the rim and then leaving. They do not descend.

The things that live here are the things that have decided to. The things that decided not to have, on the whole, not come back.

## The Smudges

What else is here.

The cameras were set up near the prism for a season, in the late 1990s, by a team that has since been difficult to trace. They were motion-sensitive. They fired, over three months, eleven thousand times. Most of the photographs are of the basalt. They are of the ferns in wind. They are of the steam moving. They are of the rain, when it came, and of the night sky, which is soft-edged out here because of the moisture. Seven of them are something else.

The seven photographs were taken between three and four in the morning. They show, near the top of the prism, a smudge. The smudge is faint. It is out of focus, which is to say it moved during the exposure, but the way it moved is not the way moths move, and is not the way birds move. The smudge is the wrong shape to be either. It is the wrong color to be either — a slightly luminous grey that does not match anything in the saddle at night. It is present in one frame and absent in the next frame, with the frames separated by less than a second. There is no visible point of arrival in the first frame. There is no visible point of departure in the second. The smudge is, in both frames, at the top of the prism. It is, in both frames, the same shape.

It is not known what these are. The data was, as far as can be determined, not published. The cameras were taken down the following year. The team has not been reached for comment.

## The Structures

Whether the structures live here is a question the saddle does not help with. They are here. They have been here since at least the first survey in 1987, and they were here before that, and they will be here after this, whatever this turns out to be. They do not move. They have not been observed to move. Whether that is because they do not move or because their moving is on a different scale than observation is the kind of question the saddle leaves open.

The largest of them, the six-sided prism, has the property of being slightly warmer than its surroundings. The warmth has nothing to do with the springs. It is a faint, even warmth, distributed across the surface as if from an interior source, and it has been measured, repeatedly, at between 0.4 and 0.7 degrees above the ambient rock. The shallow depression on one of its faces, which collects rainwater and is rimmed with the same orange mineral precipitate that builds the apron of the hot spring, is the warmest point. If you put your hand against the prism on a still day and close your eyes, you can feel the warmth coming through. It does not feel mechanical. It does not feel geological. It feels, in a way that is hard to defend in a scientific register, like something that is awake. The warmth cycles. It rises and falls over a period of roughly eleven minutes, the same period as the hum from the second structure, which is either coincidence or not.

The second structure hums. The frequency is just below the threshold of human hearing, which means most people over forty do not perceive it at all, and most people under forty do, and they have, on average, elected not to stay near it for long. The people who have stayed have described the experience, in terms that vary, as "being listened to" and "being inside something's attention." The hum does not change pitch. It does not change volume, except for the slow eleven-minute rise and fall. It does not change in response to anything that has been tested. It is not clear that it is a hum. It may be the structure's way of breathing, or of counting, or of doing something for which there is not yet a verb.

The third structure does nothing. It sits at its angle, lower and more horizontal than the others, set into the rock in a way that doesn't quite repeat. It is unremarkable in every way except for one. The moss has been unable to colonize a two-meter radius around it, despite twenty years of being there, and the reason for this is not clear. The mosses advance across the basalt elsewhere in the saddle at a measurable rate, in slow visible pulses. They have not crossed the line. There is no visible barrier. The basalt inside the radius is not different from the basalt outside it. The moss simply does not come. As if there is something in the radius that the moss recognizes, and prefers not to be near.

At night, very occasionally, there is a thin line of light at one of the edges of the third structure. It is faint. It lasts between four and nine seconds. It does not recur on any predictable schedule. It is most often reported by people who have been in the saddle long enough to be tired and have, by virtue of being tired, stopped looking for anything in particular. If you happen to be looking at it from the rim at the right moment, the description in one set of field notes goes, it looks as if something inside is checking the time. The light is not the orange of the mineral crust. It is not the grey-lavender of the mosses. It is a pale, clean color that does not appear anywhere else in the saddle, and it does not stay.
