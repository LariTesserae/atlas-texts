The basin holds several kinds of life, and they live at different tempos, and none of them have settled here in quite the same way.

---

The workers do not live here. This is the first thing to make clear about them and the thing that distinguishes them from every other living thing in the basin. They arrive in shifts of two, three, or four weeks, and they leave, and they come back, and they leave again. Their whole relationship to the place is contractual, and it shows in the way they inhabit it. They are tidy. They keep to their facilities. They do not, as a rule, walk out onto the basin floor to look at the stars, and when they do, they do not walk far. They have been told — formally, in the orientations they receive on their first day — that the basin floor is not for them, that the fumaroles and the terraces and the dark stone that is warm under the hand are not for them, that the place is a workplace and not a country. Most of them comply. The few who do not, who in their third or fourth rotation begin to slip out at the edges of shifts and stand on warm patches and listen to the steam, are watched by the others, and quietly, without discussion, the watching has the texture of concern.

There are perhaps three hundred workers on site at any given time, distributed unevenly across the scattered facilities. The processing complex at the western wall has the largest concentration — forty, fifty people in a typical shift, working the tanks and the towers and the walkways that thread between them. The drill platforms have small crews of three to five. The crushing station by the river has a crew of perhaps fifteen, in part because the water requires its own attention. The railhead has its own people, and the pump stations and the monitoring posts each have their one or two. Between the facilities, the workers move by truck along the scraped-in tracks, or by foot along the catwalks that connect some of the closer structures, or rarely, on the small rail carts that run on a narrow line between the crushing station and the processing complex. They do not mingle much across facilities. A driller and a processor may know each other by face and never by name.

They are from several places. The operations language is a working English that has been stripped of its ornament and that takes its nouns from at least four other languages, none of them indigenous to the basin, because there are no indigenous languages of the basin. Before the operation, the place was empty, or close enough to empty that it is treated as empty by everyone who works it. The crews rotate in from cities at lower elevations. They fly in on the small planes that come twice a week. They drive up the switchback when they have reasons not to fly. They bring with them the habits of the places they come from — the breakfast of one crew, the cigarette brand of another, the way a third refuses to wear the masks until the supervisors are looking. These habits fade within a rotation. The basin takes them. People who have been here for years eat the same things, drink the same things, complain about the same things, regardless of where they came from. The basin has a way of smoothing its temporary people down to a common shape, the way the river smooths the stones of its bed.

What they do is the work. The work is extraction. The basin contains a mineral that the operation is mining, and the mineral is unusual — denser in the basin walls than in the surrounding mountains, denser here than in any other surveyed deposit, and the specifics of it are held inside the operation. The workers who handle it directly wear gloves and masks at all times, and the masks are not the cheap paper kind but the sealed kind, and the gloves are thick and replaced often. The material is processed through several stages — crushed, heated, leached, separated — and at each stage it is handled by people in different protective gear. There is a dryness to the way these workers talk about their work. They use the term the operation uses for the mineral, a code term that is not the mineral's real name, and they speak of "the material" and "the product" and "the load" and they do not, in any conversation that can be overheard, use the actual word. Some of them, in private, among themselves, do know the word. They are not supposed to speak it on the radio. Most of them comply.

The work is not dangerous in the way that people from outside imagine mining to be dangerous. There are no cave-ins, no gas pockets, no catastrophic failures of dams. The danger is slow and occupational. The mineral, in its raw form, is something that the body should not be asked to process, and the protective equipment is the boundary. Workers develop small things over time — a dryness in the hands, a particular fatigue that doesn't go away with sleep, an intermittent headache that they learn to ignore. There is a medical rotation. There are quarterly checks. The ones who develop the symptoms badly are quietly reassigned, and they are not talked about.

The workers sleep in bunks that are not theirs. The mess halls feed them food that is not theirs. The mess hall at the processing complex is the largest and has the most regular hours; the others, at the smaller outposts, are run by whoever has the shift and are irregular in a way that the workers adjust to. The food is good. The food is better than the food at most remote operations, because the operation has chosen to spend on this, and because the cook at the processing complex — a man named, depending on who is telling the story, something else — has been here longer than almost anyone else and has made it his business. He knows what people want after a week of altitude and processed air. He knows when someone is about to break. He does not say so. He puts more salt in the soup.

The workers have off-hours, and the off-hours are strange because the basin is a place in which there is nothing to do. There are books that have been left by previous workers, and they circulate. There are cards. There is, at the processing complex, a small common room with a wall of polaroids that the workers have taken over the years — the basin at different times of day, the herds, the steam in different weathers, the faces of people who have come and gone. Some of the faces in the older polaroids are of people no one now remembers. The off-hours are mostly spent not talking, or talking about the places the workers came from, or talking about the basin in the careful oblique way that the workers use when they talk about the basin at all, which is mostly not. There is a bar in the basement of the processing complex that is not called a bar. There is music sometimes. The music is not loud. The basin will not be loud in.

The workers who stay long enough begin to change. This is not a single change. It is a small drift. They begin to walk differently — slower, more attentive to the ground under their feet, as if they have started to notice that the ground is warm. They begin to look up at the condors. They begin to leave small offerings at the edges of the working areas — a stone set on a fence post, a shell, a piece of colored wire — not as offerings exactly, not as ritual, but as something they do with their hands when they don't know what to do with their hands. They begin to notice the smell. The smell that nobody quite identifies. They begin to wonder, sometimes, what they are extracting. They do not ask. They have been told not to ask, and they have also, after enough time, decided on their own not to ask, because the answer would not be what they want it to be.

Most of them rotate out on schedule. They go down the switchback road or onto the small planes and they go home, and the basin lets them go, and they are, within a week or two of being back at lower elevations, themselves again — alert, social, less watchful. Some of them come back, and some of them do not. The ones who do not come back are not talked about.

---

The animals live here. This is the more important fact, and the harder one to understand.

The grazers are the largest of them, and the most numerous, and the most present. There are perhaps eight hundred of them in the basin at any given census, in loose herds of forty to fifty animals that drift across the basin floor in patterns that look casual and are not. The herds are matrilineal, with the older females leading, and the bulls at the edges, and the calves — usually one per year, occasionally two — in the middle of the group, where the adults can keep them. The animals are heavy. A mature cow is the weight of a small horse. They are short-legged and deep-bodied, built low to the ground, and their coats are the color of the dark basalt of the basin walls — not quite black, not quite the iron-red, but something in between that the eye reads as the color of the place itself. Against the stone they are almost invisible until they move. When they move, they move slowly, with a swaying gait that is not quite ungainly but is not quite graceful either. They do not hurry. There is nothing in the basin to hurry from or toward.

What they eat is the basin. They put their broad muzzles to the rock and they work at it, slowly, with a side-to-side motion of the jaw, and what they are chewing is some combination of lichen, of mineral crust, of the dried stalks of the scrub plants where these have persisted through a season, of salts that the fumaroles have deposited, of substances that no chemist has quite cataloged. Their teeth are worn. The biologists who have studied them describe their dentition as *atypical* — a word that recurs in the literature on the basin. The teeth are flatter than they should be, more like the teeth of an animal that grinds stone than an animal that bites vegetation. The droppings are pale, almost chalky, and rich in minerals that are not supposed to be in animal waste. The animals seem to be running the basin through their bodies slowly, taking from it what they need and returning to it what they do not need, and in doing so they are part of the basin. They are part of how the basin does its work.

They drift. They drift from one warm patch to the next, and the warm patches are not random — they are where the fumaroles breathe, where the geothermal heat comes closest to the surface, where the air is dampest and the lichen is thickest. The herds know these patches. They return to them. They also drift to the river, to drink, and to the edges of the working areas, where the operations have set out feed stations that supplement what the basin provides. The feed stations are not strictly necessary. The biologists have run the numbers. The herds would survive without them, but more slowly, with fewer calves, with longer intervals between births. The feed stations keep the population up. Why the operation wants the population up is not something that is discussed. The herds do not need to be kept up. The herds are old.

They are old. This is the thing that the operation has never quite acknowledged, and that the biologists who come up to study them keep hinting at in their papers without saying it directly. The herds have been here longer than the operation. The herds have been here longer than the survey. The herds have been here longer than any human record of the basin, and they are adapted to the basin in ways that take more than the few thousand years that human records would allow. Their coats are the right color. Their dentition is the right kind. Their digestion is the right kind. They are not visitors to the basin. They are not even residents of the basin in the way that the workers are not residents. They are the basin, the way the lichen is the basin, and the way the fumaroles are the basin. They have been doing their work here for longer than anything with a backbone has been doing its work anywhere else that the surveys have covered.

They are not tame. They do not approach the workers. They allow the workers to approach within perhaps twenty meters, and after that they move away, slowly, with the same unhurried gait, and the movement is not fear. It is the movement of an animal that has chosen to be elsewhere. They will not be herded. They will not be driven. The operations do not try.

Calves are born in the late winter, after the worst of the cold, and they are dark from the first — darker than the adults, almost black, with coats that are denser and softer than the adult coats and that fade to the iron-red over the first year. The calves stand within an hour. They walk within two. They eat within a day. The cows are protective of them, but not aggressively so. A fox that gets too close to a calf is warned off by a low sound from the lead cow, and the warning is enough. A condor that circles too low is watched. Nothing in the basin preys on the grazers successfully, except time. The grazers live perhaps twenty years, perhaps more. The biologists are not sure. None of them have tagged one and followed it through a full life. The collars that have been put on a few of them have come off, or fallen off, or been removed by other grazers. The grazers do not like collars.

A grazer, occasionally, has an extra claw. The extra claw is small and placed at the inside of the foreleg, where it does not interfere with walking. It has no apparent function. The biologists have noted it. They have not explained it.

---

The foxes are smaller than the grazers and more common, and they are the predators that the basin has, along with the condors. They are a blunt-faced fox, the size of a small dog, with thick fur that ranges from rust to charcoal depending on the individual, and they hunt in pairs or alone, mostly along the edges of the herds, where the burrowing mammals are densest and where the young of the grazers are sometimes left briefly unattended. They are not fast, by fox standards. They are patient. They sit on the warm stones and watch, and they wait, and when something makes the wrong move they cross the distance in a low lope and they take it, and they eat it where they take it, and they do not share with their partner unless their partner was part of the taking.

Their eyes reflect light in colors that foxes' eyes do not reflect. This is reported consistently. The reflection is sometimes the expected red, but more often it is a faint green, or a pale blue, or a yellow that has too much green in it. The biologists have measured the tapetum lucidum of the few foxes that have been retrieved for study and have found the structure to be normal. The color is not explained by the structure. The biologists do not press the point.

The foxes den in the rock crevices and in the abandoned boreholes that pock the western half of the basin. They are not afraid of the operations. They use the heat that leaks from the capped boreholes to warm their dens in the winter. They come into the working areas at night and they take what is dropped and they leave. A worker who wakes in the night at the processing complex and looks out the window of the bunkhouse will see, with some regularity, a fox crossing the lit yard with something in its mouth — a scrap of meat from the mess, a piece of waste from the leaching tanks, a small mammal caught on the way in. The fox does not hurry. The yard is its yard too. The fox is unafraid of the lights.

---

The condors ride the thermals above the rim and come down into the basin in the mornings, when the air begins to warm and the columns of heated air start to rise off the dark stone. They are large — larger than Andean condors by a noticeable margin, with wingspans that have been measured at three and a half meters on the largest individuals. The biologists use the word *unusually* about them and do not qualify it. They are patient in the way that all condors are patient, riding the air for hours without effort, descending only when something has died and is worth the descent. They eat the dead. They are the cleaners of the grazers, mostly — when a grazer dies, which is not often, the condors come within hours, and they strip the carcass within days, and the bones are left to the foxes and the ravens and the slow work of the basin.

There is one condor that has a notch in the leading edge of its left wing, a small V of missing feather that is visible from the ground when the bird banks. The workers know this condor. The biologists know this condor. It has been in the basin for at least nine years, which is longer than the operations has been running. It was here first. It is, in some quiet way, the senior inhabitant of the basin, and it does not seem to be in a hurry to leave.

The ravens are everywhere. They are not the wild ravens of higher mountains, lean and wary. They are the ravens of a place where there is food to be had from people, and they have become, in the years of the operation, something close to commensal — not tame, but unafraid, focused, and opportunistic in the way that an urban pigeon is opportunistic. They walk on the rails of the conveyor galleries. They perch on the wooden poles of the power lines. They sit on the rims of the open tanks at the processing complex and look down into them. They take what is dropped. They take what is set out for them by a worker at the processing complex who has, in defiance of regulations, started feeding them from his lunch. The ravens know him. They wait for him at the same time every day.

The ravens have learned to open the small bags that the feed stations use for the grazers. They have learned to work the latches on the storage bins at the smaller outposts. They have learned that a worker who sets down a tool and walks away for any length of time is likely to come back to find the tool moved, or examined, or carried off. There is no way to keep them out. There is no reason to try. The ravens are part of the operations now, and the operations has accepted this.

---

The other fauna is small and easy to overlook.

There are the burrowers — small, blunt-nosed mammals that live in colonies in the softer ground near the river and at the edges of the warm patches. They are the size of a large rat and they are not rats. Their fur is pale and they have very small ears and they move in quick darts when they are above ground, which is mostly at dawn and dusk, and they eat the lichen and the mineral crusts and the things that the grazers leave behind. They are the primary food of the foxes. Their colonies are dense and they dig with a speed that is startling, and when a fox comes into the colony the warning call goes through the ground before the fox has crossed the first burrow, and by the time the fox is at the second burrow the colony is empty and sealed and waiting.

There are the marmot-like creatures that whistle from the rocks. They live in the cracks of the basin walls, where the heat keeps the cracks dry, and they are out in the morning when the steam is rising and the air is damp. They whistle at each other across the walls. They whistle at the condors. They whistle, sometimes, at nothing that anyone can see. The whistle carries across the basin in a way that the other sounds of the basin do not, and a worker standing on a platform of the processing complex in the early morning will hear the whistles come from several directions at once, layered, like a conversation being held across a distance that the speakers cannot see each other across.

There are the lizards on the warm stones. They are small and grey and quick, and they are out in the middle of the day when the stones are hottest, and they flatten themselves against the stone and they bask, and they eat the small insects that the heat draws out of the cracks. There are dozens of them in any given square of warm ground. They skitter when a foot comes near, and they do not skitter far, and they resume their bask.

There is a small hawk that works the river, taking the fish. The fish are in the river — there are fish in the river, despite the warmth and the strangeness, and they are fast, and they are not like any fish that the ichthyologists have identified. They are pale, almost translucent in the right light, and they have small heads and long bodies and they hold themselves in the current in a way that suggests they are not fighting the current but reading it. The hawk takes them at the shallows. The hawk is not often successful. The fish are faster than the hawk.

There are the small pale sparrows that work the edges. They come and go in ways that the biologists have not pinned down — present for a few weeks in the late summer, absent for the rest of the year, replaced by a slightly different sparrow in the spring. The basin has its own migratory rhythm for these small birds, and the rhythm is not the rhythm of the surrounding mountains. The basin is doing something with them. The sparrows do not seem to mind.

There are insects. The insects are not many — the heat and the altitude and the thin air are not friendly to insects — but they are present, especially near the fumaroles and near the river, and they are the base of more of the food web than a visitor would expect. The burrowers eat them. The lizards eat them. The sparrows eat them. The fish, perhaps, eat them.

---

The plants are the specialists, and they have negotiated hard, and they have won narrow concessions.

The lichen covers everything that is not moved by hand. It is on the stones, on the walls, on the rails of the conveyor galleries, on the wooden poles of the power lines, on the metal of the pipes. It is in colors that the casual eye would call grey or black but that the patient eye finds to be a layered palette — the black of the youngest crusts, the orange of the iron-loving species, the pale sage-green of the oldest colonies, which are inches thick and have been growing since before the operation began. The lichen is the slowest of the living things in the basin. It grows at the speed that the basin grows, which is the speed of stone.

The lichen is what the grazers eat. Most of what the grazers eat is lichen, and most of what the burrowers eat is lichen, and most of what the fish, perhaps, are eating at the bottom of the river is lichen that has been washed off the stones. The lichen is the foundation of the food web, the way the soil is the foundation of the food web elsewhere. The basin has no soil. The basin has lichen, and the lichen is doing the work that soil does, slowly.

The moss grows only where the steam keeps the ground damp — in fat clumps around the fumaroles, along the edges of the travertine terraces, in the few shaded spots at the base of the western wall. The moss is green in a way that the rest of the basin is not green. The moss is bright. Workers who walk out to a fumarole to check a gauge will sometimes stop and look at the moss for a moment longer than the gauge requires, and they do not talk about why.

The scrub plants are few. There is the low woody thing with the silver leaves that hugs the rock crevices and that has small flowers that no one has ever seen open, and there is the tough grass of the riverbanks that has grown into strange tussocks that the wind shapes, and there is the small white flowering plant that blooms only after a rain and only for a few days. The white flowering plant is the most beloved thing in the basin. When it blooms, the workers know, because the ravens know, because the foxes know, because the whole basin seems to pause for the few days that the flowers are out. The flowers are white against the dark stone. They are the only white in the basin. They are gone before the next week is out.

---

And the basin itself.

The basin is not alive in the way that the lichen is alive, or the way that the foxes are alive, or the way that the workers are temporarily and conditionally alive. But the basin is doing something, and the doing is not the same as the doing of a stone, and the workers who have been here long enough have stopped arguing about whether it counts as doing.

The basin is cycling water. It is moving minerals. It is feeding the lichen and the moss and the scrub plants and the grazers and the burrowers and the foxes and the condors and the ravens and the fish, and through them it is feeding itself. The heat comes up through the stone and the water comes down out of the air and the minerals move from one part of the basin to another and back, and the operation is inside this cycle, and the workers are inside the operation, and the workers who have been here long enough begin to feel that they are inside the cycle too.

The basin smells, in the mornings, of something that nobody quite identifies. The smell is not sulfur. The smell is not the mineral that the operation is mining. The smell is organic, and warm, and old, and it is the smell of a place that has been doing its work for a long time. The workers who stay long enough learn the smell. They learn to notice when the smell is stronger or when it is fainter. They do not talk about it.

The basin, in the evenings, after the condensation has lifted, glows. Not visibly. Not in any way that a camera catches. But a person standing on a slope at dusk can sometimes feel that the ground beneath them is faintly lit from within, a warmth that suggests light without producing any. The geologists have measured the heat output and found it consistent with the geothermal. The workers who have been here long enough do not need the measurement. They know.

The basin holds all of it. It holds the workers and the grazers and the foxes and the condors and the ravens and the burrowers and the marmot-like creatures and the lizards and the hawk and the sparrows and the insects and the fish and the lichen and the moss and the scrub plants and the white flowers that come after the rain. It holds the pipes and the conveyor galleries and the power lines and the trucks and the rail carts and the small planes. It holds the processing complex and the crushing station and the drill platforms and the railhead and the landing strip. It holds the secret mineral that is being extracted and the sealed containers that carry it away. It holds the smell that nobody identifies and the glow that no camera catches and the small anomalies of the animals and the colors of the mineral crusts that do not quite repeat.

The basin is warm and damp and quiet, and it is doing its work, and it has been doing its work for longer than anything in it has been alive, and it will be doing its work after everything in it is gone. The animals seem to know this. The stones seem to know this. The workers who have been here long enough seem to know it too, and they do not talk about it, because there is nothing to say that the basin is not already saying, and has not been saying, and will not be saying for as long as the basin holds its heat and runs its water and grows its lichen and feeds its grazers and lifts its steam into the thin air.
