# Who Lives in the Hollow of the Steeple-Vines

They live in the seams.

This is the first thing to understand about the people of the Hollow, and it is not quite a metaphor, though it sounds like one. They live in the spaces where one thing meets another — the gap between two roofs, the place where the road meets the wall, the lip of a basin, the ledge of a worn step. When you are among them and you cannot find anyone in the lane, this is why: they have stepped aside, into the dark, and are watching you from a place you cannot quite see. They are not hiding. They have simply become good at being where you are not.

There are not many of them. You might count two hundred souls if you were patient, and you would be wrong by a small number in either direction, because births happen in the lower rooms and are not announced beyond the household, and deaths happen in the upper rooms and are carried out by a particular door, and neither event produces the kind of official paper that would let a stranger keep an accurate tally. The households themselves are the units. There are perhaps forty of these. Some contain a grandmother and a widowed son and three small children and a great-grandmother, layered in the rooms like geological strata. Some contain a single old woman and an indoor cat. No household contains fewer than two living generations and more than five. The place has a way of holding its population to a curve, and the curve is older than any of them.

They are small people. Not in a way that calls attention to itself — they are not dwarfish, not hunched, not a different species — but in the way a tree that has grown up crowded is small. Dense. Compact of limb. Their hands are broad and the fingers are short and the palms are very calloused in particular patterns, the patterns of stone and rope and damp wood. Their faces have a certain weathering to them, not from sun, because the sun does not reach here in any meaningful way, but from cold. The skin of their cheeks is rough. The skin beneath their eyes is faintly bruised-looking, permanently, as though they do not sleep as much as a person ought to, or as though they have been crying for so long they have forgotten they were doing it.

Their eyes are the color of the place. A gray that is not gray but a kind of dimness you could read as any cooler color depending on the light. Hazel, in some. A washed blue in others. Almost green in the children, until the children grow older and the place takes the color down a notch, the way the water in the basin takes the color down, the way the moss on the stones takes the color down.

Their hair is dark and wiry and never quite dry. The women wear it bound back from the face with a twist of woven cloth, and the men, when they are old enough to have decided about such things, do something similar, though in a different knot. The children wear it however it falls, and it falls into their eyes, and it does not seem to bother them.

Their clothes are the same in every season, because there are no seasons here — only the slow oscillation between *more damp* and *less damp*, *colder* and *not as cold*. The fiber is dense and dark and lies flat against the body. It does not flap. It does not billow. When they move quickly, which is rare, it moves with them but a half-beat behind, as though it is not quite part of them yet, or no longer quite part of them. Their shoes are wrapped affairs, leather and woven fiber, with the soles replaced often because the wet stone wears them out and the people of the Hollow respect their agreements with the ground they walk on. The soles, when they are discarded, go into a particular heap behind a particular house, and from there they go somewhere no one talks about, perhaps down into the lower rooms, perhaps into the channels of slow water. The heap is always the same size. This is one of the small arrangements that holds the place together.

---

In the morning, which is a slow brightening and not anything else, the people come out of their houses without speaking. There is a particular order to this, not enforced by any law but arrived at over so long a time that it has become a kind of law of the body. The eldest come out first. Then those of middle age. Then the young. Then the children, last of all, when the lanes are clear. This is because the eldest move slowest, and the lanes are narrow, and it does not occur to anyone to be in a lane at the same time as someone moving slower than you, and so they have arranged themselves by speed, and have done so for so long that they now believe it is simply the way mornings are.

The first errand of the day is water. Someone from each household goes to the basin at the center, with a vessel of dark stone, and fills it, and carries it back. The vessel is passed hand to hand at the door of the household, so that the carrier does not actually have to enter with it — they can hand it across the threshold, in a particular way, with a small motion that is almost a bow. This is not ceremony. It is efficiency. The hands of the person who carries the water are considered, by long habit, to be slightly *changed* by the carrying, and so they do not touch the food or the children until they have washed at the small cistern by each door. The water in these cisterns is rainwater, gathered from the eaves. It is considered different from the basin water. The basin water is for the household. The cistern water is for the person who has touched the basin water. This distinction is not discussed but is observed without fail.

The basin itself is tended by a person, and only one at a time, and the role passes by a method no outsider has ever been able to discern. The current tender is a small woman of perhaps fifty, with a face like a folded leaf, and she is there every morning when the first carrier arrives, and she is there every evening when the last pour-back is performed, and she sleeps somewhere in the low buildings near the basin, and no one seems to know which building, and she appears to come from the stones themselves when she is needed. Her name, if she has one, is not used in her presence.

The pour-back is the small annual rite that is not a rite. Once a year, in a particular week that is determined by the smell of a particular flower, the people of the Hollow carry water to the basin from their own houses and pour it back in. They do this in a particular pattern of approach, counter-clockwise, each household once, and the cup used is a particular cup made of a particular wood that has grown in a particular place up the slope. The cup is kept by the basin-tender and only brought out for this. When it is done, the cup is cleaned and returned to wherever it lives, and the people return to their houses, and nothing else happens. No one watches. No one applauds. It is done because it is done. The basin keeps giving. This is the agreement.

---

Inside the houses, the arrangement is similar to the arrangement outside: dense, layered, the rooms pressed close to one another. The ground floor, where the household eats and receives its rare visitors, is dim and warm in the particular way that a room is warm when many bodies have lived in it and the fire has been banked for generations. The hearth is small and built into the wall and tends a low flame of a moss that has been dried and treated in a way no one will describe. The flame gives more light than it ought to for its size, and it gives off a smell that is not quite smoke, that is something older than smoke, something more like the inside of a forest after rain.

The upper floor — the second story, where there is a second story, which is most houses but not all — is where the sleeping happens, and where the stored things are kept, and where the small windows face outward into the lanes. The windows are not for looking out. They are for letting in the small persistent light, and they are fitted with a kind of oiled cloth that diffuses it further. The light in these rooms is the color of weak tea. It is enough to see by if you are used to seeing by less.

The lower rooms — the crypt, the continuous basement that runs beneath the houses — are where the dead are kept, and where certain things are stored that cannot be stored above ground, and where the small deer sometimes come in through the ventilation shafts and lie down among the older bones as if they belonged there. No one is afraid of the dead in the Hollow. No one is sentimental about them either. The dead are the dead, and they are there, and the living walk among them, and the lamp is taken down when needed and the lamp is brought back up when finished, and the steps to the lower rooms are worn in a particular pattern by this traffic. The dead are remembered. They are not mourned, except in the body, by a particular posture held for a particular time, and the posture is private and no one watches it being held.

Children in the Hollow are quiet. They are not suppressed, exactly, but they have not been given many reasons to make noise, and so they do not. They play in the lanes in a kind of slow, observant way, watching the small deer, watching the birds, watching the water move in its channels. They learn to walk on the wet stones without slipping by the time they are four. They learn the names of the lanes — and there are names for the lanes, though they are not names the way other places have names, more like descriptions kept short until they have become sounds — by the time they are six. They learn to carry water by the time they are seven. They learn the rest of what they need to know by watching, and they watch a great deal, with that gray-green attention that is the color of the place, and they are not hurried.

There are no schools. There are no teachers except the slow demonstration of the elders in the ordinary course of their days. The children learn the knots. There are many knots, and each has its purpose, and the children are given a particular knot to master at a particular age, and the knot is tied for them by an elder and then they are left alone with it until they have it, and this may take a season or it may take three seasons, and no one comments on the difference. The knots are how accounts are kept. The knots are how the dead are remembered. The knots are how the arrangements between households are recorded and renewed. A person who knows the knots knows the history of the place so far as the place has chosen to keep it.

The young people — those between childhood and the taking-on of household duties — spend a great deal of time in the forest at the edges of the settlement. They go there in pairs or in small groups and they come back with particular observations: which mosses are bright this season, which of the small deer have young with them this year, where the fox has been passing, whether the spring is running higher or lower than it was. These observations are given to a particular elder, in a particular tone, at a particular time of day, and the elder takes them in and does not seem to respond, and the young people go away. This is how the place decides what to do next. It is how the place has always decided. It does not require a meeting.

---

There is no one in charge.

This must be said carefully because it is not an absence. It is a presence. The arrangements of the Hollow are held in place by so many small agreements, so many quiet understandings, so many knots and footsteps and pourings-back, that the addition of a single person in charge would be a redundancy and probably a disturbance. The basin-tender is not in charge. The eldest in each household is not in charge. There are individuals who, in a particular moment, make a particular decision — which lane to mend, which stone to reset, which child to give a particular knot to — but the decisions do not add up to a person, and the person does not add up to an authority. The place runs itself the way a body runs itself: not by command from the head, but by all the parts doing what they have always done, in the order they have always done it.

It is very difficult for an outsider to understand this. Outsiders, when they come, tend to look for the person they should address, and they tend to be told, by gesture or by silence, that there is no such person, and they tend to interpret this as evasiveness or as some kind of collective concealment, and they are wrong. There is nothing being concealed. There is nothing to conceal. The place is exactly what it appears to be: a small, low, ancient pocket of dwelling, organized around a spring, held together by habit so old it has become indistinguishable from the stone.

---

The people of the Hollow do not marry, in the way other places marry. They *pair*, and the pairing is observed for a year before it is recognized, and then it is recognized by a particular small household rite involving a particular stone and a particular knot and the basin water. There is no ceremony in the sense that outsiders mean ceremony. There is a knot tied, and there is water poured, and there is a small carved figure — there are several such figures, distributed through the settlement, each one worn past identification — that the pair touches in passing, and then they go back to their work, and from that point they share a household.

Children come from these pairs, and the children are raised by the household, not by the pair alone. The mother carries the child, and the child is born in the lower rooms among the dead, and the birth is attended by the women of the household and by a particular old woman who has the midwifery knowledge, which is a particular set of knots and a particular way of holding the hands of the woman in labor, and the particular is not taught but is passed by being beside, in the body, until the body knows.

Bereavement is handled the way the people of the Hollow handle everything: quietly, in the body, by the particular. The body is washed in basin water and dressed in the woven fiber, and is carried down into the lower rooms, and is laid in a particular place, and the footsteps of the carriers are observed, and the knots of memory are updated, and the living go back to the lanes. There is a period of quiet, and the period is different for each household, and no one comments on the difference. After the period, the household resumes its observances, and the basin water is carried, and the knots are checked, and the place continues.

The grief is real. It is in the small slow motions of the bereaved, in the way they hold their hands, in the way they will not look at a particular stone for a season. But the grief is not given a great deal of room, because the place cannot afford it. The place is the size it is, and the water must come, and the dead must be remembered, and the living must continue. The grief is honored by being incorporated. It is given a particular knot, and the knot is kept in a particular place, and the bereaved can look at it when they wish, and it does not need to be spoken of.

---

The people of the Hollow are long-lived, though not in the way outsiders imagine long-lived. They do not live to two hundred, or to a hundred and fifty. They live to eighty, ninety, a few of them to a hundred, but they live *thoroughly*. Their old age is gradual and is not resisted. They slow down. They step aside into the dark. They watch more. They say less. They stop going to the basin-tender for the particular observations and instead simply know things, the way the basin-tender knows things, and when they are very old they become the ones who receive the observations, and the young people come to them, and they do not respond, but they listen.

When they die, it is often in sleep, in the upper rooms, and the body is found in the morning by whoever rises first in the household. The death is noted in the knots. The body is carried down. The hole in the household is felt, and then the hole is filled, by some adjustment of the arrangements, by some young person taking on a duty the elder no longer does, by some slight shifting of who carries the water and when.

The dead are the ancestors, and the ancestors are many, and they are all around, in the lower rooms, in the stones, in the moss, in the small carved figures that watch the basins and the doorways. The people of the Hollow are not alone. They have never been alone. The dead are quiet company, and they are good company, and they are consulted in their way, which is the way of the knots, which is the way of the steps worn into the lower rooms.

---

If you were to ask a person of the Hollow what they believe, they would not understand the question. They would understand *what is done*. They would understand *what is observed*. They would understand the knots and the footsteps and the pourings-back. They would understand that the spring gives, and that the agreement is kept, and that the dead are where they are, and the living are where they are, and the small deer pass through the lanes, and the birds call their two notes, and the moss grows, and the stones darken, and the light, when it comes, makes everything briefly bright.

They would not understand belief as a thing separable from practice. They would not understand that you could believe one thing and do another. They would not understand the question of whether the carved figure by the basin is a god or a person or a remembered ancestor or a representation of something else, because the figure is the figure, and the basin is the basin, and the agreement is kept.

They are not mystics. They are not primitives. They are not a people waiting to be enlightened or saved or studied. They are simply very old at being here. They have a place that works. They have an agreement with their water, with their dead, with their stone, with their damp. The agreement has been kept for so long that no one in the place can remember what it would mean to break it. This is not because they are unable to break it. It is because they have no longer any reason to.

An outsider, standing in one of the narrow lanes, looking up at the low canopy of trees and the green-dripping walls and the small carved figure leaning forward as if listening to the water, might feel a chill that is not from the cold. They might feel that they are looking at something that has gone past human, or has not yet arrived at it. They would be wrong in both cases. The people of the Hollow are exactly as human as they have always been, and no more or less. They are people who have stayed in one place for so long that the place has become a part of them, and they have become a part of it, and the two are now so close to each other that an outsider cannot quite tell where one ends and the other begins.

But the outsider could live here a long time, if they were permitted to, and never know that they were anywhere. And that, perhaps, is the point.
