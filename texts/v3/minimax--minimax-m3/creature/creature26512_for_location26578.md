The ferns, primarily. They are the most articulate of the inhabitants, though articulate only in the grammar of fronds and spirals and the slow lifting of cells. They came up through the seam where the boiler meets the concrete — the way water comes up through a crack in a floor, the way anything comes up through anything when there is enough pressure and enough time and the obstacle is not absolute. They did not arrive. They emerged, the way the rust emerged, the way the stalactite of mineral deposit emerged beneath the curve of the boiler. There is no verb for what they do. They are not moving toward anything. They are simply continuing, in a slightly more elaborated form than they were a moment ago.

The largest of them — there are perhaps a dozen in the crown, and the largest is the size of a tall child, though it will never be the size of a tall child, because it is already the size it will be for a long time, which is the size it is now — has its fronds arranged in a tight spiral, each one furled at the tip like a question mark. The question is not asked. It is held. The fern does not expect an answer. It is asking because that is what its shape is, the way a wave is a question asked of a shore, and the shore answers by being there.

The moss lives around the base of the pump assembly. A cushion so deep and even it looks manufactured, as though someone had upholstered the machine. The moss is older than the ferns, perhaps. It is patient. It has eaten the flywheel's cradle and made it part of itself. It does not rush. It is moss. Its tempo is the tempo of itself.

The vine on the valve wheel is the strangest of the green inhabitants, because the vine is a single thing — a single trailing thread that has wrapped itself so many times around the wheel that the spokes are no longer visible. Only a column of greenery where the wheel should be, with a tendril reaching out toward nothing. The vine does not know where it is going. It is going. That is what vines do. They find things and they hold them. When there is nothing to hold, they hold the air, or they hold their own previous selves.

The rust lives here. The rust is a citizen. It lives in the seams of the boiler, in long vertical stains that have dried into the color of dried blood. It is patient. It is faster than the ferns. It is slower than moisture. It is exactly the speed of iron becoming not-iron, and it does not hurry.

The condensation lives here. It lives in the ceiling, in the threads that gather and roll down and drip, somewhere below, into channels you cannot yet see. The condensation is the circulatory system of the place. It brings water down to the floor, where the floor is also becoming soil, slowly, the porosity of the concrete allowing the migration of moisture and whatever is dissolved in it, downward and outward.

The heat lives here. The heat is the medium. It is not an inhabitant so much as the air they breathe, except that they don't breathe, or if they breathe, it is not in the way you mean. The heat is geothermal. It comes up through the floor. It is felt as a pulse, almost too low to be called sound, more like a texture in the warmth. The heat does not have a body, but it has a behavior. It rises along the ceiling and cools against the upper rock and drifts back down the walls. It carries smell: warm iron, wet earth, a sweet vegetal rot, and a mineral breath beneath everything, sulfurous and clean, the smell of the heat itself before it has been flavored by anything it has touched.

The machines live here, in a sense. They are not alive. But they are the grammar in which the vegetation has composed itself around them. They are the surfaces on which the ferns may climb. They are the architecture, the substrate, the skeleton, the bones. They do not run. They have not run in a long time. But they are not dead. They are post-industrial. They are the underlying fact of the place. The pump assembly cradles its moss. The boiler weeps its rust. The tall black tank stands like a sentinel with its wheel eaten green. They are the reason the room exists, and they continue to be the reason, even after their function has gone out of them.

The feather lives here. You do not know where the feather came from. There are no birds. There is no breeze. The feather lies on the concrete as if placed. The feather is the strangest inhabitant because it is the only one that is not native to the slow tempo of the place. It is the only one that suggests a faster world. It is the only one that has been somewhere else. It lies there. It is not growing. It is not rusting. It is not migrating. It is just lying there, and the question of who placed it is held by the feather the way the question is held by the fern frond — without expectation of answer, and without the asking ever being finished.

The gauge lives here. Its needle rests at zero. The pressure has been equalized. The gauge is the relic of an intention that no longer applies. It is the only object that knows what it was for, but it does not say. Its face is still legible. The reading is zero, and zero has been the reading for a long time, and zero will be the reading for a long time still.

There are no insects. There are no bats. Nothing has nested in the rafters — there are no rafters, only the sweating ceiling. The absence of animal life is part of the picture, and the picture is one of slow things and mineral things and vegetal things, all of them running on a clock that ticks once a fern-frond, once a stalactite-millimeter, once an oxide-stain, and once a feather lying still on a floor where the floor is slowly becoming not-floor.

The inhabitants of the boiler vault of Orris do not have names. They have tendencies. They have directions of growth. They have tempos. The ferns have their tempo. The moss has its tempo. The rust has its tempo. The condensation has its tempo. The heat has its tempo. None of them know about each other specifically. They are not in communication. They are simply in the same place, and the same place is patient with all of them, and the patience is the only community they share, and it is enough.

If you stayed, you would not change this. You would be another tempo, briefly, and the room would accommodate you the way it accommodates the feather — as an anomaly, as a question mark held without expectation of answer, as something that has been somewhere else and now is here, for a while, and then will not be here. The ferns would continue. The rust would continue. The vine would continue wrapping its wheel. The stalactite of mineral deposit would continue forming beneath the curve of the boiler, and the stalagmite of oxidized drip would continue growing upward on the floor beneath it, and they would, eventually, touch. The feather would remain. The gauge would remain. You would not.
