The inhabitants of this place are not a collection. They are a single long-running argument among themselves, and the forest is the sediment of the argument, and the rain that never stops is what the argument is made of.

Begin with the small. Begin with the workers, because the workers are the most of what is here, and what is most here is what built the place.

The workers are ants. They are leaf-cutters — *Atta*, the genus that has spent more years, by some measures, doing what it does than almost any other lineage of large animal on the continent. Each worker is the size of a clipped fingernail. Each worker is a fraction. There is no individual. There is only the fraction in motion, and the motion is what is alive. A worker standing still is, for a short while, a body. A worker walking is part of a sentence the colony is speaking.

A lane of them, moving, looks like a rope made of bees. The bodies are reddish-brown, the color of old coffee grounds, and they are dense on the lane the way cells are dense in a tissue. A worker in the middle of the lane does not turn around. A worker in the middle of the lane has never turned around. She is four months old and will die before she has seen where she is going. She carries a piece of leaf above her head like a sail. The piece is green. It will not be green when she arrives. By the time she has carried it down into the dark, it will be one more ingredient in a paste that she — or rather, others of her line, the smaller ones, the ones that never leave — will not eat. They will feed it. They will feed it to something that does not walk, something that has never walked, something that is white and pale and spread in sheets through the underground chambers like a slow thought.

The fungus. The fungus is the colony's stomach. The fungus is also a thing that lives here, with its own rights, and is treated accordingly — weeded, fed, pruned, its sexual parts kept down. The colony is the fungus's body, the way a forest is a tree's body. The fungus is fed leaves it cannot digest on its own. The fungus is fed by an animal that cannot digest leaves on its own. They have made each other possible. The fungus is the oldest partner. The fungus has been doing this, in some form, since before the ants arrived to do it with.

The lanes do not run in straight lines. They run like roads laid down by people who were given no surveyor. They follow contour. They follow the underside of root slabs. They bank around the buttressed bases of great trees with the slow, committed geometry of a thing that has been given the same job for a thousand generations and has gotten good at it. There are trunk trails, main avenues, that have been cleared of leaf litter, swept clean by the constant passage of bodies, packed to a slight shine by the oils from a million cuticle. There are minor lanes branching off. There are junctions. At the junctions there is, if you kneel, a kind of traffic circle — a small milling of bodies, antennae tapping, the negotiation of which way to go expressed not in language but in the chemistry of touched air.

On the workers' backs, riding them, are mites. The mites are not passengers. They are also workers, but of a different firm. They are *Macrocheles*, or something like them, or one of the many lineages of mites that have made the ant lane a habitat. They ride because the ant lane is a highway and a clearing and a marketplace all at once. They disembark at junctions. They groom the workers of parasites. They eat the small fungi that would, if left, infect the colony. They are, in their way, the colony's sanitation. They are also a thing the colony tolerates, and the toleration has the texture of a long-running tenancy that no one has thought to evict because the rent is paid in service and the eviction would cost more than it returns.

Bigger things walk the lanes too, when the lane permits. There are beetles the size of a thumb segment, with shells so polished they reflect the green dusk. There are flies. The flies are the size of a thought, and they dart and hover above the lanes like punctuation above a sentence. There are wasps, parasitic, that hover over the lanes for hours, waiting for a worker to separate from the line by a centimeter. A centimeter is enough. The wasp lands, lays, and is gone. The worker carries on. The worker is going to die. The worker is going to die because of this, but not yet, and the worker does not know, and the not-knowing is part of what the worker is.

The fungus gardens, deep down, are not visible from here. They are the place the lanes are all going to, and the place the lanes are all coming from. A nest the size of a small room can hold millions of workers. A nest the size of a car holds more. A nest the size of a small house holds as much biomass as a cow, and is older, and will outlast any cow. The queen, somewhere in the dark, in the chamber that is hers and that no other body enters, is laying eggs at a rate that makes the laying seem less like an act and more like a condition, like a tide. She is fat. She is older than every worker in the colony, and she is older than most of the trees above her, and she will be replaced, and the replacement will be her daughter, and the daughter will be mated on a single afternoon in the canopy, where the winged males fly up into the dusk and are eaten by birds, and the queen comes down with sperm stored in a sac she will use for the rest of her life, which can be fifteen years.

Above, on the floor, the foragers work in the open. They are the most exposed. They are the ones who cut. A single forager climbs a low branch, opens her jaws — which are serrated, which are not jaws but two blades set against each other in a scissor — and begins to snip. The snip is a curve. The forager is cutting a crescent. The forager is cutting one piece of a puzzle, and the puzzle is bigger than her. When the piece falls she will pick it up, climb down, find the lane, and walk. She will walk for an hour. The piece will not wilt in the hour. The piece has been treated, on the way out, with a secretion from a gland on the forager's thorax. The secretion is a fungicide. It is also a primer. It tells the fungus below what is coming.

A column of foragers going up a tree looks, from below, like a slow red vein pulsing against bark. The bark is also not just bark. The bark has lichens on it. The lichens are inhabited by tardigrades, by rotifers, by mites of yet another sort, by the eggs of small moths, by things too small to name. The tree is a building. The tree is a city. The tree has, in its bark, more individual animals than the foragers are aware of, and the foragers are not aware of any of them. The foragers are aware only of the leaf.

In the canopy, higher up, where the air is cooler and the light is filtered through leaves the size of hands, larger animals live their slow lives. A three-toed sloth — *Bradypus*, the brown one, or *Bradypus variegatus* — hangs from a cecropia. She has been hanging there for so long that a community of moths lives in her fur. The moths lay eggs in her dung. The moths are *Cryptoses*. They ride her, when she descends once a week, to defecate at the base of her tree. She defecates. The moths go to the dung. The moths lay their eggs in the dung. The moths hatch. The new moths climb the tree to find her, or find another sloth. The moths cannot live without her. She does not live because of them. She is indifferent. The indifference is the shape the relationship takes when one party is slow enough to be the substrate for the other's life. The moths are her weather. She is their continent.

She eats. She eats leaves, but she eats them with such reluctance, with such a digestive delay, that the leaves she has eaten yesterday are still recognisable in her stomach today. She is a fermenter. She is a slow fermenter of cecropia. She descends, once a week, a slow arc that takes an hour. The descent looks like the unfolding of an event that has been compressed. Her claws — three of them, curved like parentheses — release one branch at a time. She has algae in her fur. The algae make her green. The algae are not algae but cyanobacteria, and the cyanobacteria are her camouflage, and the camouflage works because in this light, everything is green, and green is invisible, and invisibility is the only predator-avoidance a sloth has.

The creak you heard, in the description of the place, is her. It is her moving. It is the joints of a body that is mostly tendon and patience, lifting itself from one branch to another in a movement so slow it can be measured in minutes per inch. When she defecates, the falling is the percussion. The dropping lands on the floor. The dropping is a package. The dropping is a long-stewed leaf. The dropping is, to the ants below, a delivery. The ants dismantle it. The ants carry the pieces of it down into their fungus. The sloth, the moth, the ant, the fungus — these are not separate stories. They are the same story, told in different sizes.

In a hollow tree, somewhere, a colony of bats sleeps. They are small bats. They are *Rhinophylla* or *Carollia*, fruit-eaters, the size of a thumb with a thumb-sized leaf folded over their backs. They roost by day. They leave at dusk. They do not hunt here — they eat figs, cecropia fruit, the soft pulp of things that are mid-rot in the canopy. They are pollinators. They are also seed-dispersers, and the seeds they pass are the seeds of the next canopy, and the next canopy is what they will eat, and what their young will eat, and what their young's young will eat, and the cycle is closed in a way that does not need to close to keep going.

On the floor, in a clearing that is closing, where a fig has fallen and split, the ferment of the fig is a habitat. The ferment is a small ecosystem. A fly is laying eggs in the ferment. The eggs will hatch into maggots. The maggots will eat the ferment and become flies. The flies will lay eggs in another ferment, or in a sloth's dung, or in a mushroom that has come up overnight and is already collapsing. The ferment is a station. The flies are commuters. They do not live long. They do not need to. The next generation is in the next thing.

The fungi are also inhabitants. Some of them glow. The glow is a green-white, the color of a phone screen in a dark room, the color of the underside of a wave. The fungi that glow are *Mycena* or *Neonothopanus* or one of the small bioluminescent ones whose chemistry is, still, not fully understood. They glow at night. The glow attracts insects. The insects land. The insects are inoculated with spores. The insects fly off. The spores are carried. The fungi do not glow for the insects. The fungi glow for themselves, or for the process they are part of, or for no reason at all. The glow is a by-product. The by-product is also a function. There is no difference here between a thing that is done and a thing that does something.

A beetle walks the floor. The beetle is a *Trachyderes* or a *Chrysophora* or something with elytra so iridescent they look painted. The beetle is alone. Beetles of this size do not live in lanes. They live in territories. They live in logs. The log they live in is a log that has been on the floor for thirty years and is now a long hill of soft wood with a population of beetles, termites, fungi, nematodes, and mites inside it, in layers, in succession, in a temporal sequence stacked horizontally so that the beetle at the front end of the log is in a different community than the beetle at the back end, and the two beetles are a thousand years apart in evolutionary time, though they are six feet apart in space.

A caiman sleeps in the black pool. The pool is warm. The caiman is in the pool because the pool is the temperature a caiman wants to be. The caiman does not eat often. The caiman does not need to. The caiman is patient. The caiman is the patient thing, the way the process is the patient thing. It has been here longer than the ants have been ants. It has been here longer than the sloths have been sloths. The caiman is from a lineage that was here when the basin was a different shape, when the trees were different trees, when the air was different air. It has been updated. It has been the same. It has been the same by being updated. It opens one eye when something splashes. The eye is a yellow slit. The eye is the size of a coin. The eye regards you with the total disinterest of a thing that does not eat mammals and has not eaten one in a long time and is full of fish. The eye closes. The caiman sinks. The water closes over it. The water takes a long time to be still again.

A catfish, in the same pool, has eaten a fruit that fell from a tree a hundred yards away. The fish does not know this. The fish does not know anything. The fish has a stomach full of seeds, and the seeds will pass, and the seeds will be deposited, in a packet of nutrient, somewhere downstream, and the seeds will germinate, and the trees will grow, and the trees will fruit, and the fruit will fall, and a fish will eat it. The fish is part of the dispersal. The fish is a courier. The fish does not know.

In the canopy, the orchids are inhabitants. They are epiphytes. They are not parasites — they are tenants of the architecture. Their roots grip the bark and take nothing from it. They take from the air. They are fed by mist. They bloom with flowers that mimic the female of a wasp species, and the male wasp comes to the flower and copulates with it, and the pollen is on him, and he flies to the next orchid, and the next orchid is pollinated, and the orchid has reproduced by deception. The wasp is the pollinator. The wasp does not know. The orchid does not care. The arrangement is older than both of them.

A tarantula sits in a burrow at the base of a buttressed root. The tarantula is large. The tarantula is a thing of the floor, and the floor is hers, and her burrow is a hole in the soft earth that she has lined with silk. The silk is not a web. The silk is a tripwire. She sits at the mouth. She sits for weeks. Something walks across the silk. She comes out. The thing is gone, in the dark, in her chelicerae. She does not eat often. She is patient. The patience is the same patience as the caiman. The patience is the same patience as the sloth. The patience is what the place selects for.

A millipede walks the floor. The millipede is dark red, the size of a finger, the legs moving in a wave. The millipede is eating the leaf that the ants cut. The millipede is the first stage of the breakdown. The millipede eats. The millipede's gut holds a community of microbes that no one has catalogued. The microbes break down the leaf's cellulose. The millipede defecates. The defecation is a small pellet. The pellet is rich. The pellet is what the ants come for, sometimes, if the pellet is in the wrong place — that is, in a place the ants want for a lane. The pellet is moved. The pellet is processed. The pellet is part of the cycling.

A nematode, smaller than the period at the end of this sentence, is in the pellet. The nematode is the size of a thought. The nematode eats bacteria. The bacteria eat the pellet. The bacteria are in the pellet. The pellet is a planet. The pellet is a place. The pellet is mid-process. Nothing here is finished.

And that is what lives here. Everything mid-process. Everything in lanes. Everything in chambers, in vents, in pools, in the deep warm dark. Nothing that is an individual in the way you are an individual. Everything that is a part of something larger than itself, and the larger thing is not a thing but a relation, and the relation is what is alive, and the relation is what has been alive for a very long time, and the relation does not need you to be there to continue, and the relation will continue after you are not there, and the relation is the patient thing, and the patient thing is the place.
