What lives at Beryl Bend. You have to work at it, because the place will not give you the obvious answer, and the obvious answer is the right answer if you were standing anywhere else. The marsh has no birds. The basalt has no insects. There are no fish breaking the surface, no little scuttlings at the margin, no frogs in the reeds, no frogs at all. If you came looking for the ordinary kind of life — fur, feather, wing, the small bright scurry of a thing that has to eat — you would come away with the heron's feather in your hand and nothing else to show for the trip. The dome is not, on its surface, alive.

It is alive in other registers. You have to read it the way the place reads itself, which is slowly, and by what is there and what is not there and what is there only as a record.

---

Begin with the honest inhabitants. The small ones, the visible ones, the ones that will not mind being named.

The lichen on the lead flange. A pale, crustose lichen, grey-green, the same color as the verdigris it has helped to make. Lichen is two organisms at once — an alga inside a fungus, married into a single slow appetite — and it has been growing on the flange since the flange was new, and the flange is no longer new. The lichen is the only thing on the dome that you can confirm is alive with your unaided eye, and it is not, strictly, an animal. It is patient in the way animals are not patient. It will outlast you. It will outlast the housing.

The marsh, and the marsh is not a creature but a process, and a process can be inhabited, and the marsh inhabits itself. The reeds, sparse, ragged, holding the feather. The cracked pale mud between them, on which nothing walks. The standing water, the warm vegetal sweetness, the slow conversion of one kind of stillness into another. The marsh is patient in the same way the lichen is patient. It is eating the concrete path back, slab by slab, at the rate of a finger's width per year, and it will, eventually, eat the path entirely, and the dome will be ringed by water, and the housing will still be there, and the lichen will still be eating the flange.

The water inside the rock. The condensation that gathers in the fissures and runs in slow patient threads and never quite drips. The water is doing chemical work — dissolving metals out of the basalt, transporting heat up through the fractures, painting the eastern face a faint copper-green at the moments when the breathing shifts. The water is the most active thing on the dome by any measure of motion. It moves at the speed of patience. It is, if you stand long enough, doing almost all of the visible labor of the place. It is also, in a sense that the engineers would not put on a sign, the blood of the dome.

These are the small inhabitants. They are not what the dome is about. They are what the dome is made of.

---

Then the stranger ones, which the dome insists are present even though they will not, on any day you visit, show themselves to you.

There is something under the rock.

You will never see it. It lives — if it lives, and the word is the right word — somewhere below the basalt, somewhere down the throat of the three boreholes where the pipes go. The sign does not name it. The instruments do not name it. The institution that maintains the instruments has chosen, with care, not to name it. They call the assembly an *Instrument Housing* and the pipes a *Vent*, and a vent is a hole that lets something *out*, and the word choice is deliberate. A vent is not an exhaust. A vent is what you build when you have decided that what is on the other side has weight, and motion, and a right to be elsewhere.

What does it do. It breathes. The pipes throb in time with it. The weeping of the eastern face is partly its exhalation, and on the shift-hours the threads thicken and turn briefly bright with something dissolved — a chemistry that did not arrive from nothing. There is heat down there that comes up through the basalt and makes the air shimmer at the surface and gathers the clouds over the dome and turns them to nothing as they cross. The thing under the rock is not a metaphor for the heat. The heat is its byproduct, or its companion, or its skin. You are not equipped to say.

It has been here longer than the housing. The pipes were built *to* it. The four redundant housings elsewhere — the ones you have not been shown, and will not be shown, and which exist in the plural the way *Vent* exists in the singular on the sign at the water's edge — were built to it. The Subbasin Office has, at minimum, four other architectures listening to it, and may itself have been reshaped around it the way a coastline reshapes itself around a current.

It does not care about you. That is not a slight. It has not noticed you. The perturbation you are causing is small and is being recorded and will be accounted for in the next several hours of humming, and then the accounting will smooth out, and you will be a deflection in a paper that has not been written yet. The thing under the rock has been here a long time. It will be here longer. It is older than the breathing and it will outlast the breathing and it will outlast the institution that gave the breathing a name it didn't ask for.

---

There are the people of fourteen.

They do not come to Beryl Bend, or they come rarely, on a schedule subject to review at Main, by appointment only. They are not here now. They are somewhere — *Main*, *Subbasin*, *14* — which are places that have addresses and likely receptionists and likely filing cabinets in which the records of Beryl Bend Bore sit in folders of a particular color. They are an institution, and an institution is a kind of creature, and this one has habits.

The habits are visible. The enamel sign at the water's edge — somebody maintained it, somebody kept the letters sharp, somebody decided that a stranger arriving at the dome should be told the rules. The brick of the housing is intact. The chimney has not fallen. The pipes are sealed to the rock with lead that has gone powdery with age but has not failed. The verbiage on the sign is careful — *Vent* not *Exhaust*, *Instrument Housing* not *Pump House*, *Schedule subject to review* not *Schedule*. They have decided how to talk about the place, and they have decided well, and the decisions are visible in the language the way a beaver's decisions are visible in a dam.

They will come, eventually, or they will send somebody, and somebody will walk the broken path in waders and lift the iron cap of the housing and look at the paper that has been written by then — the paper on which your presence has been inscribed as a small angular deflection — and read it, and take it back, and another paper will be started. Their visits are punctuation. Between visits the place runs itself.

The people of fourteen are polite to the place. They have not named what they listen to. They have given it, instead, an address. They do this so that, on the days when the deflection is large, they will know *where* to write the report, even if they do not yet know *what* to write in it.

---

There is the one who painted the third line.

It is not the same paint. It is not the same hand. It is older — older than the enamel, older than the bureaucratic seal of fourteen, or it is contemporary with some earlier version of fourteen, a fourteen before the office was called fourteen, a fourteen that maybe still called itself by the name of a person. Whoever wrote *its listening don't shout near the pipes* had the grammar of someone who used language carefully but not formally — who thought in the present continuous, *its listening*, as if the act of listening were the principal and ongoing activity of the place; who placed the apostrophe nowhere, or lost it, or never set it; who signed nothing.

The warning is true. The pipes do not like shouting. You can feel it — or you can imagine feeling it — in the way the hum tightens when you move too quickly along the path, in the way the mooring ring's stub tilts in its mud when you lean near it, in the way the reeds at the dome's edge lean, very slightly, *away*. The place is sensitive along its instrumented seams, and the person who painted the third line knew this, and thought it worth a brushstroke in a different blue to tell the next visitor.

They are gone. They are not coming back. They left a record of themselves, the only record, in a paint that is now flaking at its lower edge where the moisture has got under it. They cared about whoever came after. They are an ancestor of the dome.

---

There is the heron.

The heron does not live here. The heron *stops*. There is a feather caught on a reed at the marsh's edge — a long grey plume, the color of the basalt in low light, bleached slightly, the barbules beginning to separate along their length. The heron came to the marsh and hunted or attempted to hunt and found nothing — no fish, no frog, no insect — and stood for a while anyway, because herons will stand for a while even when there is nothing to stand for, and then took off again, and lost the feather on the way out. The reed held it. The reed will hold it until the reed itself falls, which is not soon.

The heron might come back. The heron does not know what the dome is. The heron does not care that the dome is instrumented. The heron will not be perturbed in the way you are perturbed, because the heron is a different shape of thing, and the pipes are calibrated for your kind of perturbation, not the heron's, and the deflection the heron would cause would be wider and less precise and would read, on the future paper, as something the people of fourteen would not know how to name, and they would write *probable avian passage* in a margin, and they would be wrong about the precision and right about the direction.

The heron is the closest the dome comes to a wild thing. The feather is its whole biography.

---

There is the instrument.

You do not, ordinarily, count an instrument as a thing that lives. But the housing has been here longer than anything else has, and is not in any hurry, and that is a quality the dome itself has, and you cannot quite say which one is the original and which is the copy. The brick is the same temperature as the basalt by a margin you cannot feel with your hand. The iron cap has rusted in a pattern that is partly lichen and partly weather and partly the slow patient decision of the metal itself about how it wants to fail. The chimney is still pointing up. The pipes are still throbbing.

The instrument is a kind of body. It has a heartbeat — the hum. It has a breath — the in, out, in, out of the air in the pipes. It has a metabolism — the weeping, the waxing and waning, the copper-green dissolved matter that runs down its face and is not quite sweat and is not quite rain. It has a memory — the paper, the future paper, the recordings. It has a voice, very low, which is not its hum but the off-key quarter-tone and half-tone shifts that happen when the thing under the rock shifts in its dreaming.

The instrument is not alive. But it is the most alive-seeming thing on the dome, and that is because it was built to be a body for something that is not a body, and it has, over the years, started to take on the qualities of that something, the way a glove, after long wear, takes on the shape of the hand inside it.
