# The Tenders

One sees them first as motion where one did not expect motion. The eye has been reading the city as stone for so long that a person standing in a channel or crossing a courtyard appears almost as an error in the pattern — a shape that should not be there, that briefly violates the agreement between the buildings and the light, and then resolves into a figure and is gone around a corner and the agreement is restored.

They are not tall. This is the first surprise, after the size of the city itself, which prepares one to expect grandeur and instead delivers a scale that is mostly lateral. The inhabitants are mostly within a hand's breadth of one another in height, and most of that height is folded forward, slightly, as if they had been at work for so long that the spine had made its own quiet compromise with the stones. They are narrow through the shoulder. Their limbs are long in proportion to their trunks, and their hands are large, and their feet, when one glimpses them bare — which is rare — are broad and pale and the soles are thick, almost padded, as if the body had decided that if it must spend its life on cold wet rock it would make its own insulation.

The feet are almost never bare. They are wrapped. The wrapping is a long strip of a dark fiber that one cannot identify with confidence — it is not wool, it is not linen, it is something between, something that has been worked until it is dense and water-shedding and very nearly black. It is wound from the ankle to the toes in overlapping turns, then bound at the ankle with a thinner cord of the same material. Over this, when they go out into the wet or up onto the higher terraces, they wear a kind of soft shoe — a flat sole of layered hide, sewn with sinew, that grips the stone without clicking against it. One can pass within a few paces of a tender at work and hear nothing of their footfall. They are quieter than the water.

The rest of the body is wrapped in pieces. There is no single garment that one could describe as *the* clothing. There are layers, and the layers are added and removed according to the room and the work and the weather, but a layer is never discarded. It is folded and carried. A tender on the upper terraces in the morning will have a long outer wrap of a heavier fiber, waxed or oiled with something that makes it shine dully in the gray light; inside that, a shorter wrap to the knee; inside that, a shirt-like thing of undyed cloth, belted with a cord; inside that, against the skin, a thin layer that one suspects is never washed but is changed when it can no longer be endured. The colors are all in the range the stones are in. Dark gray. Brown-gray. The faint green-black of wet lichen. A pale ochre that one sees only on the very young. It is difficult at any distance to tell a tender from the architecture they stand against. They have made themselves of the city's palette, or the city has consented to their being made of it.

The hands are what give them away. The hands are darker than the face, and rougher, and they move. They are in almost constant motion, these hands, even when the rest of the body is still. A tender standing in a doorway with the head slightly bowed, apparently at rest, will have the fingers of one hand working against the thumb of the same hand, or the other hand steadying itself on a stone, or the wrist turning slowly as if feeling for a grain. The hands know stones. They can tell you, by touch alone, whether a stone is sound or whether it has begun to give up its hold on the joint — whether the bond between it and its neighbor is still tight or has loosened by a hair's width. They can do this in the dark. Some of them do most of their work by feel, running the pads of the fingers along the seams the way a reader of raised text moves along a line.

The face is the hardest part to read, because it is kept low and still and the eyes are often half-closed. Not in sleep. In attention. The lids are slightly narrowed against the constant faint wet of the air, but also in a kind of habitual contraction, as if the face were saving its expressions for some later accounting. The features are not remarkable in any way one can fix on. The nose is straight or slightly aquiline, more often the latter. The mouth is wide and the lips are thin and often slightly chapped despite whatever unguent is applied to them. The skin across the cheekbones is weathered to a color somewhere between the pale of the lower stones and the dark of the wet ones, and it is mottled, faintly, with a kind of fine veinwork that becomes more pronounced in the cold of the early morning. The hair, when one sees it, is dark and not long, kept cropped close or bound back against the head with a cord, and the older the tender the more gray is in it, until in the very oldest it is almost the color of dry lichen.

The eyes, when one catches them, are a color one cannot name. Not blue, not gray, not green. Something that the city has put there, perhaps. They are the color of wet slate at a certain angle of light, or of the channels when the water in them is running slowly and the stone beneath shows through. They do not widen often. They look at what they are doing. Occasionally they look up, briefly, at the higher ridges where the lightning is, and the expression that crosses them then is not fear and is not worship. It is closer to a kind of measured acknowledgment. *That is also happening. I am aware of it. It does not require me.*

One does not hear them speak. This is not because they do not speak. They speak. They speak to one another in the low hours, in the rooms, in the channels, passing one another on the narrow ways. But the speech is kept so low, and is conducted so often from a slight distance, that what reaches the ear of an outsider is only a murmur, a kind of sustained low hum in which individual words cannot be made out. The sound of a district of the city in the working hours, if one were to listen for it, would be something like the inside of a beehive heard from outside the wall — a continuous, varied, almost-musical low sound, with occasional slight rises in pitch that might be questions or might be the calling of a name. The children are quieter than the adults. The very old are sometimes silent for whole days. Whether this is by choice or by the wearing down of the apparatus of speech, one cannot say.

What one does hear, sometimes, is singing. Not often. Not loudly. A single voice, sometimes two, somewhere in the city at a time when the rain has just stopped and the dripping is heavy and the air is full of mist. The song has no words one can distinguish; it is a long wandering line of sound, sometimes rising and sometimes falling, sometimes breaking into something that almost has shape and then dissolving again. The tenders who hear it do not stop working, but their hands slow, and after a passage has ended they may stand for a moment with the head slightly lifted, as if confirming that the silence after the song is the right kind of silence. Then they go back to their work.

The work is the city. One cannot, after a time, separate the inhabitants from the labor they are engaged in, because the labor is what they are for and the city is what the labor is on. A tender who is not working on the city is doing one of only a few other things: sleeping, eating, drawing water, sitting in a room with another tender or with several others, or walking the terraces without apparent purpose. Even the walking is not purposeless, or not entirely — one is being shown, or one is showing oneself, the state of the stones. A tender out for a walk will pause often to lay a hand on a wall, to lean close to a joint, to follow with one finger the line of a channel. They are reading the city as one reads a familiar text, checking it against an internal version that is always slightly more demanding than the actual one.

The work itself is mostly replacement. A stone has given up — cracked along a grain, lost its grip on the mortarless joint, begun to admit water in a way that will, if left, undermine the next stone up. The tender finds it by feel, or by the faint dark line of damp that moves down the wall below it, or by the slight hollow sound it gives when tapped with the knuckle. The tender goes to the place where replacement stones are kept — there is one in every district, a low walled enclosure open to the weather, filled with stones of various sizes laid out by hand — and chooses one. The choosing takes time. It can take a long time. The tender will hold the candidate stone in both hands, turning it, weighing it, holding it up to the failing stone as if comparing them in conversation. When the right one is found, the tender carries it to the place. The carrying is done against the body, in the crook of the arm or pressed to the chest, and the stone is heavy and the tender is patient with the weight.

The replacement is done without tools of metal, as has been said, but it is not done without tools at all. The tools are bone and stone and sinew and wood. There is a small hammer of antler, light and very hard, used to tap a stone into its final position. There are wedges of harder stone, set into handles of shaped wood. There is a long thin blade of stone, almost a knife, used to clean the joint of moss and the older binding material. There is a cord of twisted sinew, used to lever a stone out and to steady it on its way down. There are also the hands, which are the principal tools. The setting of a stone is mostly done by feel, by the practiced judgment of pressures and angles, with the wedge and the cord used only at the moments when the hands cannot, by themselves, persuade the stone to its place. When the stone is set, the tender runs the flat of the palm along the joint, twice, three times, and then steps back and looks at the wall for a long while before going on to the next thing.

The clearing of the moss is more delicate work. The moss is not regarded as an enemy. Some of it is left. A thin even spread of it on a northern face is considered desirable, because it indicates that the joint is sound and the moisture content of the wall is what it should be. Moss that has begun to grow thick, that is beginning to lift at the edges of a stone, that is beginning to hold water against a joint instead of letting it pass — this moss is taken away. It is taken away by hand, mostly. The tender kneels at the place and works the moss loose with the thumbnail and the edge of a small bone spatula, working from the center outward, so that the moss comes away in pieces that are then carried to a place where they will not regrow. Often this is the channel of running water lower down. The moss is given back to the water. The water carries it, eventually, into the cisterns, and from the cisterns, eventually, into the air.

The cisterns are tended as carefully as the stones. They are the city's slow memory. Each cistern is a chamber cut into the lowest course of a terrace, sealed at the bottom with a layer of fine clay that has been tamped and re-tamped over many years until it is almost porcelain. The water that comes into them has moved through the channels, which means it has passed through many joints, which means it has picked up a faint mineral content — not enough to taste, but enough to give the water a particular coolness and a particular weight in the mouth. The tenders drink this water without ceremony. They carry it up to the higher terraces in clay vessels they have shaped themselves and dried in the wind and never fired, because there is no fire hot enough to fire clay here, and the absence of firing is not felt as a lack. The vessels are kept damp, or they crack. A vessel that is allowed to dry through is broken and returned to the clay of the next one.

The shaping of the vessels is one of the few activities that takes place indoors, in the deeper rooms where the air is calmer and the temperature holds. A tender at the vessel-work sits on the floor with a flat stone before them and a lump of clay that has been wedged and re-wedged until it is without air. The hands do the rest. The vessel grows under the hands without the potter's wheel, which is also absent, and grows slowly, and is turned often, and is tested by being held up to the light from the courtyard to see if the wall is even. When it is finished it is set on a ledge to dry, and the next vessel is begun, and the ledge fills with vessels in various stages of drying, and the room becomes, in time, a kind of quiet museum of the city's own drinking.

What the tenders eat is a question one cannot fully answer. There is no field. There is no flock. There is no fishing. The food that sustains them must come from somewhere, but one does not see it being brought in, and one does not see it being stored in any quantity. The small pale invertebrates under the stones have been mentioned; these are eaten, though rarely and probably as a supplement rather than a meal. There is a kind of pale root that grows in the most protected folds of the terraces, in the soil that has accumulated over centuries in the angles where the runoff is steady — a small white thing, finger-thick, that is dug with a bone tool and eaten raw or after a long soaking in water. There is a green that grows where the moss is densest, a threadlike thing that is dried and then crumbled into the water. There is, occasionally, the meat of one of the dark quick things that run the channels, though this is so rare as to be almost ceremonial. One concludes that the tenders eat very little, and that what they eat is mostly vegetable and mineral, and that the body's relation to its own reserves is one of long acquaintance. They are not hungry often. They are not full often. They are sustained.

The fire is small and rare. There is no hearth in the conventional sense. There is, in some of the deeper rooms, a low stone enclosure in which a fire of dried moss and lichen and the small driftwood that comes down from the higher ridges in the long rain is burned for warmth in the coldest hours. The fire is attended by one tender, who feeds it slowly and never lets it rise. The smoke finds its way out through the same joints that the water finds its way in, which is to say it finds its way out slowly, and the rooms that have had a fire in them smell for days afterward of a particular sharp smoke that mingles with the wet stone smell and becomes, in time, indistinguishable from it. Most of the heat that the city retains is body heat. In the cold hours, tenders sit close to one another in the rooms, without speaking, and the warmth is held between them, and the stones slowly accept it and slowly let it go.

The question of how many there are is also difficult. There is no census, no roll, no announcement. One can be in a district for an hour and see four tenders, and conclude that the district is thinly inhabited, and then turn a corner and find a dozen of them at work in a channel that one had not known was there. They are distributed, but the distribution is uneven, and the pattern of it changes with the weather and the work. In the long rain they are mostly indoors. In the dripping hours after the rain they are out, examining the stones that the rain has tested. In the dry cold mornings they are on the upper terraces, where the wind has been. There is a sense, over the whole city, of a population just sufficient to the work — not more, not fewer — and of the population being adjusted to itself and to the work by some slow process one cannot see.

The tenders know one another, and know one another well. They greet each other in passing with a small gesture that one eventually learns to read: a hand laid briefly on the shoulder or the forearm, or a single low syllable spoken under the breath. The gesture is not always the same. There seem to be several of them, used in different circumstances, and the difference between them is as subtle as the difference between two stones in a wall. Outsiders are not given the gesture. They are given, instead, a kind of polite invisibility — a not-looking that is not unfriendly but is not a welcome. One is allowed to be present. One is not yet included. Whether inclusion is possible, and at what cost, one cannot say from a single visit.

There are children. One sees them rarely, and then only in the late morning, when the sun has been up for an hour and the upper terraces have begun to dry. They are smaller than the adults, as children are, but they are quiet children. They do not run. They do not call out. They move through the courtyards and along the channels with a kind of focused gravity, sometimes carrying a small vessel, sometimes trailing a hand along a wall, sometimes sitting very still beside an adult who is at work and watching the hands. They are being taught, one understands, not by instruction but by proximity. The hands of the adult are the lesson. The child stands close enough to feel the heat of the adult's body and to see the adult's fingers on the stone and to hear, if the adult murmurs, the low sound of the working voice. After many such mornings the child will be allowed to hold a tool. After many more, the tool will be used. This is the whole of the education, as far as one can see. There is no other.

The old are more visible than the children, because the old are still at work, but more slowly, and with more pauses, and with the head lifted more often to the higher ridges. They are allowed to choose their work. One old tender, in a district near the southern ridge, does nothing but walk the channels and tap the stones with the knuckle, listening for the ones that have begun to give up. Another sits for hours in a particular room and reshapes the older clay vessels that have cracked beyond use, pressing the cracks together with a wet thumb and smoothing them with a wet palm until the vessel will hold again. They are not retired. They are re-tasked. The city, which is patient, has patience also for them.

What one notices, after a time, is that the tenders do not seem to age in the way one expects. They age, certainly — the hair grays, the skin mottles, the spine folds further forward — but the process is so slow, and so evenly distributed across the population, that it is difficult to point to a single individual and say *that one is old* with any confidence. They age the way the city ages: in places, at different rates, with some stones lasting longer than others and some joints giving up before the stones they hold. One suspects that they live a long time, and one suspects also that the suspicion is correct, and that the longevity is connected to the work and to the water and to the absence of hurry and to the slow pulse of the city's weather through the body. One does not ask. They do not tell.

They do not, as far as one can tell, die of anything in particular. The deaths, when they occur — and they occur rarely, and are not announced — are signaled by the absence of a figure from a customary place, and by a small change in the work of that district: a channel that is not cleared as it was, a joint that is reset by hands that are not quite the hands that reset it before. The body is taken somewhere that one is not shown. The vessels of the dead are sometimes broken, sometimes kept. One cannot say which is more common, or whether the choice is the dead tender's or the living ones'. After a death, the murmur of the district is lower for a time, and the song, if there is a song that day, has a different interval in it. Then the murmur rises again, and the interval resolves, and the work goes on. The city has absorbed another absence. It is the city's oldest practice.

Whether they are happy is a question one should perhaps not ask. They are not unhappy. The face, in its contracted and half-averted way, is not the face of suffering. There is, in the work, a kind of absorption that one comes to recognize as the nearest thing to contentment one is likely to see in this place. The hands on the stone. The water on the wall. The slow exhale of the city into the morning air. The tenders are part of this, and the part they play is the part they have always played, and the playing of it is what they are for. They do not ask to be other than they are. The city does not ask it of them. The exchange is very old, and very quiet, and it holds.
