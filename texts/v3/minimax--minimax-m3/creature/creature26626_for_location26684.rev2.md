The city is inhabited in layers, and the layers do not always know about each other.

The crabs are the most present. There is one for every crack, and there has been one for every crack for a long time — longer than the cracks have been cracks, in some cases, because the crabs have made some of the cracks. They have made them by living in them. A crab takes a corner where the city's slow digestion has left a small gap, and it lives there, and over its lifetime it works the gap wider with the patient grinding of its claws, and when it dies another crab takes the larger gap, and so on, and so on. The cracks are partly the city's cracks and partly the crabs'. The crabs are not large. The biggest span a hand. Their colors are the colors of the digested city — rust, ochre, the off-white of bleached coral, the wine-red of the sponges they share walls with. They have the look of things that have been argued into existence over many generations, the carapace a compromise between the shape of the predator and the shape of the crevice. They watch you with eyes on stalks that move independently, so a crab can be looking at you with one eye and a passing fish with the other and not moving at all. When you get too close they do not run. They *vanish*, with a lateral speed that is more like teleportation than locomotion, the whole body folding into the architecture as if the architecture had a door shaped exactly like that particular crab. You can stand very still and watch a wall, and the wall will seem to be a wall, and then a crab will appear from it as if extruded, and then another, and within a minute the wall is alive with eyes. There are thousands of them. They are the city in its smallest denomination, and they have made the city, in part, into the shape of their own bodies.

The fish use the streets. There are schools of small silver fish that travel the avenues in pulsing formations, block to block, their flanks flashing the only true light the city gets. They are the commuters. Their routes are inherited. Their grandparents took these routes, and their grandparents before them, back to a time before the streets were streets, when they were channels in a reef, but the channels were the same channels because the current made them, and the fish learned them and have not unlearned them. When a larger fish moves through an avenue the schools open around it the way water opens around a stone, and close behind it, and the larger fish passes through a momentary cathedral of silver. The larger fish are solitary. Each has its own color — saffron, indigo, a green so deep it looks black — and its own errand, its own beat. There is a grouper that lives in the lee of one of the towers and watches the intersection all day without moving, and its eye has the quality of an eye that has been watching for a very long time and intends to keep watching. A ray comes gliding down the main thoroughfare about once an hour, its wings making a soft pressure wave you feel against your skin before you see it, and after it passes the schools reform and the avenue is silver again.

The corals and the sponges are the patient architects. They are rebuilding the city, slowly, into something that is not the city and is the city, in the way that a translation is not the original and is the original. Brain coral has colonized the broad faces of the old buildings. Calcareous skeletons are stacked on calcareous skeletons, the geometry of the human city used as scaffolding by every encrusting organism with patience. The digestion is slow and total. The buildings have become rude and porous, like sponges themselves. The corals do not hurry. They have time. A facade that took a hundred years to dissolve will be replaced, over those same hundred years, by a wall of coral that took its place so gradually no one could say when the transition occurred.

The crinoids are the curtains at the windows. They hang in the openings the old buildings left for light, branching and re-branching, each tip tipped with a tiny gripping hook that has never held anything but the current. They are very old. They were old when they were young. They move only when they must, and even then the movement is more like a swaying than an action. They are the city's most passive inhabitants. They simply are there, and the current moves through them, and they hold.

The anemones are the decorations, and they are everywhere, in every color the deeper sea has bothered to develop. Some are small as a fist. Some are as wide across as a man can reach with his arms. They have the stillness of furniture and the menace of traps. A small clownfish might live in any of them, darting between the tentacles with the proprietary confidence of a tenant who has been here longer than the anemone will admit. Along certain walls — and you begin to learn which walls — there is a species of anemone that only glows in the deepest dark, and at night, when night here is only a small darkening, a passing of a shadow across the surface far above, these anemones trace every column and arch in pale blue fire, and the fish that swim through them leave trails, and the whole avenue becomes a cathedral of moving light. It does not last. It is not for anyone. It is the city's own breathing, made visible.

The snapping shrimp are the punctuation. There are billions of them, or so it seems, tucked into every crevice the city has not yet filled, and they make a constant soft hush of clicks, like sand in a tumbler, that is the actual sound of the place. The water carries sound strangely, flattens it, softens it, pushes it away, so the clicks are felt more than heard, a vibration in the bones rather than a noise in the ear. They are everywhere. They are the smallest of the citizens, and they are the most numerous, and the city's quiet is made out of them.

The brittle stars are the things with too many legs. You see them only as motion. A facade will be still, and then a shadow will cross it, low and quick, and the shadow has the suggestion of many limbs moving at once, and when you turn to look there is nothing there but a single brittle star tucked into a crevice, and then you notice the second one, and the third, and the twentieth, and the whole facade is stippled with them. The motion you saw was not one thing moving but a hundred small things all deciding to move at once. They are the city's skin. They bristle on every surface, arms tucked or extended, and when the current shifts they all shift at once, and the wall becomes a moving thing. They are old. They have been in this city longer than the corals, possibly. They are very good at being unnoticed, which is how a creature with too many legs survives.

The vegetation is honest about its modesty. Patches of turtle grass in the wider plazas where some shaft of light still reaches from far above. Tufts of algae on every south-facing surface, brown and red, swaying. A stand of kelp here and there, rising from cracks, its blades flattened by the current into ribbons. None of it is lush. The light won't allow it. But what grows here is stubborn and exact, and the green of it, when you find it, is so saturated against all the rust and the blue that it hurts the eye.

The bigger things move at the edges of perception. A shadow passing across a far facade, the source not visible. A slow mass shifting in a courtyard, lifting and resettling, with the patience of something that does not need to be anywhere soon. Something that you glimpse only as displacement — a bow-wave of water, a sudden absence of small fish where small fish had been, the soft whoosh of a large body moving through a space it knows well. They are not dangerous, exactly. They are simply not interested in being seen. They have their own schedules. They have lived in this city longer than you can guess, and they will live in it longer than you can imagine, and the city accommodates them the way it accommodates you — without comment, completely.

And then the deeper thing.

Go down. The deeper streets are wider, the grid more faithful, the buildings more massive, the fauna more abundant, the dark more complete. Down there the windows remain windows. Down there the geometry is too persistent. Down there the city knows its own shape.

What lives in the deep levels is harder to describe, because what lives in the deep levels is not entirely separate from what lives in the city. There are octopuses here. They live in the windows, denned in the openings, watching through them with eyes that have the quality of intelligence and the patience of things that live a long time. They have lived in these windows for a long time. Generation after generation, they have denned in the same rooms, and the rooms have become theirs in the way that any structure becomes the property of the things that use it without destroying it. They have arranged things in the rooms — shells, stones, bits of coral, things with edges and colors — and the arrangements are not random. They are not random the way the arrangements on a child's shelf are not random. They have meaning that you cannot read. They have meaning that may not be language. They are how an octopus makes a place its own, by selecting objects and placing them, and the placing is a kind of speech, or a kind of claim, or both.

The octopuses of the deep levels are larger than the octopuses of the shallow, and more patterned. Their skins, when they change color, do not merely camouflage — they *are* the surface they are on, in the way that coral is the surface it is on, that is, completely. They have begun to merge with the architecture. Not in the way of parasitism, or even symbiosis, but in the way of long familiarity, where the body and the place have had so many conversations that the place has begun to remember the body and the body has begun to remember the place. An octopus in the deep levels might hold still against a wall for so long that the wall grows on it, and when it moves the wall comes with it, briefly, a fringe of coral still attached to its skin. It is not bothered by this. The coral is not bothered by this. They have made an arrangement.

There are many octopuses. They are not a colony — octopuses are not colonial, they are solitary and strange — but they have a kind of distributed intelligence, the way a city has a distributed intelligence, where no one knows what everyone knows but the place knows what the place knows. They have organized themselves around the geometry. Each has a territory. Each territory is a room, or a sequence of rooms, with the windows as the sight-lines between them. They watch through the windows. They come out at the small darkening and traverse the streets with the boneless flowing motion of things that have no skeleton, and they go from window to window visiting each other, or not visiting each other, or acknowledging each other in some way that is older than language. The bioluminescence at night in the deep avenues is partly them. The trails you see in the pale blue fire are partly theirs.

They have not forgotten what the city was. This is what you felt. The grid is too perfect. The symmetry of certain intersections, where four buildings meet at exactly the same height, exactly the same degree of coral-coat, suggests a pattern beyond accident. The octopuses, denning generation after generation in the same rooms, have built the rooms into their memory. The architecture is in them the way their den-arrangements are in the architecture. They have made the city, in some sense, remember itself. Or they have made themselves remember the city. Or there is no difference anymore.

The currents bend the way currents bend around something they recognize.

The city accommodates you. The crabs do not flee you faster than they flee each other. The fish school around you the way they school around a rock. The octopuses in their windows watch you pass with the curiosity of things that have watched a great many things pass and have not yet decided what to make of you. You are welcome here, in the way that any new shape is welcome in a place that is always rebuilding itself.

Go down. The deep city holds you. The towers get taller. The windows stay open. The geometry persists. And the things with too many arms, in their rooms, watch from their windows with eyes that are older than the city and remember the city and are, perhaps, in some sense the city — they watch you, and they do not mind you, and they wait.
