Who lives here, then.

The first thing to say is that the categories don't hold. There are people, yes, and there are animals, yes, and there are things that fit neither, and there are things that might be one or the other depending on who you ask and which day of the week it is. The postmistress — we'll come to her; she's central — keeps a careful taxonomy in the back of her ledger, but the taxonomy is more for her own amusement than for any hope of getting it right. She has a column for *seen*, a column for *felt*, a column for *probably*, and a column that is just a series of small drawings she has done over the years, none of which match each other.

The people, then. There are about three thousand of them, though the number is a courtesy. They are a mix of miners, masons, smelter-workers, the small number of company men who come up the rail and don't stay, the engineers who come up the rail and stay too long, the clerks, the children, the elderly, and a few whose occupation no one can quite determine. They are mostly dark-skinned from the dim light and from the lichen-dust that gets into everything. Their eyes have developed, over generations, a particular wideness — an eye-shape that takes in as much of the flat silver light as possible, that doesn't waste any of it. They blink less often than lowlanders. They find direct sunlight, when they descend to it, painful and rude.

They are quiet people, on the whole, but not in the way of shy people. Quiet in the way of people who have learned that the mountain is talking and that you do not, as a rule, talk over the mountain. They speak in a kind of low murmur that carries well in the damp air. Their conversations tend to be long and unhurried, with long pauses in them that are not silences — they are the gaps where you are letting the mountain have its turn. Visitors find this unsettling. The locals find visitors unsettling for the opposite reason.

They are long-lived. No one quite knows why. The census records, going back nine years (and in some places further, where the records have been kept in the old way, in the patterns on the streets), suggest that people here reach ages that lowlanders consider improbable. A woman who was a girl when the mine opened is still sharp-eyed and works the smelter's second shift. A man who came up the first rail is still mending his roof. The children grow slowly — slower than they should — and so, when they are grown, they have more years in them. The lichen does the opposite, the postmistress notes in her ledger; the lichen has not been alive long enough to be old.

The postmistress.

She is the person, or she is the persons, or she is the office. Her name changes. Her handwriting changes. She is a clerk, and she is the mayor, and she runs the smelter on the night shift, and she keeps the goats, and she is, by the testimony of several townspeople (and the testimony of one of the goats), the goat. This last is not a metaphor, or it is a metaphor that has stopped being a metaphor, the way the streets have stopped being streets and become gardens.

She has been here longer than anyone. She keeps the ledger. The ledger began as a record of ore shipments and became a record of weather and became a record of births and became a record of the things seen in the shafts and became, by year six or seven, something more like a journal, or a grimoire, or a love letter to the mountain. She writes in it most evenings. The ink she uses is the same dark mortar that sets between the stones, thinned with water from the cisterns. It does not fade.

She is small and broad and moves through the town with a particular kind of patience, the way a great ship moves through still water. She is the person you go to if a thing is wrong, and she is the person you go to if a thing is right, and she is the person you go to if you cannot tell which. She has a key to every door in town, including the ones that don't have doors. She knows where all the children are at any given moment, including the ones who think they are lost. She is unmarried, or she has been married to the town for so long that the marriage is no longer remarked upon.

The miners.

They are a separate sort. They go up the slope in the early shift, when the cloud is thinnest and the light is most flat, and they disappear into the cut in the rock face that is the mine's mouth. They come back down at dusk, or what passes for dusk here, which is just a slight thickening of the dimness. They carry ore. They also carry, in their faces and in the way they hold their shoulders, something they have brought back from inside the mountain, which is not the ore.

They are devoted to small rituals. They leave cheese in certain shafts. They leave flowers in others. They speak to the stone. Not prayer — the town doesn't have much use for prayer, the mountain being already present and not needing to be addressed. More like a courtesy, the way you might speak to a colleague at the start of a shift. Some of them hum as they work, and the hum, after a while, settles into the rock and becomes indistinguishable from the mountain's own hum, which is the hum that the stone gives off, that the miners feel in their teeth.

The older miners have a particular look. Their eyes are the eye-shape the townspeople have developed, but more so. Their hands have a slight tremor that is not quite a tremor. They have begun, over the years, to develop a faint luminescence of their own — not in the eyes, like the goats, but in the skin of their hands and forearms, where the ore-dust has worked its way in. In the dim light of the mine they are easy to spot. They glow a little. They have learned to live with it.

They are not unhappy. This is worth saying. They are tired, sometimes, and they are quiet, mostly, and they are devoted to the mountain in a way that the lowlanders find difficult to understand, but they are not unhappy. They have a sense of being in on something.

The children.

They are pale, even for the place — pale because they spend less time in the ore-light and more time in the vapor from the chimneys. They play in the vapor when the wind is wrong and the breath of the smelter hangs in the terraces. They run through it and it closes behind them and they come out damp and smelling of nothing in particular and slightly older than they went in. The vapor, the adults say, does not harm them. The adults are probably right.

They have named several of the things in the shafts. They have a long list of names, kept orally, that they pass down to each other with great seriousness. Some of the names are good names. Some of them are nonsense syllables. Some of them are the names of lowland children the miners have told them about, attached now to things in the rock. The names change over time. The children do not seem to mind. They treat the things in the shafts as friends, or as pets, or as the kind of older sibling who is rarely home.

They grow slowly. They have the town-eye, but more pronounced in them — wider, taking more in. They find the dimness normal. When one of them is taken down the mountain to the lowlands, to a school, to a doctor, to a relative, they find the bright light of the lowlands almost unbearably sharp, and they squint through the visit and come back as soon as they can.

The florist.

She is not a florist in the way lowlanders would understand. She cultivates the white flowers that grow in the cracks and at the edges of the cisterns, the ones that smell like perfume left in a drawer. She has tried, for fifteen years, to grow them in pots, in boxes, in stone-cut beds in her garden behind her house. She has failed, consistently, and with great care. The flowers grow where they grow, which is wherever water gathers of its own accord and the masons have cut a small pan for it. They do not transplant.

She has made a kind of peace with this. She is one of the few people in town who has tried to impose a lowland order on the place and has accepted, finally, that the lowland order is not the order here. She is slightly famous for this. The postmistress mentions her, in the ledger, as someone who has understood the town.

The goats.

The goats are the most visible of the fauna and the most beloved. They have been here since the beginning, or near enough. They have developed, over generations, the luminescence in their eyes — a soft pale green, mostly, that is visible in the dimness and that makes them, when they move through the cloud, look like a constellation shifting position at hoof-speed. The children count them when they cannot sleep.

They are fond of the small purple onion-relative that grows in the cracks of the paving stones. They eat it readily. They have been seen, occasionally, eating the lichen — though only the unclassified lichen, the lichen in colors that don't quite fit on a chart. Their milk has a faint taste of metal, like the water. Their cheese is what the miners leave in the upper shafts for the beast.

They are kept by the postmistress, or by whoever the postmistress is that week, or by the postmistress in her aspect as the goatherd. They are also, in some sense, kept by the town. They wander freely through the terraces. They are not herded. They choose where to be, and they choose, mostly, to be where the people are.

One of them is the postmistress. This is the part of the ledger no one argues with.

The dogs.

The dogs are stretched. This is the only honest way to put it. They have grown, over the generations here, unnaturally long in the body — long and low and liquid, as if they have been pulled out gently, like taffy. They move with a kind of grace that is not the grace of lowland dogs; it is closer to the movement of eels, or of water finding its level. They navigate the stepped terraces with a thoughtlessness that suggests they have forgotten how to fall.

Their eyes do not luminesce. Their eyes are very dark, almost black, and they reflect almost nothing. In the dimness they are almost invisible, which is how the town prefers them. They are quiet dogs, mostly. They do not bark — they have developed, over the years, a kind of low whistle instead, very like the birdsong, and the two sounds are difficult to distinguish at a distance.

They are devoted to the children. They are devoted to the goats. They are not particularly devoted to the adults, who they regard as a kind of weather.

The birds.

There are several species. None of them migrate. There is nowhere to go, and the going would be difficult — down through the cloud for forty miles to the lowlands, which are bright and sharp and smell wrong to them. They stay.

Their song is not a song. It is more like a tuning — a low whistle, sustained, that has the quality of someone adjusting an instrument. It is most audible in the early morning, when the cloud is at its thinnest and the light is most silver. The miners say it is the mountain being tuned. The children say it is the birds practicing. The postmistress has not recorded an opinion.

The birds are small and dark and have beaks that are slightly too long. They eat the lichen. They eat the small white flowers when they can. They nest in the eaves of the buildings, in the soft moss that has grown there, and their nests have a faint smell of metal and of nothing.

The beast.

It lives in the upper workings of the mine, in the shafts that are no longer worked, in the places where the ore has given way to something else. It has been seen nine times. The descriptions never match. The postmistress has all nine in the ledger, drawn in her own hand, and the drawings do not match either.

It is the size of a goat, or the size of a small pony, or the size of something that has no size. It has a shape, but the shape changes. It is darker than the rock, or lighter, or the same color, or a color that doesn't have a name. The miners leave offerings for it — cheese, mostly, sometimes flowers — and the offerings are taken. Always taken. The miners do not know by what.

It is not dangerous. This is the part that the lowland engineers, when they come up, find hardest to credit. A thing in the upper workings of a mine, taking offerings, not dangerous. But it is true. It has never harmed anyone. It has, on two occasions, been found asleep across a tunnel in such a way that work could not proceed, and on both occasions it woke, slowly, and moved, and the miners passed by without incident. It seems to be more a feature of the mine than an animal that lives in it. It seems to be, in some sense, the mine's way of being visited by itself.

The others.

The unnamed things. The things that are seen in the shafts and not quite seen. The postmistress's ledger has a column for them and the column is the longest column.

They are shapes that move without quite moving. They are absences with weight. They are sometimes mistaken for the small green-gray lights that the shafts give off, but they are not the lights; they are what casts the lights, if the lights are cast. They have been given names by the children and have been given other names by the miners and have been given, in the ledger, a series of careful drawings that are mostly the postmistress's attempts to draw what she cannot quite see.

They are not dangerous. They are not safe. They are something else, for which the town does not have a word, and the town has decided, on the whole, to leave them alone. They are part of what is here. They are part of why the place is what it is.

The lichen.

It lives everywhere. It is, in some ways, the most populous inhabitant of the town. It grows on every surface that is damp, and most surfaces are damp, and it grows in colors that don't quite fit on a chart — pale violet, gray-green, a kind of lilac, a kind of color that might be silver if silver were warmer. The masons leave places for it. The terraces have small ledges and crevices cut into them that are not for any other purpose; they are for the lichen, and the lichen uses them.

It is the oldest thing here, by the lichen's reckoning, which is the most reliable reckoning. It has been here longer than the mine, longer than the town, longer than the cloud in some readings and the cloud has been here longer than the lichen in others. It grows fast, unlike everything else. It is, the postmistress notes, the only thing in the town that is in a hurry.

It is what gives the place its dimness, in part — the air is full of lichen-spores, fine and almost weightless, and they scatter the light in a way that flattens shadows and softens edges. The town looks, through the lichen-dust, the way things look under water. The town is, in a sense, under water, in the lichen's reckoning.

The mountain.

It lives here too, in the way that anything that hums lives. It has been measured. It has been surveyed. It has been mapped by people whose maps do not match each other. It has given off, in the past few years, a smell like rain on hot iron in the older shafts. It has begun, in places, to give off the small lights. It hums at a frequency that is felt in the teeth of anyone who stands in the right place.

It is not, the town has decided, an it. It is more like a place that has opinions. It is more like a place that is paying attention. It is more like a place that is, slowly, deciding what it wants to be, the way the stone is deciding, the way the terraces are deciding. The miners are devoted to it. The postmistress has dedicated her ledger to it. The lichen has been growing on it for longer than it has been itself.

It is what the town is built on, or inside, or underneath. It is what the cloud sits on, or what sits in the cloud. It is the thing the underview is, when the underview is most itself — a vast dim space, full of cities-that-are-not-cities, full of forests-that-are-not-forests, full of a sea-that-is-not-a-sea, all of it pressing up against the cloud from below, the way the town presses down into the mountain from above.

What lives here, then, is a town, and a mountain, and a cloud, and an ore, and a lichen, and a beast, and a postmistress who is also a goat, and a herd of goats whose eyes are pale green constellations, and a pack of stretched dogs that move like water, and a flock of small dark birds that tune the morning, and a series of children who play in the vapor and name the things in the shafts, and a series of miners who leave cheese in the upper workings and come home glowing faintly in the skin of their forearms, and a florist who has made peace with not being able to move the flowers, and a series of things that are not quite shapes, and a small green-gray light in the shafts that is sometimes mistaken for stars and is not stars.

What lives here is a place that is slow, and damp, and dim, and strange, and that has been for longer than it hasn't been, and that goes on.
