# The Inhabitants of the Spore-Plain

What lives here does not live *in* the place. It lives *as* the place. The distinction matters because everything on the plateau has, over a span of time too long to be called history, become continuous with the surface it stands on — and so to describe what lives here is to describe something that does not quite separate itself from the ground, the wind, the light, the long bruise of the horizon. The creatures of the spore-plain are not visitors to a habitat. They are expressions of one.

---

## The Carpet

Let me start with what is barely alive, because on this plateau the question of what counts as alive is itself a kind of weather, always shifting, never resolving.

The carpet is composed of plants that do not have a common name because no one who named them survived the transit between worlds that would have made naming possible. They are best thought of as a single organism that has not yet figured out how to admit it. A handful pulled up from the dust and held in the palm would be a fistful of pale straw with a faint amber core; left in the wind for an hour it would become a fistful of dust that still, faintly, holds a shape. The plants are dry in the way that paper is dry — they have not so much lost their water as completed a long negotiation with it, arriving at an arrangement where dryness is not a deprivation but a state of being.

They grow in tussocks. A tussock here is not a single plant; it is a federation, a small republic of root and stem and spore-bearing body that has decided, over many generations, to occupy a particular footprint. The tussocks on the ridge-tops are older, taller, gnarled into shapes that look almost deliberate — almost architectural, almost like cairns. The tussocks in the valleys between are younger, smaller, paler, and they move. Not fast. Not in any direction a person would call traveling. But over a season a tussock can drift by the length of a forearm, pulled by the prevailing wind, leaving behind a faint scar of disturbed dust where its roots once anchored. A tussock that dies does not fall. It dries where it stands, hollows out, becomes a kind of straw igloo, and within that igloo the next generation begins as a powder, as a thought, as a possibility held in suspension until a gust passes through and the structure collapses outward and the spores are taken.

The spores are the second skin of the place. They hang in the air for hours after any disturbance. They catch the light at the right hour of the day and the entire atmosphere becomes faintly gold, faintly thick, as if the air has remembered it was once something denser. The spores do not need to land immediately. They can wait. They are patient in the way that only very small things can be patient, the way dust is patient, the way a held note is patient. When they land they do not germinate unless the landing is right. Some wait a season. Some wait a century. Some are taken by the fauna and passed through a digestive system that has, over evolutionary time, become a kind of mobile germination chamber, and are deposited, in the warm mineral dust of a scat, in exactly the chemistry they needed to begin.

The plants photosynthesize. Yes. Off the shifted-spectrum sun, yes. Off trace hydrocarbons, yes. But there is also, in certain of the larger tussocks, a faint internal luminescence — not enough to read by, not enough to navigate by, but enough to be noticed by someone walking the ridges at night, enough to make the plateau look, in the deep hours, like a city seen from very far away. No one has ever explained this luminescence. No one is going to. It is part of what the place is.

## The Reapers

If the carpet is the body of the plain, the machines are its digestion.

They are called the reapers, in the mouths of the people who come to take water, which is to say they are called that in a translation that does not quite capture what they are. The name suggests intent. The machines do not have intent. They have function, in the way a hurricane has function. They are the way the plateau keeps itself from becoming a forest.

A reaper, seen from a distance — and they are usually seen from a distance, because the sound of one approaching is something the body understands before the mind does — is a low, broad shape that moves across the ridges with a kind of patient inevitability. Up close it is something harder to hold in the mind. It has a wide, flat underbelly studded with what appear to be teeth but are more like the cutting surfaces of a threshing machine crossed with the molars of something that has never been a mammal. Its top is a humped carapace of a metal that has gone the color of dried blood and then gone past dried blood into something darker, something almost black, something that drinks the light rather than returning it. It moves on limbs — jointed, articulated, ending in feet that are shaped for the dust and that leave prints the way a snowshoe leaves prints. From the carapace, vents open and close in a slow rhythm, and from the vents come the heat that allows the larger reapers to glide, on a skirt of superheated air, a hand's breadth above the surface of the plain. The smaller ones crawl. The very largest — and they are rare, and they move slowly, and the locals speak of them the way travelers speak of glaciers — seem to half-crawl, half-surge, as if the ground itself were rising to meet them.

What they eat is the upper growth of the carpet. They take the tussocks down to a height of perhaps a finger's width. They do not eat the roots. They do not eat the dust. They eat only what would, if left, become the next canopy, the next obstruction to wind, the next disruption of the equilibrium that has held for a thousand years. They are, in this sense, the memory of the place — they remember what the place was before the last growth cycle, and they keep it close to that memory.

Behind a reaper, the plain is gray. The ash it leaves is thin, and it is warm, and it is rich in a way the undisturbed dust is not — rich in the broken chemistry of what was eaten, in the nitrogen of the shredded cells, in the carbon of a thousand incomplete lives. Within a season the first green-yellow shoots rise from the ash. (They are not green. They are the color of weak tea, of diluted honey, of the inside of a fist unclenched. But they are new.) Within three years the scar is invisible. Within fifty years the place where the reaper passed is indistinguishable from the place where it did not.

When a reaper fails it does so gradually. A joint seizes. A vent cools. A limb drags and then stops. The machine does not collapse; it simply ceases, in the way a clock ceases, and where it stopped it stays, and the plants come up around it, and the dust fills the spaces between its limbs, and in a decade it is a hill, and in half a century it is gone, taken by a passing reaper on its own errand, ground down and mixed with the next meal of carpet. The locals say the reapers do not die. They say they are *reincorporated*. The reapers do not contradict this because the reapers do not speak.

The sound of a reaper is the sound that lives under every other sound on the plain. It is a grinding that is also a tearing that is also, in the larger machines, a kind of singing — a harmonic that rises off the carapace when the wind is right, when the vents are timed a certain way, when the angle of the sun is such that the metal expands in a particular pattern. The harmonic is not melodic. It does not resolve. But it is unmistakably *made*, the way a bridge is made, the way a wire is made, and it is the closest thing the plateau has to music.

No one knows how many reapers there are. They are counted in the same way the tussocks are counted, which is to say they are not counted. There are more than a person can hold in the mind. There are fewer than there were a thousand years ago, or perhaps more; no one has ever compared one year to another and produced a number that meant anything.

## The Drifters

The fauna of the plateau fly. This is the first thing the eye learns and the first thing the ear unlearns, because on the ground the fauna are also present as sound — as the constant layered calls, as the wing-beat heard from inside a thicket, as the sudden silence that falls over a valley when something passes overhead that the smaller things have learned to be still for.

The largest of them are called, in the mouths of the water-takers, the longwings. They are what you would imagine if you imagined a creature designed to ride a wind that comes in rolls the size of buildings. They have wingspans that, in the oldest of them, can be wider than a house is long, and the wings are not feathered in the way that wings are feathered on wetter worlds; they are paneled, ribbed, made of a material that is more like pressed fiber than like quill, and they are translucent in a way that lets the sun through in colored bands. A longwing riding an updraft over a warm ridge, with the light at the right angle, is a stained-glass window that has decided to move. They are slow. They bank in circles that can take a quarter of an hour to complete, and at the top of each circle they fold their wings and drop, and the drop is a kind of scream, and then they catch the air again and rise. They eat the medium-sized fauna. They eat the smaller reapers when a reaper fails and is still soft enough to be torn. They are not predators in the way that predators on other worlds are predators. They are participants in a system.

The medium fauna are the bands — the streamers, the small bright things that move across the plain in columns, in rivers, in sheets that are a thousand bodies thick and a hundred meters long. They are the size of a thumb joint. Their wings are chitinous, and they catch the light, and when a band passes through a sunlit stretch of air the light is broken into pieces that drift down through the still air for hours afterward, the way motes drift in a room with a single window. The locals call this drift the *fall-light*, and they consider it a kind of weather. The bands move together. They are not swarming in the way that bees swarm, with a queen and a logic; they are moving together in the way that a flock of starlings moves together, with no leader and no plan, only a shared response to the wind and to the food and to each other. A band that loses members does not mourn. A band that gains members does not welcome. It simply continues, slightly altered, the way a river continues.

The smallest fauna are not seen so much as felt. They are in the dust. They are in the air. They are the reason the air smells the way it does — that library smell, dry, sweet, faintly alive. They are the pollinators of the carpet. They are the food of the bands. They are so numerous that their absence would constitute a different kind of weather. They do not have names. They do not need names. They are the medium through which the larger fauna exist.

The fauna do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They are made of the same dry chemistry as the plants, and they get their water — the trace water, the water held in the chemistry of their food — from eating. They eat each other. They eat the spores. They eat the small dead reapers, the dust of failed machines, the calcium and the iron and the faint held moisture of a hundred previous lives. Their digestive systems are long, slow, patient, and they extract everything. What they excrete is dry and pale and becomes part of the dust within a season.

Their sound is the third layer of the plateau's constant noise. Under the wind, under the reapers, the fauna call. The calls are not songs. They are signals, in the way that a cricket's call is a signal — there is information in them, but not in any code a person could learn. The longwings have a low hooting that can be felt in the chest before it is heard with the ear. The bands have a high thin buzz that, when a thousand of them are calling at once, becomes a kind of shimmer in the air, almost visible. The smallest fauna make no sound that a person can hear, but the cumulative effect of their tiny movements, their wings, their legs, their constant coming and going in the dust, is a kind of hush that underlies everything else, the way the hum of a city underlies the city's louder noises.

## The Dust Itself

I have left the dust until last because the dust is what remains when everything else has been taken into account.

The dust is not abiotic. It is plant. It is spore. It is the shed skin of the tussocks, the dried husks of generations, the fine powder that is left when a tussock has completed its life and collapsed outward. It is also fauna — the bodies of the smallest things, the scat of the larger things, the broken wings, the cast husks. It is also machine — the rust of the reapers, the fine metallic powder that the reapers shed as they move, the dust of the dust of failed machines that were ground down by other machines. The dust is everything the plateau has ever been, reduced to a common substance.

The wind takes the dust and moves it. Not far. Not fast. But constantly. In long slow drifts that take a season to cross a valley. In standing waves, in rollers, in slow crested dunes that march across the plain and then, when the wind changes, march back again. The dust is the memory of the place. It is also the medium of the place. It is also, in a way that no one on the plateau would say out loud, the place itself — because the tussocks rise from it, the reapers sink into it, the fauna nest in it, the wind carries it, and when a person stands on the plateau they are standing not on a ground but on a slow accumulation of everything that has ever been here, waiting, very patiently, to be something again.

## The Whole, Together

To stand on the spore-plain is to be inside a system that does not have you. The system has the plants. The system has the reapers. The system has the fauna. The system has the dust and the wind and the shifted-spectrum sun. The system does not have a person. A person on the plain is a foreign body, a temporary thing, a mote that has wandered into the machinery and will, in a season or in a century, be incorporated and forgotten.

This is not a hostile forgetting. The plain does not reject a person. It simply continues. The wind does not pause for a person. The reapers do not turn aside for a person. The fauna do not flee from a person or flock to a person; they are indifferent in the way that weather is indifferent, and the indifference is not cruelty. It is the form of the place.

What lives here, then, lives in a system that is held in place by being constantly disturbed. The reapers crop the plants. The plants regrow. The fauna eat the regrowth. The fauna die and become dust. The dust feeds the plants. The wind moves the dust. The wind shapes the ridges. The ridges determine where the reapers travel. The reapers, traveling, disturb the dust. The cycle is not cyclical; it is turbulent. It does not return to a beginning. It moves. It has been moving for so long that the movement has become the place.

And the place is bright. And the place is loud. And the place is, in a way that has nothing to do with persons, alive.

If you stand here and close your eyes you are not lost. You were never the point. The point is the going-on of things, and the going-on does not pause, and does not notice, and does not, in any way that can be measured, care. But it is, if you stay long enough and listen long enough, very beautiful. The beauty is not for you. It is the beauty of a fire, of a river, of a thing that is itself only because it has not stopped.
