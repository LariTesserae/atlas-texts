What lives here is not what you would call living, and yet the place is not dead. It has tenants, of a kind. Several kinds, and they do not know about each other.

**The algae** are the closest thing to natives. They write the walls. They are patient and pale — adapted to the narrow band of wavelength that survives the descent, the light the water does not eat. They climb the white tile of the lobby in green-brown strokes that look like handwriting because they follow lines, threads, the path of least resistance, the path of moisture, the path of whatever dissolved mineral the brick is willing to lend them. On the wrought-iron legs of the bench in the old waiting room, they make lacework. On the marble lip of a fountain that no longer spills, they make a soft green mustache. They are slow in the way that geology is slow, and almost as indifferent. They do not know the bench is a bench. They do not know the lobby is a lobby. They know warmth, wavelength, mineral, and they extend toward what suits them. They are the only inhabitants still doing something that resembles growth. Everything else is maintenance.

**Below them, the chemistry continues.** The brass has gone verdigris because the water has taken the brass at the atomic level and is remaking it into something else — a slow transaction, a patience measured in decades. The chrome of the automobiles is being eaten by calcium. The marble has softened into a kind of grain. These are not really inhabitants. They are processes. But the processes have direction. They are the digestion of the upper world by the lower one, ongoing, never quite complete, the long hydrolysis of a civilization into its constituent salts.

**Then there is the mechanical heart.**

The generator in the basement of the municipal building has been turning over for so long that its fuel source has become folklore. Perhaps it draws from a tank filled once and never emptied. Perhaps it has been rigged, in the decades since the water rose, to siphon from the old gas lines, the legacy of a city that burned things underground. Perhaps it runs on something stranger — methane from the slow anaerobic working of the drowned streets, a fuel the city is producing from its own digestion. No one alive knows. The point is that it turns. The pumps, somewhere in the sub-basements, pump. The treatment facility, if it is a treatment facility, cycles. The motors, deep in the iron labyrinth below the sewer lines, turn because they were built to turn and have not received the signal to stop.

These are the strangest inhabitants. They have no metabolism in the biological sense. They have no reproduction, no intention, no awareness. But they have rhythm. The hydrophone pressed against the masonry of the old bank picks up the cycling — a pulse, an interval, a pulse. It is the heartbeat of something that is not alive. It is the heartbeat of a system that maintains itself because the alternative is to stop, and stopping requires a decision, and there is no one left to make the decision, and so the decision is never made, and the system continues, and continues, and continues.

**The sodium lamp** on the corner of Fifth still burns behind its shattered glass. It throws its amber light onto the façade of a building whose name is being eaten by algae and softened by immersion. It throws the shadow of a lamppost onto the wall. The shadow is a shape the lamp does not know it is making. The lamp does not know there is no one to see it. It burns because burning is what it does. The shadow is a kind of art — the only art produced here — and it is produced by a mechanism that has no aesthetic sense, that has no sense at all, that simply continues to throw its patient amber onto whatever surface the geometry of the corner presents.

**The water itself, in a sense, is alive.** It has stratified by salinity. It has thermal layers that do not mix. It has found its dissolved-oxygen equilibrium. It circulates through the corridors of the grid that was never meant to hold this much, in slow convection currents that a diver can feel but not see. It is a body of water that has organized itself. It is not a lake. It is not an ocean. It is something more like a lung — exchanging gases with the atmosphere thirty feet overhead, exchanging minerals with the brick below, exchanging nothing with anything else, because there is nothing else to exchange with.

**Taken as a whole, the place is a kind of organism. Or a kind of machine.** The distinction has blurred past recovery. The algae write. The lamp paints. The pumps pulse. The water stratifies. The brass changes. The equilibrium holds. None of these things know about the others. None of them coordinate. And yet the system continues in something resembling balance — a frozen metabolism, a thinking without a thinker, a dream without a dreamer. A system at rest with itself. A reservoir that is also a body. A body that is also a cathedral.

**No animals.** Not even the ghosts of animals. The rats — and rats would have outlasted the humans, you'd have thought — did not come down, or did not stay, or never came at all. The birds are above, where the weather is. The dogs are gone. The absence has its own presence, the way an empty theater has a presence. You feel watched by the lack of watchers. The hydrophone, pressed to the iron spine of a fire escape, will give you the cycling of the industrial heart, but it will not give you the small warm scufflings of mammals, the click of claws on tile, the wingbeat against a window. Those frequencies have been silent for longer than the building has been wet.

**And the diver, when the diver comes.**

The diver is not an inhabitant. The diver is a visitor, intermittent, in the gear that no one wears anymore. The diver comes down through the thirty feet of dark water and spends an afternoon weaving through apartments where fish never learned to live, gliding under the canopy of trees that died and stood and were mummified in place by the cool and the dark. The diver takes nothing, leaves nothing. The diver is a seasonal migrant — present for a few hours, then gone for months, then back. The place does not know the diver is there. The place does not register the diver's passage. The hydrophone will not record the bubble streams. The algae will not grow differently around the diving mask left behind for a moment on a lobby chair. But the diver, perhaps, registers the place. The diver is the only thing in the reservoir that carries a memory of the surface, of the upper world, of what the city was before the walking stopped.

Above, the boat waits. The diver surfaces. The city above — if it is still a city — goes on without looking down.

Below, the algae write another line on the tile of a lobby whose address no one alive can give you. The lamp paints another shadow on a façade whose tenant signed a lease in another century. The pumps cycle their patient interval. The water holds. The brass changes. The equilibrium, which is not death and is not life and is not waiting for anything, persists.

The place does not know it is abandoned. The place continues. The place is a reservoir now, and a reservoir only requires that the water be kept, and the water is being kept, and somewhere a generator runs, and the amber light on the corner of Fifth still throws the shadow of a lamppost onto the wall, and the scarf still waves or drowns, and the cool is maintained, and the stillness holds.

The reservoir does not look up.
