# The Hands of the Place

They are not numerous. A few thousand across the length of the plateau, perhaps fewer; the birds in some seasons outnumber them, and the stones outnumber them by orders of magnitude the mind cannot hold. They are a small people in a vast city, and they are not distressed by this. The city was here before them and will be here after them. They are the part that tends.

They are somewhat shorter than a standing man would be, and somewhat narrower in the shoulder, and they have been shaped — over generations, by long patience — to the architecture they tend. A stranger unused to the city must duck often, must turn sideways in the narrow gaps between platforms, must learn that the route is not a line but a braid. They have forgotten that they do this. The passages fit them. They fit the passages. The arrangement is old.

Their skin is dark, but not the deep brown of strong sun. The sun here gives little heat, and so the pigment is the pigment of weather — of cold, of damp, of thin air, of generations that have stayed still while the world around them changed. The skin takes on a slight gray in the deeper cold and a faint olive in the warmer passages. The eyes are large and dark, protected by folds that are slightly heavier than elsewhere on the face, as if the constant wind were considered when the face was made. A person here without those folds is a person from elsewhere, or a child not yet grown into them.

Their hands are what one notices, if one notices anything at all about them. They are not large hands. They are hands that have been made by a particular kind of work — the work of looking at a stone for a long while before deciding where it goes. The fingers are broad at the tips and slightly flattened. The nails are short and thick and often chipped at the edges where they have been used as small tools. The pads of the fingers are smooth, almost glazed, from years of handling wet stone. A child here begins to handle stone almost from the time it can walk, and by the time it is grown the pads have taken on the texture of the stones themselves — cool, slightly damp, without the fine ridges of a hand that has done other work. They are hands that know what they are touching.

Their feet are wrapped. This is one of the first things one sees, after the silence. The wrappings are layers of hide and woven fiber, bound with cord, dark with use, often damp. They protect the foot from the cold of the stone, and they give the foot a surer purchase on the wet surfaces. A person here without wrapped feet is a person in some distress — a stranger, a child not yet taught, or someone whose wrappings have failed and who must return to the room to make new ones. The wrappings are renewed often. They are not repaired indefinitely; they are remade. The skill of making them is taught early, the way other skills are taught early in other places.

Their clothing is layered and close. Wool from the small pale animals kept in the most protected folds — there are a few such animals, kept more for their wool than for anything else, perhaps not even kept but simply allowed. Cloth woven tight. A long tunic, belted, falling to the knee or below. Over this, in the colder hours, a second layer, and a third. A hood, often up. The hands emerge; the wrists are covered. The face is half-covered when the wind is up. They do not wear bright colors. The dyes available are the dyes of lichen and moss and certain pale roots, and these give browns and dark greens and the black of certain lichens that grow only on the upper walls. A person in red is a stranger. A person in white is a person in mourning or in some private observance. A person in yellow is a person who has been somewhere else.

They do not speak above a murmur. This is not because they have chosen silence as a discipline, though they may have. It is because the city does not require noise, and they have become a city-shaped people. A voice at full volume in this place is jarring, almost offensive — it carries off the stones and into the mist and comes back changed. They speak in low tones, in long vowels, in the consonants that pass through stone without startling it. A conversation here is often half-heard at a distance and only fully heard when one is close, and the closeness is not the closeness of strangers. When two of them meet on a terrace they do not stop. They fall into step for a few paces, or they pass with a single low word, or they sit for a moment on a low wall and the conversation is conducted in such low tones that a person ten feet away hears only the murmur of it, like the murmur of water in the channels. They are not unsociable. They are fitted to the place, and the place is fitted to quiet.

Their tools are bone and stone and sinew. There is no metal anywhere among them, and there cannot be. The damp would have it. The cold would have it. The stones have outlasted any metal that might once have come here, and they have made a world without it. A hammer of hard stone, weighty, bound with hide to a handle of antler. A chisel of bone — sometimes the long bone of a small animal, sometimes a tooth, ground and shaped and polished until it will take the edge of stone and not shatter. A wedge of stone, set with care. A lever of wood — and there is a little wood here, though not much; what there is is precious, brought down from the higher ridges or obtained in trade with peoples from elsewhere that one rarely sees. Sinew for binding. Gut for string. The tools are not numerous. Each is kept. Each is repaired until it cannot be repaired, and then it is broken down and the parts are used elsewhere. Nothing is thrown away. Nothing has been thrown away in a long time.

Their vessels are clay. Clay is one of the things the plateau gives them. There are deposits of it in certain of the folds — pockets of fine gray clay that holds its shape when it is shaped and that fires in the small fires they keep. The vessels are simple. A pot for water. A bowl for food. A flat tray for the drying of certain things. The forms are not varied; the same shapes recur again and again, in slightly different sizes, made by hands that have made them before. A pot here is not decorated. It is shaped, it is dried in the wind, it is fired in a small careful fire, and it is used. When it cracks it is mended with a paste of the same clay, or it is broken down and the pieces are used to mend other vessels. There are no broken pots in this city. There are only pots in various stages of use.

Their fires are small. A fire here is not a fire for warmth, because the city is not cold in the way that requires a great fire — it is only cool, and the body adjusts, and a great fire would be a disturbance. A fire is for the firing of clay, or for the softening of sinew, or for the occasional burning of a thing that must be burned. The fires are fed with the dried moss and lichen and the small pale roots and the dung of the few animals. They burn low. They give off little smoke, because the fuels are dense and dry and the draft is good. A fire here does not announce itself across the city; it is known only in its immediate district. The smell of a fire is the smell of the city itself — moss and lichen and damp stone, with an undertone of something resinous that one cannot quite place.

Their food is sparse. They are not a hungry people, but they are not a well-fed people either. They eat what the plateau gives them. The mosses, in certain seasons, can be eaten — not as a staple but as a supplement, prepared in a particular way, dried and rehydrated and cooked slowly. The lichen is bitter and is eaten rarely, and only by those who know which kind. The grass that grows in pale tussocks along the channels can be eaten at certain times of the year. The small pale invertebrates under the stones are gathered with care and prepared with care and eaten in small quantities. There are the few animals, and these are killed rarely; when one is killed the meat is shared across a district in a way that is understood rather than announced, and people come and eat and do not speak much and go back to their work. A meal here is not a large thing. It is a bowl of something, eaten slowly, often in silence, often while sitting on a low wall or on the edge of a terrace. It is fuel for the day's work, and the day's work is the maintenance of the city, and the maintenance is the point.

Their work is the stones. This is what they do. They have always done this. They go out into the city in the slow hours and they look at the stones. They look at the joints. They look at the channels. They look at the moss where it has begun to choke a joint, and the lichen where it has grown too thick on an upper wall, and the water where it has begun to take a wrong path. They identify the stones that have finally given up — the ones that have cracked, the ones that have been displaced by a season of frost, the ones that have lost their fit. They remove these stones with care, often two people working together, using the levers of bone and wood to lift them. They set new stones in the places where the old ones were, and the new stones are not new — they are stones that have been shaped for this purpose over weeks or months, fitted with patience to the place they will go. The new stone is set. The joint is cleared. The moss is removed, or trimmed, or allowed to grow elsewhere, where it will not choke. The channel is reset. The cistern is checked. The work is done slowly, in the cool hours, by people who do not seem to be in any hurry about it. There is always more work. There will always be more work.

They do not seem to be in any hurry. This is the most noticeable thing about them, after the silence. They move through the city in the slow hours, and the slow hours are most of the hours. They are not lazy. They are not idle. They have learned the pace of the stones. A stone does not hurry. A joint does not hurry. A channel does not hurry. The water moves through the city at the speed of water through stone, which is slow. They have become people who move at this speed. A stranger unused to this pace feels that they are barely moving, that they will never arrive; but they do arrive, and when they arrive they are not out of breath, because the body has learned the pace.

They have few children. The city is not a place that feeds many bodies, and it cannot feed many new bodies either. A woman here might bear one child, or two, in the course of her life, and the bearing is not easy in the thin air. The children are raised carefully. They are kept in the warmer rooms near the central fires of a district in their first year, and brought out slowly, in the second year, when they can be wrapped and carried. They learn to handle stone almost from the time they can walk. They learn the wrappings. They learn the murmur. They learn which stones can be moved and which cannot, and they learn this by being corrected, gently, by older hands. A child here does not play loudly. A child here plays in the small passages, with small stones, in low voices. By the time it is grown it is a person who knows the city in a way that a stranger cannot learn in any amount of time.

There is no chief that one can see. There is no council, no hall of assembly, no formal speaking-place. There are elders, in the loose sense — people who have been here longer, who know certain stones, who can identify the work of a particular season by looking at a particular wall. When a decision must be made — about which stones to reset first, about whether a cistern needs to be moved, about how to handle a long rain that has caused some damage — the elders are consulted, but the consultation is not formal. It is a matter of sitting on a low wall with a particular person and murmuring for a while. The decision emerges. The decision is not enforced by anyone. It is simply the decision that the murmuring produced, and people begin to act on it. This is how it has always been, as far as one can tell. It may be how it has always been, as far as they can tell.

They have rituals, though they do not call them rituals. There are markings on certain stones — in certain places, in certain folds — that may be marks of counting or marks of observance or marks of nothing at all; one cannot tell by looking. There are the ways of passing one another on a terrace — the slight inclination of the head, the single low word, the falling into step for a short distance. There are the meals that are shared when an animal is killed, understood rather than announced. There are the times when a person dies. The body is wrapped in cloth and carried to a place, one of the most protected folds where the walls shelter the ground, and it is laid there and stones are placed over it and the place is left. The dead are not mourned loudly. They are remembered in the murmur. They are remembered in the work — the stones they knew, the channels they cleared, the joints they reset. A person who has done a great deal of work lives on in the city itself, in the places their hands have touched. They do not disappear from the city when they die. They are in the stones.

They do not travel much. The city is vast, and a person might live a whole life in a single district and not see the whole of it. There are some who travel — the stone-shapers, who must go to the places where the clay is and to the places where new stones are prepared; the wool-gatherers, who must go to the higher folds where the few animals are kept; the rare traders, who go to the edges of the plateau and meet, in some quiet way, with peoples from elsewhere. But these are not common. Most people are most of their lives in the district where they were born. The districts are not different from each other in any obvious way. They are the same construction, the same stone, the same damp, the same channels. They are separated by full half-days of walking, and a person who walks from one to the other sees, over the course of the walk, the city becoming the same city, over and over, in different folds of the plateau. The districts may not have names. They may simply be the next district, and the next.

The strangers who come — and they do come, sometimes, though not often — are received with a particular kind of patience. They are not welcomed loudly. They are not turned away. They are given a room, in the district where they arrive, and they are watched, with care, to see what they will do. A stranger who tries to speak above a murmur is gently corrected, by example rather than by instruction. A stranger who walks too quickly is allowed to tire and then is shown the pace.
