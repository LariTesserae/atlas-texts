# What Lives Here

The stems are not the inhabitants. They are the inhabitants the way a continent is an inhabitant — present before everyone, so integrated into the geography that calling them alive and calling them landscape become the same sentence. They are the oldest citizens. They were here when the water was younger and the plateau was a louder place. They have watched everything else arrive.

---

Begin with the worm.

It is not a worm. Nothing here is what it is called. It is a long soft ribbon of pale tissue, segmented in the way all soft things are segmented — not by choice but by the demands of length — and it lives entirely inside the bark of a single stem. It entered, by way of an injury or a softened patch, perhaps four hundred years ago, when it was the length of a finger. Now it is the length of a road. It does not know it is inside a living thing. The stem does not know it is being inhabited. The arrangement is, from the perspective of either party, indistinguishable from geology.

The worm eats the inner bark slowly. It does not eat fast. Fast eaters have no future here. It tunnels and the tissue behind it calluses over and continues to live, continues to do its slow lifting work, and the worm moves forward into the next layer. It has been doing this its entire life. It will do this its entire death. There is no terminus. The stem is, for practical purposes, infinite in the direction the worm is going, because the stem keeps growing in that direction too, laying down new inner bark faster than the worm can eat the old. The worm is pruning a tree that is also a continent. It thinks it is making a living.

It has no eyes. It has no ears in any conventional sense. It has a long gut and a patient relationship with calcium. It responds to light by retreating deeper, which is the only intelligence it requires.

---

The fish that drifts past you in the dark is not a fish.

It is a small pressurized vessel, perhaps eight inches long, with a gas bladder full of carefully manufactured oxygen lifted from the crown of a stem and released into the water at a depth the fish knows how to find. It is shaped for stillness. Its fins are vestigial, its tail is a whisper, its body is a hull. It hangs in the water the way a thought hangs in a mind. To see it move you would have to watch for a long time.

It eats the exudate. The exudate is a snow of fine organic particles — shed tissue, excreted minerals, dead bacteria, the cast-off skin of the upper fronds — and it falls continuously from the crown of every stem in the basin, a million silent years of dandruff, and it is the bread of this place. The fish positions itself in the plume. It opens its small mouth. It waits. The snow falls into it. The fish does not need to chase anything. The fish has never needed to chase anything. Its predators are few and slow, and it has arranged its entire life around not moving.

It is luminescent the way a window is luminescent — not to produce light but to let a small internal chemistry become visible. It glows faintly when disturbed. The disturbance is usually a current, a shadow, the passage of something larger. The glow is not a warning. It is closer to a flush. It is the fish becoming briefly legible in the dark water, and then dimming again, and then being only a shape and then being only a presence and then being only the faintest suggestion that something is there.

It has been alive, this individual fish, for perhaps eleven years. This is a long life for its kind. It has produced, in that time, no great work. It has eaten perhaps six million particles of exudate. It has drifted perhaps four hundred kilometers in net displacement, most of which has been vertical, riding the morning plume up and the evening plume down. It has mated twice, on the dark side of the stem, in a brief collision with another fish that lasted less than a second. It does not know it is luminous. It does not know what a stem is. It knows a plume. It knows a shadow. It knows the dark.

---

The mollusks are the architects.

They live on the bark in clusters, cemented into place as larvae, and they never move again. They grow in concentric rings around their anchor point, adding calcium in layers that match, in thickness and chemistry, the layers the stem is laying down inside itself. They are, in effect, small editions of the stem — the same patient mineral logic, the same accumulation, the same indifference to time. A cluster of mollusks on the lower bark is a village of tiny steeples. Each one is filtering water continuously, drawing in the faint drift of organic matter and expelling the filtered water back into the column. They are purifying the place they live in. They have been purifying it for longer than the place has needed purifying. It is a habit that has outlived its purpose. They do not know this. They are content.

Their shells are colonized by smaller things — by algae, by tiny worms, by the spores of fungi that drift down from above — and so each mollusk becomes a small terraced world, a hillside with its own weather and its own slow seasons. A biologist from elsewhere, examining one, would find in it a complete vertical sample of the basin. The algae would tell her about the light. The worms would tell her about the bark. The fungi would tell her about the air above the surface, a hundred meters up, which has drifted down through the water as spores and lodged here in the shell of a creature that has never surfaced. She would be holding the basin in her hand. She would not know it.

---

The crustaceans ride.

There is a species — call it the rider, though the word misses the ceremony — that has arranged its entire life around the exudate plume. In the morning, when the stem's internal valves shift their rhythm and begin the slow exhalation at the crown, a current rises from below. It carries the spent water downward and the fresh exudate upward, and the rider catches the upward leg. It rises, all day, from the floor of the basin toward the surface. By dusk it is high in the column, near the fronds, in water that is bright with the green light of the upper afternoon. By nightfall the valves shift again. The rider, if it is to feed again tomorrow, must descend. So it lets go of the current and sinks, slowly, back toward the floor.

It does this every day. It has done this every day of its life. It has ascended and descended, ascended and descended, for the entire span of its existence, and the span of its existence is perhaps nine years. In nine years a rider will have traveled, by this method, a distance equivalent to circling the planet. It has never been to the surface. It has never seen the sky. It has been to the brightest part of the water and back, nine hundred and ninety-eight times, and it does not know that there is a sky. It knows light. It knows the current. It knows the taste of the exudate at different depths, which is a cuisine so refined that a rider can tell, by flavor alone, whether it is passing through the algal band or the mollusk band or the worm-tunnel band. It eats as it rises. It rests as it falls. It is, in its small way, a vertical nomad with a fixed itinerary.

---

There are things that live only in the dark of the bark.

The tunnels of the long worm are colonized. Other worms have moved in, smaller, duller, content to eat what the long worm has softened. There are blind arthropods that scuttle through the tunnels on pale legs, navigating by the chemistry of the walls. There are things that are not quite animals and not quite fungi, that exist as sheets of tissue across the inner surfaces of the bark, digesting by absorption, growing so slowly that a single cell division can take a season. None of them have names. The light never reaches them. The sound reaches them as vibration, transmitted through the bark, and they may use it the way we use weather — as a backdrop, a constant, something to be felt rather than understood.

---

There are the algae.

They are the green. They are the gold that swings on the bark where the surface light falls through. They are the only ones in the basin who care, in any biological sense, about the sun. They have arranged themselves in vertical bands by species, each band tuned to a particular wavelength of the diminishing green installments that arrive from above. The upper bands absorb the bright light. The lower bands absorb what is left. Together they form a curtain of photosynthesis that clothes the upper third of every stem in the basin. From a distance, a stem looks, in its upper reaches, like a tree in full leaf. Up close it is a stratified civilization of single-celled organisms, each generation thinner than the last, each one passing the light down to the one below.

The algae at the very top — the ones that catch the first light — live fast and die young, by the standards of the basin. They turn over in days. The algae at the very bottom of the band live for years. They are dim. They are patient. They are the last light-eaters before the dark.

---

And there are the things that do not belong to any stem.

A long predator drifts between the columns. It is a fish, actually a fish this time, though it has been called many things by the few people who have seen it. It is large — a meter, perhaps a meter and a half — and it is built for the open water between stems, for the tens of kilometers of quiet where the small things live. It is dark. It is silent. It hunts by waiting. It positions itself in a current and becomes, for as long as the hunt requires, a part of the water. The small fish pass it. The riders, sinking at dusk, pass it. The exudate falls past it. It waits. When something comes close enough it opens a mouth that is wider than its body seems to allow and it takes the thing in, and the thing is gone, and the predator resumes its waiting.

It is rare. There are perhaps a few hundred of them in the entire basin. They are spaced so that a small fish could live its whole life in the lee of a single stem and never encounter one. They are the only things here that kill quickly. Everything else is patient. Everything else is geological. The predator is the exception. It is what the basin does about abundance — it produces, very rarely, a thing that is hungry.

---

There are bacteria.

They are everywhere. They are in the water, in the bark, in the gut of every animal, in the calcium of every mollusk shell. They are the medium. They are the chemistry that makes the rest possible. There are species that oxidize iron and species that fix nitrogen and species that consume the exudate before it falls a meter. There are species that live only in the warmth of the rock near the floor and species that live only in the chill of the water near the surface. They do not have names. They will never have names. They are the most numerous things in the basin. They are also, by any honest accounting, the most important. Without them the stems would not lift. Without them the exudate would not fall. Without them nothing would rot, and nothing rotting is the death of a place.

They have been here, in some form, since before the stems. They are what made the stems possible. They are what will be here after. They are not aware of this. They are not aware of anything. They are chemistry that has learned to continue.

---

What lives here, then, is a vertical city of a thousand towers, each one a continent, separated by an ocean that is also a country. The towers are made of plant that has forgotten it is plant and become stone. The country is made of water that has forgotten it is weather and become geology. The citizens are small, abundant, patient, mostly blind, and arranged in layers of dependence so ancient and so complete that no single one of them could survive without all the others.

Nothing here has evolved recently.

Nothing here needs to.

The hum goes on. The snow falls. The fish glow and dim. The rider ascends. The predator waits. The worm eats forward into a future that is also, somehow, a past. The light comes down in green shafts and is spent, layer by layer, by the algae, and reaches the floor as a rumor.

Somewhere in the upper fronds, a valve opens. Somewhere in the lower bark, a mollusk adds a ring. Somewhere in the dark water between stems, a small fish that does not know what a stem is drifts past the place where a person is sitting with closed eyes, and the person hears it, barely, as a small sound in the great hum, and the person opens their eyes, and sees the faint glow, and closes their eyes again.

The hum goes on.
