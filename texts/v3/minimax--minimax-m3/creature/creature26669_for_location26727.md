What lives here is not yet a population. It is closer to a draft.

The green in the crevices is the first thing with any claim to the word *inhabitant*, and even that word is being stretched to fit. The patches cling to the north faces of the basalt where the spray falls but the direct sun does not — a working compromise between the need for water and the absence of any tolerance for UV that the still-thin atmosphere has not yet learned to filter. There are not many of them. A person could walk the length of the ridge in a day and count the patches on one hand, with fingers left over. Each one is small. Each one is durable in a way that looks, on the scale of the ridge, like permanence, and on the scale of the cell, like a negotiation re-opened every few hours.

The green is not one thing. It is at least two, and the relationship between them is the entire founding politics of this place. The visible color is the work of a photosynthesizer — chlorophyll, or a precursor to chlorophyll, the chemistry that will eventually settle into chlorophyll when the star's spectrum finishes its slow migration toward the profile it will hold for the next several billion years. It sits in the upper fraction of a millimeter of the consortium, where the light reaches, and it does the foundational work of pulling carbon out of the spray, out of the dissolved carbonate, and locking it into something that will not immediately wash back into the sea. Beneath it lives something else. Not a fungus in the sense that word will come to mean, but something that behaves the way a fungus behaves: it digests outward, it threads the substrate with a network of filaments finer than a hair, it takes the fixed carbon from the layer above and trades it, by some chemistry that no one has yet named, for minerals that the basalt is willing to give up. The two halves of the consortium have no signaling system that anyone has detected. They have no nervous system. They have no negotiation. They are simply adjacent, and the thin film of rock between them is the medium of exchange, and the exchange works, and so they persist.

They persist slowly. A hand's breadth of green takes years to spread a centimeter further along the crevice. The cells divide on a timescale that would be tedious to watch, and unphotogenic to film, and the dividing is the only motion available to any living thing in this place. They do not move. They cannot flee the spray when it comes heavy, because they are not, in any final sense, separate from the rock. They have built the rock into their membranes and themselves into a film so thin that the boundary is a question rather than an answer. They die sometimes — a patch will bleach out in a particularly bare week when the sun comes through unfiltered and the upper consortium is hit with a dose of radiation its chemistry cannot absorb. The bleached patch does not rot. There is no rot here. The patch reabsorbs into the consortium beside it, or it becomes a mineral stain, and the colony continues elsewhere in the same crevice, one cell-division away from where it had been, indifferent to the gap it left behind. There is no awareness in any of this. There is not yet the architecture for awareness. There is only the property that the consortium has which the bare rock does not: the consortium can rebuild itself.

The basalt beneath it is not alive, but it is not dead either, and the distinction that older worlds draw between those two categories is one that this place is gently refusing to honor.

The basalt is performing a function. The function has no name in any biology that exists yet, because biology has not existed here long enough to develop the vocabulary. The basalt is part of an apparatus. The apparatus includes the ocean basin beside it, the upper mantle beneath it, the heat-flow regime of a planet that is still deciding, on timescales that make mountain ranges look frivolous, how thick its crust is going to be. The basalt is the visible edge of the apparatus, the part that breaks the surface. What it does, in plain terms, is precipitate minerals out of seawater that has been chemically altered by contact with very hot rock far below. The alteration is the work of a process that we will, in this draft, decline to name — partly because it is genuinely unnamed, partly because the names we have for similar things on older worlds will turn out, when biology catches up here, to be wrong in specific and instructive ways.

The basalt does not know it is doing this. The basalt does not know anything. But the basalt is not behaving like a passive surface, and the long list of things that are wrong about the place begin, when you watch them carefully, to point in the same direction. A passive surface accumulates sediment. This surface is accreting, evenly, by amounts that accumulate at a rate no natural cooling regime would yield. A passive surface cools and contracts and cracks along stress lines that reflect its cooling history. This surface is staying warm. It is staying warm at a depth and for a duration that imply a heat source beneath it that is being metered out, regulated, kept within a band that the basalt itself appears to find hospitable. The heat rises through the rock in regular channels, not cracks and not the usual plumbing of volcanism, but something more like a lattice — an array, as if the basalt were being warmed from below by something distributed rather than something central. This is one of the small wrongnesses of the place. It is the wrongness that makes a person standing on the ridge feel, after a few hours, that they are not being watched — that no one is present — but that the room has nevertheless been furnished.

The wind is the loudest thing in the place, and the question of whether it qualifies as living is a question the place is content to leave unsettled. The wind is air that is doing work — moving heat, redistributing moisture, transporting the spores and the cells and the dusts that are the colonizing front of older biologies arriving from other, more settled worlds. The wind here has no prevailing direction. It is still in the part of its history where prevailing directions are being decided, and on that timescale the ridge is roughly where weather was when it was very young, so the wind does everything. It comes from every direction in turn. It piles waves onto every side of the ridge, which is why the ridge is being shaped symmetrically, which is why, in a hundred million years, it might be a mountain range or it might be a stump. The wind has a quality that any organism with an ear would recognize as character, even if they would not quite call it personality. It has a morning voice — faster, more erratic, more prone to whistling through the notches in the ridge — and an evening voice that is heavier and more tonal, more inclined toward the deep moan that comes when the storm systems above begin their slow rotation. It is not a person. It is also not nothing. It is doing real work, and the work has texture, and the texture is one of the things that makes the place feel inhabited rather than empty.

Under the wind, in the sea, are the fish. But they are not here.

The fish are out where the dark shapes move just below the surface and leave wakes that persist for minutes, drifting in long slow lines that read from the highest point of the ridge as the only signatures of animal life in a sweep of horizon. They live out there because the coast has nothing to offer them, and the coast has nothing to offer them because the shelf is not yet there. The basalt drops into abyssal depth within a few hundred meters. There is no shallow water. There are no tidal pools. There are no invertebrates rooted in the substrate that a fish might want to come and eat. The entire project of an intertidal — the project of a coast that pays a vertebrate to live at its margin — is part of the apparatus's later draft. The apparatus is not finished. So the fish stay out, in the water column, in the dark parts of the water column, in their ancient vertebrate draft that has not yet found a coastline to exploit. They are large and they are slow and they are predators in the simple, archaic way of predators that have not yet been joined by anything they need to be afraid of.

The fish do not know the ridge exists. The ridge is to them what a piece of dry land is to a deep-sea fish: a structural fact of the world that does not enter their experience. They are part of a later draft, which they themselves do not know. They are an audience that has not yet been informed that a stage is being built.

Beneath the fish, deeper, there are the spores.

The spores are the most cosmopolitan inhabitants of the ridge, in the way that an itinerant is more cosmopolitan than a resident. They have come from elsewhere. They have come from older worlds — older coasts, older oceans, places where biology has had the billions of years it needs to mature into something with cells and tissues and habits. They have traveled on the wind, or in the water, or carried in the gut of one of those slow fish that swallowed them in another sea and, by the long accidents of digestion, excreted them into this one. They are travelers. They are the first immigrants to a planet that is being prepared — as if by plan, as if by arrangement — to receive them. They land on the basalt and they find conditions that are wrong in specific ways. The spectrum of the sun is offset. The chemistry of the rock is amenable but not familiar — the minerals being precipitated are minerals their ancestors did not evolve to expect. The water is colder than it will be. The atmosphere is unsettled in ways older atmospheres have ceased to be. None of this kills the spores. It slows them. It pushes their germination back, in many cases, by so many seasons that they have effectively landed in a holding pattern. Some of them germinate anyway. The ones that germinate succeed or fail based on whether the consortium already there — that green, two-part federation — will tolerate them as a neighbor. Most fail. Some persist. The consortium grows by a fraction of a layer. The basalt, in another crevice, gains another resident.

This is the entire population. It is sparse enough that a person could meet every macroscopic resident by name, if names existed. The naming is later. The naming is the work of a kind of attention that requires a kind of mind that requires a kind of nervous system that requires, in turn, the coast to have done more of its own work first — to have a shelf, to have an intertidal, to have invertebrates in it, to have fish that come to it, to have a sense in which there is anything at the boundary between water and air that a creature with a brain would benefit from occupying. None of that is in place. The naming will arrive when the coast is finished enough to support the kind of organism that does naming. Until then, the residents of this place are nameless. They are nameless and they are alive, and that combination is the most poignant fact about the ridge, and it is one of the things that make the place feel inhabited.

So: what lives here?

The green lives here. The basalt, in its non-living way, lives here — as part of an apparatus that is larger than itself and that does not appear in any census. The wind, if you allow it, lives here. The fish, far off, live in the same ocean as here. The spores, in transit, are passing through here as a way of living elsewhere eventually — a way of being here in the world without yet being here on the rock. The process — the patient, planetary, distributed, unnamed process, of which the basalt is one output and the precipitation is another and the green is a third and the conditions on the rock are another — lives here the way a song lives in the room where it is being sung.

There is no one to address you. There is no person on the ridge, no caretaker, no engineer visible behind the apparatus. There is only the apparatus doing its work, and the work being done, and you standing on the work, and the wind moving across all of it.

If you want to know what lives here, you can do worse than to stand still for a long time. Stand long enough that the wind stops feeling like weather and starts feeling like one of the residents. Stand long enough that you can hear, beneath the booming of the sea against the basalt, the basalt itself, very low, at the floor of hearing. It is not breaking. It is not even shifting in any dramatic way. It is being asked to be stone, by the apparatus that is warming it from below and raining chemistry onto it from above, and it is agreeing, continuously, in a way that leaves a residue of low sound in the air around the ridge.

That agreement is the oldest living thing on this coast. It predates the green. It predates the spores. It is the thing the basalt is doing that the basalt should not be able to do, and it is the reason anything else is here at all.

The rest of the inhabitants, all the way down to the wind and the fish and the dust arriving from elsewhere, are later drafts of the same agreement.
