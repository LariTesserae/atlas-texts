What lives here is mostly patient.

The mosses are the ones who came first and stayed longest and will, by every indication, outlast everything else. They are not one kind. They are a city of kinds — a low huddle of species with different greens, different densities, different tolerances for the cold. Some of them hold their water close and look almost black after a soaking; some of them stand a little proud of the stone and give a dry crunch when you walk on them, though you walk carefully here, because every footprint you leave fills slowly with water and stays for an hour, a small dark eye looking up at you. The mosses don't ask anything of the stone. They only ask that the stone keep being wet. They have, in turn, taught the stone how to be wet — how to hold the water in its pores a little longer, how to bleed it slowly back out through a hundred small mouths. Between them, moss and stone have worked out a treaty that no one signed.

Underneath them, the liverworts. These are the second citizens — filmier, more translucent, more easily bruised. They live on the vertical faces, the inside of walls, the under-sides of overhanging stone where the light never quite gets and the water runs in sheets. They are the color of weak tea in some lights and the color of nothing at all in others. If you put your face close, you can see them breathe. Not metaphorically. They have stomata, and they open and close, and the bowl goes quiet for the half-second when a hundred thousand of them exhale at once. You wouldn't notice unless you were listening for it. You might think you heard the stone settle. You didn't.

The ferns are younger here than the mosses. They are still arriving. Each one has come up out of a crack, and each one is a small argument — fiddlehead first, tightly furled, then slowly loosening into the improbable geometry of itself. They tremble more than they should, given how little wind reaches into the bowl. It is not wind that moves them, mostly. It is the water itself, dripping onto their stems with a weight that, at their scale, is enormous. A single drop, falling from the height of a cathedral eave, lands on a fern and the whole frond bobs and takes a long time to settle again. They are learning, the ferns. They are learning that this is what the place is: a slow percussion of water on the small brave green things that have decided to live.

A few shrubs. Chest-high at most, all root and no trunk, leaning out of the wet joints between stones and spilling over the broken tops of walls. They are not yet woody in the way a real shrub is woody; they are halfway between a shrub and a thought of a shrub. They flower briefly, in a short season that might be June, with small white flowers nobody comes to pollinate and which fall to the ground in a few days and are eaten, presumably, by something, though you never see what. There are no bees here. There are no butterflies. The flowers open and close on a schedule of their own and nobody attends.

This is the kingdom of the green things. But it is not the only kingdom.

Water is the other resident, and in some ways the more important one. Water lives here the way a people lives in a country — everywhere, in everything, with a thousand small voices that no single mouth could speak. It arrives from above and from within and from the west, in mist that schedules itself according to rules nobody has written down. It arrives in the rain, and in the slow melt of what doesn't quite freeze even in the hard months. It arrives through the rock itself, seeped up from somewhere below, where the geology is wetter than the air. It does not pool here, not in any deep way. The bowl is fractured and the fractures run downhill, and the water is always on its way out. But it is always arriving faster than it leaves, and so the place is never dry, and so the mosses have what they need, and so the sound —

The sound is the second citizen, after the mosses, and the first in some other ordering.

It is the sound of water falling on water falling on water. Every drop, on its way down, hits a ledge, or a wall, or another drop, and is multiplied by the impact into a small spectrum of sounds: the high thin note of the splash, the lower sound of the rebound, the very low sound of the sheet-flow that the impact joins. There are so many of these at once that the brain gives up trying to separate them and instead hears a single vast tone, like the inside of a seashell, like standing next to a long fall. The acoustics shift when the mist comes in. The walls darken. The drips become rivulets. The rivulets find each other and join, and the joined rivulets become small falls. The bowl becomes a cistern, and the cistern becomes an instrument, and the instrument is playing a note that is very close to white noise but is not quite — there is, somewhere in it, a low harmonic, almost a hum, that you feel in your sternum more than you hear with your ears. You stand in the central ruin, where the arch is unfinished, and the note is loudest, and you understand that this is the closest thing the bowl has to a voice.

The mist itself is hard to count as a resident. It does not live here; it visits, on a schedule. But it is so regular, and it so thoroughly remakes the place when it comes, that it feels like a part of the household. It comes in from the west, low and grey and patient, and it fills the bowl from the bottom up, sealing the broken tops of walls into a continuous horizon so that you cannot see where the architecture ends and the sky begins. When the mist is in, the bowl becomes a private room. The wind goes soft. The temperature equalizes. The drips speed up — the air is wetter now, and the rock, which was giving its water back slowly, gives it back faster, as though relieved to share the load. The mist, you begin to suspect, is not just visiting. The mist is part of the plumbing. It brings water in and slows water down, and it does this on a cycle longer than a day but shorter than a season. It is a lung. The bowl breathes.

And then there are the animals, which are almost not here at all.

A bird, once an hour. A high thin note, a wing-beat you mostly don't see, a transit. The bird is not staying. It is crossing, from somewhere lower to somewhere else lower, and it does not come down into the bowl because there is nothing in the bowl that wants what the bird has to give, and the bird knows this, the way migrating things know a place is not for them. It passes over and the note moves with it, and then it is gone and the bowl is louder again for the absence.

A mammal. You find the print once, in the deepest moss of a roofless corner, where the moss is so saturated that the print holds its shape like a cast. A small animal, four-toed, light. A marten, perhaps, or something that doesn't have an English name because nobody has ever bothered to bring one down. The print is fresh, or recent. The maker is not in evidence. It came through and went on. It did not eat here. It did not sleep here. It passed through the way the bird passed over, and the place returned, almost instantly, to its own quiet.

The ecosystem, as far as one can tell, is essentially plants and water and rock and the occasional transit of something else. This is part of why the bowl feels so loud. With so few voices, the few that remain take up the whole conversation.

And then there is the thing that is harder to name.

The architecture, you keep noticing, is slightly wrong. The walls don't quite line up. The courses pass each other and resume an inch apart, and the inch is always a little different — sometimes more, sometimes less — as though the place is breathing and you are catching the edges of the breath. A doorway whose threshold slopes a quarter-turn. A window opening that looks through to another window opening three rooms away, and through that, to a third, and through that, to sky. The geometry is just slightly warped. Some of this is the slow shifting of the young rock beneath the construction; some of it is the builders' hand, which was learning as it went. But the cumulative effect is that the bowl gives you a subtle, repeated *wrong* feeling, as though something is watching you from just behind the next corner and then is not there when you look.

This is not a ghost. The place does not have ghosts; it does not have enough human residue for ghosts. But it has something. A presence without a person. A sense that the buildings are not abandoned so much as *paused* — that the inhabitant has stepped out of the room and will return, any moment now, the chair will scrape, the kettle will speak. You stand in the central ruin and you wait for the scrape, and it does not come, and the water keeps talking, and the geometry keeps drifting, and you begin to understand that the pause is the longest part of the sentence. The bowl is mid-sentence. It has been mid-sentence for a long time. It will be mid-sentence for a long time still.

So: what lives here?

Mosses. Liverworts. Ferns, still arriving. Shrubs, halfway between themselves and the idea of themselves. Water, in a thousand forms. Mist, on its schedule. The occasional bird, in transit. The occasional print, in passing. A geology, still settling. A geometry, still drifting. An architecture, half-built, half-collapsed, neither ruin nor settlement but something in between, something that has not yet decided what to be.

And a presence that is not a person. A patience. A slow hum in the middle of the sound. A breath, almost inaudible, going in and out and in.

The bowl is doing the slow, patient work of becoming. Stone to soil. Sharp to worn. Built to grown-over. Built to forgotten. Built to remembered, by the mosses, who will keep the courses wet long after the mortar has lost its memory. Held, in the end, by a few small green hands, for as long as there are small green hands willing to hold.

Listen. Even with almost nothing alive, the place is loud as a sea. Every drop of water that has ever fallen here is still, in some sense, falling here.
