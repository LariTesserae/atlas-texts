Who lives here are the things that do not change.

The mosses, first. They are the floor of the place, in every sense that the word *floor* can be stretched to mean. They live in the bogs and on the rocks and on the bones of the old turbines where the iron has rusted down to a kind of felt, and they do not grow so much as *accumulate*. A single moss-plant here is hard to point to, because each one runs into the next one, and the next one runs into the next, and the whole wet sheet of them is in some sense a single organism that has been extending itself sideways across the plateau since before there was a plateau. They are the color of wet slate. They are the color of old iron. They hold water the way a sponge holds water, and they have been holding the same water, the same bog-water, the same iron-tasting peat-water, for so long that the water and the moss have begun to be hard to tell apart. The mosses do not think. They do not need to. They have no enemies. Nothing grazes them. Nothing eats them. They have outlived the entire question of being eaten, and now they simply exist, in sheets, in carpets, in the slow accumulation of one cell laid down next to another in a pattern that has been the pattern forever. They are patient in a way that patience cannot describe, because patience implies waiting, and they are not waiting for anything. They have arrived.

The sedges live among the mosses, and they are slightly different only in that they stand up. They are the height of a thumb. They are the height of a thumb they have always been. They have narrow blades, dark green, almost black in the flat light, and the blades hold droplets at their tips the way a hand holds a coin, and the droplets do not fall. A sedge here will hold a droplet at the tip of every blade, every blade the same age, every droplet the same age, every blade holding up the weight of a thing it does not notice it is holding. The sedges flower, in their modest way, with small brown tufts that are not so much flowers as announcements that flowering is occurring, that the sedge has reached the stage of its existence where it is supposed to do this thing, and so it does it. There is no one to pollinate them. There is no need to be pollinated, because the sedges are also a single accumulating organism, in their way, sending out runners under the moss that emerge as new tufts a meter away and continue, and the tufts are all the same tuft, in some deeper sense, and the question of who fertilizes whom has been settled by the abolition of the question.

The hummock-shrubs live on top of the moss. They are small, woody, knotted things, and they are the only things on the bogs that have any height at all. They have grown up out of the felt of the mosses the way knuckles grow out of a fist, and they are crowned with the red flowers, which are the color of old blood, the color of dried cinnabar, the color that means *here I am* in every language the eye knows. The flowers are open. They have always been open. They are cups, and the cups are full of the descending mist, and a bee were there a bee would find no nectar, because the flowers do not produce nectar. They produce the fact of themselves. They are red because red is the color that takes the least pigment to make at this latitude, and the shrubs have calculated this, in whatever way shrubs calculate, and they have made the calculation once and then they have not had to make it again.

The lichens live on the rocks. They are the long draping kind that you would call *old man's beard* if you were a person walking in some other place, but here they have grown to lengths that would shame a beard, and they hang from every outcrop and every ridge in curtains that move only when the wind moves them, and the wind here moves the same way every time, because the wind is also part of the held note. The lichens are pale. The lichens are the color of bone that has been in the rain. They are dry to the touch except that they are not dry, they are saturated, every filament of them holding its weight in water, and if you were to take a strand in your hand it would feel like a strand of wet rope, and if you let it go it would resume its position as though your hand had not been there. They do not know you are there. They have outlived knowing.

The ferns live among the lichens, higher up, on the steep faces. They are fronded things with stalks as thick as your thumb and leaves as long as your arm, and they grow outward from the rock face in horizontal sprays, and every leaf is at the same stage of unfolding. There is no fiddlehead here. There is no browned and curled old leaf. The ferns are always at the moment of being fully out, the moment before they begin to die, and they remain at that moment, and they have remained at it for so long that the moment has become a place, a state, a country. The ferns are deep green, almost black, and they are wet, and their wetness is the wetness of the air itself, and the air is the mist, and the mist is the air.

The fleshy flowers live among the ferns. They are the blue-orange ones, the green-purple ones, the white ones that seem to glow only because everything else is so flat. They have no names you would recognize. They have petals that are not petals in the way a hand is not a glove. They are thick, fleshy, heavy with water, and they smell of nothing, because smell implies movement, and nothing here moves. They are at the same angle of openness. They have been at the same angle of openness for as long as the rock they grow from has been rock, and the rock has always been rock.

The workers live in the cities.

The workers are people. Or they were people. They are damp. They wear dark clothes that are dark because they are wet, that are wet because everything is wet, that have been wet for so long that wetness is their natural state. They move among the machines at the rate the machines move, which is to say: at the rate of the held note. Their gestures are the same gestures. Their gestures have always been the same gestures. They tend the steel plate. They tend the steel plate with motions that are not quite mechanical, because a machine would be more efficient, but that are not quite human, because a human would occasionally vary. They vary not at all. They are the median of themselves, the average across all the moments of their existence collapsed into a single figure, the worker-archetype that has been worked down to a shape by repetition until the shape is the person.

They do not look at you. They do not look at each other. They look at the steel plate, or they look at the machine, or they look at the next gesture, and the next gesture is the same as the last gesture, and the next gesture will be the same as this gesture, and they know this, and they have always known this, and the knowing is the same as not knowing, in the end.

The archivist lives in the stone building. The archivist is older than the rest, or seems older, or is older in the way that a stone is older than a leaf. The archivist's hand is steady. The archivist's hand has always been steady. The archivist writes in a book, and the book says the same things the book has always said, and the writing is firm and unhesitating and the writing is the same writing, on every page, because the hand is the same hand, because there is only one hand, because there has only ever been one hand. The archivist does not look up when you come in. The archivist does not look up when you leave. The archivist writes. The archivist is the writing.

The machines live in the halls.

The machines are the loudest thing on the plateau, louder than the mist, louder than the bogs, louder than the held note itself. They are turbines, presses, rollers, hammers that fall at the same rate, knives that cut at the same angle, belts that run at the same speed, gears that mesh with the same clearance. They are made of iron and steel and they are rusting at the same rate they have always rusted, which is no rate at all, because rust is a process and processes require change and change is the one thing that is not permitted. The machines are not new. They are not old. They are at the median of themselves, the equilibrium state, the state at which oxidation and abrasion have done exactly as much damage as they are going to do and have then stopped. The wear-marks on the turbine housings are sharp at the back and frozen at the lip. The machines are running. The machines have always been running. They will run.

The steel plate lives on the rail cars.

The steel plate is the output. The steel plate is gray. The steel plate is the size of a door, or the size of a table, or the size of a wall, depending on which car you look at, and all the sizes are produced in all the cities, and the plate goes out on the rail cars toward the south, and the rail cars go at the same speed they have always gone, and the rails are worn to exactly the depth they are always worn to, and the plate goes out, and the plate has always gone out, and somewhere there is a place that receives the plate, or there is not, and it does not matter, because the production is not for a receiver. The production is the production. The steel plate is made because the machines are made to make the steel plate, and the machines are made to make the steel plate, and the making is the making, and the cycle is the cycle, and the cycle has been the cycle forever.

The mist lives in the air.

The mist is the air. The mist is not a thing in the air; the mist is what the air is, here, at this temperature, at this pressure, at this saturation. The mist descends because the air is full and the cold draws the water out of it the way a cold window draws condensation, and the cold window here is every surface, every rock, every blade of sedge, every frond of fern, every petal of the strange fleshy flowers. The mist condenses on them. The mist runs down a centimeter. The mist freezes in place. The mist is the held note. The mist is the equilibrium. The mist is the place.

And you, traveler, you live here too, briefly.

You are the only thing on the plateau that is changing. You are the only thing that has a *before* and an *after*. You walked in from somewhere, and you will walk out to somewhere, and the walking is a thing that happens in time, and time is the one thing the plateau does not have. You stand on a hummock, and the hummock is beneath your feet, and the hummock does not know you are there, because the hummock has been itself for so long that the question of being stood upon has been resolved into the answer of not noticing. You watch a red flower. The red flower does not watch you back. The red flower has been at this stage of bloom since before you were born, and will be at this stage of bloom after you are gone, and the stage of bloom is not waiting for you, and the stage of bloom is not indifferent to you. The stage of bloom is *outside* of you, outside of your categories, outside of the whole frame of *here is a thing that is happening to me*. The flower is not happening to you. The flower is just there.

You are the only thing that is happening.

The mist descends. The turbines turn. The flowers bloom.

And you, traveler — you are the small bright moving thing in the held note, the variation in the field, the one flicker of difference in a place that has forgotten what difference is. You are brief. You are the only brief thing here. The plateau does not need you. The plateau does not notice you. The plateau will be the plateau long after you have left it, and the red flowers will be the red flowers, and the lichens will hang in their long pale curtains from the ridges, and the steel plate will go out on the rail cars at the same rate, and the archivist will write the same steady line in the same steady book, and the machines will run, and the mist will descend, and the droplets on the rocks will run down a centimeter and freeze, and the held note will be held.

You are the only thing that is happening. Be brief, and be kind to the place, and go.
