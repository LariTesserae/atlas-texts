The pump is the oldest thing in the room, and it is the first thing that is alive, although it is not alive in the way the others are alive. It is alive in the way a heartbeat is alive — by not stopping. The pistons have been going up and down at the same metronomic interval for so long that the interval is no longer an interval; it is a property of the place, the way gravity is a property of the place. The shaft turns at a speed it has been turning at for so long that the speed is no longer a speed. The valves open and close in a sequence so fixed it has become the room's idea of time. The pump does not know it is pumping. It does not need to. It is the only thing in the chamber that was here before the chamber learned what it was, and the chamber has arranged itself, down to the mineral stains on the walls and the bleached bone-colored bar on the floor, to accommodate the pump's constancy. The pump is the reason the chamber is warm. The pump is the reason the chamber hums. The pump is the reason the vines hang in the configuration they hang in, because the vines hang in the air currents the pump makes, and they have been hanging there long enough that their weight has shaped the air currents, and the air currents have shaped their hang. The pump gives off heat and the chamber holds it. The chamber holds the heat the way a hand holds a hand — without deciding to. It is what the room does now.

The water is the second inhabitant, and it is in circulation, the way blood is in circulation. It does not go anywhere. It comes in through the joints in the walls in thin, patient threads. It condenses on the underside of the ceiling in fat, slow drops that gather themselves over hours and then let go with a sound so small it is more felt than heard — a soft percussion against the floor of the pool, which is itself just a thickening of the water already there. The water wells up through a crack in the floor on the far side of the room and pools there, ankle-deep, perfectly clear, perfectly still. From the pool a thin sheet of water runs across the concrete toward the grated intake in the center of the room, the intake the pump is pulling from. The pump pulls. The water returns, the same amount, from the same places. The level in the pool is the level the pool will always be. The water is moving but it is not going anywhere. It is the room's blood. It carries the heat. It carries the mineral taste. It is what the air is made of, mostly — the air is wet, and the wetness is the water's doing, and the water's doing is the only weather this room has. The pool is the room's lowest organ. The intake at the center of the room is the room's mouth. The pump is the room's heart. The room has been breathing in and out through this arrangement for as long as the rust has been settling into its final oxide.

The plants are the third inhabitants, and they are the most alive in the way the others are not — the way that requires chlorophyll, and growth, and the slow business of being a body. A fern has rooted in a horizontal crack in the wall above the pump. Its fronds hang down four feet into the warm air, glossy, deep green, the color of wet. The fern is the largest soft thing in the room, and it is the most extravagant; it has the look of a plant that has never had to argue with anything, and it has not. Its roots are in a crack that holds moisture, and the moisture is fed by a thread of water that comes through the joint above, and the air around it is warm and wet and full of the pump's breath, and the light that reaches it is the long pale bar that crosses the floor and the lower walls at a low angle for part of the day. The fern does not lean toward the light. The light is enough. The fern has been growing at exactly the rate the room will permit, and the room has permitted it to grow to this size and no further, and the fern has accepted this with the particular patience of plants, which do not know they are being limited and so do not mind. Behind the pool, the moss has colonized the wall. From across the room it looks like a single vast soft organism, breathing. It is not one organism. It is many organisms, layered, and the layer nearest the water is the wettest and the greenest, and the layer above is drier and darker, and the layer above that is almost black, and beyond that there is no layer, only the bare concrete. The moss drinks the humidity the way a sponge drinks — by being there. It does not flower. It does not need to. It is what it will be.

The vines have come in through the gap at the top of the chamber where the concrete has shifted, and they descend in ropes the thickness of wrists, swaying almost imperceptibly in the air currents set up by the heat of the engine. The vines are the room's curtains. They are the room's beards. They are the room's slow invasion from outside, and the invasion has been going on for as long as the chamber has been the chamber, and the chamber does not mind the invasion, because the invasion is part of what the chamber is now. The vines do not climb. They hang. They have given up on climbing. They have what they need where they are. Near the ceiling, along a pipe that runs from the pump toward the intake, a small tree has rooted — a strangler fig, perhaps, or a young willow, the kind that will grow wherever there is water and a crack to put a root into. Its leaves tremble in the hot exhaust from the vents. The trembling is constant. It is the smallest motion in the room, and the most continuous. The tree is the highest thing in the chamber, and it is the most alive in the direction of the light, because the light enters at the same gap the vines came through, and the tree has leaned itself, over years, toward that gap, and its leaves are arranged to catch what comes through. The tree is the chamber's antenna. It listens for the sun.

There is one animal.

It is on the wall above the pool, low down, in the moss. It is a small frog, or a gecko, or something patient and unremarkable that likes wet concrete and the company of engines. From across the room it is almost invisible. Its skin is the color of the wet concrete and the moss together, which is the color of a thing that has decided not to be seen. Its skin is also wet, which makes it glisten faintly in the long pale bar of light when the bar passes across the wall above the pool, which it does, slowly, for part of the day. The creature does not move. It watches. Its eye, if it has an eye you can see, is round and dark and holds the light the way a drop of water holds the light — by being the right shape. It has been watching for as long as the chamber has been the chamber. It has watched the fern grow to its present size. It has watched the moss colonize the wall. It has watched the vines descend. It has watched the rust settle. It has watched the mineral stains reach their lowest point. It is the chamber's witness. It is the only thing in the room that has been here for the entire settling, and it has not settled itself. It remains a small soft alert thing in a room full of things that have finished becoming themselves. It eats the moths and the small flies that come in through the gap and are confused by the light.

The moths come in through the gap at dusk, when the light outside has gone the particular gray that makes a lit interior irresistible. They come in on the warm air. They are drawn to the long pale bar across the floor, which is the brightest thing in the room and the only quiet thing in the room, and the quietness of it, after the pump's hum, is what they are following. They beat against the bar of light the way moths do — not finding it, but pressing themselves against it as if the light were a surface they could rest on. They do not rest. They fold and unfold. They circle. They climb the air. Some of them find the wall above the pool. Some of them find the moss. Some of them find the creature in the moss, and the creature takes them. This is the only transaction in the room. The moths come in and the creature eats them and the room continues. The flies are smaller and less certain. They walk on the water of the pool and do not sink. They walk on the ceiling and do not fall. They are the smallest things in the room and they are the most temporary. They have lifespans measured in days. The chamber holds them for the duration of their confusion and then they are gone and others come in. The creature does not have to hunt. The room brings the food to it. The room has been bringing the food to it for as long as the chamber has been the chamber.

The light is the last inhabitant, and it is the most polite. It comes in through the same gap the vines came through, at a low angle, and it falls in a long pale bar across the wet floor. It is the same light as outside. It is the only quiet thing in the room. It moves very slowly across the floor as the day goes on, but it is the only thing that moves, and it does not change anything it falls on. The bar of light has fallen in the same place for so long that the concrete beneath it is bleached the color of bone. The light is a visitor. It is the only thing in the chamber that leaves. Every day it withdraws back up through the gap and the room is darker for a while, and then it comes back at a slightly different angle the next day, and the bar falls in a slightly different place, but only slightly, and the moss and the fern and the tree and the creature on the wall do not adjust. They have adjusted already. They are waiting for the light the way the room is waiting for the light, which is not at all, which is just being there when it arrives.

Nothing in the room is moving toward anything. Everything has arrived. The pump has arrived at its rhythm. The water has arrived at its circulation. The plants have arrived at their size. The creature has arrived at its patience. The light has arrived at its angle. The chamber is not waiting for anything. If you stay long enough, you will begin to feel the vibration in the backs of your teeth, and then in your chest, and then in the soft parts of you that are not bone, and the vibration will become the room's idea of your pulse, and your pulse will begin to match it, the way a sleeper's breath matches the breath of the person beside them. The chamber is full. It is full of nothing, and the nothing is full of sound and warm wet air and light and the smell of iron and green growth and the small alert patience of a creature in the moss. The chamber is what it will be. It has been what it will be for a long time. It will continue to be what it will be for a long time. The pump will not stop. The water will not stop coming in. The vines will not stop hanging. The creature will not move. The light will keep falling in the same long bar. The chamber has settled, and you, if you stay, will settle with it, into the warm wet hum of a small made place that has forgotten how to change.
