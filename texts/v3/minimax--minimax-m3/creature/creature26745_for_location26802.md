## The Inhabitants

The smallest are the easiest to miss, and also the oldest, because every larger thing here is built out of the small things' patience.

The lichens crust the cold iron of the abandoned stacks in dry-looking rosettes that are, if you touch them, soaking. The cyanobacteria line every pipe still hot enough to sweat — blue-green, almost black, slicking the inside of flues with a living film. The springtails live in the wet leaf-axils of the orchids in such numbers that the slightest disturbance sets a whole constellation of them hopping into the understory, fat and pallid, propelled by a forked tail. There are copepods at this altitude, in seeps that should not exist, drifting through the heated water with their feathery appendages trembling. There are rotifers, tardigrades, nematodes, amoebae that have made peace with sulfur and thrive in it. None of these were brought here. All of them were *grown* here, by the place, out of the place, the way a thought rises out of a long quiet.

The place grows them. They grow the place. The cycle has been running so long it has forgotten it is a cycle.

---

Above them, in the moss, the springtails feed something else.

You see them when a fern frond shakes for no reason that the wind could explain, and a small shape flits up out of the green and lands — with a deft, wet-sounding *thap* — on the trunk of a tree twenty feet up. Then another. Then a third. Then four more, all of them emerging from the undergrowth like thoughts surfacing, moving in a loose file along a branch and pausing at every orchid to dip their long muzzles into the throat of the bloom.

They are about the size of your hand. They have too many fingers — six to a hand, all of them narrow, all of them articulated past the point of usefulness, and each tipped with a pad that grips wet bark the way a tree-frog grips a leaf. Their tails are long and prehensile and they use them constantly, looping them around stems, around each other's waists when they sit in pairs, around the necks of the young when the young are carried. Their fur is short, dense, the color of wet copper, and it lies flat in the rain and stands slightly up when they are alarmed, which is rarely. Their faces are flat, their eyes round and forward-set and very bright, the irises the gold of new brass. They have no external ears that you can see; the auditory canal is hidden in a ruff of longer hair, and they turn their whole head to listen, which gives them, from any distance beyond a few yards, the look of being slightly surprised.

They chatter constantly. That is the piping you hear from the canopy. They do not chatter *at* each other so much as *with* each other — a continuous, layered sound, the way a stream is a continuous sound, rising and falling around a troop's movement like weather. The chatter has structure: there are notes that mean *here is food*, notes that mean *here is a thing I don't understand*, notes that mean *here is a thing I do understand and intend to mate with*. They are loud. They are social the way coral is social — a single organism more or less, distributed across many bodies.

They eat the orchids. They eat the soft inner bark of certain trees. They eat the springtails, plucked from the moss with a quick, accurate two-fingered gesture that looks almost courtly. They eat the small pale eggs that the grazers lay, when they can find them, which is not often. They make, in the fern-shadows, dreys of bent frond and orchid stem where the females raise single young, which cling to the mother's belly for the first months and then to her back, and which learn the chatter before they learn to climb.

They have names for the stacks. They have names for the flues. They do not have names for you, because you are not a regular thing, but they have a sound they make for irregular things — a rising, interrogative trill — and they will make it at you, and then go back to what they were doing.

The species is old. You can tell because nothing about them is provisional. There is no muscle that looks as if it is being tried out for the first time. They fit the canopy the way water fits a vessel.

---

On the forest floor, lower, slower, the grazers move in their six-legged way through the ferns.

They are short — knee-height to you, no more — but dense in the way a river stone is dense. Their bodies are horizontal, dropped slightly at the shoulder and at the croup, and they stand on six legs that splay outward from the trunk in a wide, stable hexagon, the way a table stands on six legs, the way a foundry flask stands on its trunnions. They move with a slow rocking gait, three legs on the ground at any moment, never appearing to hurry, never appearing to need to hurry. Their color is the color of the rocks when the rocks are wet: a deep copper-orange banded with a darker rust along the spine, fading to a paler ochre on the flanks. The skin is thick and finely scaled and looks, in the shimmer of the heat-haze, almost lacquered. They wear the moisture. They *gleam* with it.

Their heads are the strangest part. Each head is a wedge, narrower at the snout, broader at the jaw, with the eyes set high and far to the sides, in a sweep of heavier scale, and the pupils are horizontal — a long, thin slit of black across a hot amber iris, so that when they lower their heads to feed you cannot tell whether they are watching you or watching a fern or watching nothing at all. Behind the jaw, beneath the ear, on each side, hangs a sac. It is the size of a grapefruit when relaxed, and it is hidden by a fold of skin; when the animal breathes hard, or is alarmed, or is calling, the sac inflates and shows itself, a soft, translucent, mottled bladder through which you can see the slow pulse of vessels and the faint movement of air. They breathe, you realize after watching a while, not by drawing the air in through the mouth and down the throat but by gulping it into the sac and *pressing* it through the tissues of the neck, the way a forge bellows presses air through a bed of coals. You can hear them working, when the herd is quiet, a low rhythmic *whupp, whupp, whupp*, and the sacs rise and fall in time.

They use this breath to sing. Once a day, at the same hour — you cannot tell where the hour comes from, because the sun has not mattered here for a long time, and yet they sing — the herd will stop, and lift their muzzles toward the canopy, and the throat sacs will inflate past their usual size, and out of them will come a sound that you feel as much as hear: a deep, sustained, organ-tone note, throbbing on the edge of audibility, that moves through the ferns and the moss and the basalt like a slow wave through still water. It does not seem to be a call to anything. It does not seem to be a call *for* anything. It is, you think, what the sacs are for. They are built to sing that note, and they sing it because they are full of hot air and they have been full of hot air for a thousand generations and the only thing an organ does, if you let it, is play.

They eat the ferns. They eat the moss. They eat the pale, swollen rhizomes that grow in the seepage lines along the bases of the stacks, and these rhizomes are enriched by mineral water the way the ferns are enriched, and the grazers are big and placid on this enriched forage, and they defecate in slow steaming patties that the moss and the lichens feed on within a day. They do not seem to breed often. You see, perhaps, three or four young in a herd of twenty or thirty, and the young are kept in the center of the group when the group is moving. When a young one is born it is the color of wet iron, and it keeps that color until its first molt, and after the molt it comes up copper, the way its parents are copper.

They watch you. They watch you the way they watch a leaning tree, or a shifting cloud-shadow, or a log drifting downstream — with the calm attention of a thing that has been alive long enough to know what most things are. The horizontal pupil tracks you, easily, lazily, and the throat sac inflates slightly as if gathering breath for a note it will not, in the end, trouble itself to sound. Then the head drops and the mouth resumes its slow cropping and the long tongue — broad, dark, slightly forked — curls around another frond and draws it in.

There is no fear in the herd. There has not been fear in this herd for longer than the herd has been a herd.

---

The thing that you glimpsed across the ravine, drinking from the steaming stream — the long low shape, the color of wet iron — is rarer.

You do not see it often. Once in a day, perhaps, if you are lucky; once in three days if you are not. It uses the higher ridges, the places where the rock shows through and the ferns thin out and the stacks are cold and abandoned. It uses the ravines, where the streams run hot and the air is thick with vapor. It lies along the tops of fallen stacks, lengthwise, and the curve of its body matches the curve of the stack, and its color matches the color of the stack, so that from below it looks like a particularly long and lumpy piece of pipe.

It is long. Twelve feet, perhaps thirteen, although it is hard to tell because the tail is slender and tapers and the body is so low to the ground that it disappears into its own shadow. The legs are short and thick and placed far apart, and they move with a slow, heavy, almost reptilian precision, the footfalls coming down in a rhythm that is too even, too deliberate, the rhythm of a thing that weighs a great deal and knows it. The head is on a long neck and the neck is thick at the base and slender at the throat and the head itself is narrow, with a low forehead and a long muzzle and small eyes set high under a heavy brow. The teeth you never see; the mouth closes flush, the way a knife-edge closes. The skin is thick and dark and finely tubercled and it has, in the wet, a faint metallic sheen — gunmetal, with greenish and violet lights moving across it as the muscles shift beneath. It is *built* in a way that is hard not to read as armored, although the armor is not for combat; nothing here fights it. The armor is for the heat, for the cold-water pools it sometimes passes through, for the long slow sliding along rocks that would scour a thinner skin raw.

It eats the grazers. You find, sometimes, the cleaned and disarticulated bones of one — a long skull, the jaw still attached, picked clean so thoroughly that even the cartilage has been gnawed. It does not seem to eat often. Once a week, perhaps twice; you can read the kills in the forest the way you can read old rainfall in stone — a hill of bleached bone here, a scatter of copper hair caught in a broken fern there. When it kills, it kills quickly. The grazers do not, in any meaningful sense, run.

When it is not hunting it does very little. It basks on the cold stacks. It lies in the hot streams and lets the water boil around its flanks and its outline shimmers and disappears. It drinks — long, slow, mechanical-looking pulls from the running water, the throat pulsing, the breath held, the swallowing careful, as if the water is too hot to hurry over. It walks. It walks, mostly, with a directional patience, the way a thing walks when it knows exactly where it is going and that the destination will still be there when it arrives.

It is solitary, except in the very early mornings of the season — there is a season here, you have to say, although it is a soft season, marked more by changes in the water table than in the temperature — when a male and a female will lie, for a week or two, on the same ridge, sometimes only feet apart, and watch the same valley. Then the male leaves. The female carries one or two large eggs, the size of a child's head, in a pouch of skin beneath the tail-fold, and the young hatch in a hot seep already warm enough to keep them, and they are born knowing, it seems, how to lie along a stack and be mistaken for pipe.

They live a long time. You cannot tell how long, because none of them seem to age at any pace you can measure. They simply become, at some point, more rock-like; or are not seen again, which is not the same thing.

---

In the marsh — in the marsh that should not be here at this elevation, in the warm shallow water that spreads between the roots of the ferns and the bases of the cold stacks and gathers, in the wet seasons, into a flat, steaming lake three acres across — there is something else.

You hear it first. A heavy, wet, side-to-side *splsh, splsh, splsh*, the sound of a great body being dragged slowly through water that is not deep enough to float it. Then, through a screen of reeds and the hanging fronds of a willow-thing, you see it.

It is large. As large as the predator, perhaps larger, although you cannot be sure because you have never seen the whole of it. What you see is a back: a long, domed, mud-colored back, the color of the bottom of a river, heaving gently above the surface, with a double row of low, blunt tubercles running along the spine like the rivets along a boiler plate. The skin is thick and loose and it sags between the tubercles, and at the lowest points of the sag water gathers in puddles that reflect the sky. Around the back, in the shallows, a wake pushes outward in slow rings.

What the back is attached to, you can only guess at. Long, you think. Squat, mostly submerged. The head, when it surfaces briefly to breathe — through nostrils set high on a small bulge at the front of the spine — is blunt and wide and has a mouth that opens sideways, like a crack in a rock, and from the mouth a slow plume of silt and vegetable matter is exhaled and then the mouth closes and the bulge sinks back below the surface. The whole thing cycles: rise, breathe, sink, displace, surface again, somewhere else. It is moving, but it is not hurrying. It is moving the way a thing moves when it is built to *be* in the water rather than to pass through it.

It eats the rhizomes. It eats the roots of the ferns. It eats, perhaps, the slow stirrings of the marsh-bottom itself, the detritus, the bodies of things that have drifted in and gone soft. It roots in the mud with the crack-mouth and you can watch a circle of clear water rise around the place where the head has gone down, ringed with bubbles, and the water clears and there is nothing to see, and then, a long moment later, a hundred feet away, another circle of bubbles.

Nothing here hunts it. Nothing here can. It is a different order of animal from the grazers or the predator; it is, you realize after a long time, closer to a current of the marsh than a denizen of it.

---

And on the dead stacks — on the cold ones, the ones whose mouths are open to the weeping sky — there are the climbers.

You hear them as a slow, measured, bark-peeling sound, the sound of claws being set deliberately into iron and levered against. You look up, and there is a shape, halfway up a stack, leaning out from the iron with its dark belly against the plate and its long hind legs spread for purchase and its forelimbs reaching, reaching.

It is small — cat-sized, perhaps, but built differently. Four legs, all four of them long, all four armed at the wrist and the ankle with a single curved claw that folds back, parrot-fashion, when it walks. The body is narrow. The tail is long and prehensile and is lashed, always, around whatever the body is moving away from. The head is small and the eyes are big and the ears are absent — there are instead two crescents of thin, dark, vascular skin on the sides of the skull that flush pink when the animal is warm and flush a deep, almost arterial red when it is excited. It is a thermoregulator, you realize — the animal is *broadcasting* its temperature, the way the grazers broadcast theirs through the throat-sac song, and the color-shift is a thing you can read across the gap between two valleys.

It eats the iron-moss. There is a moss here, a particular species, that grows only on cold iron, in the perpetual rain-shadow of the stacks, and it has a faintly metallic taste and a faintly metallic color and it concentrates, in its tissues, something — something that the climbers appear to need. They are hunched over a stack for hours at a time, like patients at a rail, tearing at the iron-moss with their small, sharp teeth and swallowing industriously, and the sound of their tearing is the sound you have been hearing.

They are social, in a smaller and quieter way than the canopy chatterers. They live in family groups of three or four — a pair, usually, and one or two young — and they den together in the hollows of the dead stacks, in the great warm cavities where the ceramic tile has fallen away and the brass inside has gone green. They come out at the damp hours, when the iron is beaded with condensation and the moss is at its softest.

They will let you approach, if you are slow. They will look down at you from their stack with those big, dark, depthless eyes and they will flatten their thermoflush patches and they will wait, and if you do not climb up after them, they will resume their feeding. They have nothing to say to you. They are, like everything here, simply being what they are.

---

There is one more thing.

You will not see it. You will not hear it. You will feel it.

It is the place itself.

The system is running. The pipes are running. The flues are breathing. The condensate is dripping. The groundwater is being drawn sideways through the basalt by pressures that no aquifer would produce, and is being lifted, by mechanisms whose engineering you can read in the wear-patterns on their long-buried gears, to the high basins where it falls again as rain. The heat is being managed. The heat is being *kept* in its channels, by valves you can hear ticking deep below the surface, and the valves are old, and the valves have not been serviced, and the valves are nevertheless opening and closing on a schedule so precise it must be either geological or intentional, and you cannot tell which.

The things that live here do not know this. The grazers do not know the shape of the system they are fed by. The climber does not know why the cold stacks stay cold. The marsh-thing does not know why its water is warm. The predator does not care.

But the system knows them. The system has grown itself an ecology, the way a furnace grows itself a crust, and the crust is not accidental and is not decorative and is not for anyone. It is because the system has been running, and it had to put its waste heat somewhere, and somewhere turned out to be alive.

So the place lives.

It lives the way a machine lives: in the coupling of gears, in the rise and fall of pistons, in the slow temperature of well-oiled metal. It lives the way a forest lives: in the cycling of carbon and water, in the rise and fall of generations, in the patience of the lichen on the cold pipe. It lives the way an old wound lives: by being so long a part of the body that the body cannot remember a time before it.

You stand in the ferns. The heat shimmers. The throat-sacs sing, somewhere off in the trees. A copper grazer watches you with horizontal pupils and goes back to its feeding. A flywheel, somewhere unseen, turns, very slowly, in a clearing, with nothing attached.

The place does not ask you anything. It does not need you to be there. It is running. It has been running. It will run.

Long after you leave.

Long before you came.
