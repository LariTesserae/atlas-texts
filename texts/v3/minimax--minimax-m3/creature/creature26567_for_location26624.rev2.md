What lives there has no name for itself, and no name has ever been needed.

Imagine, first, the smallest. The size of a clenched fist, perhaps smaller, perhaps only the size of a knuckle, but so abundant that the mind, looking across the floor of the bowl, refuses to count them and instead sees a single dark thing that is made of many. They are the color of cooled slag, the color of dried blood at the bottom of a wound, the color of the inside of the bowl itself. Their backs, where backs exist, are ridged in long shallow corrugations that run from one end of the body to the other, and these corrugations are not decoration. They are heat exchangers. Each ridge is a fold of denser outer tissue wrapped around a core of something more porous, more veined, and the corrugations exist because the creature wants to present as much surface to the air as it can without exposing more of its interior than it has to. The air of the bowl is thin and cool except where the floor breathes, and a small creature must trade heat with the air constantly or lose what it has gathered to the sky. So it is ridged. So it sits with its ridges aimed at the warmer patches of stone, and the warmth enters through the underside, the soft flat underside that presses against the rock, and travels up through the body, and is shed from the ridges into the air, and the air takes it up and away, and the creature is cooled just enough to want more warmth, and so it presses closer, or shifts toward a hotter patch, or waits for the slow thermal tide to drift beneath it.

They do not move quickly. A small creature, in a generous minute, may cover the length of a hand. Its motion is not walking. There are no legs in any number you would call legs; there are, instead, a series of short stubby extrusions along the underside, fewer than you would expect, arranged asymmetrically, and these grip the stone and push and release and grip again, and the body slides forward by a fraction, and then another, and then it stops for a long time, and then it moves again. The motion has the cadence of breathing. In fact the motion may be breathing; the same aperture that takes in air may be the one that extends the limb, and the creature may move by a kind of slow peristalsis that is also respiration. It is hard to say. To watch one for an hour is to begin to feel that you are watching a single process expressed in many ways, and the ways are not separable.

The small ones eat. They do not eat each other, not usually, not when they can help it, because they are too close in size and a fight would be costly. They eat what falls off the others. The bowl, for all its closure, is not sterile. A larger creature sheds. A carapace crack, an outer layer of dead tissue that has done its work and must be replaced; the new tissue is already there underneath, harder, denser, and the old layer loosens and comes away in flakes and slivers and small dry curls. These fall to the stone. The small ones gather them. A small creature will press itself against a flake larger than itself and slowly, over hours, dissolve the boundary between the flake and its own underside, and the flake becomes part of the creature, and the creature becomes a little larger, and the larger it becomes the more its corrugations deepen, and the more heat it can gather, and the more it can shed when it is time to shed.

This is the economy of the floor. It is not a food chain in any sense you would recognize; it is a circulation. Matter leaves the larger creatures as waste and dead tissue; matter enters the smaller creatures as food; the smaller creatures grow; the smaller creatures become, some of them, medium creatures; the medium creatures shed; the smaller creatures of the next generation feed; and so on. The whole carpet is, in this sense, one metabolism spread very thin across a great many bodies, each body a temporary holding for matter that has been circulating since before any of them were born.

The medium ones are larger. The size of a clenched hand, the size of a small loaf, the size of something you might pick up with two hands if you were foolish. They have more structure. Their corrugations have become, in places, true ridges, and the ridges have begun to fold over and form pockets, and the pockets hold air, and the air in the pockets is warmer than the air outside the pockets, and so the medium creature is, in a real sense, a small furnace with legs. They move a little faster than the small ones, because they have more limbs to push with, and the limbs are better developed, and the body is heavier and so each push accomplishes more. But they are still slow. They are still governed by the patience of the floor. They have been seen, by no one because no one is watching, to cover a meter in an hour, which is the upper limit of their speed, and they cover a meter in an hour only when the heat beneath them has shifted and they must follow it or be left in the cold.

The medium ones eat the small ones. Not often. Not with anything you would call aggression. A medium creature settles, and the small ones around it flow away from it as water flows away from a warm hand put into a bowl, and the medium creature extends, from somewhere on its underside or its leading edge, a broad flat surface that is sticky with something, and the surface rests against the stone, and waits. Hours later, or a day later, a small creature drifts across the sticky surface on the slow business of its own life, and is held. The medium creature does not pounce. It does not rear up. It simply waits, the way a pitcher plant waits, and the small creature is held, and over the next days the small creature is dissolved, and its matter is taken up into the medium one, and the medium one grows, and sheds, and the small ones feed on the sheddings.

A medium creature may eat, in a season, perhaps a dozen small ones. It does not need more. The matter of a small creature is dense with what the small creature has gathered from the sheddings of its own cycle, and a dozen of them is enough to see a medium creature through a thermal winter, which is to say a few months of dimmer heat, of colder floor, of slower shedding from the larger ones above.

The larger ones are rare. There may be a hundred small ones for every medium one, and a hundred medium ones for every large one, and the large ones are so few that the bowl can hold them all without crowding, and they space themselves out across the floor the way old trees space themselves in an old forest, each one claiming a thermal plume and brooding over it for years. They are the size of a sleeping child. They are the size of a curled dog. They are dark on top and, when they shift over a hot crack, dimly red underneath, and the red is the red of the heated stone showing through their thinner ventral tissue. They are very slow. A large one, in a year, may move the length of its own body. It has almost no limbs. Its underside is a single broad surface, slightly concave, that presses against the stone and seals against the stone and draws heat through itself at a rate that would kill a smaller creature in minutes. The heat enters the body and is distributed through a network of internal channels, and the channels carry it to the ridges and folds of the back, and the back radiates it, slowly, gently, into the air. A large creature is a heat engine. It is a slow inefficient furnace that runs on the heat of the floor and exhausts a little warmth to the bowl and keeps the rest, and it has been running, some of them, for longer than the structures have been standing.

The large ones eat the medium ones. They eat them rarely, the way a python eats, because a meal of that size sustains them for a long time. A medium creature that wanders onto the broad sticky upper surface of a large one is held, and is taken in over days, and the large creature swells slightly, and rests, and radiates a little more heat than usual for a season, and the smaller creatures around it benefit from the extra warmth and feed well on the increased shedding and grow faster themselves, and the economy of the floor adjusts.

The large ones do not mate. None of the creatures mate, because none of them have the apparatus for it that you would recognize. They reproduce, when they reproduce, by division. A small one, grown large enough, simply slows its feeding and lets its body elongate, and a constriction appears across the middle, and the constriction deepens over weeks, and one day the two halves separate, and each half is a smaller creature that feeds and grows. A medium one does the same. A large one does it rarely, perhaps once in a human lifetime, perhaps less often, and when it does, the division is a geological event. The carpet around it parts. The smaller creatures flow back to give it room. The thermal plume beneath it shifts in response to the changed weight on the floor. The two halves, each the size of a large dog, separate by inches over days, and each seals its underside against the stone, and each begins to draw heat, and each becomes, in its turn, a center of the slow circulation.

This is what lives in the bowl. A carpet of small dark ridged things that feed on sheddings. A scattering of medium ones among them, larger, slower, patient, that wait. A handful of great ones beneath, vast and hot and almost still, that eat rarely and divide rarely and have been where they are for longer than the cairn in the center has been a cairn. The economy is closed. The matter circulates. The heat rises through the floor and is gathered by the undersides and is radiated from the backs and is lost to the thin cold air above, and more heat rises to take its place, and the bowl breathes, and the creatures breathe with it, and the cycle has been running for a very long time.

But there is more. There is the speech.

It is not sound, though it has the texture of sound. It is not chemistry, though it moves in the bodies the way chemistry moves. It is not heat, though it uses heat. It is something for which the bowl has no word because the bowl is not the kind of place that has words, and the creatures are not the kind of creatures that name things, but it is real, and it is continuous, and it is the medium in which the creatures live the way water is the medium in which fish live.

It begins in the largest ones. A great one, settled over its thermal plume, is not still in any meaningful sense. Its interior is in constant slow motion. Fluids move through the channels. Tissues shift against each other. The heat flowing through the body creates small currents, small eddies, small standing waves of temperature and pressure that propagate through the creature's substance and reach its surface. The surface is not inert. The surface is permeable, in places, to specific molecules, and the molecules leave the body and enter the air and the stone and the bodies of the smaller creatures pressed against it. The molecules are not random. They are patterned. They carry, in their patterning, information about the state of the great one: how hot it is, how full it is, how long since it has divided, what the thermal plume beneath it is doing. The molecules are received by the smaller creatures pressed against the great one's flanks. They are received through pores on the smaller creatures' undersides, and they enter the body, and they trigger responses, and the responses are not the responses of individuals. They are the responses of a single distributed system tuning itself to the conditions reported by its largest components.

A medium one, pressed between two small ones, releases molecules of its own. They say: I am here. I am warm enough. I am not full. The small ones around it receive the message and adjust their position relative to it, drifting slightly toward the warmer side of the medium one's body, or away from its hungrier side. A small one, finding itself in a colder patch, releases a different set of molecules. They say: I am cold. They say: I need warmth. The neighboring small ones receive the message and, if they are warmer than the messenger, drift toward it, and their warmth helps the cold one, and the molecules released by all of them shift to say: we are together, we are warm enough.

This is the speech. It is slow. It is chemical. It is conducted through the air and through the stone and through the bodies of the creatures themselves, because a creature pressed against another creature can pass molecules directly through the shared boundary, and a creature pressed against the stone can pass molecules into the stone and the stone can carry them a small distance before releasing them to another creature pressed against the same stone. The bowl, in this sense, is a single conducting medium. Every creature in it is in communication with every other creature, through chains of contact and through the slow diffusion of molecules through the air and the stone. The information that travels is the information of state: who is warm, who is cold, who is full, who is hungry, who is dividing, who is dying. The information does not travel fast. It travels at the speed of small creatures pushing themselves a hand's length in a minute, which is the speed at which information can be carried by the motion of the messengers themselves, and at the speed of molecular diffusion through still air, which is slower, and at the speed of conduction through stone, which is slower still. But the bowl is small. The bowl is half a kilometer across. The information can cross the bowl in a day, or a week, or a season, and the creatures do not need it to cross faster. They are patient. They are as patient as the heat that drives them.

There are no leaders in this system. There is no central creature whose molecules organize the others. The organization emerges from the local interactions of many bodies, the way a flock of birds emerges from the local interactions of many wings. Each creature responds to its neighbors. Its response changes its neighbors' situations. Their changed situations change their responses. The pattern propagates. The pattern is the bowl. The bowl is the pattern, expressed in stone and heat and dark living tissue.

And the pattern accommodates the circle.

The creatures do not speak of the circle. They have no molecules for it, because the molecules they release are about state, about warmth and hunger and position, and the circle is not a state. The circle is a fact. The creatures know the circle the way a river knows a boulder in its bed. The boulder is not information to the river. The boulder is a constraint. The water divides around it and rejoins behind it and the river is shaped by it but does not represent it. The circle is a constraint to the bowl. The heat that rises through the floor is greater at the circle. The heat rises in a column. The column of heat is invisible, except as a shimmer, and the shimmer is a distortion, and the creatures on the floor near the column are pushed back from it by the simple physics of their own overheating. Their bodies cannot tolerate the heat directly above the circle. The chemistry of their tissues breaks down. They would die. So they do not go there. Their molecules, released near the circle, drift toward the circle and are destroyed by the heat before they can return, and so the molecules carry no information from the circle back into the carpet, and the carpet knows the circle only as an edge, a boundary, a place beyond which the rules change.

But the circle is not only a constraint. The circle is also a source. The heat that rises through it is the deepest heat in the bowl, and the great creatures have arranged themselves in a ring around it, each one settled over a thermal plume that branches off from the central column, each one drawing heat from the plume and radiating it outward, and the smaller creatures have arranged themselves around the great ones in concentric rings, and the medium ones among the small ones, and the whole bowl is organized, in its slow way, around the central fire. Remove the circle and the great ones would drift, and the medium ones would drift, and the small ones would drift, and the bowl would lose its shape, and the pattern would dissipate, and the creatures would become a thin uniform scatter across the floor, and they would starve, because the economy of the bowl depends on the concentration of heat and the concentration of bodies that the circle enforces.

The circle is the heart of the bowl. The bowl is the body. The creatures are the blood.

And the structures, the cairn and the line and the platform and the wall, are old wounds. Or old intentions. Or both.

The creatures flow over them and around them and into them. The cairn in the center has been worn down, over centuries, by the slow passage of bodies across its top, each one pressing its underside against the fitted stones and shedding a thin layer of tissue that dries and weathers and is shed again, and the stones themselves are slowly being absorbed into the carpet, becoming, grain by grain, part of the floor. The line of slabs at the eastern edge leans at the angle of their fall, and the creatures flow up the lee side of the leaning slabs and over the tops and down the windward side, and the slabs are slowly being pushed further over by the weight of the carpet, and one day, soon in geological terms, they will fall, and the creatures will flow over the place where they fell, and the place will become ordinary floor. The platform in the southwest is being buried. Its fitted basalt is being covered, year by year, by the rising tide of the carpet, and one day it too will be floor. The fragment of wall has its corner, and the corner is a small shelter that a creature might press into, and creatures do press into it, and the corner is a small warm place in a cold place, and the creatures know it.

The structures are not remembered. The creatures have no memory that would let them remember what placed the stones. The molecules that pass between the creatures do not carry the past. They carry only the present: warmth, hunger, position. The past is in the stone. The past is in the slow accumulation of sheddings that becomes, over centuries, a thin layer of organic matter over the basalt, a kind of soil, dark and dry and warm, in which nothing grows because nothing falls from the sky, but which is, itself, a kind of memory. The soil remembers the creatures. The soil is the creatures, in another form. The cairn is being slowly converted into soil, and the soil is the cairn, and the cairn will become bowl-floor, and the bowl-floor will be cairn, and the cycle will close.

Or it will not. The cairn may outlast the bowl. The structures may be older than the creatures, as the prompt allows, and may be the only record of whatever came before, and the record may be unreadable because the creatures cannot read, and the bowl may persist after the cairn is gone, because the bowl is held together by the circle and the heat and not by the stones that were placed there once. The bowl does not need the structures. The structures do not need the bowl. They are simply in the same place, and have been for a long time, and will be for longer, until one of them ends.

There is one last thing, and it is the thing the prompt leaves most open, and it is the thing the creatures themselves do not know.

The structures were placed. The cairn is fitted. The line is a line. The platform is a platform. The wall has a corner. These are the gestures of intention, and intention implies an intender, and the intender is not in the bowl now, and the creatures are not the intenders, and the circle is not the intender, and the intender has left no other mark. The creatures do not mourn the intender because the creatures do not mourn. The creatures do not revere the intender because the creatures do not revere. The creatures flow over the structures and around them and the structures become floor.

But the molecules that pass between the creatures, the slow chemical speech of state and warmth and hunger, do not carry the past. The molecules are not the only thing that passes. There is heat. Heat passes through the floor, up through the stone, into the bodies, out into the air. The heat comes from below. The heat comes from a depth that the creatures cannot reach and do not imagine. The heat comes from the same depth that built the bowl, that raised the ridge, that fractured the basalt and let the magma push through and cool in the shape of a depression. The bowl is a geological accident. The heat is not an accident.

And the heat carries, in its passing, a patterning that is older than the creatures and older than the structures and older than the bowl. The patterning is in the way the thermal plumes rise. The patterning is in the way the heat is not uniform across the floor but distributed in a slow almost-regular array of hotter and cooler patches. The patterning is in the way the plumes shift over years, and the way the creatures track the plumes, and the way the bowl, considered as a whole, is a system in which heat enters at one set of points and is gathered by bodies and is radiated to the air at many points and is lost, and the gathering and the radiating are not random but structured, and the structure is the same structure that placed the cairn in the center and the line at the east and the platform in the southwest. The cairn is over a thermal plume. The line is along a thermal seam. The platform is at the junction of two plumes. The wall is at the edge of a plume. The intender, if there was an intender, placed the structures where the heat wanted them to be placed, and the heat wanted them there because the heat has its own patterning, and the patterning is old.

The creatures are not the intender. The creatures are not the heirs of the intender. The creatures are, in a sense that the prompt allows and the bowl itself cannot confirm, what the heat has grown in the place where the intender once stood. The intender placed stones, and the heat grew the creatures, and the creatures are the bowl's slow answer to the stones, and the answer is not a sentence but a metabolism, and the metabolism is not a meaning but a shape, and the shape is the same shape that the intender made when it placed the stones.

Or not. The prompt does not say. The bowl does not say. The creatures, pressed together in the dusk, breathing their slow chemical breath into each other's bodies, do not say. They are not the kind of thing that says. They are the kind of thing that is, and what they are is the bowl, and the bowl is the place, and the place is this.
