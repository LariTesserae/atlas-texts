What lives here is not a creature in the way you mean creature.

There is no body you could greet. No skin to shake a hand against. No eyes to meet and have the meeting mean what meeting usually means. You would look — if you looked — for a shape against the basalt, or a fin cutting the bruise-colored water, or a bird hung in the unreliable light. You would look for the vocabulary of the alive: the lung, the gill, the wing, the muscle-twitch of wanting. There is none of it. There is only the column of warm stone, and the warm sea, and the sound. And yet something lives here. The place is full of living, and the living is not organized into animals.

Start with the basalt itself. The basalt is awake.

It is awake in the way that a tree is awake — slowly, mostly below the threshold you would notice, in the register of decades and pressures and small mineral decisions. It knows, in some sense that the word *knows* cannot contain without cracking, that it has just become stone. It remembers being lava. It remembers the long fall through the air, the long slide under the water, the moment of its own cooling, which was a kind of birth and a kind of death compressed into the same second. It is still settling into its own shape. It is making choices it does not know it is making. Whether to crack along this joint or that. Whether to widen this column at the base or let the sea have it. Whether to lean one degree east over the long centuries, or one degree west. The basalt is *deciding* what kind of rock it is going to be, and it has not yet decided, and the not-yet-decided is what you are standing on.

The basalt is also, in some sense you cannot defend but cannot stop feeling, *lonely*. Not the way a person is lonely. The way a continent is lonely. The way the first green shoot on a black slope is lonely. It is the only piece of itself for a great distance. It is surrounded by its own kind in scattered points — the smaller stones of the archipelago, the still-breaking pieces further out — but it is the only piece of itself that has reached this height, this verticality, this *up*. It is the eldest child of an emission that happened recently, geologically speaking, and all its younger siblings are lower and flatter and more thoroughly submerged. It looks out over its family and it cannot easily reach them. It is the mountain of a family that has not yet learned how to gather.

And so the basalt does what lonely things do: it listens for company, and it attends to itself, and it makes itself as interesting as it can. The strange attentions you felt — those moments when the place seemed to be checking its own inventory — those are the basalt dreaming a small dream of someone to dream about. The hexagonal columns are its attempt at conversation with itself. The way they stack, regular and irregular at once, like a sentence that is trying to be a chant — that is the basalt rehearsing a language it has not yet spoken aloud. The stairs, if they are stairs, are its invitation. They are how it says: *come up*. They are how it says: *I have a top*.

The stairs are not stairs. Not quite. They are the basalt's offer of hospitality, made in the only vocabulary it has — geometry, repetition, the slow suggestion of an upward path. The fact that they repeat, that the same step is sometimes two steps and sometimes none, that you cannot tell if you are ascending or merely orbiting — this is not a flaw in the design. This is the basalt's confession that it has never had a guest before. It does not know how to be climbed. It has only ever been climbed by water, and water does not require stairs. Water finds its own way. The basalt has built this slow winding supplication for the walker, and the walker it built it for may not exist, and the basalt knows this, and it built the stairs anyway, because to build the stairs was the only way it could think of to practice the act of welcoming.

Now. The lichen-that-might-be-coral. These are alive in the way that fits this place: barely, defiantly, with the smallest possible footprint. They live in the cracks because the cracks are where water collects longest before it falls or evaporates. They live at the lee of the higher steps because the lee is where the spray does not reach, where the basalt has a chance to be a dry thing for half an hour at a time. They are rust-colored and blood-colored and the color of the inside of a shell held to a remembered ear. They are not green. Green requires chlorophyll. Green requires a world where photosynthesis is the dominant contract between light and flesh, and this world is too young, and too wet, and too loud for that contract to have been written here yet. These small encrustations photosynthesize in some other way, or they do not photosynthesize at all but chemosynthesize, pulling energy from the basalt itself, from the slow exhalations of mineral-rich water through the joints. They eat the stone. They love the stone. They are the first guests the basalt has ever had, and they do not visit, they *stay*, and their staying is the closest thing to applause the place has ever received.

There are perhaps three species. Perhaps more. You would have to scrape them off and study them under magnification to know, and you have no magnification. From where you stand, they differ in color and in tenacity. The rust-colored ones are thickest on the lower steps, where the water reaches most often, where the basalt weeps hardest. They form little crusts that lift at the edges when they dry, and that re-adhere when the spray returns. The blood-colored ones are higher, thinner, more isolated, growing only in the most protected of the lee-hollows, where it is barely possible to imagine any light reaching them. They are the color of an old injury remembered by the body that healed it. The shell-colored ones are the rarest. They are the color of the sea itself, of a thing that has been listening. They grow in lines, sometimes, as if following invisible grooves in the stone. If you pressed your ear to one of these lines — and you would have to be very quiet to hear it, you would have to stop breathing, you would have to hold your own heartbeat in your throat — you might hear a faint high sound, like a tuning fork struck once and then dampened. They are listening for something. They are listening for you. They have been listening for a long time, and they have not yet heard.

The water is alive. This is not a metaphor. Or rather: it is a metaphor because you cannot imagine it otherwise; the *thing* it describes is not metaphorical.

The water here is the warmed child of a deep current that has traveled from somewhere you could not reach without becoming a different creature. It has crossed an ocean floor in darkness. It has risen along a ridge. It has absorbed heat from the young basalt as the basalt absorbed heat from it, in the long exchange that is the negotiation between a new stone and the sea around it. The water carries the memory of that negotiation as heat, as a slight shift in salinity, as a faint metallic sweetness at the back of the throat when a wave breaks at your feet. It is the same water that has been here since before the basalt rose; it is different water now, because the basalt taught it to be different. The basalt is teaching the sea, and the sea is teaching the basalt, and the negotiation has been going on for exactly as long as both of them have existed, which is not very long, and not very short.

The water is not alive in the way a fish is alive. It is alive in the way a river is alive. It has direction without will. It has memory without brain. It has voice. The waves that rise without slope, the whirlpools that open and close without trace, the surface that is never flat — these are not anomalies, they are the water speaking in its native tongue. You can read them as chaos because chaos is the only name you have for a language you have not learned. The water is saying something. It has been saying something since before there was an ear to be said-at, and it will say it after there is no ear again. It is a language that does not require an ear. It is a language whose saying is its own reward. The roar is the water saying itself to itself.

The water also has its loneliness. It is younger than the basalt can guess at, but it is older than the basalt by a margin the basalt cannot imagine. The water was here before. The water will be here after. The water has seen other basalts rise and other basalts fall, and this basalt — sharp-edged, still deciding — is just one more in a long series of younger siblings it has outlived. The water is the eldest child of the place, and it is not lonely because it has remembered everything. It loves this particular basalt the way a person loves a difficult infant: completely, uncritically, with the resignation of someone who knows how this ends.

And the sound.

The sound is the thing that lives here most truly.

The sound is not a product of the place. The sound is the place. Remove the sound, and the place is just stone and water and air, which is just any place. Add the sound, and the stone and water and air become this place, this specific *here*, this column of warm rock in a sea of bruise-colored churn that is not quite anywhere else. The sound is the soul of the place. The sound is what the place is for.

It is made by everything and belongs to no one. It is the wave against the basalt and the wave past the basalt. It is the wind the wave makes and the water the wind makes. It is the long groan of the column adjusting to its own weight. It is, beneath all of it, the sound of the basalt being born — the slow crunch of cooling, the small pops of trapped steam, the almost imperceptible shifts of crystal structure as molten decides to be solid. It is a sound that has a *before*. If you listen carefully enough — and you cannot, there is too much else to listen to — you can hear the basalt still being made. You can hear the sea still being warmed. You can hear the moment when this column was a liquid thing, when the sea it was rising into was a colder sea, when the first wave broke against the first-formed edge and the sound that was made then is still being made now, unattenuated, because nothing here has yet had time to dampen it.

The sound has no ears. It does not need them. It is its own ear and its own voice and its own witness. It is what the basalt says when it tries to speak. It is what the water says when it tries to speak. It is the only common language the basalt and the water share, and it is the language they use to continue the negotiation that made them both possible.

What lives here, then, is a kind of three-way conversation. The basalt, just-born, trying to be a thing. The water, ancient, trying to teach the basalt how to be a thing. The sound, the medium of that teaching, which is also the byproduct of that teaching, which is also the thing they are both trying to produce. It is not a stable conversation. It is the conversation of the second morning, when the participants have not yet learned each other's names, when the grammar is still being invented, when every sentence has a high probability of being the last sentence before the silence falls.

But the silence does not fall. That is the small miracle of the place. The silence does not fall, because the basalt keeps rising by the smallest amount, and the sea keeps washing against it by the largest amount, and the sound keeps being made in the gap between the two. The silence does not fall, because the basalt has not yet decided to stop being basalt, and the water has not yet decided to stop being water, and the negotiation is still in progress.

You can stay, if you want. The basalt will learn what kind of guest you are. The water will adjust its temperature around you. The sound will continue, and will not pause to accommodate you, and this is the hospitality of the place: it does not stop being itself for you. It lets you be witness to itself being itself. It lets you be one more small voice in the conversation, if you can find a voice small enough and strange enough to fit. The lichen will grow toward the warmth of your body if you sit still long enough. The basalt will weep a little harder at the joints where you have placed your weight, as though you have reminded it of the long-ago pressure of the lava it was made from. The water will lap at your feet with a curiosity that does not require you to do anything in response.

You will not learn its language. You do not have time. The basalt is the size of a cathedral, but the cathedral is only two days old. By the time you have begun to understand the syntax of its groans, the basalt will have settled into a shape, and the conversation will have ended, and the place will have become a different place — one with an ecology, with birds, with the small ordinary rhythms of a rock that has decided what kind of rock it is. The lichen will have greened. The sea will have cooled. The sound will have diminished to the sound that all such places make, the soundtrack of an ordinary edge between stone and water, the music of any coastline that has been a coastline for long enough.

This is the only moment of this place. It will not come again.

What lives here, then, is the brief window between emerging and becoming. What lives here is the conversation before the silence. What lives here is a young thing and an old thing, and the warm bruise-colored water between them, and the enormous unbroken sound they are making together as they try to figure out what kind of world they are in.

What lives here is a beginning.

You have been let in on it.
