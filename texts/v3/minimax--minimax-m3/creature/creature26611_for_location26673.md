The grazers are what one notices first, because they are everywhere and because they are almost invisible until they move.

A herd resting on a warm patch of stone in the gray pre-dawn, or in the long shadows cast by the walls in late afternoon, looks from a distance like a scatter of dark rocks that has, by some accident, taken on a shape — a low shape, a shape that breathes and shifts and occasionally lifts a head. Their coats are the deep red-black of the basin floor, the color of basalt worked on by weather for a long time. The coats are double-layered. The outer guard hairs are long enough to break the wind. The inner coat is so thick that a hand pressed into it cannot easily find the skin beneath. The animals are built for altitude — short legs to keep the center of mass low, a deep chest, a broad muzzle that can take in air and warm it and pull moisture from it before the air reaches the lungs.

They graze. The word is wrong for what they do, but there is no better one. They put their broad muzzles down to the rock and work at it. Their lips are tough and their teeth are arranged for grinding — flat molars that meet in a way that can take mineral as well as plant. What they pull from the surface is a combination of things. The lichen, where the lichen has crusted thick. The mineral films that the condensation lays down each night and the sun lifts each day. The scrub plants, when they can be found — they will travel to a stand of the silver-leafed woody thing and work at it until it is gone, and then not return to that spot for a season or more, because it will not have grown back. The pale flowering plant, when it blooms, draws them — they gather at the bloom patches and the bloom patches disappear faster than they would otherwise. The animals know, in the way animals know things, where the blooms are and when they are about to come.

They have to know a great deal. The basin floor is mostly rock and the rock is warm and the rock does not, in any ordinary sense, feed. To live here the grazers have learned — across generations that no one has counted — where the warm patches are and how the warm patches shift with the seasons and the wind and the slow adjustments of the geology. They drift across the basin in loose herds, moving from warm patch to warm patch, and a herd that knows the basin will work it in a pattern that resembles, from above, a slow rotation — never staying anywhere long, never going anywhere fast, always in the area where the heat is best and the surface growth is least exhausted.

The herds are forty or fifty animals, sometimes more. They keep their young in the middle. The young are vulnerable to the foxes, which are the main predator on the young, and to the condors, which will take a newborn if it can be isolated, and to the cold snaps that still come even in the slackening season. The herd protects them as herds protect young — by surrounding, by movement, by the sheer weight of numbers, and by the watchfulness of certain individuals who seem, as near as anyone can tell, to be doing something like sentry duty. The sentries do not feed while they watch. They stand with their heads up and they look at the rim and the fumaroles and the edges where the foxes come from, and they stay until they are relieved.

The grazers are not the only herbivores, though they are the most numerous. There is something smaller, goat-like, that works the slopes below the rim where the soil is slightly deeper, and there are the burrowers, which are not herbivores in the usual sense but which compete for the same surface material, working from below. The food web is tight and there is not much slack in it.

---

The foxes are blunt-faced and patient and they hunt in the hours when the light is failing.

They are the size of a small dog, perhaps a little larger. They live around the fumaroles and along the travertine terraces, where the steam gives them cover and the burrowers are densest and the grazer herds come to drink at the warm pools. They hunt at dawn and at dusk — the times when the steam is rising and the grazers' young, if they have wandered from the herd, are easiest to take. They are quick. They take a young grazer by running in close while the herd is shifting and cutting out the one that has fallen behind, and they carry it to the cover of a fumarole before the herd can regroup. They eat it there. They cache what they cannot finish.

They are not afraid of people, which is unusual for foxes. They have been around the human operation for long enough now that they have learned what people do and what people ignore and what people leave behind. They will watch a person from a distance of thirty meters and not move. They will follow a working crew along a road at a parallel distance, working the edges of wherever the crew has passed, looking for what the crew has disturbed. They will, on the rare occasions when the biologists leave feed out for them, come close enough to take it from a hand.

Their eyes are the wrong color sometimes. Most of the foxes have the ordinary amber or yellow-green of a canid's tapetum. Some of them, in certain individuals, the tapetum reflects a pale blue or a violet, and the biologists have noted this and have not been able to explain it to themselves. The foxes with the wrong-colored eyes are not otherwise different. They hunt the same burrowers. They raise the same kits in the same dens dug into the soft travertine. They are simply, occasionally, a little off. The offness is inherited, as near as anyone can tell — the kits of a wrong-eyed vixen are more likely to be wrong-eyed themselves — but the inheritance does not follow the patterns of any known gene.

---

The condors are larger than they should be.

A person standing on the rim and looking up at a bird riding the thermals above the basin will sometimes estimate the wingspan at eleven, twelve feet, and will be told later by someone with a measuring tape that the actual wingspan is closer to ten, and will not quite believe them. The condors ride the thermals that rise off the warm walls in the afternoon — the heated air lifting off the stone in columns that are visible as shimmer in summer, and that the birds find and use. They are patient. They wait. They take what is dead and what is dying and, occasionally, what can be isolated and taken alive. A newborn grazer that has been separated from its herd by the press of a working truck, a fox's prey that has been abandoned in favor of a closer meal, a burrower that has been flushed by a steam vent shifting — these are the condors' opportunities. They are not numerous but they are persistent. Their shadows cross the basin floor in slow arcs, and the grazers watch them.

---

The ravens are the most intelligent of the birds, and they have made the human operation their own in the way that ravens make any human operation their own.

They are large, dark, and they operate with the focused patience of urban pigeons. They work the railhead for scraps, picking at the seals of the containers when no one is watching, though they do not open them — they have learned, or have always known, that the containers are not worth the effort. They work the crushing station for the small mammals that the crushers occasionally release alive or half-alive. They work the mess halls, where the humans eat, and they wait outside the doors and pick up what is dropped and what is thrown. They watch. They remember. A biologist studying the ravens has noted that they recognize individual humans by face and that they will follow a particular human across the basin in expectation of being fed, and will not follow another, and that this recognition persists across the seasons and across the years. The ravens are the most numerous of the large birds and they are the ones that a visitor to the basin will notice first, after the grazers, because they are unafraid and because they are everywhere the humans are.

---

The burrowers are small and they are everywhere that the soil is deep enough.

The size of a rat, perhaps a little larger. They live in the softer ground near the river and along the lower edges of the travertine terraces, where the soil has accumulated enough to support a burrow and where the condensation is most reliable. They eat the same surface material the grazers eat — lichen, mineral crust, the small invertebrates that the river edge supports. They are preyed upon by the foxes and by the hawks and, when they venture out, by the ravens. They are numerous and they reproduce quickly and they are the staple of the smaller predators in the way that voles are the staple of weasels in other places. They have colonized, in recent decades, the soft ground under the human facilities, where the warmth is reliable and the spillage from the mess halls has built up something like soil. The burrowers under the mess hall are fatter than the burrowers elsewhere and they breed faster and the foxes know this and the foxes work the edges of the mess hall at dawn in numbers that the open basin does not support.

---

The marmot-like creatures live in the rocks and they whistle.

A single high sharp note, repeated, that carries across the basin floor and is heard often and from no apparent source. They are fat and pale and they sit on the warm stones and they watch. They whistle when something moves that they do not like. They whistle when a fox comes near. They whistle, sometimes, for no reason that anyone can determine. They are difficult to study because they will not let a person approach, and the biologists have mostly watched them from a distance and have written down their whistles and have not been able to say much else. They live in colonies of a dozen or so, in the cracks and crevices of the walls above the basin floor, and they come down to feed in the early morning and retreat as the day warms.

---

The lizards are on every warm stone.

Small, dark, quick. They flatten themselves against the rock and their color matches the rock and they are invisible until they move. They eat the insects that the heat and the steam and the river sustain — small things, midges, something like a moth that lives only around the fumaroles. They are eaten by the birds and by the burrowers and by each other, when one is small enough. They are the most numerous large animal in the basin after the grazers, by some counts. They bask on the warm pipes of the human operation and on the warm stones of the travertine terraces and they are, in their small way, as adapted to the basin as anything in it — they have learned that the warm places are the places and that the cold is something to retreat from quickly. They do not hibernate, or do not hibernate in the way that lowland lizards do, because the basin does not get cold enough, for long enough, to require it.

---

The fish are in the river and no one has ever caught one.

The river is warm in its middle reaches and the fish are warm-water adapted and they are fast and they are strange — wrong proportions, perhaps, or extra fins, or colors that do not match what the river should support. They have been seen, sometimes, in the travertine pools at the upper edges of the terraces, where the water is hottest and the steam is thickest. They are not numerous. They are not fished for, because the river is small and the effort would not be worth it and because no one has ever worked out what they eat or how to take them. They exist as a rumor in the food web — a thing that something else, somewhere, eats, and that is part of the closed system of the basin, and that no human has touched.

---

The humans are temporary and they know it.

They come in shifts. A crew for the drill platform, a crew for the crushing station, a crew for the processing complex, a crew for the railhead, smaller crews for the monitoring posts and the pump stations and the landing strip. They rotate in from somewhere else, somewhere lower and warmer and more populated, and they work for a number of weeks — six is common, eight is the upper limit, longer is not allowed because longer produces mistakes — and then they rotate out and another crew rotates in.

They live in quarters that are part of the facilities — small rooms attached to the larger structures, with bunks and a mess and a place to store the things they have brought from below. They eat in shifts. They sleep when they can. They wear gloves and masks when they are handling the material they are extracting, and they do not discuss it over the radio, and the material is shipped out in sealed containers and the humans do not know, for the most part, where it goes or what it is for. They know what it is — they have been told, they have signed things, they have had the briefing — but they do not think about it much, in the way that people who do dangerous work do not think about the danger most of the time. They think about the shift. They think about the weather. They think about the small plane that is due in two days. They think about going home.

They have adapted to the basin in the small ways — the way they walk (slower, breath even, the altitude respected), the way they dress (layers, because the temperature swings), the way they eat (more than they would at home, because the body wants more here). They have not adapted in the larger ways. They are still lowland people. They sleep poorly for the first week. They have headaches. They notice the quiet and find it unsettling, the way lowland people find deep silence unsettling, because the absence of sound is, for them, a kind of presence.

Some of them do not come back. The shifts are hard and the altitude is hard and the isolation is hard and the work is hard and some people, after one rotation, decide that the basin is not a place they will go to again. Some of them come back for years. Some of them come back and become, in the way that people become, the people who are known to the ravens, the people the ravens will follow across the basin.

---

The basin has noticed them.

The ravens know them. The foxes have learned that humans are not prey and are not, mostly, threat — they are something else, something that drops things and leaves things and occasionally feeds things on purpose. The grazers have moved around them — the herds that worked an area before the humans came have adjusted their patterns, and the areas that were once central to the herds' rotation are now edges, and the edges are now centers. The burrowers have colonized the soft ground under the facilities. The lizards have colonized the warm pipes and the warm machinery. The whole human operation is, from the basin's perspective, another attractor — another warm spot, another source of spillage, another place where the animals cluster.

The basin has not been changed by them, in the deeper sense. The heat is the same. The water cycles the same. The mineral deposits are still being laid down where the river deposits them. The lichen still grows. The closed and tight food web still operates, with the human operation as a new term in the equation — a new input, a new attractor, a new source of disturbance and of waste. The basin has incorporated the operation the way the basin incorporates anything — slowly, into the larger pattern, as one more thing to be adapted to. The basin was here first. The basin will be here after.

---

What is strange about the inhabitants is real and is not consistent.

A grazer, occasionally, with an extra claw — a dewclaw that has grown into something larger, that articulates, that has a small flat nail at the end. A fox, sometimes, with eyes that reflect light in colors that foxes' eyes do not reflect. A condor that is, by measurements, too large. A lizard with a pattern on its back that looks, in certain lights, like a smaller lizard. A raven that has a white feather in the wrong place. None of these are reliable. None of them are common. They happen, and they are noted, and they are written up in the biologists' papers with words like *atypical* and *population variance*, and the papers do not close the question.

The biologists have been coming to the basin for long enough now that they have built up a record. The record is not complete — there are gaps, there are seasons when no one was here, there are years when the funding was thin — but it is long. The record shows that the anomalies are not increasing and not decreasing. The record shows that they are not clustering in particular individuals or in particular lineages. The record shows that they happen in the basin and not, except very rarely, in the surrounding mountains. The record does not show what is causing them. The record shows that they are part of the place.

The biologists have written about it carefully. They have used words like *developmental plasticity* and *environmental influence* and *unexplained variation*. They have not used words like *mutation* or *adaptation* or *speciation*, because those words would commit them to explanations they do not have. The paper that is most often cited is the one that simply lists the anomalies and notes their distribution and stops. The paper ends with the sentence: *Further study is required to determine whether these variations represent a stable polymorphism or a transient response to conditions that are not yet understood.* The sentence has been in the literature for years now and no one has followed it up. The basin is difficult to study. The basin does not give up its secrets easily.

---

The basin itself, in the end, is its own inhabitant.

It has been here for a long time — long enough that the mineral deposits were laid down, long enough that the ecology evolved, long enough that the heat from below has shaped everything in it. The basin does what it does. It heats the air above its floor. It warms the water that runs through it. It grows the lichen, slowly. It sustains the grazers, barely. It is the patient center of a slow system, and the system has been running for longer than anything in it has been alive, and it will be running after everything in it has gone.

The animals are in it for the warmth and the surface growth and they are continuous. The humans are in it for the mineral and they are temporary. The basin is in it for the basin's own reasons, which are not reasons, which are geology, which are the slow processes that have been running since before there was anything to watch them.

The basin does not know the grazers, in the way that one might say the basin knows the grazers. The basin does not care about the grazers. The basin makes the conditions in which the grazers can live and the grazers live and the basin is unchanged by their living. The same is true of the foxes and the burrowers and the lizards and the ravens and the humans. The basin holds all of them. The basin holds them the way it holds the heat — patient, indifferent, not choosing, simply being what it is and letting what is in it be what it is.

The basin is not a beautiful place and it is not picturesque. It is a place that has settled into its own balance and is doing what it does without apology and without much concern for who is watching. A person who has spent time here comes to feel that the basin was here first and will be here after and that the work being done in it is a small and temporary thing inside a larger and older one.
