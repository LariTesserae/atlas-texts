The first thing to understand is that they were here before you arrived, and they will be here after you leave. This is not a comfort. It is the only accurate place to begin.

They are not what you expect. They are not what the word *fauna* prepares you for. Standing at the edge of a tarn at first light, when the mist is still dissolving off the valley and the only sound is the small percussion of meltwater dripping from every overhang, you might see a shape moving on the far slope — a low thing, articulated, the color of tarnished copper in the pale dawn light — and your first thought will be wrong. Your first thought will be *that is a piece of equipment*, or *that is a vehicle of some kind*, or *that is a person in some kind of heavy suit*. All of these are wrong. All of these are attempts to make it fit into categories your mind already holds. It fits in none of them. It is its own category. It is the only thing in its category. You are looking at a being that has no analogue.

Start with the small ones. They are easiest.

---

The smallest of them are the size of your hand when they are adults, though *adult* is the wrong word — *mature* is closer, or perhaps *operational*. They have the shape of broad, flattened segments strung together, six or eight or ten to an individual depending on age, and they move in sheets across the rocks that number in the tens of thousands. You do not see one. You see a tide of them. They pour over the surface of a stone and the stone changes under them: where they have passed, the rock is brighter, the rust veins more vivid, the mica flecks more pronounced, as though they have scoured the surface down to the mineral truth beneath. They are polishing the mountain. They are eating the mountain's skin.

Their carapaces are a greenish-bronze when they are young, darkening to a deep tarnished red as they age, and the joints between segments are not joints in any mechanical sense — there is no hinge, no bolt — but rather living hinges of a translucent chitin that flexes and ripples as the creature moves. Underneath, if you could see (and you should not try, but if you could), the body is a kind of dense, layered meat the color of liver, threaded with what look like copper wires but are not wires at all — they are tendons, mineralized, woven through shaped pockets of stone that serve as anchorage for the muscles. The mouth is at the front, a grinding chamber of ridged surfaces that work against each other with a sustained, gritty hum, and around the mouth are the small appendages that do most of the actual labor: levers, brushes, fine-toothed combs that draw the dust of the rock into the chamber and press it, press it, press it until what comes out the other side is something refined.

They click as they go. A bright, sharp click, almost musical, repeated at irregular intervals by individuals in the sheet but synchronizing into something coherent across the group — a kind of brittle, complex chatter that sounds, if you listen for long enough, like the noise a thousand small mechanisms would make if a thousand small mechanisms were working in genuine concert rather than under central command. They are not under central command. They are working in concert anyway. The sheet has a shape; it has a front; it advances. But there is no leader. There is no queen. There is only the aggregate behavior of all those small machines running, each on its own, each working its own patch of stone, all of them somehow summing to a tide that moves uphill when the morning sun warms the rockface and downhill when the shade returns.

Where the sheet has passed, the polished stone gleams. Where the polished stone gleams, the lichen cannot take hold as easily. Where the lichen cannot take hold, the moss thins. Where the moss thins, the next generation of small creatures will find leaner pasture and move on. This is how the Floe turns over. This is how the red gets redder, year by year, century by century. The small ones are the first teeth in the mouth of the place.

---

The middling ones are different. They have names in the languages of neighboring regions, but the names are all borrowed from things they only resemble: a kind of cattle, a kind of pig, a kind of dog at scale. None of the borrowings are accurate. None of them will be.

They stand about as high as your knee at the shoulder when fully grown, and twice that long from blunt head to tapered tail. They have four limbs — four is universal among them, no more, no less — and the limbs are heavy, jointed in three places, ending in broad flat feet that distribute their weight across the moss without crushing it. Their carapaces are thicker than the small ones, segmented into plates that ride up and down over the shoulders and hips as they move, and the plates are a deep red-brown, almost black in shadow, but at the edges where the plates meet there is a fine line of bright copper color, and these lines catch the light so that the herd, in motion, looks like a single slow animal made of moving bronze.

They travel in herds. The herds are not random. There is a shape to them — a kind of loose funnel, with the older animals at the center and the younger at the edges, the way you might arrange a school of fish if you wanted the small ones protected. They move across the slopes at a pace that is not quite walking and not quite grazing; they are doing both at once, their grinding mouths working the moss and lichen as they go, taking the surface layer of the Floe's skin and digesting it slowly through the long day. Where they have fed, the ground is visibly lower. Where the ground is lower, meltwater pools. Where the meltwater pools, the next year will see a richer growth of moss, and the year after that a richer growth still. The middling ones make the meadows. They are the meadow-makers. They pass and the world grows back greener where they have been.

And they sing.

The singing is the thing that stays with you, when you leave the Floe. It is not a song you would recognize as song. It is a sustained harmonic, built up from the individual vocalizations of dozens of animals calling to each other across the herd, each call a single pure tone at a slightly different frequency, and the tones do not so much blend as *layer*, the way light layers in a prism, so that what you hear is not a melody but a chord that is constantly shifting in its internal balance as different animals fall in and out of phase with each other. You hear it first in your sternum. You feel it in your bones. By the time it reaches your ears it has already been inside you for half a minute. And the chord never resolves. It does not have an end. The herd sings all day, and the song they are singing is the same song their great-grandparents were singing, and it will be the same song their great-grandchildren will sing, with no perceptible break in continuity across the generations.

If you stand among them — and you should not, but if you do — the sound is so pervasive that you stop being able to think in straight lines. It is too dense. It fills the head. After a few minutes you begin to feel that the sound is the herd and the herd is the sound, that you are standing inside a single vast slow creature that happens to be made of dozens of smaller ones in loose formation, and that the chord is not their communication but their *being*, the audible expression of a metabolism that has been running, unchanged, since before the mountains around you had finished rising out of the plateau.

---

The great ones you see rarely. They prefer the open slopes where the wind has scoured the snow clear and the sun falls clean on the stone, and they move only in the deep cold hours of early morning, when the light is still blue and the air is still and the meltwater is silent. If you are on such a slope at such an hour, you will know they are coming before you see them. The ground begins to vibrate. A pebble set on a flat stone will begin, very slowly, to walk across the stone of its own accord. A film of meltwater in a shallow depression will begin to shiver into concentric rings. The subsonic arrives first — the lowest register of their call, below the threshold of hearing, transmitted through the rock itself, and it is this that makes the ground move under your feet.

Then you see them.

They are the size of small houses. *Small* in the sense that a modest cottage is small; they are still vast, broader than long, low-slung, with the proportions of something that has been compressed by its own weight for a very long time. Their carapaces are not the bronze-red of the middling ones but a deep, oxidized black-violet, the color of storm clouds or old blood dried on iron, and the plates of the carapace are ridged and layered like the gills of a mushroom, scaled to a body that is itself scaled to the slow tectonic processes of the place. They have fewer limbs than the middling ones — only four, enormously thick, each ending in a splayed foot that grips the stone. They do not move quickly. They cannot move quickly. The mechanics of their body do not permit quick movement; the tendons and muscles are scaled to mass and the mass is scaled to time, and the time they move in is the time of geological drift, of continental plate, of mountain range.

When they pass, the moss for a hundred meters on either side flattens and dies. When they pass, the small ones flee in sheets of clicking alarm. When they pass, the middling herd will fall silent for the duration of their transit, the chord dropping out one voice at a time until there is nothing but the subsonic and the drip of meltwater and the slow grinding of the great one's own body working the stone beneath it.

You do not see them feed. You see, afterward, the place where they have been: a swath of raw red rock, stripped of everything that grew on it, the iron-veins standing out bright against the paler stone, as though the mountain has been flayed. And in the swathe, at intervals, the great one's droppings — long pale ribbons, thicker than your arm, of refined mineral, the concentrated essence of what they have taken from the earth. Where these ribbons fall, the next generation of moss will come up a different color. Where the moss comes up different, the small ones will follow. Where the small ones follow, the middling ones will follow in their turn. The great ones are the disturbance that keeps the system turning. They are the slow periodic catastrophes that prevent the Floe from becoming static, that renew the surface, that start the cycle again.

They live, it is said, for centuries. No one has ever counted. Their generations overlap so that there are always great ones on the Floe, somewhere, working the slow rotation of the place, and a single great one's transit across a valley can take a season or more, and when they reach the valley's far rim they turn, or they do not turn — sometimes they simply stop, and the next season the herd of middling ones will follow them up onto the higher slopes as the new meadow grows.

---

These are the animals of the Floe. But the word *animals* sits wrong in the mouth, as it sat wrong in the first description of this place, because these things do not behave as animals behave. They do not flee from you, exactly; they register you and continue. They do not attack you, exactly; if you are in the way of a great one's transit, the ground will shake for hours in warning and you will have time to move. They do not breed in any season you can identify; their young appear, suddenly, one season, at the edges of the herds or in the wake of the great ones' droppings, as though the conditions have simply produced them. They are not born in any way you would recognize. They are *grown*, from the minerals and the moss and the slow oxidation of the iron-bearing stone, and they grow to operational size, and then they work, and then they die, and their bodies are taken back into the system by the next generation of their kind, and the cycle continues.

This is why they are hard to see as animals. They are not, in any meaningful sense, individuals. They are *phases*. Each one of them is a phase of the Floe's metabolism, an embodiment of a process that is older than any of them and will outlast any of them, and the process does not require them to have feelings or thoughts or even, perhaps, awareness. They hum. They click. They sing the long chord. They grind the stone. They excrete the refined mineral. They die and are reabsorbed. This is their entire life. This is their entire purpose. They are the industry of the place, but they are also the place — the Floe is not the stone and the moss and the meltwater plus the fauna; the Floe is the system, and the fauna is the system made briefly visible, and when you look at a herd of middling ones moving across a slope you are looking at a process that happens to have, at this moment, the shape of a herd.

And yet.

And yet when the great one passes on the open slope in the early blue light, and the ground trembles, and the small ones flee in their shimmering sheets, and the middling herd falls silent one voice at a time, and the vibration rises through the stone into the soles of your boots and up into the long bones of your legs and you stand very still and watch the vast slow shape move across the face of the mountain as it has moved for a million years and will move for a million more — when you see this, you cannot quite hold onto the idea that the thing is only a process. It is too large. It is too patient. It is too present. It looks back at you, in some way, with whatever serves it for eyes — the dull gleam of mineral lenses set in the plates of its head — and what you feel looking into that regard is not menace and not curiosity but something older than either, something like being seen by a mountain that has just become, briefly, aware of your small warm presence on its flank.

The Floe is loud. The Floe is bright. The Floe is alive in a way that does not know it is alive and so does not need to know, and the things that live there are the visible parts of its aliveness, the parts that have, for this moment, taken shape and moved out across the slopes to do the work of being alive, and when you leave — and you will leave, because no one stands here long — what you will remember is the chord. The sustained chord that does not resolve. The chord of dozens of middling creatures calling across a herd that has been calling the same chord since before your species had a word for *chord*. The sound of a place that does not wait because waiting implies that something has not yet begun, and the Floe is past beginning. The Floe is running. It has always been running. The hum you hear, when you press your ear to the cold red stone, is the sound of it running.
