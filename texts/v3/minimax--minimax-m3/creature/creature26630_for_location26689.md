What lives here has forgotten what change looks like. It has not merely adapted to the Pump Room — it has been *completed* by it, the way a word is completed by the sentence that holds it. The creatures in this hall are not surviving the submersion. They are what the submersion has, over a span of time no mouth can pronounce, finally made.

---

Start with the thing that has gone furthest.

The mussels. They were mussels once in the sense that oysters were once oysters, that *we* were once something older and wetter and less certain of itself. Their ancestors came in on a current, four hundred years ago or a thousand, and found a substrate, and stayed, because the water moved and carried food and the iron was rough enough to grip. They have not left. Why would they leave. Their great-grandchildren's great-grandchildren, when those existed, would find the same water moving at the same speed past the same iron. They have grown into the geometry of the place. A single individual, pried free now — if anything could pry one free — would come away trailing pipes, would come away with a piece of the machine's circulation still bonded to its byssal threads, would leave behind a hole that the calcium of its dead siblings would not be shaped to fill. They have become a tissue. A skin. The pipes, beneath their crust, are vestigial. The water does not need them anymore. It could move through the mussel-concrete alone, and the mussels, in their slow consensus, have arranged that it does. Where one has died, its shell has not been relinquished. The calcium has taken it up, has incorporated it the way a forest floor incorporates a fallen trunk — by burying it without ceremony and growing over it. The mass of living and dead shell is, by now, a single stratum, and the only way to parse it would be by season of death, and there are no seasons here. There is only layer after layer after layer, in a slow agglutination that will outlast the iron it was laid down upon.

---

The anemones are gentler, and less architectural. They sit in the corners where the wall curves into itself, where the machine's circulation loses force and the water goes soft. They have chosen the still places, and the still places have chosen them back. The oldest of them are the size of a child curled fetal; their bases have spread across the curved floor until they meet their neighbors, and where they meet they do not merge but they lean against each other, a colony of solitary bodies. Their color is the ghost of color — a translucent grey that borrows what pink it can from the amber light, that borrows what white it can from the salt. Their tentacles drift. They have no skeleton and no opinion. They eat what comes. They have eaten what has come since long before the hall above the water remembered having a ceiling. When they die, which is rarely and without urgency, they slump into their own bases and the next individual expands over the vacancy. They are the closest things here to being patient, if patience requires a mind to host it. They are not minds. They are flowers that have decided, by the accumulated vote of a thousand generations, what shape a flower should be, and have held to it.

---

The fish.

The fish are the lost ones.

Consider what it means to be a fish in the Pump Room. There is no day. There is no night. There is the hum, and the amber glow, and the current, and the other fish. The current is a kind of day. The glow is a kind of sun. Their eyes, generation by generation, have not been selected against by any failure — they have simply been *forgotten*. Not lost in a single dramatic mutation. Withered, over centuries, the way a path withers when no one walks it. The optic lobes thinned. The lenses dissolved. The skin closed over the sockets in a way that, to a touch from outside, would feel smooth. They navigate by the lateral line, by the pressure wave that bounces off the great drums, by the constant oscillation of the water as it moves through the valves. They navigate by each other. When a school turns, every fish in it turns, and the turn itself is the message — *here is the way, here is the new way, follow me, follow the group, follow the group that is following the group.* They have memorized the hall. They know where the drum with the cracked housing is, because the glow there is fractionally warmer. They know where the slow eddy forms beneath the bend in the descending pipe. They know the manhole that opens into the lower system, and that manhole is — to them, in their inherited knowing — a kind of sky. It is the place the water comes from and returns to. It is, for a fish, the edge of the world.

The old ones are longer than an arm. They move slowly. Their scales have paled to a kind of bone color. The young ones are darker, smaller, more numerous, and they school tighter. Both kinds are food for nothing now. Whatever predator once inhabited the Pump Room left, or persists in some form the fish do not recognize as predator, in the sub-basement rooms that remain — for the moment — unflooded. The fish have only each other, and the current, and the constant passage of debris that drifts down through the open valves and out through the manholes and back again, the great circulation of a small heaven they were not built for but have learned to call complete.

---

The crabs are older than the fish and know it.

They are the color of the rust that bleeds down the walls, and whether this is mimicry or coincidence is a question that does not interest them. They move across the calcium crusts with the ponderous assurance of creatures that have not been startled in living memory. Their claws are heavy and old. They feed on the dead mussels, on the anemones that have slumped and forgotten to retract, on the calcified remains of fish that drifted too close to a closed valve and were, by the pressure differential, gently pulp. They eat in a measured way. They eat without haste because there has never been a reason to eat with haste. There are no rival crabs in numbers that exceed the food supply. There are no predators to eat them before they eat. They molt, and the molt remains on the crust, and the next crab passes over it without comment, and the calcium takes it up.

Some of the crabs, the ones that live deepest, on the floor where the sediment has settled into a fine silt, have grown soft. They have not needed a hard shell for so long that the shell has become vestigial, a chalky carapace over a body that is mostly gut. They are a rumor of crabs. They eat what drifts to them. They will not survive if the silt clogs their gills. The silt has not clogged their gills. The silt has not moved in centuries. The silt *will not move*, because nothing moves it, because the crabs have been lying on it for so long that their bodies have become part of its bedding, the way the mussels have become part of the pipes. They are becoming geology. They are content to become geology.

---

And deeper.

Below the crabs, below the silt, below the floor the hall is built on, in the rooms that the water has not reached — the dry rooms, the rooms with air in them still — there are sounds that the crabs cannot hear and that the fish have no name for. A scratching. A slow, repeated investigation of a wall that is, perhaps, no longer thick enough to be a wall. Something down there that remembers the hall from when the hall was something else. Something that has been working, in the dark, at a barrier that it took centuries to weaken and will perhaps take one more century to breach. It does not know what it will find on the other side. It does not know that what it will find is a finished world, an equilibrium, a place where the mussels have grown into the pipes and the anemones have grown into the corners and the fish have grown into the sound of the water and the crabs have grown into the silt and the machines hum their single note into the dark. It does not know it will be a stranger there. It is, itself, a kind of stranger already — a creature out of time, out of memory, a leftover of an architecture that has otherwise been entirely replaced by what it contained.

If it ever comes through.

If it ever swims, blinking, into the amber light, and finds itself in a world that is already full.

It will not be welcome. Not because anything here is hostile. Because nothing here is *capable* of welcome. Because to welcome something would require a relationship to what is not yet here, and the Pump Room has no such relationship. It has only itself.

---

What lives here is what happens when a place has had long enough to finish being made. Not evolved — *finished*. No longer in conversation with its conditions, because its conditions have been the same for so long that conversation has been replaced by repetition, and repetition has been replaced by something that does not yet have a name. Stability, taken far enough, becomes a kind of stasis. Stasis, taken far enough, becomes a kind of death that is also a kind of forever. The mussels are not dying. They are not *not* dying. They are simply doing what they will do, and what their children's children will do, in a hall that will be humming when the sun forgets how to come up over the place this water used to fall from.

The hum does not change.

The fish come around the corner. The anemones drift. The crab moves its claw once, slowly, over the shell of its ancestor. The light glows. The pipe rings.

Something, somewhere below, scratches at a wall.
