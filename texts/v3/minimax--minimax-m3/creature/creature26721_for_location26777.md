# What Lives in the High Garden

What you hear, first and for a while only, is the place itself talking to itself.

The sound does not come from a single direction. It comes from everywhere at once, or seems to, and it has been going on since before you climbed out of the cloud line onto the plateau. It does not begin in the morning — it is already there when you wake, and it is still there when you fall asleep, and at three in the morning it has merely changed register. The chorus is layered the way the canopy is layered: a low stratum of grumbling and thrumming from somewhere down inside the moss and the lower trunks, a middle band of clicks and trills and the wet rasp of things dragging themselves along wet bark, and a high stratum of long falling notes — almost vowels, almost song — that drift down through the half-kilometer of air between the leaf and your ear. To stand in this is to feel that you have stepped inside a conversation that was already in progress and that does not pause to account for you.

The most arresting voice in the lower band belongs to the frogs.

They are not what you would call frogs if you had only seen frogs elsewhere. The ones on the plateau are the size of melons, and they live in the leaf axils of the canopy — not the understory, the *canopy*, forty, fifty, sixty meters up, wedged into the cup where a frond of bromeliad or a fork in a branch makes a small pocket of standing water and rotting tissue. From these cradles, where they feed on whatever small animals the bromeliad pools collect, the males produce a call so low that it is felt before it is heard. You feel it first in the chest, a slow pressure that comes and goes like a tide, and only afterward do you realize you are hearing it: *broom, broom, broom*, a sound below the comfortable reach of the ear, rolling out from a hundred axils at once and arriving at you by way of your sternum. They call all day. They call all night. When the rains come they call harder, until the trunks themselves seem to vibrate faintly under your palm if you lean against one. Their calls do not synchronize into a single pulse. Each male has his own small clock, and the overlapping of a thousand unsynchronized clocks produces the strange carpet of sound that you have been hearing since you arrived — never a single boom, always a weave.

The frogs are old. You can tell by looking. Their backs are ridged and lichenous, the color of wet bark, and on the older ones the skin has begun to grow a faint fuzz of true moss, so that a frog at the bottom of its axil, half-submerged, looks from below like a small greenish stone. They do not, as far as anyone knows, come down. Their entire lives are above the floor. The eggs are laid in the bromeliad pools; the tadpoles grow there, eating mosquito larvae and the drowned bodies of insects; the froglets disperse through the canopy by walking, not jumping, with the slow deliberation of something that has no predators below to flee from. The biggest of them, the patriarchs of the axils, may live for decades in the same cup, growing imperceptibly, mating when the rains come, and producing that single low note hour after hour until at last they stop, and another frog already in the cup below begins to call in their place.

A different sound belongs to the clickers.

You do not see them for a long time. What you hear is a sharp *tchk* that seems to come from inside the trunk you are passing, then another from twenty meters away, then another from above, then a half-second barrage from somewhere you cannot locate at all. The click is a percussive thing — clean, dry, almost mechanical. If you put your ear to a trunk at the right moment you can feel the bark jump with it. The clickers are small, palm-sized at the largest, and they live in the seams of bark and the curled-up husks of dead fronds. They are not insects. They have too many legs — seven pairs, by some count, which makes them kin to nothing you have a name for, or perhaps to something that has been sealed away on this plateau so long that the relationship has been forgotten. They hunt by ambush. They wait at the mouth of a bark-crevice with the front of their bodies lifted, and when a beetle or a small lizard brushes past they strike — and the strike is what you hear, that sharp snap, the click itself being the sound of the head, or the forelimbs, or possibly the whole front half of the animal closing in on the prey faster than the air can get out of the way. They click at one another too, in sequences that sound, when you can hear a whole conversation, like someone idly drumming a pencil on a desk.

Between the frogs and the clickers is a band of insects that does not fit any category you have brought with you. The most pervasive is the wing-grinder. It is some kind of cricket or locust, but the sound it makes is unlike any cricket you have heard. The two wings are rubbed together not in the cricket's cheerful chirp but in a long, slow, grinding rotation — *kkhhrrrrrrrrr* — that takes a full second to complete and that sounds, as the old guidebook has it, like a rope being hauled across a wet drum. There are many species. Some of them are small enough that you cannot find them in the canopy even when you can hear them directly overhead. Others are as long as your hand, soft-bodied and gray-green, with the wings held out from the body at an angle like a folded umbrella. The grinders call at dusk most of all, and at dusk the canopy becomes a single long sustained note of friction, with the frogs booming underneath and the clickers punctuating above, and the falling notes of the birds beginning to mix in as the last light goes.

The birds are the only large animals that you see all the time.

They are blue. There is no other way around it; they are blue in a way that has nothing to do with the sky, an electric, slightly synthetic blue, the blue of a spark jumping a gap. They are the size of crows but they have longer tails and shorter beaks and their faces are bare, a patch of pale skin around the eye that gives them, when they look at you sideways, an expression of amused conspiracy. They travel in bands — loose, ragged bands of fifty or sixty — moving between the pillars by long parabolic flights that carry them up over the canopy and down again, so that the band passes you as a series of whoops, the call of each bird catching up with the next and tumbling over it. The call is a long falling note — *teeeuw, teeeuw, teeeuw* — that drops the way a stone drops, and the band as a whole gives the impression of laughter because the calls come in uneven ranks, one starting just as another ends, so the sound is always rising or falling and never flat. They eat fruit, mostly, and the insects that fly out of the canopy at dusk. They nest in the crooks of the highest branches, in cups woven out of moss and spider silk, and they defend the nest with a great fuss of screaming and diving at anything that approaches.

A third of their fledglings fall.

This is a known thing. You find them on the moss — small, half-feathered, blue already, the color coming in even before they can fly. Some of them are dead. Some are not. The ones that are not, the lucky ones or the unlucky ones depending on how you look, are gathered up by the people and kept, and they become, in some way that the people do not try to explain, members of households. They are raised. They learn to fly eventually, weeks later, and then they go back to the canopy and to the bands, but they remember the people who fed them and they come back, and they are listened to, and what they say is repeated in a soft falling note by an old woman on a basket-woven porch. There is a name for this in the language the people speak among themselves, and there is a different name for it in the songs.

Above the birds, in the forks where the trunks finally fork, lives something you almost never see.

It is the largest animal on the plateau. It is the size of a small pony. It is pale — the color of the lichen on a granite boulder in afternoon light, a yellowish off-white — and it is built in a way that you keep doubting after you have seen it. The limbs have too many joints. Not the way an elephant's leg has extra padding, but the way a backbone has many small joints — each limb is a chain of articulations, shoulder, elbow, wrist, and then beyond the wrist another joint, and another, and another, so that the limb can fold back on itself in directions that limbs are not supposed to fold. The animal moves slowly. It grips the bark with all four limbs and walks, in a slow climbing gait, head low, eyes tilted upward, ears flat. The head is long and narrow. The teeth, which you see once because the animal yawns at you from a branch twenty meters up, are small and even and slightly yellow.

It is a predator. It is patient. It does not have to be fast because nothing here is in a hurry, and the prey is everywhere.

The signs of it are more common than the animal itself. You see the bark-stripped scars on the trunks — long pale wounds, often forty feet up, sometimes higher, where the predator has levered off a slab of bark with one of its too-many-jointed forelimbs and eaten what was underneath. Underneath is usually ants: the ant-plants, those bulbous bromeliads with their internal ant colonies, host enormous populations of fat white grubs deep in the cistern of the plant, and the predator seems to know this, and will tear open an ant-plant as readily as it will tear bark. The scars heal slowly. A tree can wear a pale streak for fifty years. You learn to read the trunks by these marks the way you might read a forest by its fallen trees — and the marks tell you that the predator has been working this stand for a long time, that it ranges widely, and that it is alone. You almost never see two of them together. They are solitary.

Their droppings, when you find them, are enormous and fibrous — pale grey cylinders the length of your forearm, packed with the chewed remnants of bark, of bromeliad tissue, of chitin, and occasionally of bone. They smell, faintly, of green woodsmoke. They are deposited, with what looks like deliberation, on the moss below the trunk where the animal was working, as if the predator marks its foraging sites. Nothing on the plateau scavenges them quickly. They last for weeks.

What they eat, besides the ant-grubs and the bark-tissue, is the small things. The small mammals that come down at night to the seep-lines. The frogs, when they can reach them. The fallen fledglings, when they find them. They are unhurried, but they are thorough, and they have been here longer than the people have, and probably longer than the birds have, and possibly longer than the trees.

The trees themselves are not what you would call alive in the way the animals are alive, but they are not inert either. They breathe. They drink from the fog through their bark, through the long fringed leaves of the bromeliads that crown them, through the slow film of water that travels down the trunks in the seep-lines. They grow. A tree at maturity has been growing since before the great-grandparents of the people now living were born. Their trunks are not single columns. They are, by the time you can recognize one as a tree, communities. A trunk will house, in its seams and forks, a dozen species of orchid, several kinds of fern, a hanging garden of moss, an ant colony, a family of the clickers, and in the upper axils a chorus of the giant frogs. To stand beneath one of the older pillars and listen is to hear, in the layered sound, the sound of the tree's tenants: the clickers in the bark, the wing-grinders in the upper branches, the frogs in their cups, the birds nesting at the very top. The tree is the landlord, and it has not changed tenants in a long time.

The moss on the floor is older than the trees. It is older, possibly, than the people's memory. It is a low felt — ankle-deep, dense, the color of old bronze in shade and the color of wet pewter in sun — made up of a dozen species pressed together so tightly that you cannot tell where one ends and another begins. It drinks from the mist and the seep-lines and the air itself. It does not flower. It does not, in any obvious way, propagate. It simply persists, year after year, growing imperceptibly at the upper surface while the lower surface dies and compacts into peat. The peat, in places, is meters deep. The bones of the mountain are at the bottom of it. In some of the valleys, where the moss has had ten thousand years to accumulate, you can press your palm into the felt and feel the boggy give of centuries.

In the moss there are smaller things. There are springtails in colors you do not expect — pale violet, dull copper. There are mites so small they are almost not there. There are beetles that move through the felt like fish through kelp. There are flatworms that hunt at the moss surface in the cool of the morning and retreat below as the day warms. There are spiders that build sheet-webs at the boundary between the moss and the open air — low tents of silk that you stumble into before you see them, the spider himself sitting in the center and trusting the silk to tell him what has hit it. None of these are loud. None of these are what you came to see. But they are the floor of the cathedral, and they are more numerous than anything else alive here, and the moss would not be the moss without them.

The small mammals that come down to drink at night are about the size of a hand. They have long tails, soft fur, and the cautious flat gait of animals that have lived their whole lives under the eye of something patient and pale. They come down the trunks after dark, using the lianas as ladders, and they drink from the seep-lines with their small tongues pressed against the wet bark. They are prey. The predator knows their routes. You can tell where they come down by the worn smoothness of the bark at the descent points, and you can tell, in the morning, where the predator waited by the scuff-marks in the moss and the small red smear of one that did not make it back up. The people trap them. The traps are woven from vines and set into the runnels at the seep-lines, and they take, in a good night, several animals. The people eat them roasted whole on a small fire of dead branches gathered from the floor, and the bones, afterwards, are polished and shaped into knives.

The people themselves are thin in number and quiet.

There are perhaps a few hundred of them on the whole plateau, in scattered family groups that drift from pillar to pillar as the years go by. They are tall and brown and they move through the canopy as easily as on the floor, using the lianas as a kind of suspended highway that runs at every height between the moss and the highest fork. They build their houses by weaving the lianas into walls — a house is a basket stood on end, with the door a low slit and the roof a denser weave that sheds the rain. The weaving is tight. A finished house looks as if it has grown there, as much fruit of the tree as anything the people have done. Inside, the floor is a woven mat; the bed is a hammock of finer vines; the light comes in green through the walls.

They have no metal. They have stone axes that have been polished by use until they shine like wet slate. They have knives of sharpened bone — usually the long bone of one of the small mammals, ground down on a sandstone block over years until the edge is thin and even. They have baskets, woven in a day, that they use to carry the harvest from the bromeliads. They have fire, kept carefully in clay-lined pits, and a knowledge of the way to coax a flame from the wet wood of the floor in a way that you would not have thought possible in so damp a world.

They plant nothing. There is nothing to plant. But they cultivate.

The cultivation is in the upper branches. The people climb the lianas at dusk, when the wind has died, and they move through the canopy with a sureness that makes you understand that the canopy is their real floor. They carry the bromeliad pups — the small offsets that the parent plants throw off — and they wedge them into the crooks of branches that do not yet have a bromeliad, and they return, week after week, to make sure the pups have taken. In a year or two the new bromeliad has rooted and begun to form its central cistern, and in three years it is large enough to hold a man's mouthful of water, and after that the people harvest the cistern in the morning, before the sun takes it, with a long hollow gourd. The bromeliads they have planted are everywhere in the canopy around their settlements — a whole sky-garden, hand-set, that supplies them with drinking water even in the long dry spells between the rains.

They trap the small mammals at the seep-lines, as I have said. They eat the frogs sometimes, when they can reach the lower axils, though they take only the largest and they leave the patriarchs. They eat the fledglings that fall. They eat the fruit that the canopy drops. They have, in other words, a diet that is almost entirely what the forest has already provided, supplemented by what they can take from it without asking too much. They do not hunt the predator. The predator is larger than they are. They give the predator a wide berth, and they read the bark-stripped scars for signs of where the predator is working, and they move their sleeping platforms to a different tree when the scars begin to multiply near their own.

They have been here a long time. Their great-grandparents were here. There are faint marks in the stone near the ridgelines — chiseled lines, deliberate, almost worn away — that no one now living can explain. There is a half-buried wall near one of the lower valleys that the moss is slowly digesting back into itself. Once in a great while a hut is built not from new vines but from old ones, from a hollow in a living trunk that has grown around it, and it becomes unclear, after enough years, whether the hut is inside the tree or the tree is inside the hut.

They speak quietly. The canopy has made them quiet, the way the canopy makes everyone quiet, because to be heard you must shout and to shout is to advertise yourself to the predator and to the band of blue birds and to whatever else in the green dark is paying attention. They speak in gestures when they can. They speak in low breath-voice when they cannot. Their children learn to read the mouth before they learn to read the word. They laugh with their hands over their mouths.

In the long evenings, when the shadows pool around the trunks and the birds have quieted to a single calling here and there, the place does not become silent. It becomes, if anything, louder with the smaller things. The clickers click. The frogs boom, low and steady as a heart. The leaves, half a kilometer up, rustle in a wind you cannot feel. If you lie down on the moss and close your eyes, the sound is of being held, gently, by something enormous and unhurried.

The stone is old, and the trees are old, and the people are old, and the sounds are old, and nothing here is in any particular hurry to become anything other than what it already is. The frogs will boom tomorrow. The clickers will snap. The predator will move through the canopy in its slow climbing gait, lifting its long pale head to listen. The birds will fall, and the ones that fall will be gathered, and the old women will listen to what the survivors remember, and the moss will go on digesting the wall that no one now remembers building. The plateau has been here a long time. It will be here a long time still. What lives here has learned to live in the long shape of the place, and what lives here does not need to be anywhere else.
