The people of the Settlement are not loud. They have learned to be small in the air. Their voices have settled into the working register, the one that carries across a workshop without rising, the one that can be heard over the hum of the separators without effort. They speak and the words are brief and the words are understood and no one repeats them. They are lean, mostly, and the lean is not the lean of hunger but of use. Their skin is the color of the dust, which is to say a faded tan overlaid with a fine grey that does not wash off entirely, because the dust is in the air and the air is what they breathe and the breathing deposits it. Their hair, whatever color it began as, has taken on the same dusty cast, and some of them wear it cropped close to the head so that the heat has less to hold. Their eyes are squinted against the high pale haze, and the squint has become a permanent feature, not in the muscles exactly but in the way the face has settled, the way the lids are half-drawn even when they are looking at something close.

They wear layers. The outer layer is canvas or treated cloth, once a color and now closer to the color of the basin, and the layer beneath is cotton, and the layer beneath that is the skin, and all three are damp in the morning and dry by noon and damp again by evening, and the cycle of dampness and dryness has worn the cloth thin at the cuffs and the knees and the elbows. They wear boots that come up to the shin, laced or buckled, the leather hard and pale with use, and the boots are always dusted and never clean because clean is not a state the boots can hold for long. They wear gloves when they work, and the gloves are thick and dark and stained, and the fingertips of some of them are worn through, and they are given new ones when the wear reaches a certain point, and the old ones are not discarded but kept, and some of the older workers have gloves they have worn for years, gloves that have become part of the hand in a way that is not metaphor but use.

They move on Settlement time. The pace is not slow exactly, but it is not the pace of the world outside the basin either. There is a patience to the way they cross a yard, a patience to the way they climb a stairway between tiers, a patience to the way they stand at a gauge they no longer read. Their footsteps are soft because of the dust. Their hands move with the certainty of repetition, the way the ore-cars move, the way the pipes carry their loads. They do not look at the spire. They have looked at it. They have made their peace with it, or the peace has been made for them by the cycle.

The older ones are different only in degree. Their hands have the color of the leather of their gloves, and the gloves have the color of the rock, and the rock has the color of the dust, and the dust has the color of the air. Their eyes have the cast of the thick green glass in the windows, and the windows are warped, and the warping has migrated into the eyes so that looking at an older worker through one of the windows is like looking at them through their own eyes, and the looking is not quite stable. They cough. The dust is in their lungs. They have coughed for years, and the cough is part of the working sound of the Settlement the way the hiss of steam is part of the working sound, and the cough is not a sign of sickness but of use, the way the wear on the gloves is not a sign of damage but of use. They cough and they go back to work. They cough and they speak in the working voice. They cough and they watch the ore-cars go up the rail-lines with their covered loads, and they do not ask, and the not-asking is a kind of peace that the Settlement has arranged for them, or that they have arranged for the Settlement.

---

The machines live here too. They are not visitors. They have been here longer than most of the people and they will be here longer than the rest, and they do not know this, or they do not care, or the distinction between knowing and not knowing does not apply to them.

The hum you hear from the last ridge before you come down into the basin is the hum of the machines, and it is not one hum but many, and each machine has its own note, and the notes are slightly out of phase with each other and the interference pattern of the out-of-phase notes is what you actually hear — a slow rising and falling that is not quite a melody and not quite a noise but something between, the way the heat is not quite a temperature and not quite a presence but something between.

The separators are the oldest. They stand in the lower tier in long rows, each one a squat cylinder of riveted plate with a small window of the thick green glass and a gauge that no one looks at anymore because the gauges were calibrated for a different grade of ore, and the ore has changed or the gauges have drifted or both, and the separators run anyway, on Settlement time, and what comes out of them goes up the pipes to the next tier and what does not come out goes back to the basin or into the ground or into the air as the slow heat-shimmer that you can see against the rock behind the stacks. The separators do not chatter. They do not bang. They breathe. The breath is the hum and the breath is the slow heat-shimmer and the breath is the way the whole lower tier feels when you stand in it, like standing near a sleeping animal.

The pumps are quieter. They are in the powerhouse at the western edge and they push the pale weak-tea water up from the basin to the upper tiers, and the water goes through the mills and the separators and the spire and comes back down changed, and the pumps do not know that the water is changed, or they do not care, and the pumps hum in their own note and the note is below the note of the separators and you feel the pumps more than you hear them — in the chest, in the floor, in the way your teeth sit in your jaw.

The ore-cars are the patient ones. They move on the rail-lines that climb the slope toward the higher ridges, and the rails are old and the cars are older and the wheels are scarred and the bearings have been replaced so many times that the original bearings are only a memory in the workshop and the new bearings are already wearing. The cars move at the pace of the Settlement, which is not the pace of the world, and when you watch them from a walkway you can see the slow patience of them, the way they do not hurry, the way the loads under the covers do not shift or settle but ride as if the cars know the grade and the curves and are negotiating them rather than fighting them.

The spire is the loudest. It rises from the highest tier and you are not invited up there and you do not go, and the steam comes from it in a slow white column that does not disperse but hangs and is taken east by the prevailing wind, and the column is visible from anywhere in the basin and is sometimes visible from the ridges, and the people who live in the Settlement do not look at it. The spire hums at a frequency you feel in the fillings of your teeth, and the hum is the loudest sound in the Settlement, and the Settlement is not a loud place, so the hum is not loud in the absolute sense but loud in the relative sense, and you feel it in your bones when you are in the upper tier and you feel it less when you are in the lower tier and you do not feel it at all when you have left the basin and are on the far side of the last ridge, although it is still there, and you know it is still there.

---

There is life here that is not the people and not the machines. It is small and it is patient and it does not announce itself.

The orange crust in the cracks of the basalt is the most visible of it. It is a thin layer, almost a film, and it is the color of rust and it is slightly damp to the touch in the early hours and dry and brittle by afternoon. It is in every crack that holds a little moisture, and it is on the underside of the corrugated roofs where the condensation gathers at dawn, and it is on the lower edges of the settling tanks in the eastern quarter, and it is on the inside of the pipes that carry the pale water. No one cultivates it. No one removes it. It is part of the Settlement the way the dust is part of the Settlement. It is mineral, or it is alive, or it is what is happening between the mineral and the alive, the way the Settlement is what is happening between the rock and the construction. It does not move. It does not grow in any way you can see over the days you are there. But it is there, and it is slightly damp when everything else is dry, and that is its way of being alive, if it is alive.

The pale many-legged thing you see on the second day is faster. It moves across a stretch of bare rock between two workshops and it is gone before you can see it properly, and what you see is a low pale shape with too many legs that move in a ripple, and the ripple is fast, and the shape is the color of the dust, and it is gone into a crack in the basalt or under a sheet of riveted plate or into a shadow you cannot follow. You are told there are bats. You do not see them. You are told there are things larger that come down from the higher ridges at night, things with eyes that catch the yard-lights, and you do not see them either, but you hear them sometimes — a soft huffing in the dark, and the sound of claws on corrugated iron, and once, far off, a sound that is not a howl and not a bark but something between. The things from the higher ridges are visitors. They come down because the Settlement is warm at night and the warm draws them, and they scavenge at the edges, and they do not come into the Settlement proper, and the people of the Settlement do not go out at night into the basin, and the arrangement is understood by both sides and is not discussed.

There is no grass. There are no trees. There is nothing you can identify as a plant. There is the orange crust, and the small fast pale thing, and the bats, and the things from the higher ridges, and there is the dust. The dust is not alive but it moves, and the movement of it is a kind of presence. The dust is everywhere. The dust is in the air and on every surface and in the lungs of the people and in the workings of the machines, packed into the bearings and the gears and the slides, and the machines are kept running by the constant attention of the people to the dust, the way the dust is kept moving by the constant motion of the people and the machines.

---

What you notice, after a while, is that the boundaries are softening. The newer additions to the Settlement are not painted and they are already taking on the rust-orange of the crust. The older buildings are faced with the brittle pale tile that cracks and shows the redder body underneath, and the body underneath is closer to the color of the rock than to the color of the tile, and the rock has the same faint sheen, the sheen of something that flowed and stopped. The wall on the east side is partly a retaining wall and partly a cliff face and the engineers have stopped, perhaps, trying to tell which is which. The pipes that walk on their own legs across the slope toward the higher ridges have the look of something that is both walking and rooted, and the legs of the pipes are sunk a little into the rock in places, and no one is sure if the sinking is settling or growth or the slow consumption of the rock by the pipe or the slow consumption of the pipe by the rock.

What lives here is the Settlement itself. Not the people and not the machines and not the rock, but what they are becoming together, the thing that hums in the heat and sweats in the morning and dries by noon and sweats again by evening, the thing that moves the pale water through the mills and the separators and the spire and back to the basin, the thing that coughs the dust and breathes the heat-shimmer and does not ask what is in the ore-cars and does not ask what is in the spire and does not need to ask, because the asking would be outside the cycle, and the cycle does not leak.
