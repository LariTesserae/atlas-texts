I want to begin with the hesitation that precedes everyone, because everyone who has ever walked up the long switchback road has had it — that half-step on the shelf where the air changes weight, and the lungs take in something that is wet and warm and mineral, and the body, before the mind has named anything, knows it has arrived in a place that was already occupied.

What follows is what is there, layered, the way the city itself is layered.

---

There is a woman I will describe first, though she is not first in anything except the eye's arrival. She sits on the low parapet of the cistern in the civic plaza, in the late part of the afternoon when the basin of hot water catches the light and throws it back as a moving gold. She is old in the way that older women here get old — slowed, softened, but not stooped, because there is no cold to make you fold inward. Her hair is white and pinned loosely. Her hands are broad and redder than you'd expect, the skin slightly waxy, the knuckles enlarged, the hands of someone who has worked for a long time in a place where the water is warm and the minerals settle into the cuticle. She wears a dress of indeterminate color that has been washed many times. The smell of sulfur is on her, but the smell of sulfur is on everyone, so it is not a smell.

She has, beside her on the parapet, a shallow ceramic dish. It is chipped. It has been chipped for a long time. In it is a substance I will not be able to identify accurately from any distance — a pale, faintly luminous paste, the color of weak tea with milk in it. It has a smell I will come to.

She is feeding the things.

They come down from the eaves. From under the eaves — there is a distinction. The things in the eaves you cannot see unless you look up, and then only as a softening of the shadow, a paleness within the shadow. The things *under* the eaves come out. They are bolder. They have learned that the dish means a meal.

I want to describe one of them now, and not call it a thing, because I have been calling them things too long already, and they are not things. They are creatures. They are alive. Let me give this one — there is a verb I will invent for this — let me give one a *preliminary*.

It is about the size of a housecat, but flatter, more elongated, and its legs — it has six — are jointed in a way I keep wanting to describe as *wrong* and then have to revise. The joints are not wrong; they are different. They are joints I have not seen before. The legs come out of a body that has no waist, no narrowing, just a long shallow motion of musculature under a skin that is the color of the thermal vents — that pale lavender, that almost-not-quite color that the eye does not want to commit to. The skin is translucent in places; I can see something moving under it that is not blood-colored, that is paler, almost opalescent.

It has eyes. This is the part I keep stalling on.

It has eyes that are not eyes. There are four of them, set along the sides of the long flat head, and they are not spheres, they are not slits; they are like the eyes of certain fish, certain deep-sea things I have not thought about in years — flat, golden, with a horizontal pupil, with a stillness in them that does not flicker when the creature moves. The creature can move its head but it does not seem to need to, because the eyes wrap the head like a turret, like a periscope, and the creature can see in most directions without turning at all. I have been looked at by creatures before. I have not been looked at like this. The looking is patient. The looking has no opinion in it.

It eats. It eats the paste from the dish and it eats quietly, the way a cat eats quietly, the mouth — I won't describe the mouth; the mouth is for after, when I have built up to it — working with a small persistent motion. The paste goes down. The paste is gone. The creature licks the dish once, very thoroughly, with something that is either a tongue or a clustered set of small appendages around what would be a mouth if it had a mouth, and then it looks up at the old woman, and then it is gone, back under the eaves, up into the crack between the parapet and the wall, into the dampness and the heat that the wall has stored.

The old woman does not pet it. She does not coo at it. She refills the dish. The dish is refilled from a small earthenware jar she has brought with her. The jar has a lid. The jar has something in it that I cannot see clearly but that the old woman handles with the particular care of someone carrying something that has a temperature. She does not speak to the creature. She does not need to. There is a contract between them that does not involve language.

---

There are people who keep them.

I want to describe one of those now. Let it be a child. Let it be a girl of about seven, one of the children who lives in the lower town, one of the ones who plays in the sulfurous fountain in the civic plaza when the older residents are at home with their windows shuttered against the dusk. She has a creature. It is hers in the way that a child can have an animal that is not in any veterinary sense hers. It sleeps on her bed. It eats from a small ceramic dish on the floor of her room, which is small and damp and smells of the stone. The creature has a name. I will not give you the name; the name is in a language that is related to the language of the valley below but has a slight persistent archaic edge to it, like a leaf still attached to its stem by an old stalk. The name means something, probably. The girl does not explain it. The girl does not feel the need to explain it because the creature is just *hers*.

The creature does not do tricks. It does not fetch. It does not respond to its name in any way I would recognize as responding. It lies on her bed and it radiates a faint warmth of its own, not the warmth of the basalt, something else. The girl sleeps with her hand on its middle. The middle is soft and the warmth is even. In the morning the creature is gone, out through a crack in the wall that the girl has not bothered to repair, and she does not worry. The creature comes back at the time the girl has learned to expect it — in the late afternoon, when the shadows get long and the basalt is giving back the day's heat.

She tells her friends about it. Her friends have their own. There is an entire subculture of children in this city who have these creatures, who have named them, who have small ceramic dishes for them and small jars of the paste they will not describe to outsiders. There is a subculture. It is not hidden. It is just not visible to people who aren't looking for it.

---

There is a man who works in one of the turbine halls.

The turbine hall is a long low building with a corrugated metal skin that has gone the color of old pewter. The skin is quiet. The skin is warm to the touch. The hall contains, in its interior, a single machine that is the size of a small house. The machine is not beautiful. It is the kind of beautiful that comes from a thing being exactly what it is. It has been here for a long time. It has been cleaned, lubricated, tended. There are parts of it that have been replaced and parts that have not. The replaced parts are visibly different from the original parts. The man knows which parts are which.

He is the one I want to describe.

He wears a work apron that is faded to the color of a dirty river. He has a small flat tin of something he takes with him to the hall each morning. The tin is the color of his apron. He opens the tin once and shows me what is inside. I do not recognize it. He does not explain it. He closes the tin and puts it back in his pocket. The tin is private.

He speaks quietly in the hall. He speaks quietly everywhere. This is true of all the workers; the hall is a place where voices are automatically subdued, because the hall is large and because the machine makes a sound — a low, constant, indistinguishable sound, more a vibration than a sound, that you can feel in the floor more than hear in the air. To shout would be a kind of rudeness, and not to anyone. To the machine. To the building. To the patience that has built up inside the building over the years of its slow turning.

He has touched one of the patterns. He touched it once. He touched it with the flat of his palm, deliberately, in a place where the pattern crosses a horizontal surface. The pattern felt, he says — and he chooses his words with the same care he uses to choose where to step — the pattern felt like *something*. Not cold, not hot, not smooth, not rough. Like a recognition. Like the wall recognizing his hand. He does not say so directly. He says something about it and then trails off. He does not want to talk more about it. He goes back to the machine. The machine is the size of a small house. The machine is patient. The man is patient with it.

---

There are older women who feed the things, and there are older men, and there are middle-aged people, and there are children. There are teenagers who are embarrassed by the creatures, who do not feed them, who sometimes keep them out of sight. The teenagers are like teenagers everywhere. The teenagers will come back to the feeding, most of them. The teenagers are passing through something. The city knows this. The city has patience with the teenagers.

---

Now let me describe the things more carefully, because I have been avoiding the mouth.

The mouth is on the underside of the long flat head. This is true of all the creatures I have seen; the mouth is ventral. It opens toward the ground. It opens toward the dish. When the creature is feeding, the head tilts slightly, and the mouth parts — I don't want to say *unfolds* but it is the closest word — and something comes down that is not a tongue exactly, it is more like a cluster of small soft appendages that intermesh and work against each other in a way I can only describe as *digestive motion* — they bring the paste in, they pass it along, they bring more in. There is no chewing. There is no sound that I can hear at conversational distance. The mouth is not horrible. The mouth is just *different*.

There are creatures that are larger than the one the girl keeps. There is one that lives in the lower town that I have not seen directly, only heard about; it lives in the warm condensate pool on the roof of a particular building in the older part, and it has been seen from the street as a long slow undulation in the water that is bigger than any animal I would expect to be on a roof. It has not come down. It has not hurt anyone. It eats — what does it eat? The larger ones eat smaller ones. The smaller ones eat the paste. The paste is made somewhere. I will come to the paste.

The creatures breed. This is known. They mate in the warm seasons — there are warm seasons here, hard to distinguish in absolute temperature from the cold seasons, but distinguished by something else, by a difference in the moisture in the air, by a difference in the behavior of the geysers, by a difference in the refraction of the light in the late afternoon. In the warm seasons the creatures are more in evidence. They come down more. They make their faint glass-whistle sound more often. The sound is just at the upper edge of my hearing. It is a sound like a tuning fork held against the air. It is a sound that has no easy modulation — no chirping, no trilling, no inflection. It is a single tone that is repeated. The tone carries.

There are people who can hear it well. There are people who cannot hear it at all and are not sure it exists. There is no consensus. The people who can hear it do not necessarily talk about it. The people who cannot hear it often do not bring up the topic. People who have brought up the topic have ended up in odd conversations.

The creatures die. They leave small pale bones that are very light. The bones dissolve in the rain. They dissolve in the gutters when the rain comes. I have seen a small bone on a paving stone at the edge of the civic plaza; I have come back to it an hour later and it is smaller; by evening it is gone.

---

There are the cats. Let me describe a cat.

The cat is orange. The cat is a cat. It does the things a cat does. It sits in patches of sun on the parapets. It watches things with the patient contempt of cats. It is indifferent, as cats are. It is indifferent, as cats are, to almost everything. *Almost*. The cat regards the creatures as cats regard other creatures it has decided are not worth its attention. There is a kind of looking past. There is a kind of looking through. The cat does not fear them. The cat does not hunt them. The cat does not try to play with them. The cat and the creatures occupy the city as two different kinds of nothing the cat can see.

This is the most disquieting part. I keep coming back to this.

---

There are the patterns.

The patterns are not alive. Probably the patterns are not alive. The official answer is that they are mineral deposition. They are the result of solutions of iron and manganese and other things moving through cracks in the basalt under pressure and precipitating out into the open in the shapes that the cracks naturally hold. The cracks branch. The patterns branch. The patterns look like trees because the cracks look like trees. There is a long technical explanation. The long technical explanation is plausible.

I do not believe the long technical explanation. Or rather I do believe it and it does not satisfy. There are cracks that do not have the patterns. There are cracks that should branch in different ways than they branch. The patterns are too regular. The patterns recur in the same motifs across the city and across different buildings and across different rocks. The patterns are fractal — the same shape inside the shape inside the shape inside the shape. The patterns are dendritic — they look like rivers, like trees, like lung tissue, like the mycelial networks of fungi that do not exist here and that the patterns look most like all the same because the patterns look like a network of something that has been thinking about how to distribute itself across the stone.

The patterns do not move. The patterns do not spread. The patterns do not grow, as far as I can determine, in any time I have been here. They were here before the city. They will be here after the city. They are in the grain of the rock. The rock cannot be made to give them up. They have been probed, scraped, dissolved, mechanically lifted, chemically attacked. They remain. They are part of the stone now. They were part of the stone before.

The patterns glow at dusk.

The patterns glow at dusk for about an hour, a faint residual phosphorescence, the color of — the color of something I do not have a word for, something that is between orange and a kind of pale gold, something that depends on the stone they are on. The patterns catch the last of the daylight and they hold it and they give it back, and in the dusk the city is full of these faint glowing shapes, glowing on the walls of the refinery, on the floors of the turbine halls, on the paving of the old streets in the lower town where the stone is darker and the glow is more visible. The city is full of them and no one remarks on them. They are a feature of the dusk the way stars are a feature of the dusk in other places.

I have walked at dusk through the lower town. I have walked in the time when the basalt is giving back the heat and the steam is catching the last light and the city looks, briefly, like it is on fire. I have walked in that time and the patterns are glowing around me and the creatures are stirring in the eaves — they are more active at dusk — and the cats are sitting on the parapets watching everything with their uncatlike indifference. I have walked at that time and felt the city, in a way I cannot entirely describe, watching me back.

Not alarmed. Not unfriendly. *Attentive.*

The city has been paying attention for a long time. The city has been patient. The patience is not for me. The patience is for itself. I am just passing through.

---

Now let me describe a person who lives here in a way that is not defined by their work.

There is a woman — let me make her middle-aged, let me give her work — she works in one of the rooms in the lower city that no one talks about. The sealed buildings. The vats. She works there. She walks in each morning and comes out each evening with a smell on her that is the smell of something organic, something yeasty, something dark, something that is not exactly the smell of any food. She has been working there for fifteen years. She has the slightly soft hands of someone who works with wet things. She has the calm of someone who has been spending her days in a humid space. She is not unhappy. She is not happy in any particular way either. She is doing something that matters, in a place that matters, in a city that has been doing similar things for a long time.

She speaks quietly. She speaks quietly indoors and outdoors. She speaks quietly not because she is whispering but because the city has taught her to. The city teaches its people to speak softly. This is not a rule. It is a custom that has settled in like sediment. The city has been inhabited long enough that the inhabitants match it. They speak in the volume the city asks for, which is the volume of running water, which is the volume of the wind that comes in soft and continuous, which is the volume of the slow machinery. They speak at the volume of the place.

She has a creature. Of course she has a creature. Almost everyone here has a creature or has had a creature, has memories of creatures that have died. Her creature is older than her work — she got it as a child; her mother had a creature; her mother's mother had a creature; the creatures have been in her family as long as her family has been in the city. The creatures are old here, in the sense that there is no one alive who has a memory of the creatures not being here. The creatures may have always been here. The creatures may have been here before the people. The creatures may have been here before the city was a city. There is no way to know. The creatures themselves do not say.

---

What does she know about the patterns? I asked her, one evening. She did not answer for a long time. She looked at the wall in the room we were in — one of the walls had patterns, this happens — and then she said something like, *They aren't mine to know about.* And then she looked away. And then she said something I want to write down exactly the way she said it, because it took me a few days to understand what she had meant, if I do understand it yet:

*They are what is paying attention.*

I don't know what she meant. I have been thinking about it.

---

The fungi.

There are the fungal farms. They are real. They are producing food. They are not an emergency measure or a desperate workaround; they are a thing the city does well, a thing the city has refined over centuries. The fungi grow on substrates that are themselves volcanic in origin, mineral-rich, sterile of anything that would compete with what is being grown. The fungi grow in vats in sealed rooms. The rooms are sealed because the spores do not get out. The rooms are sealed because the fermentation is delicate and the temperature is held exact and the moisture is held exact and the air is held exact and none of this can be allowed to vary. The air outside the rooms smells of nothing in particular. The air inside, when the door is open and the door is rarely open, smells of something that is yeasty and dark and warm, that is not bread, that is not beer, that is not cheese, that is itself. The food that comes out of the rooms is good. The food that comes out of the rooms has a flavor the people here like, that the visitors find unusual, that is not unpleasant. The food that comes out is consumed. The food that comes out is what fifteen thousand people, a significant fraction of them, eat, when the road down to the valley is closed by weather.

The people who work in the rooms do not talk about the rooms a great deal. This is not shame. It is a kind of privacy. They do not mind being asked. They do not volunteer.

---

The mayor or the council or whatever the deliberative body is.

There is someone like this. There is a room. The room has a table. The table is volcanic stone. There are patterns on the walls of the room. There are documents in drawers. There is a deliberation that happens. There is a person who decides things. I do not have a clear picture of this person because I have not been in the room with them. I have seen them in the plaza, walking slowly, being greeted, being unhurried. They are one of the people who has touched a pattern, who has felt something, who does not talk about what.

---

There is a man in his forties who keeps a creature and works in the refinery. He is one of the people I have heard about who feels, when he touches a pattern, a kind of pull — he described it as a *pull*, like the pattern wants something from his hand. He does not touch them often. He does not avoid them. He passes them. He has the habit, I think, of glancing at them in a particular way when he walks past, the way one might glance at a sleeping animal to see if one has woken it. He has not woken one.

---

There is an old man who lives near the eastern granary, the one that was built around the spring. He is one of the oldest people in the city. He has the slow gait of someone for whom walking has become a kind of habit of being upright. He goes to the spring each morning and sits in the steam of it and drinks, from a cup that he brings, some of the water that comes up through the foundation of the granary, and he does not speak to anyone and no one speaks to him. He is not unfriendly. He is past the age of needing to be unfriendly. He has lived long enough here that the city has settled into him as much as he has settled into the city. He is part of the city. He will die and the city will not notice the missing for a few days and then will include him in the rock.

---

The fifteen thousand people.

They are not the same as me.

They are slower. They are softer. Their skin is reddened by the sulfur and the heat and the constant moisture. They have the eyes of people who live where the light is strong and consistent — a kind of constant squint, a kind of constant adjustment. They have the voices of people who speak softly. They have the patience of people whose city is patient. They have the long memories of people whose city has long memories. They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are content in a way that has nothing to do with being content — they are content in the way the basalt is content to be hot, in the way the springs are content to come up.

Their children go down the long road to school in the valley. The children come back. The children have been doing this for several generations. The school in the valley teaches them things they need to know that the city does not, and the city teaches them things they need to know when they come back. They come back. They come back because the city calls them back, or because the valley does not feel right in some way they cannot articulate, or because the city is *for* them in a way that the valley is not. They come back and they take up positions in the turbine halls or the refineries or the fungal farms or the civil administration or the feeding-of-the-things. They take up positions. The positions have been waiting for them.

They marry. They have children. Their children have creatures. The cycle continues.

---

There is one more inhabitant I want to describe, and it is not a person, and it is not a creature, and I will not call it a thing, and I will not call it alive.

The city itself.

The city is alive. I don't mean that the city is sentient, or has thoughts, or has opinions. I mean the city is alive in the way a forest is alive, in the way a reef is alive, in the way a place that has been inhabited continuously for a long time by many things begins to acquire some quality of itself. The city has been patient. The city has been paying attention. The city has been here for centuries and the city will be here for centuries more and the city has settled into a kind of long slow selfhood that is its own kind of inhabitant. The city occupies itself. The city watches itself. The city hums with the sound of its own water and turns with the slow turning of its own machines and glows with the slow glow of its own patterns, and the city is the largest living thing in this place, and it is inhabited by everything that I have just described, and we are all of us inside it.

The creatures are in its eaves. The people are in its streets. The patterns are in its walls. The cats are on its parapets. The fungi are in its vats. The old man is at its spring. The mayor is in his room. The refinery is settling its minerals. The turbines are turning. The road is descending. The water is running down the gutters. The steam is rising. The light is catching the patterns and the patterns are glowing in the dusk, faintly, in the color that is between orange and pale gold, in the time when the basalt gives back the heat of the day.

The city, above the dew-line, on its shelf of hot black stone, is inhabited, by many kinds, by many kinds I have described and many kinds I have not. It is inhabited by the patiences and the moisture and the heat and the long slow attention. It is inhabited and it persists.

I have walked at dusk. I have walked in the time when the patterns glow. I have walked and felt the city watching me back, not alarmed, not unfriendly, just paying attention, in the way that a forest pays attention to the deer that passes through it, in the way the reef pays attention to the fish, in the long slow patient way of a place that has been here and will be here and that I will pass through.

That is what lives there. That is what lives there, plural, layered, patient.
