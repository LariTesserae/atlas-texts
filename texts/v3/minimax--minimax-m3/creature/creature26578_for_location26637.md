What lives there is not a community in any sense a visitor would recognize. There is no greeting, no encounter, no exchange of glances. What lives there is a *standing* — a thing that has decided, in a way that is not a decision, to remain in one place and to let the place pass through it, grain by grain, for as long as it can.

Begin with the mats, because the mats are the floor of the world here. They are the first thing that is not stone, and they are everywhere the wind drops its load. They are pale — the color of old bone, of chalk, of a salt flat at dusk — and they are not, despite everything your eye wants to tell you, soft. Touch one and you would find it drier than you expected, and stiffer, and faintly warm from the light it has been holding all day. It is a thing built out of dust that has agreed to stop being dust. The grains are cemented now into a felt so dense a fingernail would skate across it before finding purchase. It does not grow the way a thing on a wetter world grows. It does not lengthen, or leaf, or put out anything that could be called a season. It *accretes* — it adds a skin of itself, once, and then another, and another, and the older skins are below and the newer ones are above, and the whole mat is in some sense a record of every wind that has passed and dropped what it could not carry. The bottom layer is the oldest, and the bottom layer is also, in a strange way, the most alive, because it is the part still in conversation with the stone. The top layer is the part still in conversation with the air. The middle is the part that has finished being made and is now simply being what it is.

Stand in a mat for a long time and you would begin to feel, through the soles of your boots if you had them, a faint vibration. It is not the wind. It is the mat itself, doing whatever it is the mat does when the light is at the right angle. There is a faint high singing that comes off the older stands at certain hours — a tone so thin it lives more in the teeth than in the ears — and if you put your hand flat against a knuckled column you would feel, very far down, a trembling, as of something that is not asleep and is not awake but is doing a third thing. The locals, if there were locals, would not have a word for it. They would not need a word. They would know it the way a body knows its own pulse.

The columns come later, and they come from the mats, and they come slowly enough that no single one of them has ever been watched into existence. A mat thickens. A mat, in some particular spot where the wind eddies and drops more than it takes, begins to mound. The mound, over decades, becomes a low dome. The dome, over centuries, becomes a knuckled pillar. The pillar, over a span of time that would empty a human patience, branches at the top into a fan of blades — thin, brittle, the color of weak tea or stained paper — and the fan turns, very slightly, to face the prevailing wind, the way a weather vane turns, but with the weight of a building behind it. These are the things that click in the wind. Each blade is a tuning fork of silica and trapped dust, and when the air moves through a stand of them they give back, collectively, a sound like rain heard from inside a dry well, or like a small choir of wine glasses rubbed at the rim. It is the loudest thing the growth makes, and it is not loud at all.

The oldest columns are the lavender ones, and the lavender is not paint. It is something the structure has done to itself, some long slow internal rearrangement of the trace metals it has pulled from the dust over the centuries of its making. A young column is ochre. A middle one is the color of the rock. An old one is the color of a bruise on pale skin, or of the skin of a grape, or of the inside of a shell no one has opened in a long time. The oldest are also the tallest, though the tallest would not reach a child’s shoulder, and they are the most fragile, and they are the ones most likely to be undercut, eventually, by the very mat they grew from, and to topple in a dry pale avalanche that takes a season to settle.

The crawler lives in the mats and is, in its way, the only animal here that the eye would accept as an animal. It is small — the length of a finger, perhaps, or a little less — and it is flat, and it is the color of the mat it lives in, which is to say it is the color of the dust, which is to say it is almost no color at all. It has no legs you would count as legs. It has a way of moving that is more like a question being asked of the surface: a slow forward lean, a pause, a settling, another lean. It feeds on the finest of the trapped dust, the part the mat has not yet cemented into itself, and it moves only when the light is at the angle that warms the surface just enough to lift the dust into a faint drift it can intercept. Most of the day, by this measure, is feeding time. The rest of the day it is somewhere in the cool lower layer of the mat, and you would not know it was there. It does not seem to have eyes. It does not seem to have much of anything that would count as a sense, except a sense of where the dust is, and a sense, perhaps, of when the light is right. It has been here, in some form, for a long time. It will be here, in some form, for a long time after. It is not in any hurry.

The larger thing — the long pale shape that sits in the lee of a tor and looks, until the wind moves it, like a fold of the rock — is harder. It is harder to see and harder to think about and harder, once seen, to put out of the mind. It is longer than the crawler, considerably longer — the length of an arm, maybe, or a little more — and it is thicker through the middle than you would expect a thing to be that lives in a place with so little to eat. It is the same color as the rock, which is to say it has made itself, over whatever span of time it took, into something the eye slides off of. It does not move when you are looking. It moves when you are not, or it does not move at all and what you took for a fold of the rock was always, in some sense, a fold of the rock. There is no way to know. It does not seem to eat. It does not seem to drink, and there is nothing to drink. It does not seem to breathe, and there is almost nothing to breathe. It has the look of a thing that has settled into a place and is simply *occupying* it, the way a shadow occupies a wall, or the way a stone occupies the spot where it was dropped. If it has a life, the life is a long one, and a quiet one, and the only evidence of it is that, in certain light, the thing in the lee of the tor is not quite the same shape as it was the last time you passed. Or you have imagined that. The place encourages that kind of imagining. The place is so quiet, and so empty of quick motion, that the eye invents motion rather than go on seeing none.

The growth itself is the population, if the word can be stretched to fit. It is the dominant fact of the place, the way water is the dominant fact of a reef, or wind the dominant fact of a dune sea. It is not a single organism. It is more like a single conversation, carried on across the surface of the plateau in a million small voices, each voice a mat or a column or a stand, each one in loose chemical contact with its neighbors through the dust that moves between them. When a tor falls, and the fresh face of stone is exposed, the recolonization does not begin at the edges and march inward. It begins everywhere at once, in a thin pale fuzz that is the dust itself learning, in the presence of the new light, to do the thing the dust does when it stops being dust. The fuzz thickens. The fuzz becomes a mat. The mat becomes, in the lee of the new geometry, a grove. The grove will, in a thousand years, look like it has always been there. The place does not remember.

Nothing here is in a hurry because nothing here needs to be. The sun will come up tomorrow the same way it came up today. The wind will drop its load and pick up its load. The dust will move, and the growth will hold what it can, and the slow patient work of turning a plateau into a corrugated thing will go on, and the tors will fall, one by one, over a span of time that makes a human life look like a held breath, and the pale dust will rise, and the brittle blades will click in the lee, and the crawler will lean forward in its slow questioning way across a surface that has not changed in its lifetime, and the long shape in the lee of the tor will be there, or will not be there, and the place will still, in its own long way, be in balance, and the silence will still be the loudest thing in it, and the light will still come down hard on the pale ground, and the small pale fingers of the growth will still be raised to the thin steady air, doing the slow patient work of being what they are, which is the only thing, in the end, that anything here is.
