# What Lives in the Chimney

Something does. The place is not empty. It has not been empty since the second year after the doors were bolted, when the first fern poked a crozier out of a cold seam three hundred feet up and unfurled toward a light it had never seen, only sensed as a difference in humidity, and unfurled anyway.

The chimney is inhabited. Inhabited is not the right word. The chimney is *worked*. Worked the way a hive is worked, the way a tideflat is worked, the way a forest is worked — by every creature in it, against every other, with no one in charge and no one keeping accounts, the books balanced in a million small transactions every second, in the long ledger of the drips.

## The drips

The drips are the first inhabitants. The drips are the only original inhabitants. Everything else is a guest of the drips, or a guest of a guest of the drips, and the drips are a guest of the chimney, and the chimney is a guest of the cloud that passes over the open mouth, and the cloud is a guest of the sea.

The drips begin as vapor. The vapor enters at the top. The vapor is held, for a while, in the warm saturated air of the column, and then it lets go, and what lets go is not a drop but a *choosing* — a molecule of water deciding to leave its sisters and join a surface. It joins the underside of a fern. It joins the rim of a bracket. It joins a ledge where a bat is hanging, and rolls down the fur of the bat's back, and the bat does not wake. It joins a thread of moss. It joins a crack in the concrete and sits in the crack for eleven months before it lets go of that, too, and falls the rest of the way.

A drop, when it lands, is the size of a thumbnail, sometimes, or the size of a coin, or the size of a fist if it has gathered from a wide ledge. It lands on a leaf and the leaf bows. It lands on the sediment and the sediment dimples. It lands on a beetle and the beetle freezes, not because it minds the water but because the dimple in the sediment beside it is the size and shape of a small predator, and the beetle waits to see if the predator will move, and when the dimple does not move the beetle goes on with its line.

A drop lands on the back of your hand and is warm because the air is warm, and the air is warm because the turbine is warm, and the turbine is warm because the turbine is doing the thing it has always done, which is turn. The drop is not rain. Rain is cold and has a parent. The drop is an orphan, distilled, recycled, older than the moss. The drop has been in this chimney a hundred times. The drop has been a fern and a beetle and a bat and a crack in the wall and is now a drop again, and will be a fern again, and the bookkeeping is perfect and no one is keeping it.

## The skin

The first real inhabitants are the things that learned to live on concrete.

They are not many. They are not varied. They are the *first* — the spores that came in on the first rains, on the feet of the first beetles, in the gut of the first bat that flew down the chimney on a dare, in some lost century, and found the seams, and stayed.

The moss is the first. The moss is the foundation. The moss does not have roots. The moss is a felt, a green-black plate of tiny threads pressed into the dust of a cold seam, and it drinks what falls on it, and what falls on it is everything. The moss holds the dust. The moss holds the spores of the ferns. The moss holds, eventually, the small black bodies of the things that died on it, and from those bodies it draws the nitrogen that concrete cannot give it, and the moss grows another millimeter, and the seam is a little fuller, and the next drop falls a little slower.

There are four kinds of moss in the chimney. No one has named them. No one will. They are the chimney's mosses, and they have lived in the chimney for as long as there has been a chimney to live in, and the spores that fall from them do not leave the chimney, because the spores are heavy and the updraft is weak, and so the mosses are becoming, slowly, in the way of mosses, *themselves* — a little different at the top of the column than at the bottom, a little different on the north face than the south, the way a lake has its own trout.

The ferns came after. The ferns are the second empire. The ferns are the body's response to the moss's invitation, and the ferns are large and the ferns are everywhere, and the ferns are the dominant architecture of the place, the way the cathedral's arches are the dominant architecture of a stone church. A fern, here, is ten feet long. A fern, here, has a rhizome the thickness of a forearm pressed into a seam, and the rhizome is older than the chimney, possibly, because the rhizome is a clone of a rhizome of a rhizome, back to the first fern that took the risk. The ferns do not flower. The ferns have not flowered in a long time. The ferns have other things to do.

The fig is the third, and the fig is the strangest. The fig is a single vine that came down from above — a cutting, a seed dropped by a bird, a piece of twig washed in by a flood, no one remembers — and rooted in a seam near the top, and put out a leaf, and the leaf caught a drop, and the drop fed the vine, and the vine grew downward by a foot a year, sending out a single woody stem with small adhesive pads, and the pads held it to the wall, and the stem thickened, and the stem is now, somewhere, as thick as a man's thigh, and the stem has been growing downward for as long as anyone has been counting, and no one is counting. The fig is patient. The fig will reach the floor in a hundred years, perhaps, and when it does, the fig will not stop. The fig will go across the floor, and up the other side, and the chimney will be, in a thousand years, a column of wood, and the concrete will be a memory inside the wood, and the drips will fall on the wood and the wood will drink them, and the chimney will be a tree.

The brackets are last, and the brackets are the bones of the operation. The brackets are fungi — pale, heavy, fan-shaped things that push out of the wall in overlapping shelves, sometimes a yard across, soft on the underside and hard on top, the color of wet paper. The brackets are the chimney's composters. The brackets eat the dead. The brackets eat the fronds that fall from the ferns, the beetles that die on the seams, the guano of the bats that has washed down the wall in some past flood and lodged here, in a seam, and become a stratum, a thin dark line in the pale concrete, the chimney's only record of the years. The brackets turn the dead into soil. The soil is not much — a film, a stain, a few millimeters of dark matter behind a bracket where the spore has held it against the wall — but the soil is enough to feed a fern, and the fern feeds a beetle, and the beetle feeds a frog, and the frog feeds the snake, and the bracket that fed the fern is, in time, eaten by another bracket, and the bookkeeping is perfect and no one is keeping it.

## The floor

The floor was steel. The floor is now a foot of black sediment.

The sediment is the chimney's compost. The sediment is everything that has ever fallen and not been carried away, and nothing has been carried away for a long time, and so the sediment is, by any honest accounting, *the chimney* — a layer of selves, a packed record of every creature that has lived in the column and died in the column, beetle and bat and frond and drop, the whole long recipe of the place laid down in a black wet sheet over the riveted steel that no one has seen in a hundred years and that no one will see again.

The sediment is alive. The sediment is the most alive part of the chimney, because the sediment is where the drips end, and the drips are the food, and the food is the world.

In the sediment are the elongated things, the pale things, the things with too many legs. They are the chimney's worms, or the chimney's silverfish, or the chimney's something, and they are many and they are hidden and they are an inch long, an inch and a half, sometimes two inches, and they are the color of the underside of a fern, and they have no eyes because there is nothing in the sediment to see, only to feel, and they feel the drips, and they feel each other, and they feel the small vibrations of the beetle walking above them, and they wait. When a drop lands, they surface. When the surface is unkind, they go down. They are the floor's secret. They are the floor's most numerous citizen, and no one has ever seen one and will not see one now.

The frogs are above the sediment, on the leaves, and the frogs are small and bright and the color of a cut leaf, which is to say the color of a thing that wants to be a leaf, and the frogs are not moving, and the frogs are not moving because the frogs are waiting for a beetle, and the beetle is a yard away, and the beetle is on a line, and the line is a pheromone trail, and the line is the beetle's certainty, and the frog is the beetle's uncertainty, and the frog is more patient. The frogs are not many. The frogs are not few. The frogs are enough.

The beetles are many. The beetles are the chimney's commuters. The beetles walk the seams of the steel plate, where the sediment is thinnest, and the beetles walk in lines, and the lines are old, and the lines are the same lines that beetles have walked for generations, and the beetles are black and lacquered and an inch long, and they are looking for something, and what they are looking for is a thing that has died, because a thing that has died is a thing that can be carried, and a thing that can be carried is a thing that can be taken to a bracket, and a bracket that has been given a thing will let the beetle live in the bracket, and the bracket is warm. The beetles are the chimney's undertakers. The beetles are the chimney's postmen. The beetles carry the dead from where they fell to where they will be eaten, and this is a long walk, sometimes, and the beetle does not hurry.

The snake is one. The snake is one, or the snake is two, and no one knows which because no one has counted, and the snake is long and the color of a wet frond stem, and the snake is drawn across a gap between two ferns once, and then the snake is not there, and the snake will be there again, in a different gap, in a different year. The snake eats the frogs. The snake is the reason the frogs are not many. The snake is the chimney's ceiling, in the way that a hawk is a field's ceiling, and the snake is the only thing in the chimney that has a backbone, and the snake is a wonder, and the snake is not seen.

## The upper third

The upper third is the air. The upper third is where the drips are born. The upper third is where the light comes in.

The light comes in as a circle, far above, the color of a clouded sky. The light is the same light that falls on any forest clearing at midday. The light enters at the top and falls down the inside of the stack and is filtered, as it falls, by the humid air and by the bodies of the things in the air, and the light is gray-green by the time it reaches the floor, and the light is moving slightly, the way light moves under deep water, and the moving is the shadows of the things in the upper third, drifting, drifting.

The things in the upper third are the fauna. The fauna are not the floor's fauna. The fauna are larger. The fauna have wings.

The bats are the first. The bats are the chimney's most numerous citizen, and the bats are not one kind of bat but several, and the bats hang from the underside of every ledge and from the woody stem of the fig and from the cracks in the concrete where the cracks have widened enough to hold a bat, and the bats are folded into themselves, silent, a dense living wallpaper pressed into the wall. The bats are sleeping. The bats are not asleep — a bat that is asleep is a bat that has fallen — but the bats are quiet, and the bats are folded, and the bats are saving themselves for the dusk. At dusk the mouth of the chimney opens onto a dusk the same color as the inside, and the bats pour upward in a long dark river, ascending through the column of light and out into the canopy, and the bats are gone, and the wall is bare, and the wall is the wall again. At dawn the bats return, and the bats pour down the column of light in a long dark waterfall, folding themselves back into the wall, and the wall is the fauna again, and the sound of the returning is a second sound beneath the turbine, a continuous soft rustling, silk on stone, a sound that comes from above and never stops. The bats are the chimney's clock. The bats are the chimney's calendar. The bats go out when it is dark and come back when it is light, and the chimney's dim middle knows the time by the bats, and the floor's small things adjust their affairs to the bats' going and coming, and the beetles wait until the bats are gone to cross the open stretches, and the snake waits until the bats are back to move along the wall, because the snake does not want to be seen by the things that see by sound, and the bats see by sound.

The other things are not bats. The other things hang among the bats, longer in the limb, with wings that are not membrane but a translucent stretched skin, pale, veined, that catches the down-falling light when they shift. The other things are not many. The other things do not move during the day. The other things pour upward with the bats at dusk, in their own slow current, and the other things pour downward with the bats at dawn, and the other things are the chimney's secret, and no one has named them, and no one will. The other things are the chimney's second fauna. The other things are the chimney's answer to the question the bats have not asked.

## The sound

The sound is the turbine.

The sound is below hearing. The sound is not a roar. The sound is a single sustained low frequency that the ears stop hearing after a few minutes and the body begins to feel — in the soles of the feet, in the bridge of the nose, in the small of the back. The chimney resonates. The rebar inside the concrete has learned the frequency and sings it back, a sympathetic wobble that wanders by a fraction of a hertz as the temperature changes, as the load on the turbine shifts imperceptibly, as another section of rebar rusts through and the weight of the wall redistributes.

The pitch is not constant. The pitch is very, very slowly changing. The pitch is changing because the rebar is rusting, and the rust is taking up more space than the steel, and the rust is pressing the concrete outward, and the concrete is giving, a little, and the resonance is changing with the giving, and the resonance is the chimney's voice, and the voice is getting, over decades, a little lower, and the lower is the sound of the chimney's slow collapse, and the collapse will not happen in any time that anyone will stand here to see it, and the sound is the only record.

The sound has been running longer than the moss has been growing. The sound is older than the fig. The sound is the foundation. The sound is the thing that was here first, and the sound is the thing that will be here last, and the sound is the thing that everything else has arranged itself around, the way coral arranges itself around a current, the way moss arranges itself around a stone, and the sound is the chimney's first citizen, and the sound has no name.

## The breath

The chimney breathes. The breath is not the turbine. The breath is something else. The breath is the chimney's second sound, and the breath is above the turbine, and the breath is the slow expansion of the air inside the column as the sun warms the wall and the wall warms the air and the air rises and the air is replaced by air that comes in at the bottom, through the cracks in the bolted doors, through the gaps in the seals, through the small places where the concrete has pulled away from the steel, and the air that comes in is the air of the outside, and the air of the outside is cooler, and the air of the outside has a different smell, and the smell of the outside enters the chimney in a slow current and mixes with the warm saturated air and is breathed out, in a long pale plume, through the open mouth five hundred feet above, and the plume is visible at dawn, sometimes, when the air is cold, and the plume is the chimney's exhalation, and the exhalation is what the canopy sees, and the canopy knows the chimney is alive because of the plume, and the canopy is wrong about what is alive in the chimney, and the canopy does not care, and the plume goes up, and the plume is a cloud, and the cloud is the chimney's weather, and the weather is local, and the weather is the chimney's own.

The breath carries the spores. The breath carries the dust. The breath carries, sometimes, a beetle, lifted from a ledge and carried upward in the slow column and out into the canopy, and the beetle does not survive the canopy, and the beetle's body is, eventually, rained back into the chimney, and the body is taken by a bracket, and the bracket grows, and the breath is the chimney's circulation, and the circulation is imperfect, and the imperfection is the chimney's only leak.

## The drop on the back of the hand

The drop is warm.

The drop is the size of a thumbnail, or the size of a coin, or the size of a fist. The drop fell from a ledge three feet above, or from a fern, or from a bracket, or from a bat's back, or from a crack in the concrete, or from the open mouth five hundred feet above. The drop has a long history. The drop is the chimney's only conversation with you. The drop is the chimney's way of saying, *you are standing in the middle of something that does not need you, and the something is not hostile, and the something is not indifferent, and the something has not noticed you, and the something will not notice you, and the something will go on without you, and the something is a slow machine, and the slow machine is, in the way that all slow machines are, a kind of patience, and the patience is the drips, and the drips are the breath, and the breath is the sound, and the sound is the chimney, and the chimney is, in the end, a place, and the place is inhabited, and the inhabitants are many, and the many are the drips.*

The drop is warm.

You are standing in the chimney. The chimney is standing in the earth. The earth is standing in the weather. The weather is standing in the season. The season is standing in the year. The year is standing in the long slow rusting of the rebar, and the rusting is the chimney's only clock, and the clock is not a clock but a *working*, and the working is the place, and the place is the drips, and the drips are the place, and you are standing in the middle of it, and a drop falls on the back of your hand, warm, and the drop is the chimney's only word, and the word is *continue*.
