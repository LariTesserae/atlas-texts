What lives at Beryl Bend is, in the way that the word wants to mean it, almost nothing.

There are no birds. There are no insects. There are no fish breaking the surface with their small silver presences, no frogs in the cracked crust of pale mud, no shrews threading the catkins, no slow worm under the slabs of the broken path. The dome and the marsh hold no fauna, and you notice this, and then you cannot stop noticing it, and the noticing itself becomes a kind of evidence that the place has its own population, only not the kind that moves.

By other measures, the place is full. By the measure of what has stayed, what has made the place its shape and habit, Beryl Bend is populous the way an old house is populous: not with the living but with the long echo of how the living arrange themselves.

---

The housing squats at the center of the dome and is the most concentrated of the residents.

It is brick, knee-high, with a rusted iron cap and three runs of pipe the size of a wrist and a steel chimney pointing up like a finger. From a distance it does not look like anything alive. From a distance it looks like a small forgotten shrine, a mark on a map that nobody has redrawn. But it is squatting the way hares squat, the way toads squat, with the economy of a thing that has made its decision about where it intends to be and will not be moved from it. It has been here longer than the reeds. It has been here longer than the mud. It is the oldest thing in the landscape except the basalt, and the basalt is older only by a margin you could not measure without instruments of the kind the housing itself is.

It is not, despite the name on the enamel sign, an engine. It is a lung. It is a stethoscope. It is the kind of thing you build when you want to listen to something that is too large or too deep or too patient to be brought to the surface to be questioned, and so you bring the question to it instead.

The chimney does not smoke. The pipes do not, from a distance, appear to be doing anything. If you sit with the housing long enough, though, you begin to feel that something is being done — a slow, patient throb in the pipes, a rise and fall that has more in common with breath than with any mechanical process you have a name for. In, out. In, out. The rhythm is not the rhythm of a machine that has been switched on. It is the rhythm of a thing that has been breathing for a long time and intends to continue breathing for a long time, and the housing is only the visible side of a body whose lungs are mostly elsewhere.

You begin to feel the breathing in your ankles before you hear it. It comes up through the basalt as a low hum, a hum that is not quite a hum, a presence that is just on the wrong side of being a sound. You could time your breath to it. You find yourself trying. The marsh answers it, the water level lifting and resettling by a margin so small that you only know it is happening because the reeds nearest the dome lean, very slightly, away and then back, away and then back.

The housing knows you are here. This is the strange part.

The way it knows is through the rock. You are a small weight on a small area of basalt, and the basalt transmits the weight downward to whatever is listening below, and the listening is recorded as a perturbation, as a small angular deflection that will be drawn on a chart that has not been drawn yet. The housing is not curious about you. The housing is *attentive* to you, in the way a tape recorder left running in an empty room is attentive. You are being noted. You will be kept.

For the next several hours the pipes will hum a little differently, accounting for you.

---

The bore itself lives below.

You do not see it. The pipes dive into the rock and you cannot follow them down. The boreholes went somewhere — went down, went through the basalt, through whatever lies under the basalt, into a depth that the place has not chosen to advertise. The bore is listening to the rock and the water and the heat, and to the slow negotiation between them, and what it hears it sends back up through the pipes as a patient pressure that the housing shapes into a record.

The thing being listened to is not a thing, exactly. It is a condition. It is the slow conversation between a deep body of water and a body of heat, the kind of conversation that takes decades to say anything and centuries to say anything else. The water is hot. The rock is hot. There is a frontier somewhere down there where the two are deciding which of them is which, and the bore is sitting at the frontier the way a translator sits at the frontier of two languages, repeating back whatever either side has managed to say.

The water that comes back up — and it does come back up, very slowly, in the threads of condensation on the eastern face — is not quite the water that went down. It has acquired something in transit: a faint copper-green, the color of the verdigris on the lead flange, the color of something dissolved on the way and brought up again as a small cargo of memory. The dome weeps this water. The dome has been weeping this water for a long time. The water is, in its way, a letter. The dome is a mailbox. You have read the outside of the envelope but not the letter inside.

The thing down there is not alive in the way you are alive. It is alive in the way a tide is alive, in the way a season is alive, in the way the slow turning of a deep stratum is alive. It will be here when you are not. It was here when whoever wrote *Schedule subject to review at Main* was not. It has its own patience, which is larger than the housing's patience, which is larger than the marsh's patience, which is already larger than yours.

---

The condensation lives on the surface of the basalt and is the loudest thing.

It is not loud in your ears. It is loud in the whole field of your attention. It moves in slow, patient threads down the stone faces, never quite dripping, never quite hurrying, glazing each surface a darker grey before evaporating somewhere partway down. The threads are not the same from moment to moment. They thicken. They thin. They find new paths down the old fracture lines. They cross each other at the bright seams of glassy inclusion and pool for a moment in the small concavities before resuming their downward, evaporative argument with the rock.

You could mistake the condensation for nothing. You could mistake it for a kind of weather, a local accident of humidity and temperature that does not deserve a name. But it has a name. It is weeping. The dome is weeping, and the weeping has been going on for a long time, and the weeping is the loudest sound in a place with no other sound, and the weeping is older than the heron's feather and the fish skull and the enamel sign and the broken slabs of the path and the cast-iron mooring ring holding nothing at the foot of the approach.

The condensation is the dome's way of staying in conversation with the air. The air is mostly indifferent to the dome. The dome is mostly indifferent to the air. But the condensation is the place where the two indifferences negotiate, and the negotiation produces a slow wet shine on the basalt that catches the light and winks like the eyes of a thousand flies.

---

The basalt lives as a body.

It is hot, and the heat is not a heat you suffer; it is a heat that surrounds you, a heat that is *you* if you stay long enough. Your shadow on the rock is sharp but short, because the sun has not committed to being anything, and your palm on the brick of the housing cannot, after a moment, tell which surface is warmer. The basalt and the brick have come to the same temperature, the way two bodies in the same room come to the same temperature, the way two long companions come to the same opinion without either of them having said so.

The basalt has fracture lines. You can read the place where it was born: a seam of upthrust stone, cooled too fast, full of glassy inclusions that catch the light and wink. It has the quality of a thing that has been *here* longer than anything else has, and is not in any hurry. The heat is its memory of being born. The weeping is its memory of cooling. The fracture lines are its memory of the cooling being too fast. It carries all of these and does not put them down.

When you stand on the basalt you are standing on a body. You are standing on something that is the same temperature as you, that has been here longer than you, that will be here after you. The body does not mind you. The body pays you no further attention. The body has a long quiet business to attend to and you are a small warm thing at the margin of that business.

---

The marsh lives as a quilt.

It is patient and slow and it has been eating back the concrete path for a long time. The path was once a proper approach, three slabs of concrete laid down by someone who expected the marsh to behave, and the marsh did not behave, and the slabs have tilted one by one into the wade-depths and ended. The marsh does not apologize. The marsh does not hurry. The marsh will eventually have all of the path and then it will be done with the path and will begin on the stub of post that holds the mooring ring, and then on the mooring ring itself, and then on the housing at the center, and the housing will outlast the marsh because the housing was made to outlast the marsh, but the marsh will try.

The reeds are sparse. This is not a hostile absence. It is a thin and brittle presence, the land being tired rather than dead. A thousand tiny failures of growth read as the marsh being tired. The smell is warm, vegetal, slightly sweet: standing water in the process of becoming more interesting than it was. The marsh is becoming more interesting than it was. The marsh is becoming peat, eventually, and then coal, eventually, and then something else, eventually. The marsh is patient the way coal is patient.

The reeds nearest the dome lean, very slightly, away. They lean away because the dome is hot, and the hot air rising off the basalt disturbs the small conversation the reeds are having with the rest of the marsh. The reeds lean away as a kind of courtesy. They lean away the way a crowd leans away from someone who is humming to themselves. The reeds are not threatened. The reeds are making room.

---

A human being lived here, once, in the sense that a human being left a hand-painted line on the enamel sign.

The first two lines of the sign are official: black letters on white enamel, sharp, a constellation of rust blossoms beginning to eat the surface. *BERYL BEND BORE — INSTRUMENT HOUSING 4 — DO NOT INTERFERE WITH VENT. Schedule subject to review at Main. Inquiries to 14, Subbasin Office, by appointment only.* The first two lines are the language of an institution. They speak of housing, of vents, of offices, of appointments. They speak of a place that is administered by people who administer it from somewhere else.

The third line is not the language of an institution. The third line is hand-painted, in a different blue, clearly older, clearly added by a different hand: *its listening don't shout near the pipes*. No period. No signature. The possessive pronoun is the first thing you notice. *Its.* The sign-writer addressed the instrument. The sign-writer was concerned for the wellbeing of the instrument. The sign-writer knew the pipes don't like noise, and wrote this knowledge down in a hand that has not been here for a long time, and left it on a sign that has been rusting for a long time.

The sign-writer is a presence in the place now. The sign-writer is the way the place holds the people who have been here and are not here anymore. They are in the broken path. They are in the mooring ring holding nothing. They are in the appointments that have not been kept and the inquiries that have not been made. They are in the third line of the sign, written by a hand that cared about a thing that breathes, in a place where caring about a thing that breathes is the most natural thing in the world to do.

The visitor lives here too, now, for as long as the visitor stays.

The visitor is a perturbation. A small one. A noted one. The instrument is making a small angular deflection on a chart that has not been drawn yet, and the chart will be filed in an office that the visitor has not visited, and the deflection will be averaged out over the next several hours, and the visitor will leave, and the chart will remain.

The visitor is the most temporary resident. The visitor is the only resident who is aware of being a resident. The visitor stands on the basalt and feels the heat and hears the breathing and notices the absence of fauna and cannot stop noticing it, and the noticing is the visitor's whole life in the place.

---

The light lives here, too, in its slow attendance.

It is the kind of light that has not decided to be anything yet: the mid-morning of a place that does not really have mornings, only a long brightening and a long dimming and a long dim brightness between. The light swings across the dome as the sun crosses a sky that is clouding and unclouding in long, indecisive drifts. The light is the slowest resident. The light is the one that is always leaving and always arriving.

The light touches the glassy inclusions and makes them wink. The light touches the copper-green threads on the eastern face and makes them briefly bright. The light touches the enamel sign and does not illuminate it, because the sign is not the kind of thing that wants to be illuminated. The light touches the heron's feather and the fish skull and the cracked concrete slabs and the cast-iron mooring ring, and finds them all to be in their places, and is satisfied, and moves on.

---

You are at Beryl Bend.

Nothing has happened for a long time. Nothing is about to happen. The instrument is patient. You should be too.

What lives here is what stays: the basalt, the housing, the pipes, the deep body of water and heat, the slow weeping of the condensation, the marsh eating its long meal, the reeds leaning their courtesy, the light swinging its slow arc, the chart that has not been drawn, the third line of the sign, the visitor's small perturbation slowly being averaged out into the long patient record of the place.

What lives here does not require you to notice it. What lives here will continue to live here whether you notice it or not. What lives here has been living here longer than the word *here* has meant anything, and will live here after the word has stopped meaning anything, and the pipes will throb, and the weeping will wax, and the mooring ring will hold nothing, and the heron's feather will not move, and the sign will remain unilluminated in the bright indifferent light, and the place will hold you gently and pay you no further attention, and you will leave, and the place will not mind that you have left, and the place will go on being the place, which is what it has always been, which is what it will always be, which is patient, and quiet, and full of nothing, and full of everything, and listening.
