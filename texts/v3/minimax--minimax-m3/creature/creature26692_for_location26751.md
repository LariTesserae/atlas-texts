The people of the Hollow Reach are not tall. They are broad, low to the ground, built with the patience of the place itself — wide shoulders that slope forward into thick arms, hands that are flatter than you would expect, the fingers splayed wide and tipped with pads of darker skin that are less for gripping than for feeling. Their legs are shorter than their torsos would suggest, and they walk with a slight bow, a permanent inclination toward the earth, as though they are always listening to it with their knees. Their skin is the deep plum-grey of the dim light itself, almost translucent at the thin places — the inner forearms, the soft underside of the jaw — where the dim glow of their own blood shows through. Their hair is fine and pale, almost colorless, and it grows long and is worn pushed back and matted loosely with the oils of the moss, so that in the low light it looks like a continuation of the growth above them.

They are quiet. Not silent — never silent — but quiet in the way the chorus is quiet, which is to say that any sound they make is offered to the whole. They hum, mostly. The hum is not a melody. It is more like a position, a steady low note held in the chest that they keep for hours at a time and which varies with what they are feeling, dropping lower when something is settled, rising a quarter-tone when something is new. When they wish to speak to a particular other, they do not speak to the ear. They speak to the ground. They press a hand flat to the warm earth of a clearing and the other hand to the shoulder of the one they wish to reach, and the vibration passes through bone and through soil and is received by the one standing nearby with bare feet on the same patch of ground. In this way a conversation can be had across a gathering without anyone speaking aloud. A mother and child across a room can feel each other's presence through the floor. Two people deciding something important will sit knee to knee, hands on each other's forearms, and the resolution will arrive in both bodies at the same moment and be recognized by a small simultaneous exhalation.

They live in the long now. A child is born small and is set down on the warm earth of a clearing and is left there for the first hours of its life, and its first sensation is the hum of the gathering around it and the warmth of the soil and the slow passing of the ground-walkers' continuous migration beneath the moss. A newborn in the Reach is soothed not by being held but by being placed. The earth takes them in. The moss closes around them as they grow. Their first year is mostly lying on the ground feeling things. Their second year is reaching. By the third they are crawling. By the fifth or sixth they are walking. By the tenth they are useful, in the way that the Reach measures usefulness, which is mostly a matter of being a steady presence in a particular place — a pair of hands at a doorway that knows how wide to keep the opening, a back at a clearing that knows when the moss needs holding back, a chest that knows the right hum for a particular moment.

They eat the fruiting bodies of the moss, the pale things that release their spores. They eat them when they are fresh and the spores have not yet left, and the spores settle in the gut and pass through and take root in the warm waste that is, itself, set carefully into the moss at the edges of the clearings to feed the new growth. Nothing in the Reach is excreted and forgotten. Everything returns. The eaters and the eaten are in continuous slow exchange.

They die as the columns die — not all at once, but by degrees, the breath slowing, the hum settling lower and lower until it becomes a vibration that is felt only by those sitting close. When a person dies, the body is not removed. It is laid in a hollow of the moss, and the moss is encouraged to close over it, and the body becomes a small rise in the carpet, and the rise is visited. The ancestors sit on these rises, the way they sit on the old sealed buildings. Sometimes a child will sit on an ancestor-mound and feel, through the warmth of the earth beneath, a faint returning hum, the way that a sealed building will continue to vibrate faintly to the sound of the gathering around it for decades after it has been closed.

The Reach-dwellers do not count themselves as separate from the place. When asked, by some hypothetical asking, who they are, they would not answer with a name. They would answer with a location. *I am the one who sits at the third clearing from the column with the amber-stain.* *I am the one whose doorway is to the west of the broad-walker's looping path.* They are known by their positions in the texture, by the shape of the absence they make in the chorus when they step away from it. Their names — and they have names, kept in the family hums and the gatherings — are less important than the clearing they tend, the doorway they keep, the clearing-mound where their second-eldest ancestor rests.

When one of them walks through the settlement, they move with the slow confidence of something that has been walking the same paths for a hundred generations. Their feet know where the moss is thinner. Their feet know where the ground-walkers' trails run, and they will pause at a crossing to let a column of the small ground-dwellers pass, standing motionless until the piping migration completes itself. They step around the bright trail left by the column-dweller, because the trail is still alive and the moss beneath it is still tender. They navigate the architecture of the Reach the way you navigate a familiar room in the dark — by the memory of the body, by the warmth of particular patches, by the small differences in the hum of the ground.

They have known for a long time that they are not alone in the world, that there are places above and beyond the Reach, but the knowledge does not trouble them. They are not waiting for anything. They are not preparing for anything. They are simply here, in the long middle of a becoming that began before they had words for it and will continue after the last of them has been folded back into the moss. The dim light shifts over them. The chorus holds them. The earth, which has been warm since before they can remember, continues to be warm beneath them, and they continue to be warm upon it, and the breath passes between them in an exchange so steady and so continuous that there is no point at which one ends and the other begins.

A child sits on an ancestor-mound at the edge of a clearing and feels, through the small bones of her pelvis, the low hum of the gathering happening across the Reach, and she is content. She does not know she is content. She does not have a word for it. The word for it, if there were one, would be the hum itself, the low steady note that she is already beginning to keep in her own chest, the note that will deepen with her as she grows, that will find its proper place in the chorus when she is old enough to join a gathering and press her hand to the warm earth and feel her own hum return to her from the others. The moss breathes around her. The spores drift. The light, almost imperceptibly, shifts toward amber, and then back toward green, and then toward grey, and the shifting is so slow that only someone watching for a hundred years would notice it as motion at all.
