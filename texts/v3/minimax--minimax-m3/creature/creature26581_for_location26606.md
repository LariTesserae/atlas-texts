# What Lives There

The life of the plateau is not loud. The life of the plateau is barely there at all, and has been barely there for what you would have to call geological time. But it is there. If you kneel — and you would have to kneel, and stay kneeling for a while — you would find it.

## The Crust Things

The lichens are the first to admit they exist.

They are black, mostly. Some have a faint olive cast, some a faint rust. They grow on the stone the way a scab grows on a wound — not because they choose the stone, but because the stone is what is available. They are flat. They are pressed against the rock as if they are trying to become the rock, or the rock is trying to become them. Their edges are not edges. They fade. They thin into a powder that is just lichen, just dust, just a chemical possibility that didn't quite commit.

They grow very slowly. You would not see them grow in a day. You would not see them grow in a year, probably. A researcher would have to mark them and come back. A researcher would have to come back many times. A researcher would have to come back across a span longer than a career. The crust things are not in a hurry. They have decided that being in a hurry is not what they are for.

A lichen here is a negotiation. It is a fungus, and it is something else — an alga, a bacterium, perhaps a third party that has not yet been classified because nobody has yet been able to bring a sample back to a place with the equipment to classify it. They pool their chemistries. The fungus gives structure. The alga, in the brief hours when the temperature swings and a faint dew forms, does something with light. The bacterium does something with the air, which is mostly nitrogen and very little else. Between them, they make the lichen. The lichen makes more lichen, but only barely.

Some of the crust things have a metallic sheen. You see it when the light hits them at a low angle. It is not a trick of the light. It is actual metal, worked into their tissues — iron, maybe, or manganese, or something the periodic table would have to make room for. The lichens are mining the stone, very slowly. They are eating the plateau, particle by particle, and incorporating what they eat into themselves. They are, in a real sense, the plateau remembering that it was once something other than a plateau.

They are old. Individual colonies might be thousands of years old. Some might be older. There is no way to tell. They do not have rings. They do not have any kind of year-mark. They just have thickness, and an older colony is a thicker colony, but thickness is also a function of where you are on the colony and what the stone underneath was like. A lichen on the plateau is its own archive, and the archive is not easily read.

## The Tufts

The things that are almost moss but are not moss are stranger.

They grow in the lower places, where a faint moisture collects and is reluctant to leave. They form small hummocks, no higher than your thumb. From a distance they look like moss. From a distance, only. Up close you see that they are made of tiny, dark, stiff filaments — wires, almost, but organic. They have a sheen. They catch the thin light and give it back as something slightly different.

What they are doing is gathering water. The filaments are arranged in a way that channels the rare dew down into the heart of the hummock, where it is stored in some kind of spongy matrix. The tuft is a water-collecting device. It is a cistern. It is also, probably, alive.

You can pull a tuft apart. If you pulled it apart you would find it is layered — a crusty outside, a moist interior, a small dark center that is doing something with the moisture that has been collected. The center is not a plant. The center is not an animal. The center is not a fungus. It is a small dark thing that is doing its metabolism, which is slow, and which is based on what is available, which is not much.

The tufts are more common than they look. They are common in the sense that there are a lot of them. They are uncommon in the sense that you can walk a long way without seeing one. They are spaced out in a way that suggests competition. Each tuft has a territory. Each tuft draws down the moisture in its territory. If two tufts are too close, one of them does not do well. They are polite about it. They do not fight. They just exist at a distance that is mutually sustainable.

## The Small Dark Movers

And then there is the thing that is most like an animal.

It is small. It is dark. It is low to the ground, and it moves by repositioning itself rather than by running. You see one and you think: a beetle. You think: a spider. You think: a small dark thing with too many legs or too few. But when you look closer it is not quite any of those. It has too many legs, or its legs are not quite legs, or its body is not quite a body. It is segmented, but the segments are not crisp. It looks like a thing that has been simplified. It looks like a thing that has been reduced to the minimum viable version of itself.

It is not fast. If you approached it, it would pause. It would pause for a long time. It might pause for so long that you would think it had died, or that it had been a stone all along, and then it would move again. It would move a little. It would reposition itself. It would go on doing what it was doing before you interrupted it, which was not much.

What it eats, if it eats, is unclear. It might eat the lichens. It might eat the tufts. It might eat something else, something that is too small to see, something that lives in the cracks of the stone. It might eat dust. It might eat the metallic aftertaste in the air. It might not eat in any way that you would recognize as eating. It might just absorb.

It does not panic. That is the part that stays with you. It does not panic because panicking is not in its repertoire. It does not have a repertoire for panicking. It has a repertoire for repositioning, slowly, and for continuing.

If you watched one for a very long time — for a day, for two days — you might see it find another of its kind. They might pause near each other. They might do something that is not quite meeting and not quite ignoring each other. Then they would go their separate ways. They are not social. They are not solitary, either. They are something else. They are something that has not yet decided, or that decided a long time ago and has not revisited the decision.

They are old. The species is old. The lineage might be continuous back to a time when the plateau was different. The individual is not necessarily old — you cannot tell. An individual might live a year. An individual might live a century. The lineage has been here for longer than the structures. The lineage was here before the structures started being busy. The lineage watched the structures start being busy and decided that being busy was not its business.

## The Slow Movers

There are larger things, but you have to wait for them.

A stone that is not quite a stone. A patch of dark ground that is slightly the wrong shape. A thing that looks like a rock until you look at it across an hour and realize it has shifted. These are the slow movers. They might be animals. They might be plants. They might be something that does not have a category.

They are slow on a scale that makes the small dark movers look frantic. They move in centimeters per year, maybe. They move in millimeters per year, maybe. They are so slow that you would have to come back to the same spot, repeatedly, with marked stones and a notebook, to be sure they were moving at all.

What they eat is the lichen. What they eat is the tuft. What they eat is the stone, particle by particle, the way the lichen eats the stone. They are patient. They are not predators. They are grazers, on a timescale that makes a glacier look impulsive.

You see one and you might not see one. You might walk the plateau for days and not encounter a slow mover. Then you would see one — a low dark form against the dark ground — and you would not be sure it was alive. It would not move while you watched. It would not move for as long as you watched. But it would feel slightly wrong, the way a thing feels when it is not quite part of the stone but not quite separate from it either. The air above it might feel slightly different. The light might fall slightly differently.

If you came back in a year it would be a few centimeters away from where it was. If you came back in a decade it would be a few meters away. If you came back in a century it would be somewhere else, and you would not be able to tell if it was the same one. You would not be able to tell because it would not look any older. It does not look like it ages. It looks like it *is*.

## The Things in the Air

The thin air is not empty.

There are particles in it that might be spores. There are particles in it that might be dormant stages of things that are dormant most of the time. There are things that drift, very slowly, on the rare occasions when there is any wind at all. You would not see them. You would breathe them and not know.

What they are is unclear. They are too small to be the small dark movers. They are too small to be tufts or lichens. They are something that lives between the grains of the world — in the air, in the dust, in the rare moisture.

They might be the dispersed stages of the larger things. They might be the way the plateau reproduces itself, in a slow rain of almost-nothing that drifts from one place to another and then, occasionally, becomes a lichen, or a tuft, or a slow mover, or something else. The plateau is generous in this way. It casts its life onto the wind, very lightly, and trusts the wind to do something with it.

The wind does very little. The wind is mostly the suggestion of wind. But a suggestion, occasionally, is enough.

## The Things in the Wrongness

And then there are the things that live in the field of the structures.

You would not see them at first. You would not see them at all unless you were looking, and you would not be looking unless you knew. They are the things that live in the wrongness around the columns and the wedges and the broad low things. They are the things that have adapted to the physics that the structures are doing. They are the things that have made a home out of the disturbance.

What they look like is hard to say. They might look like stains on the stone. They might look like a discoloration. They might look like nothing. They might be there and not be there, the way the structures are sometimes there and sometimes not quite there.

They are not afraid of the structures. The structures are not afraid of them. They have come to some kind of arrangement. The things in the wrongness might feed on whatever the structures are giving off. They might be the only things that can metabolize what the structures produce. They might be part of the structures, in some sense — extensions, appendages, dependents.

If you stood near a structure and felt the wrongness in your teeth, and the faint happening beneath your feet, you would be standing in the territory of these things. They would be there, around you, doing what they do. You would not see them. You would not feel them. They would be the silent partners of the busy structures, taking what the structures make and turning it into whatever they are.

They are the plateau's response to the structures. They are what grew when the structures started being busy. They are the niche the structures created, and they have filled it completely.

## What the Life Does

The life of the plateau is patient.

The life of the plateau does not compete in the way you are used to competition. There is not enough of anything here to compete for. There is not enough water. There is not enough nitrogen. There is not enough warmth. The life of the plateau divides what is available very carefully. The lichens take the rock. The tufts take the moisture. The small dark movers take the lichens and the tufts, or take whatever else is small enough to be taken. The slow movers take the patience. The things in the wrongness take what the structures give off. The things in the air take the chance of wind.

None of it is wasted. A dead lichen becomes part of the stone that another lichen will feed on. A dead tuft releases its stored moisture, slowly, into the lower places where new tufts will grow. A dead small dark mover — if they die, if death is a concept that applies — becomes something that other things will use. The plateau is a closed economy. The plateau is a closed economy that has been running for an extremely long time, and that does not have any imports, and that does not have any exports, and that does not have a surplus.
