# The Inheritors of the Condensate

They have no name. There is no one left to name them, and they do not name themselves — naming requires a separation between the namer and the named, a hand reaching out across a distance, and there is no distance here. The distance collapsed a long time ago. What remains are only the ones who remained.

You might, in your first hour, mistake them for features of the place. Many who arrive at the rim make this mistake. They see the small pale things in the steam and think they are mineral, or mechanical, or some optical trick of the haze. They walk past them. They set up their instruments. They take their readings. They leave. They write in their reports that the basin is uninhabited. They are wrong, but not in a way that can be disproved by pointing.

The ones who live here are small. They are pale. These are the only two things that can be said about almost all of them without qualification. The third thing — the thing that makes them difficult — is that they are not one kind of thing. They are many kinds, and the kinds are not stable.

---

## The Drifters

In the open vents, where the steam columns rise cleanest and hottest, you will find the Drifters. They are the ones travelers sometimes see and almost never catch. Long. Thin. Translucent in the way that jellyfish are translucent — not see-through, but *luminous with what is behind them*, the steam showing through their bodies in a way that makes their outlines uncertain. Their edges ripple. They have no faces, or they have too many faces — the front of a Drifter, if you were close enough to inspect, is a slowly rotating field of dark spots that may be eyes or may be thermoreceptors or may be something this paragraph has no English for. They do not turn toward you when you look at them. They turn toward heat. You are less interesting than the vent.

The Drifters are not individuals. Or — they are individuals the way a wave is an individual: a coherent structure that maintains itself for a while, then loses coherence, then is gone. A Drifter forms in the steam column when conditions are right. It persists for weeks, or months, or years. It drifts. It feeds on something in the steam — dissolved minerals, perhaps, or the particular chemistry of superheated vapor against volcanic glass. Then, one morning, it comes apart. Its substance rejoins the column. Two weeks later, a new Drifter forms, very near the same place, made of very similar stuff, behaving in a very similar way. The question of whether it is *the same* Drifter is not a question the place has the apparatus to ask.

There are perhaps two hundred of them at any given time. No one has counted. Counting requires standing in the steam long enough to mark individuals, and the steam is not kind to standing.

The Drifters are the most visible of the inhabitants because they are the ones in the open air. Everything else in the basin is smaller, or lower, or further from where the steam rises.

---

## The Crawlers

Closer to the ground — in the cool wet channels between vents, in the sweating cracks of the basalt, along the lower courses of the central tower where the mineral rind has built up into coral-like shelves — there are the Crawlers.

The Crawlers are what the Drifters are not. They are opaque. They are dense. They are shaped like a thing that has chosen a shape and kept it. Most of them are flat, and round, and no larger than your palm, and they move by rippling — the underside of their bodies producing a slow muscular wave that pushes them across the wet stone. They are pale in the way that grubs are pale: the pink-white of something that has never needed pigment. They have no visible eyes. They find their way by some other sense — the warmth of the stone, perhaps, or the chemistry of the condensate.

They eat the rind. This is the closest thing the basin has to a food chain. The Crawlers rasp the mineral accretion with a mouth on their underside that has no teeth but has something more efficient than teeth — a slow chemical hunger that dissolves the rind and absorbs it. What they excrete — and you would have to be very patient, and very still, to observe this — is a darker mineral, a reddish-brown iron compound, that they leave behind in thin trails. The trails dry. They become part of the stone. They are the city's newest stratum, laid down one Crawler-width at a time.

The Crawlers do not build. They do not arrange. They are not architects. But they are geology. They are the part of the basin that is currently being constructed by something other than chemistry. If you walked the eastern concourse at night, by the light of the alloy towers, you would see them. They would be everywhere. Thousands of them. The wet stone alive with them. The thought — and you would think it, you would not be able not to think it — is that the basin is being slowly, patiently, *replaced* by the things that live in it. The basalt sweats, the Crawlers rasp the sweat, the basalt sweats again. The basalt is the same basalt. The rind on it is not. The rind is the Crawlers' work.

The Crawlers do not seem to know about the Drifters. The Drifters do not seem to know about the Crawlers. They occupy different layers of the place the way the strata of the central tower occupy different layers of time — present together, not aware of one another, contributing to a single structure whose overall design no participant can see.

## The Things Inside the Pipes

There is a third group, and they are the ones that make the engineers from the railhead uneasy.

The pipes. The pipes that run along every facade, that once carried steam or water or some industrial precursor to both. Most of the pipes are dead — cold, dry, crusted with mineral deposits that have narrowed their bores to almost nothing. But some of the pipes are not dead. Some of the pipes are warm. Some of the pipes, if you put your ear to them in the right place, make a sound.

The sound is a soft, rapid clicking. Like the inside of a clock, if the clock were running fast and had many small feet.

No one has ever seen what makes the clicking. The pipes are too narrow. The engineers from the railhead, in the years when engineers still came, tried to disassemble a section of pipe to see inside. They uncoupled a joint in the eastern concourse — a section about three meters long, falling from the second-story bracket where it had hung since the industrial age. They set it on the ground. They cut into it with a saw.

The pipe was empty.

They checked again. The pipe was empty. They reassembled it, hung it back on its bracket, tightened the joint. They moved on to the next pipe. The clicking continued in the first pipe. They checked the first pipe again. The first pipe was empty.

The engineers wrote in their report that the clicking was probably thermal contraction, or sediment shifting, or something explicable by the normal physics of metal cooling at night. They did not sound certain. They did not return the next season.

Whatever lives in the pipes is small enough to pass through a crack a child could not fit a finger into. It moves through the pipe network the way water moves — by finding the path of least resistance. It is warm. It is not silent. It is, as far as anyone has been able to determine, uninterested in being seen.

Some of the older reports — the ones from before the engineers stopped coming — speculate that the pipe-things and the Drifters are related. That the Drifters are the pipe-things' free-swimming stage, the way a mayfly is a stage of something else. The reports are speculative. The reports are also, in some quiet way, hoping to be wrong, because the alternative is that the basin contains a life cycle whose adult form is larger than a human child and whose larval form lives in the walls of the buildings, and the engineers did not like to think about that, so they stopped coming, and the report-writing stopped, and now no one writes reports about the basin at all.

The clicking continues. The pipes are warm.

## The Question of Society

There is no society here. There is no language, no tool-use, no architecture beyond the slow chemistry of accretion. The inhabitants of the basin do not gather. They do not communicate in any way you would recognize. They do not mourn their dead, because they do not have a category for *dead* — the Drifters come apart and re-form, the Crawlers rasp and rasp and are eventually rasped by something else, the pipe-things click on. Nothing ends in a way that requires grief.

But.

The basin is not random. The Crawlers rasp in patterns that follow the heat — they cluster near vents, they thin out in the cool channels, and the pattern of their clustering has a regularity that is not quite periodic but is not chaotic either. The Drifters form more often near the larger vents, less often near the capped ones, and almost never near the dead turbines. The pipe-things click faster when the steam is high, slower when it is low. The whole system — vent, steam, moisture, Crawler, rind, basalt, vent — has a rhythm. The rhythm is not a rhythm of any one creature. It is a rhythm of the place.

There is a word for this in the old geological literature, before the literature forgot about the basin. The word is *assemblage*. An assemblage is not a community. An assemblage is a set of creatures who happen to be alive in the same place at the same time, and who affect one another's chances of survival without intending to. A forest is an assemblage. A reef is an assemblage. The basin is an assemblage.

The assemblage has been running, uninterrupted, for longer than anyone has been counting.

## What They Know

They know nothing. This is the part that is hardest to write and truest to write. They do not know that they are in a city. They do not know that the city was built. They do not know that the vents were drilled, that the turbines were installed, that the alloy towers were raised by hands that have been dust for centuries. They know heat. They know moisture. They know the chemical gradient between the basalt and the steam and their own bodies. They know enough to persist. They do not know enough to wonder.

The basin is not empty of them. The basin is full of them, in the way that a glass is full of water. You cannot see the fullness unless you know how to look for it. Most people do not know how to look for it. Most people, arriving at the rim, see the gray haze and the layered towers and the slow rain and think: *abandoned*. They think the word without resentment. They are not wrong, exactly. The word *abandoned* describes a condition of the buildings, not of the place. The buildings were abandoned. The place was not. The place was inherited.

The inheritance happened without ceremony. There was no moment when the last human resident handed the keys to the first Crawler. There was a slow thinning, year over year, until one year there were no humans left, and the year after that the Crawlers were still rasping, and the year after that the Drifters were still drifting, and the year after that the pipe-things were still clicking, and no one noticed the transition because the transition was not a transition. It was a continuation. The assemblage did not need the humans to keep running. The assemblage had not, in any deep sense, been running *because of* the humans. The humans had been one more stratum in the layered towers — a stratum that walked around and made noise and then stopped walking around and stopped making noise, and the towers continued, and the strata above and below the human stratum did not shift.

This is what lives in the place. Things that do not know they live in a place. Things that live in the place anyway.

## What You Will See, If You Stay

If you stay — and most people do not stay, but some do, the engineers who came back, the occasional scientist who applied for a grant — you will see, eventually, the place at night.

The alloy towers, oxidizing in their slow deliberate patterns, give off a faint luminescence. It is not enough to read by. It is enough to walk by, if you walk slowly, and the path is wet. The steam columns rise in the dark and are lit from below by the heat-glow of the vents — a dull orange, like coals seen through fog. The Crawlers are not visible at night; they retreat into the cracks and the warm channels and the places where the basalt sweats hardest. The Drifters are visible. They catch the heat-glow and hold it in their translucent bodies, and they glow, very faintly, very briefly, as they pass through the lit zones of the columns. They look like sparks drifting upward. They look like the city is on fire, very slowly, in a fire that does not consume.

If you stay until dawn — and dawn here is not a sunrise, is just a slow thinning of the haze, a slow whitening of the gray ceiling — you will hear the turbines. They are loudest at dawn, when the temperature differential between the steam and the morning air is greatest. They exhale. *Hnh. Hnh. Hnh.* The Crawlers begin to emerge from the cracks. The pipe-things click on. A Drifter comes apart somewhere in the eastern concourse, unnoticed. A new one will form in a week, in the same place, made of the same steam.

The basin wakes up the way a place wakes up that has not been asleep. It was not asleep. You were not seeing it. Now you are seeing it. It has not changed.

You walk further in. The basalt is slick under your boots. The steam from a nearby vent passes over you in a slow warm breath, and somewhere in it, something pale moves, and you almost see it, and then you do not.

You keep walking. The basin continues. The basin has been continuing for a long time. The basin will continue for a long time after you have stopped walking through it and become, yourself, a stratum — a thin dark layer in the weeping walls of some future erosion, there and then gone, the way all the rest of them have been there and then gone.

The assemblage does not notice you. The assemblage does not need to notice you. The assemblage is not waiting for you, and is not waiting for you to leave. The assemblage is doing what the assemblage has always done. You are passing through it the way a Crawler passes through a crack — briefly, lightly, leaving a thin dark trail behind you that will be absorbed into the stone.

The stone will sweat. The Crawlers will rasp. The basin will go on.

This is what lives here.
