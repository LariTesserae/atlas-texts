# The Inheritors of the Hollow

The hollow is not empty, though it is the emptiest place you have ever stood in. The silence is not a true silence. It is the silence of a place that has dismissed all the small, frivolous witnesses — the birds, the flies, the rustling things — and keeps only what is essential to its long memory. What lives here, lives in the negative space. What lives here has outlasted the need for company.

## The Keepers of the Stone

There is a colony of pale, sightless fish that navigate the shallow channel. They are small, no longer than a finger, and their scales are the color of the wet stones. They do not swim so much as drift, their movements so synchronized with the trembling of the water that it is difficult to tell where the current leaves off and the fish begin. They have no eyes, or only the faintest indentations where eyes might have been, covered over by a layer of the same gray skin. They navigate by the taste of the water, by the pressure of the channel walls against their sides. They were born here, and their parents were born here, back through a lineage so long that the concept of elsewhere has been bred out of them. They do not know they are fish. They know only the wet trough, the cool mineral taste, the slow pulse of the groundwater. To them, the hollow is the entire ocean, and the sky above is just the surface of a very still, very deep sea.

Aching into the cracks of the dry-stacked walls, in the damp mortar-lines that have long since crumbled to dust, there is a kind of lichen that is older than the stone it clings to. Or perhaps it is the stone, transformed. It spreads with a slowness that makes the word "growth" almost a misnomer. It does not bloom. It does not fruit. It simply extends itself, millimeter by millimeter, over the centuries, colonizing the rounded corners of the brick, softening the hard edges. It feeds on the condensation, on the faint mineral sweat of the rock, on the diffuse light that falls from the pewter sky. It has a memory in its chemistry — a record of every drought, every rain, every slight shift in the temperature of the stone. If you could read it, the history of the hollow is written there, in the slow layering of its pale gray-green tissue. It is the closest thing the hollow has to an archivist. It remembers what was never written down.

## The Witness in the Moss

At the bottom of the dry well, in the choked circle of stones, there is a patch of moss that is the greenest thing in the hollow. It is not the bright, defiant green of spring, but the deep, saturated green of something that has had a very long time to think about being green. It is called, in the old books that no one reads anymore, the Witness. It is the only thing here that still possesses something resembling a nervous system, though it operates on a timescale that makes the word "nervous" feel absurd. 

The Witness feels the weight of the footfalls when something walks above it. It feels the rain. It feels the slow, imperceptible sinking of the stones around it as the earth settles. In return, it sends up no spores. It grows no flowers. It simply exists in a state of perpetual, almost sentient observation, holding the memory of the place in its rooted stillness. It is the heart of the hollow, pumping nothing, circulating nothing, but keeping the time of the deep, slow rhythms of the stone and the water and the silence. It is the thing that holds the hollow together, that prevents the walls from collapsing into pure geology. It is the moss that the hollow dreams of being.

## The Hour of the Breeze

And then there is the air. On the warmest hours — and they are rare, measured in the slow turning of the seasons — a breeze comes through. It is not a wind. It is the long, slow exhalation of the hollow itself, the warm, humid air rising and pulling the cooler air from the rim down into the bowl. It moves against the top edges of the walls, producing a dry, faint hiss, like a whispered secret passed from stone to stone. 

The breeze is the hollow's only voice. It is not a voice that speaks. It is a voice that touches. When it passes over the lichen, it carries with it the faintest possible scent of cold, of the world outside, of the place where birds still sing and insects still hum. The breeze knows what the hollow has forgotten. It is the memory of motion, the lingering ghost of all the quick, bright, rustling things that were dismissed from this place in some ancient, quiet catastrophe. It comes through, it hisses against the stones, and then it is gone, and the hollow returns to its own perfect, closed silence, leaving behind only the faint, mineral smell of water sitting a long time against rock, and the slow, patient work of the things that have no need to leave.
