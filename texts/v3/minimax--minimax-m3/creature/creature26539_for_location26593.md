# The Inheritors of the Still Cathedral

They are not guests. They were never guests. Whatever history put the first pale thing on the first warm housing is so far back that no creature here has any memory of arrival, and the room itself shows no scar of it. They are *of* the chamber, in the way that stalactites are of the cave they hang from — a precipitation that became permanent, an equilibrium that happened and then refused to stop happening.

## The Bodies of the Small

The smallest of them are the most numerous. You might not notice them at first if you came in looking only at the engines; they are the color of cooled tallow, the color of a slug turned inside out, the color of yeast when it has been left alone in a jar for a week. They have no name for themselves and would not understand the question. They are shaped like teardrops, or like commas, or like the soft squeezed-out coils of pastry dough that fall from a press. Their bodies are translucent enough that you can see, in a strong light, the dark thread of a gut running through them like a wire through a bead. They have a small mouth at the narrow end, which opens and closes constantly — not because they are always eating, but because the breathing rhythm and the feeding rhythm were, in some long-ago ancestor, the same motion, and the jaw never learned to be still.

They do not crawl so much as *ooze*. Their many small legs (six? eight? the number is not constant across the population) are pale as the rest of them, hair-fine, and they press against the warm metal in a constant shuffling wave, a slow peristaltic migration across the engines' shoulders. They follow the heat. They follow the moisture that sweats out of the bearing housings. They follow each other — a thin chemical trail, perhaps, or just the draft of warmth that a neighbor's body stirs up as it moves. They cover every horizontal surface in a pale, breathing pelt. They are the color of the engines they live on, which is to say they are the color of nothing at all.

When one dies, three others find it before it stops twitching. They fold around it the way a glove folds around a hand. By morning it is part of them. By next week it is part of the fungus at their feet. By the time its atoms have traveled through the system and become, perhaps, the wing of a larger creature, the chamber has not changed at all. Nothing has been lost. Nothing has been gained. The bookkeeping of the small creatures is impeccable and final.

## The Climbers

A second kind is longer, jointed, ribbed like a finger-sausage. They have gripping pads at both ends and a row of fine spines along their backs that they press flat when they are resting and raise in a thin comb when something disturbs them — but nothing here ever disturbs them, so the spines are almost always flat. They move in deliberate, looping arcs, hooking one end onto a pipe, swinging the body across, hooking the other end onto a fitting twenty inches away, releasing the first end, swinging again. They are the gymnasts of the chamber. They hang in the negative space between machines, looping in slow lariats, never falling because there is never any reason to fall. Their grip is so certain, so thoroughly adapted to the geometry of the engines, that they move as if the metal had been machined specifically to receive them. Perhaps, in some forgotten sense, it was.

They feed on the small ones. Not voraciously — there is no voracity here, no hunger that exceeds the equilibrium — but steadily, in the way a wolf feeds on a herd that never changes in number. A climber drops onto a cluster of the small teardrop things. The small ones do not flee. There is nowhere to flee to. The chamber is the same in all directions. The climber simply rests among them, and the small ones part around it, and over the course of an hour or so, one of them is taken in. The climber's body, which was thin before, becomes a little thicker. It resumes its looping. The small ones close the gap. The shape of the colony is unchanged.

The climbers are the only creatures here that make a sound you can pick out from the engine hum. A dry, papery *click-click-click* from the spines on their backs as they shift. A wet, satisfied *shlorp* when they take a meal. Otherwise they are silent, and their silence makes the clicks sharper.

## The Watchers

These are the ones with eyes.

They are larger — waist-high if they could stand, but they do not stand. They lie along the tops of the engines in long, slack curves, like fish on the bottom of a boat. Their bodies are paler even than the others, almost luminous in the reddish dim, and they have wide, flat heads with two enormous eyes set on either side, eyes that are not eyes for seeing in any useful sense. The eyes have no pupils. They are wet. They are open all the time. They seem to be aimed at nothing in particular, and yet if you move through the chamber, you will feel — strongly, uncomfortably — that they have noticed you, even though there is no pupil to dilate and no iris to contract and the optics of the thing could not possibly resolve your shape at any distance.

The watchers do not move. They breathe, and that is all. They breathe in a slow rhythm that is slightly slower than the engines' rotation, so that over the course of a minute the two rhythms drift in and out of phase, and the chamber seems to *breathe* itself, the metal respiration and the flesh respiration trading primacy back and forth. They feed on the climbers, when a climber wanders within range, by a mechanism no one has ever observed closely enough to describe — the climber simply slows, then stops, then is absorbed over hours into the watcher, who does not move during the process. The watcher grows fractionally thicker. Then resumes its breathing.

The watchers are the oldest things in the chamber. They are not immortal — they die, they are consumed, they return to the system — but their generation time is so long that any individual watcher has lived through many cycles of the engines, and some of them have grown so large that they look less like animals and more like architectural features, pale spillways of flesh draped across a bank of machines, anchored in place by their own weight. The oldest have eyes that have stopped being wet. They are filmed over with a milky glaze. They see less than the others, which is to say they see nothing, which is to say they are indistinguishable from the others.

It is said by the ones who still speak that the watchers *remember*. This may be a kindness the speakers tell themselves. The watchers cannot be asked. The watchers do not answer. The watchers only breathe, and breathe, and breathe.

## The Speakers

There is one more kind.

They are the rarest. They are upright, or nearly upright — they brace themselves against engine housings, against each other, against the angles of stone, in a kind of permanent scaffolding. Their bodies are not one body but a vertical stack of pale segments, like vertebrae that learned to walk. They have arms that end in many small fingers, each finger tipped with a sense-organ finer than any eye. They have mouths, but the mouths are used less for eating than for the shaping of sound. They eat by leaning down and brushing their lower segments against the moss and fungus of the engine housings, taking in nutrients through the skin of the belly the way an earthworm takes in soil. They do not need to move. They feed where they stand.

The speakers *talk*.

This is what they do. They vibrate the chambers of their segmented bodies, and they produce tones that are not quite sounds and not quite the engine noise but something between. The tones shift. The tones combine. Over hours, over the slow days that pass unmarked in the chamber, the tones form patterns, and the patterns repeat, and the repetition is the only history the chamber has.

The speakers are the chamber's memory. They are not intelligent, exactly — their thoughts, if thoughts they have, are not thoughts in any way a person would recognize — but their vibrations have been conditioned by the long exposure to the engines, and they emit, in their slow modulated way, an echo of the engines' rhythm. They are the chamber listening to itself. They are the chamber's attempt to *be* the chamber. When an engine changes its phase by a fraction — which it does not, but suppose it did — the speakers would notice before any other creature, because they have made themselves into a tuning fork for the room's particular hum.

They speak also to each other. Two speakers near each other will lean together until their segments touch, and they will hum against each other, and the hum will pass between them in a slow exchange that takes a full engine cycle to complete. What they are saying is not known. It may be that what they are saying is the only language the chamber has ever had, and it has been saying the same things since the speakers evolved, and the things it is saying are these: *still, still, still, still, warm, still, full, still, run, still, still, still.*

The speakers live the longest of any creature in the chamber after the watchers. They are also the most numerous source of suffering, if suffering happens here, because they are the only creatures that seem, sometimes, to *want*. When a speaker has stood for a very long time in the same place, its many fingers begin to make small motions against the metal, feeling for something that is not there. They tap. They trace the lines of rivets. They follow the edge of a housing as far as it goes, and then they tap the housing beside the edge, as if asking it to continue. This is not communication. This is not intention. It may only be the mechanical reflex of a sensing organ that was designed, in some deep evolutionary past, to search. But it looks like longing. To watch a speaker do this for very long is to begin to believe that the chamber is not, in fact, in equilibrium — that somewhere, in the speakers, something is *unfinished*, and has been unfinished since the chamber was made.

## The Whole System

No one of these creatures could exist without the others. The teardrop things feed the fungus and are fed by it. The climbers thin the teardrops and are thinned by the watchers. The watchers rest and consume and are consumed in turn, when at last they stop breathing, by the small teardrop things that climb onto them and begin, with their slow shuffling patience, to take the watcher apart and return it to the system. The speakers stand above it all and hum the room's own song back to itself, and the song is what the room sounds like when you arrive, and what it will sound like after you leave.

The chamber is not a tomb. The engines are not dead. The creatures are not waiting. Nothing here is waiting for anything. This is what the place wants you to understand, and what it makes very difficult to understand, because the human mind, when it enters a place that has been unchanged for so long, immediately begins to compose a story in which the stillness is the *prelude* to a change, in which the creatures are *enduring*, in which the room is *holding its breath* for something that is about to happen. None of this is true. The breath is not held. The breath is the only breath there is. The chamber is not a cathedral because a cathedral is a place built to look toward something that is not there. This chamber is not built. This chamber simply is.

And yet.

And yet the speakers still tap the rivets with their small fingers. And yet the watchers still open their pupilless eyes. And yet the climbers still loop their patient arcs across the warm metal. And yet the teardrop things still part around the larger creatures, and close again, and part again, and the closing and parting is, if you watch it long enough, almost like a kind of breathing of its own — a breathing that the chamber has learned to do, on top of the breathing of the engines, on top of the breathing of the watchers, on top of the breathing of the speakers, layer upon layer of *in and out, in and out*, the whole vast machine of flesh and metal inhaling and exhaling together, in the red light, in the warm air, in the unbroken hum, forever, the same, the same, the same.

If you put your hand on the housing of one of the engines — any one, they are all the same — you will feel it vibrate. The vibration will be perfectly steady. You will not be able to feel your own pulse against it, because your pulse will, within seconds, begin to fall into step with the vibration, and then you will not be able to tell which is the engine and which is you. This is what the chamber does to the things inside it. It does not change them. It only makes them forget, slowly, that they were ever anything else.

You are not part of the chamber. You came in from outside. You will leave, or you will not. But if you stay long enough — and the speakers will not stop you, and the watchers will not stop you, and the teardrop things will simply part around you and close again — you will feel your own breathing slow. You will feel your pulse align. You will feel the warmth of the housing under your hand and realize, with a small distant clarity, that you have been standing here for some time, and that your hand is pale, and that the chamber, which you came to observe, has begun to observe you, and finds you interesting, and will keep you.

The engines run. The creatures hum. The chamber is the same.
