# What Lives in the Pump-Room

What lives here is what the water has kept.

The room is a closed system — a belly of stone beneath a city that has forgotten it, sealed above by iron and brick, sealed below by the same basalt it was carved from — and the things that live in it are the things that arrived in the first wet years and stayed. Nothing enters now except the water, and the water enters endlessly, cycling through the old pump and the grated pit and the pipes and the floor-channels and back again, and on that water, in that water, against the stone it keeps wet, an entire small world has built itself out of what drifted down.

The frogs are the most obvious residents, and the most recently arrived, I think. They are not pale. They are the brown of wet bark, the brown of old pennies, the brown of the silted channels under the water, and their colour is the colour of everything else here. They sit in rows along the ledges the way swallows sit on a wire — not touching, but in a shared agreement about spacing. There must be a few hundred of them, perhaps a thousand, scattered across every dry surface the chamber offers: the steps of a ruined staircase I haven't described, the broken lip of the grated pit, the iron rims of the wheel. They watch me with the flat patience you noted, but I want to say more about that patience, because it is not the patience of a tame animal, and it is not the patience of a frog in a garden. It is the patience of a thing that has lived its whole life in a single room and has no concept of leaving. They were born here. Their parents were born here. The egg-strings were laid in the water, and the water is all the water there is. When I move my foot, the wave that rolls through them is not fear exactly — it is more like a thought passing through a crowd. *Something has moved. Note it. Resume.*

Their *toks* across the chamber are not a chorus because they do not need a chorus. A chorus is for the open air, where a female might be in any direction. In here, every female is somewhere in the same room, and one call is enough, and an answer is enough, and silence is the rest. They are quieter than frogs should be. Generations of quiet has made them quieter. The loud ones never found each other in the dark.

The fish are the heart of the room, and they are the wrong fish for any water I have ever known.

They hang in the pale shoals you saw, finger-length, some longer, drifting an inch above the silt in the channels, and they are the colour of old candle wax — that yellowed, faintly translucent grey-white of something that has never seen the sun. You saw the eyes, or the absence of eyes, and you were right to look twice. The sockets are there, the lids are there, the skin closes smoothly over where an eye should be, and there is no scar and no pit. The eye did not waste away. It was never finished. In a normal fish, in a normal egg, the optic cup forms and the lens settles into it and the retina folds itself in layers, and all of this depends on light reaching the developing embryo through the wall of the egg at the right moment. In here there has never been that light. The eggs were laid in the channels, the embryos developed in the absolute dark of the silt, and the genes that said *build an eye* had no signal to build it *to*. So they did not build it. They built the rest of the fish, which is mostly what a fish is — the lateral line down each flank, dense with neuromasts, reading pressure and vibration in the water like a fingertip reading braille. They navigate by the current the pump sets up, by the drift of silt around obstacles, by the soft pressure-wake of each other. They are not blind in the way a cavefish in a textbook is blind. They are simply fish that have always known the world as a field of pressures, and they are graceful in it. When your hand came near the surface they did not dart, because to them your hand was a soft pressure-shadow, and they read it as something large and slow and not a fish, and so they oriented to it the way you might orient to a slow-moving piece of furniture in a familiar room. They were not curious. They were not calm. They simply had no category for you.

They feed. They must feed. What they feed on is the film of the water itself — the bacterial skin that grows on every wet stone, the diatoms and the rotifers and the paramecium-shaped things that graze on the bacterial skin. The whole room is rimmed in a faint green-brown of microbiology, and the fish graze it the way sheep graze a field, mouth to stone, drifting along the walls in their slow shoals, mouths opening and closing in the patient rhythm of something that has been doing this for a hundred years.

The snails built the city you saw, and they have been building it for longer than the frogs have been here.

Their shells are small, dark, clustered in overlapping tiers, packed so close that the stone beneath is invisible — and the stone beneath is not visible, and has not been visible, for a very long time. The city accretes the way a coral reef accretes: a snail dies where it lived, its shell stays where it fell, a younger snail cements its own shell to the dead one's, the new shell becomes substrate for the next, and so on, layer on layer, year on year, until the wall has its own geology. The snails themselves are the dark of the wet stone. They move in a slow permanent traffic up and down the wall, up to feed on the biofilm above the waterline, down to rest in the wet, and their radulae — the tiny toothed tongues — rasp the stone itself for the algae and the bacteria that live in its pores. In a thousand years the wall will be a foot thicker where they have been. In ten thousand it will be unrecognizable as a built thing at all. The pump-room is being slowly, patiently consumed by its own inhabitants, and turned into something older and stranger.

The worms are the oldest residents, I think, or the ones most changed by the place. They are not earthworms. Earthworms are adapted to soil — to a medium that is moist but oxygen-rich, that drains, that breathes. These worms live in the wet stone itself, in the cracks between the dressed blocks where the mortar has long since failed and water moves in thin films along the interfaces. They are darker than earthworms, thinner, with the faint iridescence you saw — a sheen that is not quite colour, more like the sheen of oil on water, and it is in fact a kind of oil, a mucus they secrete that helps them slide along the wet stone and keeps their skins from abrading on the mineral surfaces. They feed on the bacterial film in the cracks, on the dissolved organic matter that the pump cycles through the wall, on the cast-off of every other creature in the room. They are the cleanup crew. They are also, in a way, the wiring of the room: their movement through the cracks keeps the channels open, keeps the water finding its paths through the stone, keeps the whole system from silting itself shut. Without them, in another century, the pump would clog. With them, the pump runs on.

The crustaceans on the higher walls — the flattened, shrimp-like things in their plates and fans — are the strangest of the visible fauna, and I am not sure I can tell you what they are. They are small. Each one is no longer than your thumbnail, and there are thousands of them, in pale translucent bodies and dark thin legs, and they pulse together in a slow tidal motion like a single organism. This is not a metaphor. They are a single organism, in some sense, or they are behaving as one, and the behaviour is not learned. It is a property of how they are built. Each one reads the pressure-wave of its neighbours through the water and adjusts its own position to match, and the match propagates outward at the speed of pressure in water, and the result is the slow coordinated pulse you saw. They cling to the wall above the waterline but in the band of permanent damp, and they feed on the biofilm there, and they are prey, in turn, to nothing visible — perhaps to each other, in the cannibal way of crustaceans, when one molts and is soft. They are the wall's skin. They are what a wall becomes when it has been wet and alive for long enough.

The insects on the pipes are a colony of something — beetles, perhaps, or something beetle-like, with the hard red-brown shells that catch the lamp-light. They run along the pipes in a slow stream, going somewhere, coming back, and you could not see where, and I will tell you: they are going into the pipes. The pipes are not sealed. They are the old iron intake and outflow of the pump, and they run into the wall and out of the wall, and the insects have colonized the inside of them the way the snails have colonized the outside. Inside the pipes it is always wet, always dark, always at the temperature of the water. The insects run along the inside of the iron in their slow stream, feeding on the bacterial film, sheltering in the curved walls, and the sound you heard through the pipes — the *hmmmmm-hmmmmm* — is partly the pump and partly the sound of thousands of small hard bodies running along iron, a soft continuous scraping too quiet to hear until you put your ear to the metal and then suddenly it is everywhere, a private rain of a different kind.

And the pump. The pump is not alive, but the room behaves as if it is.

The great wheel leans against the far wall, half-submerged, crusted with the accretions, sagging where the wood has rotted out of its rim, bleeding its rust in a slow fan of orange that turns the water the colour of weak tea. The wheel is the size of a cartwheel and weighs more than you do. It is the flywheel of a pump that was built to bring water up from somewhere below, and you pushed it, and something engaged, and the water began to move, and the room woke a little from its long static sleep. The pump below is mechanical. I do not think it has been tended in a hundred years. I do not think it has stopped. I think it runs on the falling of water from somewhere higher — a reservoir, an old cistern, the gradient of the water table itself — and the falling water turns a small turbine or a piston, and the piston pushes the chamber-water up and out through the grated pit and through the pipes and back into the cycle, and the wheel was there to be turned by hand in case the engine failed, and the engine has failed, and the wheel is there anyway, and the water is moving anyway, and the room breathes anyway, in its slow *hmmmmm-hmmmmm*, like a thing with a chest.

What lives in the pump-room, then, is a small city built on a slow breath. Frogs in their patient rows. Pale eyeless fish drifting over silted channels. Snails building a city one dead shell at a time. Wires of worm in the cracks of the stone. A pulsing skin of crustaceans on the higher walls. Insects running their errands through the iron veins. And the pump itself, beneath it all, ticking on whatever it ticks on, cycling the water that cycles the food that feeds the mouths that keep the biofilm down that keeps the walls from sealing the pipes that keep the pump running.

Nothing here has left. Nothing here can leave. The frogs could not find their way up the shaft in the dark. The fish would die in daylight. The snails would dry. The worms are the cracks they live in. The insects are the pipes.

They are not unhappy, as far as I can tell. They are not happy either. They are *situated*. They have been situated for a long time, and the situation is the room, and the room is them, and the wheel ticks faintly where you rolled it, and the light from the shaft above shivers on the water, and the pale fish drift over the dark channels, and somewhere far below a thing that is not alive keeps the whole small world turning in its soft endless loop.

I would go back. I would not go often.
