The birds are the first thing the place offers, and the way it offers them is through the air. They live higher than you would expect — at the very top of the curve, where the limestone begins to fold back over itself, in holes you cannot see from the rim and would not find if you went down. The holes are small. The birds are smaller. They are the color of bone, or of dust, or of the inside of a shell — a pale so unemphatic that against the cream of the wall they almost disappear, and against the sky they are only the absence of sky. Their wings are quick in a way that looks almost angry, a small furious business of staying aloft, and yet the sound they make is not angry. It is something else. A thin up-rising note, climbing, climbing, and then a stop — not a fade, a stop, as if the throat that made it has been gently closed. The note hangs in the cup of air a long time after the bird has gone. It changes as it hangs. It becomes rounder. It loses its edges. By the time it reaches you, standing on the rim, it is more a shape than a sound.

There are not many of them. Three, perhaps four, perhaps five. You count and then count again and the number is different, because they move so fast and hide so completely. They are loud in a quiet place. They are loudest in the morning, when the light first comes down the column and finds the walls still cool, and at that hour their notes layer over each other and the bowl rings faintly with something that is not quite a song and not quite a complaint. By the time the sun is high they have quieted. You see them cross the column of light as silhouettes, one after another, and you understand that they are not crossing for any reason you could name — not hunting, not migrating, not going anywhere. They are crossing because the air is there, and they have wings.

Their droppings whiten the rim and the upper ledges in long pale smears. The smell is faint and chalky, and it is one of the smells of the place, along with the damp stone and the green of the ferns and, sometimes, the dry-grass breath of the world above. If you stand at the rim long enough, you stop noticing the smells. They become the smell of being there. They nest out of sight — you never see an egg, never see a nestling, never see the work of raising the next round of pale furious wings. But you see, sometimes, a bird carrying something in its beak — a fiber, a bit of moss, an insect held by one papery leg — and you understand that behind the stone there is a household, and the household is busy.

Below the birds, on the warm flat floor, the lizards are doing nothing. Or rather — they are doing the thing they do, which is to be still. They are the color of the stone. They are the color of the stone so thoroughly that you begin to suspect they are not animals at all but pieces of the floor that have decided to breathe. A head lifts. A tail curves. A small throat pulses, once, twice, a slow metronome of blood. They are watching you with the same patience the water has. They are small. The biggest is no longer than your hand.
