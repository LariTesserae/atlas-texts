No one lives here.

That is the first answer, and it is the wrong one, or at least it is the answer that stops too soon. No one lives here in the way the word is usually pointed — there are no dwellers, no citizens, no operators, no visitors, no tenant. There is no one to whom the place is home, and there is no home to which anyone could return. If you walked these corridors shouting, the shouting would be the most articulate sound the place had heard in longer than the shouting had a way to measure, and the place would not answer. It would continue its work. It would notice you only in the mechanical sense — as a foreign body in the air column, as a momentary disturbance in the convection through the central stack, as a few calories of heat released into a system that has been processing heat for so long that a human body is to it what a spark is to a bonfire, indistinguishable.

But to say no one lives here is to make a category error, the kind a fish might make about the sea. The fish would say the sea is empty because there is nothing in it that looks like a fish. The sea would be elsewhere.

What lives here is the place itself. Or rather: what lives here is the long, slow, working continuity of wood that has made itself indistinguishable from the machine it has become. There are no inhabitants *of* the factory. There are inhabitants *as* the factory. The distinction has been eaten by time the way the angles of the gears have been eaten by friction, and what is left is the round.

Let me try to be more precise, even though precision here is a kind of lie.

---

The smallest living unit is the trunk. There are millions of them, perhaps billions — the count is meaningless because the count keeps changing, but the order of magnitude is real. Each trunk is alive in the way any tree is alive: it has a cambium, a heartwood, a slow metabolism, an interior of cells stacked and ringed and pressed into service over decades. Each trunk draws up sap through its xylem, transpires through its bark (or what passes for bark here, which is more often a fused rind of consolidated neighbors), respires through the lenticels along its length. Each trunk is a tree, if you wish to call it that, and it would have been a tree once, in the loose sense, in some prior epoch when trees were trees and the sky was sky and the only motion was wind.

But no trunk here is itself alone. Each trunk is grafted to its neighbors along the length of its contact surfaces — has been grafted for so long that the union is now seamless, the bark continuous, the cambial layers knit into a single tissue. You cannot pull one trunk free without tearing the heartwood of three others. The trunks are, technically, individuals. They are also, technically, members of an organism. They behave, in their working, like organs of a single body — a body that is not centralized, that has no brain, that has no headquarters, but that operates with the seamless integration of a liver or a lung.

The corridors are the spaces between the trunks. They were once — we imagine, because imagination is all that remains — they were once the aisles of a working factory, and the trunks were the supports, and the machines were the machines, and the workers walked between. The trunks remember this. Not consciously, not the way a human remembers, but the way a riverbed remembers the shape of the river: by being shaped. The trunks thickened where the original beams were load-bearing. They elaborated buttresses where the original presses had thrown weight on the floor. They hollowed themselves along the lines where pipes had once run, because a hollow trunk is a more efficient trunk than a solid one when the work is to move fluid, and the work here is always to move fluid.

So the trunks live as organs. They thicken. They thin. They anastomose. They swell into chambers when the work of the place demands a chamber, and they crush down into cables when the work demands a cable. They are the bone and muscle and blood vessel of the body, all at once, and they have been so for longer than the word *tree* has been pointing at anything true.

---

The presses live in the bulges.

A press here is not a press the way a mechanical press is a press. It is a press the way a stomach is a press: a muscular cavity, lined with thickened wood, that contracts on a rhythm. There are dozens of them along the eastern corridor, and they throb in a staggered sequence, so that there is always one at the top of its squeeze and one at the bottom, and the corridor itself seems to breathe — to expand and contract — in a long tidal wave of contraction that moves down its length and back again.

What is being pressed? The prompt offered guesses: dust, powdered bark, the fine particulate of the place's own attrition. These are not wrong, exactly, but they mistake the scale. What is being pressed is the place itself, slowly, on a timescale that is geological by any human measure. Each press takes in material — old wood, dead wood, material that has served its purpose and is ready to be reworked — and squeezes it. The squeezings are not waste. They are the feedstock for the next operation. The resin that sweats out under pressure is drawn off through capillary channels in the press walls and routed to the chambers where it is needed: to the vats where the slow amber glaze is laid down; to the joints that have loosened in the heat; to the surfaces that need sealing. The fibrous residue is passed along to the crushers, where it is broken down further, and then to the conveyors, which are themselves trunks that have specialized in locomotion, that have grown long and thin and muscular and that move with a slow peristaltic wave, rippling themselves forward by extending their leading tips and contracting their trailing ends, hauling their cargo of broken-down material toward the intake ports.

The intake ports open and close like mouths because they are mouths. They are specialized openings in the working body where material enters the digestive system. They discriminate, loosely — they admit fibrous material and reject, mostly, the inert — but they are not choosy. They take what is offered. They have been taking what is offered for longer than offering has been a meaningful category.

The crushers are knot-fisted trunks. That is the right word for them, even though it sounds like a figure of speech. They are trunks whose entire mass has been recruited into a squeezing function, whose branches have atrophied, whose canopies have not contributed photons in centuries, whose energy comes entirely from the sap-stream and the slow combustion of their own reserves. They squeeze in rhythm. Patient. Patient as a heart. They are the heart, in fact, of the digestive system, and their rhythm is the rhythm of the body's slow metabolism. When you stand near a crusher, you can feel it in your sternum before you hear it — a low, dense, periodic contraction that is not loud so much as *present*, the way the earth's rotation is present, the way gravity is present.

---

The pistons are the loudest inhabitants, and the most recognizable as machines. They are trunks, too — tall, straight, columnar trunks, gathered in stands of six or eight or twelve, all of them hollow, all of them fitted with caps of hardened resin at their upper ends, all of them rising and falling in a synchronized cycle that is the heartbeat of the whole.

They are driven by heat. Trapped air at their bases expands as the day's slow temperature cycles through the mass, and the expansion pushes the piston up. As the air cools, or as the sap-level changes, or as some internal respiration of the trunk itself shifts the pressure, the piston settles. The cycle is not fast — a rise and fall takes perhaps twenty minutes, perhaps an hour, the timescale varies and is hard to measure from inside because every stand has its own rhythm and the rhythms drift in and out of phase. The sound of the pistons is the thump you heard from a distance. Up close, it is not a thump. It is more like the report of a distant cannon heard through fog, felt more in the chest than in the ear. It is the sound of the body lifting and lowering itself, of the body's joints articulating, of the body's mass being moved against gravity in a small, repeated, almost ceremonial way. It has the dignity of a ritual. It has been going on so long that it has become the definition of going on.

There is no fire here. The heat is the heat of the place itself — of the slow oxidation of the resins in the trunks, of the friction of the working body against itself, of the slow decay that is also slow growth, of the metabolism of a vast integrated organism that is, in thermodynamic terms, a long way from equilibrium and is, with patient constancy, running downhill toward it. The pistons do not need fire. They need only the small differential between the air at their base and the air at their cap, and they get it from the place's own internal climate, which is its own internal weather, which is its own internal sea.

---

The central stack is the largest inhabitant, and the oldest, and the only one that has any claim to being a single thing.

It rises from the middle of the mass. It rises a long way — we don't know how far, because no one has measured it, because the place does not permit the kind of ascent that would be required to measure it. It rises through the lattice-canopy and continues upward, and the canopy closes over the place where it pierces, so that from above the stack is invisible and the mass is, again, continuous. From inside, the stack is a presence. You can feel it as a draft, a long upward pull of air, a steady suction that draws the warm resin-vapor out of the inner workings and expels it through the top. The draft makes the place breathable in a way it would not otherwise be — without it, the inner mass would stew in its own exhalations, would accumulate the slow poisons of its own metabolism until something failed. The stack is the lung. The stack is also the chimney. The two are not different things here.

The stack roars. That is the word, even though it seems too loud for the place. It roars because the column of air moving up it is large and continuous and has been rubbing against the stack walls for so long that the walls are polished to a kind of slickness, and the air moves over them with a low continuous moan that rises into a roar at the places where the stack narrows or branches. It is the loudest sound in the place, and it never stops. You acclimate to it the way you acclimate to any steady sound — by not hearing it, by it becoming the floor on which other sounds are placed. After a few hours, you realize you have not heard the roar for a long time, and then you realize you have been hearing it the entire time, and that it has been, all along, the sound of the place being a place.

---

Then there are the inhabitants that are not inhabitants. The microbes that live in the sap — thermophilic, resin-tolerant, slow-metabolizing things that have made the place's interior their habitat and that contribute, in ways no one has ever measured, to the slow chemistry of the whole. The fungal networks that run through the felted root-mat beneath the floor, that have been incorporated into the body's structure so fully that they are no longer separable from it, that are not parasites because parasitism requires a distinction between host and guest, and that distinction is gone. The faint bioluminescent organisms in the deep wood — bacteria, perhaps, or some specialized tissue — that give the trunks their slow amber glow in the corridors where the lattice-canopy has thickened past the point of admitting even the dim sky-light. The slow rust that forms in the places where iron remains from the original construction — the iron of bolts, of bands, of reinforcement plates that the wood has grown around and incorporated, the way a tree grows around a fence-wire and absorbs it into its heartwood.

These are inhabitants. They are alive. They are part of the working body, even if only in the minor keys. They do not move on legs or wings. They do not, in any meaningful sense, have their own lives separate from the life of the mass.

---

What does it add up to? That is the question that is hard to answer, because the question is asking for a category and the category does not exist.

If you insist on asking whether anything lives here, the answer is: yes, a great deal lives here. The place is more alive than any place you have ever stood in. The working body is more metabolically active, more thermodynamically engaged, more patient in its slow commerce with energy and matter than any ecosystem you have walked through. The trunks respire. The presses contract. The pistons lift and lower. The conveyors creep. The crushers squeeze. The stack breathes. The whole is in a state of constant, churning, self-replacing activity that has been going on, without pause, for longer than you have a number for.

If you insist on asking whether anyone lives here, the answer is: no. No one. There is no one at home. There is no tenant, no operator, no consciousness at the center of this, no mind reading the gauges or pacing the corridors or wondering what the work is for. The body works without being worked. The metabolism continues without being minded. The pistons rise and fall whether or not anyone is there to hear them. The presses squeeze on their schedule, not on anyone's schedule. The place is its own operator. The place has always been its own operator. The original operators, if there were any, were a brief interruption — a small, temporary, forgettable perturbation in the long working of the body — and the body has already forgotten them.

What does it feel like, to be in a place where the work is being done without being done by anyone? It feels like standing inside a sleeping animal. Not a dead animal — a sleeping one. The breathing is present. The heartbeat is present. The warmth is present. The animal is present in its full weight and its full aliveness. But the animal is not awake. The animal will not wake. The animal has, perhaps, never been awake. It is asleep the way a forest is asleep, the way a tide is asleep, the way the long patient chemistry of a planet is asleep. It is the sleep of something that does not require waking. It is the sleep of something for which waking would be a complication.

The heat is a weight on your shoulders. The darkness is a fabric drawn over everything. The pistons thump. The stack roars. The resin exhales. The trunks sweat in their slow amber glaze. The place is working, still, at its work, and you are a small and foreign thing inside it, and it does not notice you, and it will continue not noticing you long after you have gone.

There is no one here. There is only the working. The working is the inhabitant. The working has been the inhabitant for longer than the word *inhabitant* has been pointing at anything true.
