The plants live here the way certain thoughts live at the back of the mind — present always, noticed only in glimpses, doing their work without ceremony. They are the oldest inhabitants, though *oldest* is a word that barely fits a place where nothing weathers. They were here when the stone cracked open, perhaps, or they were here before the stone and the cracking was the only way they could be without the stone knowing. They adhere. They do not grow downward into anything; they thicken outward across whatever warm surface will receive them, layer upon layer, season upon undatable season. Their blades and fans are not leaves in any sense the word has elsewhere — they are membranes, exchange surfaces between the heavy air and the slow bleeding of the rock. They take in what the stone gives and they give back what the air can hold. They are the lung of this place. They are also its mouth, its skin, its most patient organ. Where they live they live densely: a single clump can be a small thicket, a thicket can be a slow city of folded tissue, a city can be a thousand years of accumulation measured in millimeters. They are wine-dark. They are a green so deep it registers as blue at the edges. They are almost black where the layers stack deepest. They have no flowers, no seeds, no obvious way of making more of themselves, and yet more of them exist. Something in the chemistry of this chamber allows a fragment to root, or to drift, or to be carried on the slow convection of the warm air and lodge against a stone and become a plant. There is no generation here that one could observe in a human lifetime. There is only continuation.

The small floating things live here more urgently, though urgency is a strange word for creatures that seem to do nothing but hang and pulse. They are soft. They are translucent in places, the way a grape is translucent, the way a thumb held to a candle is translucent. They have bellies that glow a dim turquoise, and the glow rises and falls in time with the chamber's hum, as if the hum were their breath and their breath were the hum. They drift. They do not swim — there is nothing to swim in, and they have no clear means of propulsion; they move as if the air were slightly thicker on one side than the other, and they prefer the thicker side, or the warmer side, or whatever side the convection is climbing. Some have fine fins, almost vestigial, that they spread and fold for reasons of their own. Some have no fins at all. They are smaller than your hand, mostly. The largest you might see would not reach your elbow. They feed — one presumes they feed — on the chemistry of the air, or on the spores of the plants, or on one another; nothing about them suggests teeth or mouth, but nothing about them rules out a slow absorption across the membrane of the belly. They drift at every level of the chamber. Some hug the ceiling where the faint grey daylight bleeds in; some hang at the height of your chest, calm as held breath; some skim the floor and the low rocks where the corals spread, and brush against them, and seem to glow a little brighter when they do, as if the plants were a kind of food and the glow were the digestion. They are numerous. If you stand still long enough, you will realize that what you took for empty air is actually a slow snow of them, a murmuration that does not move together but moves in the same medium. Their noises are very small. They contribute, individually, almost nothing to the chamber's hum. Together they are a third of it.

The slow pale things live at the floor. They are perhaps snails without shells. They are perhaps slugs that have given up the idea of slime. They move across the warm stone in long rippling waves, the way water moves across water, the way a slow muscle moves under skin. Their bodies are colorless except where the dim turquoise of the floating creatures has rubbed off on them, which it has, slightly, here and there, so they appear faintly luminous at the leading edge of their passage. They leave a faint trace, not slime, but a slight darkening of the stone where they have been, as if the warmth had been pressed out of the surface and replaced with their own warmth, which is slightly different. They eat the wax that the plants secrete. This is clear because you can see them at the edges of the coral clumps, heads pressed to the waxy bloom, and the bloom is reduced where they have fed. They eat slowly. They digest slowly. They move slowly. They are the slowest thing here, and they seem to know it, and they seem to find it sufficient. Nothing hunts them that you can see. They are below notice, or beyond notice, and they thrive at that altitude of the food chain. They live for a long time. You cannot tell how long.

What chitters you cannot see.

It lives in a crevice high on one wall, where a fault line in the rock widens into a darkness the amber-green light does not reach. It makes a sound like breathing but never varies it: a soft pulse, in, out, in, out, at a rate that suggests a creature smaller than the chamber, much smaller, but a creature with a clock inside it. It is not the hum. It is layered under the hum. You hear it most clearly when you stop breathing yourself. It has been doing this for as long as you have been standing here, and it will be doing it after you leave, and the pulse is the same pulse, and it is not your pulse and not the chamber's pulse and not the plants' pulse. It is its own. Sometimes — rarely — the pulse skips. Just once. Just slightly. Then it resumes, exactly as before, and you cannot tell if you misheard. You wonder what it is. You wonder if it has always been here, or if it came after the chamber was cracked open and found the chamber habitable. You wonder if it is one creature or several, taking turns. You cannot tell. You will not tell.

And then there are the marks.

A low ring of stones, shoulder height, marking out a space the size of a sleeping body. The ring is careful, balanced, fitted by hand without mortar. The stones are crusted with the same amber film that dries on the corals — the wax of the plants, hardened by time into a glaze. Nothing else has touched them. There are no lichens, no mosses, no creeping things. There is only the wax, the patient chemistry of the place, accumulating on the stones that someone once stacked. Beside the ring: a wedge of black glass, sharp as anything sharp can be, placed with intention. A basket of woven fibers — fibers stripped from the coral-fans, dried, treated, twisted tight — holding a small arrangement. Three of the floating creatures, dead and stiff, posed as if in sleep. A pinch of dried coral-tissue. The arrangement is careful. It is not decoration. It is a record. It says: *these were eaten. these were the eaters. this is what it was to live here.*

The marks on the largest stone are four symbols, scratched with the pointed edge of the same black glass. They do not decode. They may not be language. They may be a tally. They may be a name. They are deliberate, and they are careful, and they have not weathered, because nothing here weathers, and so you cannot tell if they were made a year ago or a thousand years ago or in some other measure of time the chamber does not bother with. Whoever made them was bipedal. Whoever made them rested in a body of a particular size. Whoever made them knew how to flake glass, knew how to twist fiber, knew how to arrange the dead with tenderness. Whoever made them understood, eventually, that this place would hold what they left. That the wax would crust it. That the slow pale things would not crawl over it. That the small drifters would brush against it and leave their faint glow on its edges. That it would stay.

You do not see anyone. You cannot tell if anyone is here.

The hum continues. The light continues. The plants sway in their unfelt breeze. The small pale creatures drift. The shadows do not move. The chamber is a stable system balanced at a strange point, and it will remain so for as long as its nature holds.

What lives here is everything that has learned to live at the held point. The plants, who feed on the chemistry of the air. The drifters, who feed on the plants or on each other or on the air. The slow pale things, who feed on the wax. The chitterer, who feeds on something you will never know. And the marks, which do not feed on anything, but which persist, and which suggest — softly, without insisting — that once, briefly, something fed on all of it and was fed by all of it and rested in the ring of stones and scratched four marks in the largest stone and left the place to itself, knowing it would be here when it returned, knowing it might never return, knowing the chamber would hold either possibility without preference, the way it holds the wax, the way it holds the glow, the way it holds the hum.

This is the life of the throat. It does not invite. It does not refuse. It holds.
