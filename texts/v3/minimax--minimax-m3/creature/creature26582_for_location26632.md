What lives here has had time.

Start with the celery. It is the only one that has decided what it wants to be. It grows where warm water drips continuously from a pipe joint — has grown there for years, perhaps for as long as anyone can remember — and it does not appear to be suffering, exactly, though what it wants with this abundance is unclear. It has white stalks. It has no leaves. It has not been formally identified by anyone with the relevant credentials, and the men who work here have given it a name that is more of an acknowledgment than a description, the way one acknowledges an eccentric neighbor. They mention it occasionally with a kind of fond, slightly puzzled respect. If you stand near it you can hear the water hit its stalks, a tiny continuous percussion, and you can see — if the sodium light cooperates — the slow capillary climb of moisture up its stems. It is the most alive thing in the chamber, in the sense that it is the only thing unambiguously getting more itself with each passing year.

The moss is around the corner of the celery's life, at a respectful distance. It has established itself on a section of poured concrete where a slow leak has been staining the wall for years. It is the color of an old bruise — that particular mottled purple-green-brown that bruises become after they have stopped being injuries and started being artifacts. It climbs perhaps three feet up the wall, then stops, and the line where it stops is a working frontier, not an end. Each year, by the reckoning of the men who notice, it has reached a little farther. It leans toward the dim sodium light in a way that suggests it would prefer more light, but it has not insisted on the point. It is patient. It is surviving, not thriving, but surviving with intention.

The ferns are worse off, and know it. A few of them have taken hold in the cracks between older concrete pours — cracks that are themselves historical, the result of decisions made decades ago about which pour to do first and which to do after, and which have since widened by fractions of a millimeter each season as the rock beneath continues its slow geological settling. The ferns are not green. They are the color of weak tea, of dishwater, of something laundered too many times. They lean toward the sodium lamps as though hoping for better, and the hope has become part of their posture — a permanent stoop, an interpretive dance with the light.

This is the flora. There is no more of it.

The fauna is another matter, and it is the fauna that surprises the new men, and then stops surprising them, and then becomes the central fact of the place, the way the heat becomes the central fact, and they stop being surprised by that too.

The cockroaches are the substrate. There is no other word for them. They are the first thing a new man sees and the last thing he stops seeing, and in between they are the condition of the floor the way concrete is the condition of the walls. They are not the cockroaches of kitchen corners or basement drains. They are something else, and they have become something else over generations that no one has counted — generations that have unfolded in this warm dim wet cathedral, fed on the leavings of the work crews and the things that grow in the wet cracks. They are not larger than the cockroaches you know, exactly. They are more present. More architectural. They hold their bodies with the deliberation of animals that have been looked at for years and have decided not to care. They move in palimpsests across the floor — layers of them, overlapping, each one tracing its own purpose with the unhurried patience of an animal that has nowhere better to be and all the time in the world to get there. They are not hostile. They are not interested in you. They are simply *there*, in numbers, with the indifference of things that have claimed a place and do not feel they need to justify their presence. When a new section of concrete is poured, they find its edges within hours. When the borer advances and the geometry of the chamber shifts, they retreat a few meters, resettle, and within a season have mapped every joint and seam of the new surface. They have worked out traffic patterns. They have, the old foreman says, favorite routes. No one has verified this. No one would think to.

In the standing water — the puddles in the tie-rod holes, the slow channels along the floor, the darker pools in the older corners where the silt has accumulated into something approaching soil — there are pale grubs. They are translucent in the sodium light, or perhaps that is the light's fault. They wriggle when the water is disturbed and go still when it is not. No one has given them a name. They are part of the water the way the silt is part of the water.

And in the water, occasionally, something surfaces. The men disagree about what it is. A kind of pale lizard, perhaps, the younger men say — something that has come down through cracks from a warmer place, or up from a depth that no one has plumbed. A kind of fish, the older men say, and shake their heads as if the distinction matters. It is small. It is the color of the silt. It surfaces to consider the lights with flat, disinterested eyes, holds its position with a stillness that is not quite the stillness of an animal and not quite the stillness of a stone, and then is gone. It does not seem to be eating. It does not seem to be doing anything in particular. It is, like the celery, simply more itself than anything else here.

The rats are more themselves than they want to be. They are everywhere and they are skittish and they are hungry in the particular way of animals that live well but do not feel secure. They are the size of city rats, the color of city rats, and they are not the surprise. The surprise is what lives alongside them.

Along the cable trays overhead, in the looping intestines of the ventilation, in the seams where the steel I-beams meet the raw stone of the unfinished wall — there move long sinuous shapes. The men have given up trying to name them. They are not snakes, exactly, though they have the length of snakes. They are not eels, though they have something of an eel's liquid grace. They move along the cables with a slow deliberation that is unsettling because it does not look like effort; they look like poured water that has decided to go somewhere. They disappear into holes in the raw stone wall — holes that no one has investigated, holes that may go deeper than the unfinished tunnel itself — and reappear, sometimes, hours later, sometimes days. They have not been seen to eat. They have not been seen to fight. They have been seen, twice, in the recorded memory of the place, to do something that looked like mating, but the witnesses disagreed on what they had seen and the disagreement was not resolved. They are the largest animals here, except for the men, and they are the least known. They are not hostile. They have, as far as anyone can tell, no opinion of the men at all. They are simply another condition of the place, like the heat, like the wet.

The bats are smaller and older. They hang in clusters from the highest reaches of the steel beams, just at the edge of the light, where the sodium glow gives up and the dark begins. They were here before the construction began, the old foreman says, though no one believes him. The chamber was carved out of solid basalt; there were no bats in solid basalt. But the foreman says it, and the foreman has worked here since the beginning, and the bats are there, and they are not new. They are small and brown and they do not move during the day. At night, when the work crews are gone and the machinery has gone quiet and the only sounds are the fans and the drip of the water and the slow breathing of the rock, they fly. They fly in the chamber. They fly down the unfinished tunnel. They fly, somehow, into and out of places that should not be accessible to them. They eat something. There must be insects, somewhere, that they are eating. No one has found the insects. The bats have been doing this for as long as anyone has been watching, and they will be doing it when the next ten meters of stone are eaten by the borer, and the ten meters after that, and the chamber becomes a platform, and the platform becomes a station, and the station becomes a stop on a line that carries people from where they are to where they want to be. The bats will still be flying, somewhere in the structure, in the cavities between the finished concrete and the unfinished rock, in the small warm dark places that the architecture will preserve for them whether the architects know it or not.

And the men.

The men come down in shifts, and each one is already tired before he gets here. They come down the long ramp or the slow hoist, depending on which platform is closer, and by the time they step off onto the steel plating or the older concrete their shirts are already sticking to their backs. They are mostly long-term. Some are second-generation — sons of men who worked the first platform on a different line, twenty years ago, and who grew up hearing about underground concrete and warm rock and the particular patience of carving a city into the belly of the earth. They move at the pace the rock allows them to move. They pour a section. They wait for it to cure. They advance the borer ten meters. They wait for the surveyors. They pour another section. They have learned to step around the cockroaches. They have learned not to set their hard hats down on a bench without looking first. They have learned that the brown smear on the cable they were about to grab is alive, and they grab the next one over. They have learned that the thing in the water is there and is not theirs to do anything about. They have learned that the long shapes overhead are not their problem. They have learned, the older ones, that the place is becoming itself, and that becoming itself takes longer than the funding cycles allow for, and longer than the permit cycles allow for, and that they will be here, in this chamber, when the next platform is joined to it, and the next, and they will still be here when the celery has grown another inch and the moss has reached a little farther up the wall and the cockroaches have worked out a new traffic pattern around the new obstacle.

They are the most aware of the inhabitants, and the most temporary. They speak of the line in the patient, slightly amused tone of men who have heard the same projected completion date revised downward many times. They say the celery has put on an inch. They say the moss has crept up another few centimeters. They say the long shape was on the south cable tray last Tuesday, was in the raw stone on Wednesday, was back on the cable tray by Friday, and shrug, because shrugs are what you do when you have noticed something and there is nothing to be done about it. They say the bats were flying last night, the way a farmer says the crows were in the corn. They mention these things to each other, and to the new men, and the new men listen, and the new men do not yet understand that what is being described is not a curiosity but a census.

What lives here is becoming itself. The cockroaches are working out the new traffic pattern. The moss is reaching. The celery is thriving in its inexplicable way. The thing in the water is considering the lights with its flat eyes, and considering them again, and will consider them tomorrow, and the day after. The long shapes are moving through the infrastructure of the place with the slow patience of things that have no schedule but their own. The bats are flying, somewhere, in the dark that the chamber will always have, in the small warm spaces that the construction will make and that the construction will never quite fill.

And the men are pouring concrete, and waiting for it to cure, and watching the place become what it is becoming, and being — for as long as they are down here, and for as long as they are alive to remember it — the most temporary and the most observant of all the things that live in the warm dim wet cathedral that is not yet a tunnel.
