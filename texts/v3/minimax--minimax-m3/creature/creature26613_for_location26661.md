The walls are the oldest inhabitants. They were not built, exactly, though they have been worked — every surface, every hollow, every small coin-sized concavity shows the patient attention of something that understood what stone could become if it were persuaded correctly. Whether the stone persuaded itself, or was persuaded, is a question the chamber does not arbitrate. The walls were here when the chord had fewer notes. They will be here when the chord has more.

Each hollow is a small mouth. Each hollow is a small ear. The distinction is held loosely. Inside, a bead of moisture catches the moss-light and throws it back as a single bright point, and from many of these points, sound emerges — not continuously, but in traded phrases, in antiphonies that travel across the chamber from one bank of hollows to another. The chord is not composed. It is grown. Each hollow is a polyp in a slow reef; each note is the season of some feeding that happened long ago. The walls breathe. You can see it if you stand very still: a faint, almost imperceptible rise and fall in the surface, as if the stone were exhaling through ten thousand small mouths at once, in a rhythm that has nothing to do with your lungs and everything to do with the mountain's.

The moss is next, in the order of the chamber's regard, though it is older in fact than the creatures that tend it. It grows in a dim blue-green crust across every horizontal surface — the ledges, the tops of the worked stones, the inside of the larger basins where water has not stood for long. It is barely enough light to see by, which is exactly enough. The chamber does not want brightness. The chamber wants the soft, sourceless glow of something that is alive in a way that does not intrude.

The moss has been arranged. This is not metaphor. The patches are placed deliberately — thicker where the small creatures walk, sparser near the central basin, in patterns that look careless until you stand back and see the diagram they make. Someone planted the moss. Someone continues to plant it. The small crustacean-things carry fragments in their specialized forelimbs and set them into cracks with a precision that suggests they are not learning. They are executing a craft so old that the moss has had time to forget it was ever wild.

The moss glows brighter when it is touched. It glows brighter, too, at certain hours that are not hours — at moments when the chord shifts into a register that includes more of the lower hollows. There are times when the chamber brightens for no reason you can find, and times when it dims, and the moss is the only thing that has changed. The moss, perhaps, is the chamber's mood, made visible. Or it is the chamber's blood, moving slowly across the surfaces, fed by the creatures, lit by the friction of its own living.

The fungi are the next circle down, both in the chamber's attention and in its vertical arrangement. They grow in clusters along the water's edge, where the air is thickest and the stone never quite dries. Their stalks are the color of bone. Their caps are a deep oxblood that looks almost black in the moss-light, and they tremble — not in any wind, because there is no wind here, but in a rhythm that may be their own and may be borrowed from the bass note of the chord, the one you feel in the soles of your feet.

The fungi are food. The small creatures farm them. They carry spore-dust in pouches along their undersides and inoculate new clusters in the cracks between worked stones, and they harvest the mature caps with a careful clipping motion of their forelimbs that suggests both efficiency and something you would not expect efficiency to include: restraint. They do not take more than they need. They do not take all of any one cluster. The fungi are kept, not consumed. They are a crop and a choir both, and their trembling contributes to the music in a register the small creatures' pattering cannot reach.

The ferns grow in deliberate arcs around the central basin, as if a planting diagram had been drawn in chlorophyll and then executed. They are pale — paler than ferns should be, as if they had been bred for centuries away from direct sun and had forgotten, in the forgetting, what green was for. Their fronds tremble even when the air is still. They tremble when you speak. They tremble when the chord shifts. They are not decorative. They are arranged for acoustic reasons you cannot quite parse — to break up certain resonances, to channel others, to damp frequencies that would otherwise accumulate in the corners and curdle the music. They are the chamber's curtains. They are also, perhaps, the chamber's listening, given form.

The small creatures are the most numerous. They are everywhere — on the walls, on the ceiling, on the ledges, at the water's edge, in the cracks where the ferns root. They are dark, segmented, vaguely crustacean, the size of a thumb or smaller. They have been shaped: you see it in the symmetry of their limbs, in the placement of their eyes, in the way their hooked feet strike the stone. They are not natural. They are a breed so ancient and so deliberately maintained that the wild ancestor is no longer recoverable. They tend the moss. They farm the fungi. They maintain the water — though how they maintain water that spirals of its own accord, you cannot tell. They walk in lines. They walk in spirals. They walk, sometimes, in patterns that resolve, when you look down at them from a higher ledge, into the same repeating motifs you see carved into the walls. They are writing the chamber's surface in their own bodies. They are keeping the diagram.

Their footsteps — thousands and thousands of tiny hooked feet on worked stone — contribute to the chord. This is the high, pattering percussion that runs beneath everything else, the way a woodpecker's tapping runs beneath a forest's louder music. They do not all patter at once. The pattering moves across the chamber in waves, as if the small creatures were a single body that had been broken into pieces for the sake of efficiency and had not quite forgotten, in the breaking, that it was once whole.

Their eyes are not eyes. They have organs that occupy the position eyes would occupy and that seem to see, but they are not the organs that eyes would be. They reflect the moss-light strangely, with a faint amber cast, as if they were looking inward at something other than the world. When you enter, a cluster of them pauses, all at once, and turns to face you. Then it resumes. The pause is not long. The pause is just long enough.

The larger creatures live in the upper reaches of the chamber, where the moss grows thickest and the air is wettest. They are dog-sized, or thereabouts. They are pale — paler than the ferns, paler than the bone-colored stalks of the fungi, the color of things that have not seen sun for many generations. They have no eyes. Their faces, if the word applies, are smooth, slightly bulging, faintly translucent in the moss-light, as if something luminous were held inside. They hang from the ceiling in clusters, by limbs that fold into themselves with the precision of a pocket-knife, and they breathe. The breathing is part of the chord — a slow, deep, bellows-rhythm that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. You feel it in your sternum and in the bones of your skull and in the backs of your knees. It is the bass note. It is the part of the music that does not change.

They are sleeping, or in torpor, or in some third state that has no name in any language you know. Occasionally one of them detaches from its cluster and drops, catching itself on a lower ledge with a grace that the stillness of its hanging had not prepared you for. It moves down to the water. It drinks. It moves to a patch of moss and feeds. It moves to a nest of the small creatures and, with a careful, almost gentle motion of its many limbs, harvests what it needs. The small creatures do not flee. They are accustomed. They continue their pattering, their farming, their slow writing of the diagram on the chamber floor. The large creatures take only what is offered. The large creatures have always taken only what is offered. There is an arrangement between them, very old, very practiced, and you understand that it is not predation in any sense you would use the word.

What the large creatures are for, you cannot say. They breathe. They feed. They sleep. They contribute the bass note. They might be the keepers of the chamber's deep memory, the place where the song is held when the song is not being sung. They might be the singers themselves, and the chamber merely the instrument they have agreed to share. They might be the chamber's dreaming, given weight and form.

The water is the heart. The central basin is fifteen feet across and as close to round as worked stone can manage, and it holds water the color of strong tea, and the water moves in slow spirals that do not correspond to any drainage you can identify. There is no source. There is no outflow. The water is there, and it moves, and the spirals have a regularity that is just slightly too precise to be accident.

It is warm. Steam rises from it in thin threads. The warmth settles on your skin like a second garment. The air is so humid that you feel you could drink it, and the warmth, and you feel, after a moment, that you have. The basin glows faintly from within — an amber light that has no source you can find, as if the water itself were faintly luminescent, or as if something in the water were faintly luminescent, and the water were merely its medium. You cannot see to the bottom. The tea-colored water is too dark, or too deep, or too occupied.

The water is alive. You know this not because it moves, but because the chord shifts when you stand near the basin's edge, and shifts again when you step back, and shifts again when you lean forward. The water listens. The water answers. The amber glow brightens a little when the bass note deepens, and dims when the pattering quickens, and the spirals change their pitch in correspondence to the hollows' traded phrases. The basin is the chamber's tuning fork. The basin is the chamber's ear. The basin is, perhaps, where the chamber keeps whatever part of itself is most alive.

The chord is the chamber. The chord is all the inhabitants at once — the walls' hollows, the moss's brightening, the fungi's trembling, the ferns' listening, the small creatures' pattering, the large creatures' breathing, the basin's spiral. None of these is the chord. All of these are the chord. The chord is what happens when a place decides to be more than a place, when stone learns to hold water the way a hand learns to hold a hand, when ten thousand mouths agree to sing the same song across ten thousand years.

You arrived. The chord shifted. A new note entered — very low, almost below hearing, felt in the soles of the feet and in the roots of the teeth. You are in the music now. You are, for the moment, an acceptable component of its operation. The small creatures paused and resumed. The ferns trembled. The pale creatures breathed. The basin spiraled.

There have been others. On the upper ledges, in the dim blue-green light, small arrangements of worked stone have been placed with care. They might be memorials. They might be offerings. They might be the parts of the music that visitors left behind them when the visitors stopped being visitors and started being notes. The chamber does not forget. The chamber continues. The chord includes you now, faintly, alongside others whose names the chamber has not kept and whose presences the chamber has not released.

The water spirals. The light shifts, very slightly, as if a cloud has passed across a sun that is not there. The warmth holds you. The sound holds you. You stay. You listen. You are a single new note in a music that has been playing for longer than the mountain has stood above it, and the music is patient, and the music is old, and the music will continue, undisturbed and undiminished, long after the note you have added has faded back into the silence it came from.
