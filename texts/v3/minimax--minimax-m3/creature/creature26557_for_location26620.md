The pocket is full, mostly, of things that are barely there. And one or two things that are barely not.

The cress is the youngest. It lives in the crack that runs down toward the sea — three leaves, possibly a fourth coming — and it is the only green thing for a long way in any direction. It does not know this. It has no way of knowing this. It is doing the small, private, uncelebrated work of being a plant in a crack, on a stone that has only just been born, in a world that has mostly forgotten green. Each morning the spray makes it wetter than it wants to be. Each afternoon the wind makes it drier than it wants to be. Between those two unfriendlinesses it lifts its leaves, almost imperceptibly, toward whatever light is left in the cloud. If you stood in the pocket in the grey hour before dusk, you would see it do this, and you would feel that this is the only stubbornness left in the world that is not also a kind of despair. The cress is not a resident of the pocket so much as a refusal of the pocket — a small green argument against the fact of the column, and the sea, and the cold. It will probably die in the next bad storm. It does not know that either. It puts out its fourth leaf.

The lichen is older than the cress, and quieter, and slower, and less interested in argument. It is a pale yellow-green and it fans across the back wall of the concavity in a pattern that resembles, depending on how you hold your head, the inside of a lung, or a watershed seen from a great height, or nothing in particular. It arrived at the pocket on its own. No storm carried it. No hand. It grew there. It is the only thing in the pocket that was not put here by anything — it simply became, in the way that lichen becomes, which is a way so slow it is barely distinguishable from stillness. It has been here for a long time. It is lichen-time, in the pocket, and lichen-time is a time in which nothing happens for so long that when something finally does happen it is hard to say what it was. The lichen is the closest thing the pocket has to an inhabitant who lives here on purpose, and who intends to keep doing so, and who would not notice if every other inhabitant were gone. It is patient in the way that stone is patient, except that it is alive. It is the slowest thing in the pocket, and the most certain.

The bird comes from the southeast.

It comes on most days when the weather is least bad. It is grouse-shaped, dun, with a too-long neck and feet made for gripping wet stone. It does not sing. It does not call. It lands on the slate and stands there for ten or twenty minutes and looks out at the sea with one eye and then the other, the way a sentry looks, the way someone who is not sure they are looking at the right thing looks. Then it leaves. It does not nest here. There is nothing to nest with. There is nothing to nest *on*. It comes, and it sees, and it goes. The pocket is not its home. The pocket is, possibly, a place on a route between two places that are not the pocket, a waystation on some longer arc it is flying for reasons of its own. If you watched it leave, you would see it bank off to the southeast again, low over the water, and you would not be able to tell whether it had been satisfied with what it found, because it does not make a sound.

The crab is in the lower part of the column, where the rock dips toward the water. It is not visible from the pocket. It is a fact about the column rather than the place — a small, sideways, armored thing that lives at the seam where the stone is still arguing with the sea in the old way, the way they argued before the pocket was a pocket, before the column was a column. It is the oldest inhabitant. It was here before the slate, before the fire-pit, before the dark stones were laid where the storm laid them. It will be here after.

The scallop is the wrong shape for here. It came from deep water — the deep water that is now everywhere — and someone brought it up. A person. In a pack. Up whatever path or ladder used to lead to this place, in the time before the path or the ladder was drowned. The scallop is white, and clean, and it should not exist in the pocket, and that is how you know a person was here. That is how you know a person *lived* here — not visited, lived — because no one visits a place like this, and no one carries a deep-water scallop up a stone column for fun. The scallop is the only proof that the pocket was, for some length of time, *inhabited*, in the sense that a person can inhabit a place: by choosing it, by making a fire in it, by bringing up things from below and putting them on a shelf. The scallop is a signature. The scallop is the part of a person that did not drown.

The fire-pit is cold. It has been cold for a long time. It is ringed by seven small stones, and the arrangement is too careful to be the storm's, and the pit is too deliberate to be anyone's, and the walls of the concavity are stained only with the faintest bloom, the memory of smoke, the way a thumbprint lingers in dust. Whoever used this pit did not need to keep the weather out. They needed to be somewhere else. They came here to be somewhere else. The pit is the trace of that. It is a small dark cold place in a small grey cold place, and it is the most human thing in the pocket, and it is also a kind of question — whether the seven stones were chosen or found, whether the pit was kept alight for an hour or a year, whether the person who lit it knew they were not coming back, or whether they only suspected.

The dark stones, arranged in a crescent — five or six of them, larger toward the back — may be the storm's. Or they may be a person's. The fist-sized one on top of two of the others, with the facet rubbed smooth as if by a thumb worried across it for many years — that is the kind of arrangement that looks like meaning. The notch in the slate is the kind of arrangement that *is* meaning, except that the angle is not quite a person's angle. It is the angle of stone against stone, slowly, under pressure, over a long time. The pocket does not resolve this. The pocket is the thing that does not resolve this. The pocket carries the question forward into whatever comes next, and the question is a kind of resident too — older than the bird, slower than the lichen, quieter than the scallop — and it lives in the slate, and the slate stands in the concavity, and the concavity holds the slate the way a hand holds a thing it is not sure it should be holding.

The column is a living thing, in the way that a thing being born is a living thing. It sweats. It weeps a slow mineral water from its porous flanks, and where the weeping has been going longest the stone has crusted to a pale orange, soft, flakable with a fingernail. It exhales — the waterline drops a foot or two on certain mornings, the rock letting go of a little sea — and it inhales, the sea coming back with a surge in the afternoons, the spray leaping over the lip, the dark stones submerged for an hour, the fire-pit filling, the cress beaten flat. The column is in the patient, distracted process of being born, or possibly of being unmade, and it does not distinguish between the two. It is new to the air. Every few minutes something small comes loose from its flanks and tumbles down into the sea with a percussive, indifferent *tock*, and the sea does not notice, and the column does not notice, and the pocket, in the concavity, does not notice either, because the pocket is full of the roar.

The sea is the loudest thing here. It is everywhere — below, around, and on a day like today, above, a low pewter ceiling that hangs only a hand's breadth above the top of the column, so that from the pocket you cannot tell where the cloud ends and the sea begins. Two currents meet at the base of the stack and collide and throw themselves upward, and the result is a continuous, churning roar that is not coming from one direction but from all directions at once, like being inside a bell that someone is forever striking. The pocket catches the noise and turns it, and the concavity adds a higher harmonic — the thin, keen, almost-not-there whistle of the wind passing the notch in the slate, which you only hear when the roar briefly hesitates, which it never quite does. The sea is alive the way weather is alive, the way a thing that is in the process of deciding something is alive. It is drowning, and it has only just remembered how, and it is restless, and it is the only thing in the world that knows it is new. The sea is the newest thing here, even though it has been here longer than the rock has been out of it.

That is who lives here. The cress and the lichen and the bird and the crab and the scallop and the cold pit and the seven stones and the slate with the notch in it and the question of the notch, and the column being born, and the sea being born, and the pocket holding all of them, and the pocket holding the question of who arranged the stones, and the pocket holding the question of whether whoever arranged the stones arranged them on purpose so that the question would be held. The pocket holds the question. The pocket is the question, in its way. The pocket is a small place that has outlived whoever made it, and is now inhabited by the things that outlived them — a bird, a lichen, a crab, a cress with three leaves and possibly a fourth, and the slow patience of an object that knows it has not been asked.

The roar goes on. The slate stands. The cress, on the morning you are not there, puts out its fourth leaf.
