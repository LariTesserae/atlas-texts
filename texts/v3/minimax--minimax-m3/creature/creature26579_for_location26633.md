The insects are the first thing. They are the first thing because they are the most numerous, and because they are the foundation of the sound. They live in the moss, in the bark, in the wet air, in the cracks of the dark stone, in the soft mattress of fallen leaves. They are not a colony. They are not a hive. They are ten thousand small bright timers, each one running its own cycle, and the cycles do not align, and the misalignment is the sound you hear when the wind dies down in a clearing. The sound has no name in the languages the people use, because the sound is the background against which names are heard, and you do not name the air.

They are the size of a thumbnail, most of them, and pale, almost translucent, with wings that catch the green light. Some of them glow the way the mushrooms glow — a faint blue-green, not enough to read by, but enough to mark a hand if you hold it still. They chirr. The chirr is not a song. It is a count. Each insect is counting something — seconds, heartbeats, the passing of a wet warm minute — and it does not know that the others are counting, and it does not need to know. The sum of the counts is the hum. When you stop hearing them as individuals and start hearing them as a whole, you are hearing the plateau breathe.

There are other small things underfoot, in the moss. Things that are not insects but that move like insects, in fits and starts. Things that glow in the rings where the mushrooms grow, as if the rings are lighting them. Mites, perhaps, or something that has not been named by the ecologists who came up once and gave up. They are very small. They are very numerous. They are part of the woven sound, or they make a different sound, finer, that you only hear if you put your ear to the ground. The people put their ears to the ground sometimes. They say the fine sound is older than the insects. They might be right.

In the trees, larger things move. You see them as shadows when the leaves turn silver. The long-legged one is the most often seen. It stands as tall as a man at the shoulder, and its legs are twice that, and it walks the high branches the way a man walks a path, slowly, with a kind of thought in each step. It is not a deer. It is not a primate. It is something the canopy made, over a long time, in the wet and the green. Its coat is dark, almost the color of the stone, and its legs are pale, almost the color of the trunks, and it does not seem to be in a hurry because it has never had reason to be. It eats the silver undersides of the leaves when the forest turns, and it drinks from the pools when it descends, which is rarely. It has young, once every several years — the young are born with their legs already long, already knowing how to walk the branches, already patient. The people do not hunt it. The people do not hunt much. The long-legged thing does not seem to mind the people, or to notice them, or to be anything other than what it is.

The winged thing is rarer. It is much larger than a bird should reasonably be. It crosses a clearing and is gone before you can focus on it, and what you remember is the shape — a long, slow shape, with a wingspan that blocks out a piece of the green light — and the single soft displacement of air that comes a moment after. It does not chirr. It does not hum. It is silent, and its silence is felt as a small hole in the woven sound of the insects, a brief absence. It might be a predator. It might be a scavenger. It might be something that has not been named because no one has seen it long enough to name it. The people have seen it. The people do not speak of it often.

There are other things in the trees. Small things, quick things, things that rustle and are gone. Things that come out at night, when the mushrooms are glowing, and that the glow reveals as brief, bright shapes — a face, a wing, a leg. The ecologists tried to count them. The ecologists came back with new words and a kind of tiredness in their eyes, and they did not try again.

In the pools, at the bottoms of the ravines, there are shapes.

You have been down there, once. You have not described what you saw, and you will not, here. What can be said is this: they are long, and they are slow, and they are warm the way the stone is warm, and they are not in a hurry. They do not come up. They have not come up in any living memory, including the memory of the people who have been here longest. The light that comes up from below, on certain days, is sometimes their light. The hum that the structures give off when you touch them might be their hum. The plateau might be the roof of whatever they are in, and they might be what the plateau is the roof of, and the wind that falls off the edges might be their breath, or their thought, or the sound of them being patient.

They are patient. They have been more patient than anything else here, and they are likely to outlast everything that walks on top. The people leave offerings at the pools sometimes — a stone, a leaf, a small cairn at the edge. The offerings sink, slowly. The people do not know if the pool-things notice. The pool-things do not come up. The pool-things go on.

There are people.

They are not many. There have never been many. The plateau does not support a crowd, and the wind is loud, and the food is mostly mushrooms and the silver sides of leaves and the small things that live in the moss, and a crowd would eat the plateau bare in a year. The people are dark-skinned from the constant wet and the thin sun, and lean, and they move slowly, and they do not speak above the hum unless they have to. They weave cloth from the pale bark of the trees, and the cloth stays damp, and they do not seem to mind. Their hair grows long, and sometimes moss or small ferns take root in it, and the people let them. They have been here a long time. Some of them remember the first ecologists, who came with their packs and their notebooks and their questions, and who went away with new words. Some of them do not remember anything before the plateau. They are not sure they are different from the plateau. They are not sure it matters.

They live in small clusters, in the groves at the edge of the forest, where the rock is warm and the pools are nearby. They sleep on the stone, or in the moss, and they do not build walls, because the wind would take the walls and the plateau does not ask for walls. They cook, sometimes, in the pools — the water is warm enough. They eat raw more often than not. The mushrooms are good raw. The silver sides of leaves are good raw. The small things in the moss are good raw. They drink from the flat rocks in the early morning, when the condensation beads and the rocks sweat. They are rarely thirsty. They are rarely cold. They are always damp. They do not seem to mind the damp. The damp is what they are made of, by this point.

The children are born knowing the sound. The children are born knowing the moss, and the silver of the leaves, and the warm dark stone. The children are born with the hum already in them, and the hum grows louder as they grow, and by the time they are old enough to walk the plateau alone, the hum is the loudest thing they hear. Some of the children go away. There are paths down, longer than the paths up, and the people know them, and the children who go away take the ecologists' new words with them, and the plateau permits them to go, and the children do not come back, or they come back years later, and the plateau permits them to be seen again, and they are changed, and the plateau is the same, and the wind is the same, and the hum is the same.

The people who stay have roles. The roles are not chosen. The plateau gives them, or the hum gives them, or the people find them in themselves, and the finding is slow, and the role becomes the person, and the person becomes the role, and the line between them blurs the way a cairn blurs in the rain.

The cairn-tenders walk the plateau and tend the lines of small stones. The cairns do not stay tended. You count them today, and you count them tomorrow, and the numbers do not match. A cairn is here, and then it is not, and then it is somewhere else, and the stones are warm, and the stones are the same dark stone as the plateau, and the cairn-tenders rebuild what is missing, move what has shifted, and do not ask why. They have been doing this for as long as anyone can recall. They are slightly deaf in one ear from years of listening for the stones. They say the stones are breathing. They say the cairns are for the dead, but the dead here do not stay dead in the usual way, and the cairns are not for the usual dead. They say the cairns are for the pool-things, and the pool-things shift them, and the cairn-tenders are only keeping the lines neat for something that does not require neatness. They do not seem unhappy. They walk slowly. They carry small stones in the folds of their damp cloth. They are patient with the stones the way the stones are patient with them.

The door-walkers know the doors. There are walls with no doors, and doors with no walls, and platforms that rise from the rock with no steps and no way up, and the door-walkers know which doors are open today, and which are closed, and which open onto the same room from both sides, though the room is round. They do not explain how they know. They walk the plateau in the early morning, when the air is thickest and the structures hum loudest under their fingers, and they mark the doors that have changed with a small piece of pale bark tucked into a crack.
