The bacteria live in the seam.

This is the truest answer, and the smallest one, and you will not see them if you look. They live where the steel trough meets the poured concrete of the floor, in a hairline gap that the overflow keeps wet and that no one has ever thought to swab, because the gap is too narrow to notice and no one is looking. The gap is a city, if a city is a place where many small lives are conducted in close quarters without anyone above them knowing. The bacteria are not one species. They are a stratified community, layered by who can tolerate what. The ones pressed against the wet metal are iron-oxidizers, drawing their living from the slow rusting of the trough's underside. The ones a millimeter further out are sulfur-cyclers, working the faint mineral trace the water leaves behind. The ones in the perpetually flooded zone are anaerobes, breathing what they cannot see. And the ones at the very edge of the seam, where the surface dries for an hour or two between overflows, are spore-formers, drawing themselves down into their small hard cases and waiting out the dryness, and waking when the water returns, which the water always does. They have been there since the water was first turned on. They will be there long after the bulbs have burned out and the wooden crate has gone soft on the floor. They do not know they are in a room. They do not know what a room is. They are the only inhabitants of this place who have never had to be loud to be heard.

The water lives here in transit, and transit is all it has.

It arrives vertical, expressed, hammered into a white rod of itself by whatever is pushing it from above, and the rod is not so much falling as being forced out — you can tell because a thing that falls is pulled by its own weight, and a thing that is expressed is pushed by something else's, and the water here is pushed. The moment the rod touches the basin it shatters. It becomes spray and column and mist and a hundred separate sheens on a hundred separate surfaces, and it has lost its single voice and gained a hundred smaller ones, and it lives in the room now for perhaps four seconds before it tips over the basin's lip, slides down the curved trough, and falls again into the chamber below. Four seconds. That is the water's full tenure in this place. In those four seconds it is everything. It is the sound. It is the cold. It is the moving skin of condensation on every bolt head and every flange. It is the small rivers running between the floor plates. It is the room's weather, and the only weather this room has ever had. It does not know the shape of the pipes it came from. It does not know the shape of the turbines waiting for it below, or the river that will receive it after, or the field, or the city. It knows only the basin and the trough and the air and the cold metal and the fact of being broken — of being many where it was one, of being noise where it was signal. In those four seconds it is the largest animal in the room. It is not an animal. It is not alive. It is still the only thing in the room that moves with intention, and the intention is not its own, and this is the part of the water that is most like an animal.

The sound is not alive, but it is the largest inhabitant, and it was here before the water was, because the room was built to give it a body.

The room is the body of the sound. The I-beams overhead are the sound's ribs. The basin is the sound's open mouth. The water is the sound's tongue. The trough is the sound's throat. The whole construction is a kind of throat cut into the mountain, and the sound that comes out of it is the mountain speaking in a voice it did not have before the room was made. The sound lives in the rivets. You can feel it if you press a fingertip to one — a small individual shiver, the rivet buzzing against your skin with the patience of something that has been buzzing for a long time and intends to keep buzzing. The sound lives in the pressure gauge's needle, which cannot move because the sound holds it still, the way a held hand holds a small bird. The sound lives in your sternum. The sound is the first thing any creature that ever entered this room heard, and the last thing it will hear, and if the bacteria could hear, the sound would be the only god they would know — vast, constant, unanswerable, indifferent, the same at the first second as at the ten-thousandth, the same now as it was before you arrived and as it will be after you leave. The sound does not know you are here. The sound does not know the bacteria are here. The sound does not know anything, and this is the part of the sound that is most like a god.

The rust is the slowest inhabitant, and the only one with time on its side.

It lives in the gunite beads, in the place where the ceiling meets the walls, and it is weeping now — a thin rust-colored sweat that is the first color the room has ever put on, the way a body that has been still too long begins to show its bruises. The rust is the room learning to die. The rust is the room's hair growing. It moves at a pace too slow to see in any one second, but visible if you could hold a year in your hand and pass it across the wall the way you would pass a thumb across a coin. The rust on the rim of the blue valve wheel is the record of hands that have gripped it. The rust on the bolts is the record of hands that have not. The rust on the floor is the record of the water standing longest. The rust does not come and go the way the water does. The rust only adds. The rust is patient. The rust will outlast you, and the bulbs, and the wooden crate, and the sound if the sound ever stops, and when the room is finally decommissioned and the water is shut off, the rust will be the last living thing in the room, and it will still be moving, and it will not know it has won.

The bulbs live here in a kind of half-life.

Two of them are hazed over with condensation gathered on the inside of their cages, and the light they throw is not light so much as a dim orange-tinted guess at illumination, a kind of soft uncertain glow that is more like a memory of light than light itself. The third bulb is out. The third bulb is still a body in the room. It is dead, and it is still there, and it is still warm if you put your hand near it, faintly, the way a body is still warm after the breathing has stopped. The bulbs are the only things in the room that are trying. They are trying to make a small climate inside their cages, a small wet world fed by the warmth of the filament and the coolness of the air, and the world is dim and orange and the caged light is the color of an old wound, and the bulbs are the room's small failing hearts, and no one is listening to them, and they keep beating.

The wooden crate in the corner is the softest thing here, and the most vulnerable.

The wood is dark and swollen. The wood is alive in the way that dead things are alive — it is being eaten, slowly, by the moisture, by the bacteria, by the room itself. The crate is the only object in the room that is changing shape. The crate will, in time, be a darker crate. In more time, a softer one. In more time, a heap of dark fibers on the floor. In more time, nothing. The crate is the only thing in the room that is on a clock, and the clock is the room.

The pressure gauge lives in the room as a number that is not being read. Its face is fogged. Its needle is steady. The gauge is a small analog heart, the only one in the room besides the bulbs, and its stillness is the room's pulse — regular, indifferent, the only metronome the space has. The numerals on its face are illegible. The needle does not move. The needle has decided what the pressure is, and the pressure has agreed, and the two of them are locked in a small unchanging agreement that is the closest thing the room has to a thought.

The mist lives in the room as a tongue.

It drifts. It moves in slow tongues across the room, gauze-thick, catching the dim light and turning it into something that is not quite light and not quite shadow but a third thing, a moving weather. Where the mist touches your face it is cold and tastes of iron and stone. The mist is the most intimate inhabitant. The water is too loud to touch you gently. The sound is too constant to be a presence. The rust is too slow. The mist is the one that arrives on your skin, the one that knows where your face is, the one that lays a small cold finger along your jaw and your cheekbone and the bridge of your nose and is gone, and then is back, and is gone, and is back. The mist is the room's breath on you. The mist is the room's only way of saying something to the only thing in it that could understand.

And you.

You are the one who lives here loudest, and the loudest you are is the quietest you are, because the room eats your sound before it can travel. You shout and the shout is dissolved and returned to you as a meaningless vibration in the wet air. You live in the room as a temperature — your body heat is a small warm patch in the coolness, a soft moving bubble of thirty-six-and-something degrees, and the air takes it from you and dilutes it and gives it back to you as the cold on the back of your neck. You live in the room as a smell — your own sweat, your own breath, the wool or cotton or synthetic of your coat, the small mammalian exhalations that this room has not smelled in a long time, if ever. You live in the room as a witness, and a witness is the only kind of inhabitant that needs to be conscious to count. You stand on the metal floor with your hands at your sides. You are the only thing in the room that knows what the room is for. The water does not know. The bacteria do not know. The rust does not know. The sound does not know. The bulbs do not know. The mist does not know. You know. You are the only thing here that holds the question, and the question is so loud inside your own skull that it survives the room's eating of every other sound, and the question is *what am I doing in this throat in this mountain listening to this water go where it is going*, and you do not have an answer, and the water does not care, and the room continues, and the bacteria continue, and the rust continues, and the mist continues to drift across your shins, and the needle on the fogged gauge does not move, and the third bulb stays out, and the water comes, and the water goes, and the sound is the sound of going, and the only witness is you, and you are cold, and you are not going to be here long, and the room will not notice when you leave.
