# The Inhabitants of the Upper Stoup

---

The first thing to understand about the people of the Upper Stoup is that there is no word in their language for *stranger* and no word for *neighbor* and, as far as anyone can tell, no word for *I*. There are words for *this one* and *that one* and *the one who was here yesterday* and *the one who came from the east fold three winters ago and is still here*. The language does not privilege the individual. It does not even, in any strong sense, distinguish between persons and places. The same root that means *the stone at the corner of Fael's house* also means *Fael*, and also means *the cold that settles in the joints in late winter*. None of this is mystical. It is simply how a small language, spoken by a small people, in a small place, has organized itself. They have not had need of more.

---

There are perhaps a hundred and thirty people. Fewer than there were. The people know this without counting; they know it the way they know the weather, by the feel of the town on a given morning. Fewer children in the lanes. Fewer voices at the spring. The dogs, which always used to be seven or eight, are now four, and one of these is old. The oldest living person — a woman called, in the local way, *the one who was born in the lichen year*, because that year the lichen ran orange across the western rim and the people still speak of it — says she remembers when there were more houses lit at night, more smoke from the chimneys. She does not say this with grief. She says it the way she would say that the wind is from the north today.

The people are short. Not dramatically — not as a circus — but a hand's breadth shorter, on average, than the people who live a day's climb below. Their chests are deep and their shoulders are rounded forward, the posture of people who have spent their lives ducking under low lintels and hunching against wind. Their hands are large relative to their bodies, and their fingers are thick at the base, and the nails are often broken from digging in cold ground. Their faces are weathered in a particular way: not lined so much as *smoothed*, as if the wind has taken the sharpness off everything. The skin is dark, darker than one might expect at this altitude, though no one comes here to expect anything. The eyes are nearly always narrow, not from expression but from the brightness of the plateau when the sun finds it, and from the habit of peering into fog.

Their hair is black and very straight and it grows long, and both the men and the women wear it bound back from the face, in thick coils held with bone pins. The bone pins come from the small grey animals of the deep folds, or from birds; metal pins would be a luxury beyond imagining, and in any case would rust in the damp. The people look, to a stranger's eye, much alike. They are not much alike. The differences between them — the slight differences in height, in the set of the mouth, in the way a given person holds their shoulders — are known to one another in great detail. A stranger sees a people. The people see a population of distinct individuals, each of whom has a name, a history, a particular way of folding dough or carrying water or singing to a child.

---

They are quiet. This is the first thing the dogs will tell you, and the second thing the people will tell you, if you stay long enough to be told anything. The air of the plateau carries sound so far that loudness is a kind of violence — it reaches into every house, every folded valley, every quiet corner of the moss. So the people have learned to pitch their voices low, to speak close, to keep their tempers indoors where the walls swallow the noise. They are not a sullen people. They are not a frightened people. They are a people who have made a treaty with the air, and the terms of the treaty are: *do not waste the silence*. The silence is not empty. It is full of useful information. The sound of the spring means the water is running. The sound of the wind in a certain key means weather is changing. The sound of a particular dog shifting in its sleep means a stranger has come up the goat trail.

---

Their days are short in one sense and very long in another. They rise when the dogs rise, which is when the first bird in the ferns gives its single repeated note — *a fact*, the description calls it, and that is accurate, it is not a song, it is a statement, and the statement is *I am here*. The people leave their low doorways and step out into a town that is either swimming in fog or just emerging from it. If the latter, every surface is wet, and the sun, if there is sun, will find the droplets and the plateau will glitter for an hour. The people do not remark on this. They go about their work.

The work. There is always work. The work is the same work that has been done for as long as anyone can remember, and the tools are the same tools, and the methods are the same methods. Water must be fetched from the spring — carried down in stone jars, carried up in stone jars, hauled back to the houses, used for drinking and for the thin stews that are the staple of the diet. Moss must be gathered for mortar, and pounded, and mixed with the clay that is scraped from the deeper folds. Shrubs must be cut for fuel — the waxy-leaved, low-growing things that hug the ground in the lee of stones. Berries must be gathered in their brief season and laid down in the stone-lined pits, where they will keep through the winter if the winter is not too hard. The roofs must be checked after every wind, and the worst cracks chinked, and the beams that are failing must be propped, and the props will themselves fail in time and must be replaced.

There are the small grey animals. These are trapped by a few of the people — the ones who do not mind going out onto the open plateau, who do not mind the long walk into the broken country at the far edge of the rim. These people are slightly different from the others. They are quieter, in a particular way. They come back from their trips with their catch and with a look on their faces that the others recognize and do not ask about. The trappers are useful and they are necessary and they are slightly feared, in the way that anyone is feared who has stood alone in a place that does not know them.

There is weaving. There is always weaving. The wool comes from the small grey animals and is rough and short and must be worked many times to make it into anything wearable, but it is warm and it is what there is. The looms are simple wooden frames, salvaged from older looms, with new pieces lashed on as the old ones wear through. The sound of the loom is one of the characteristic sounds of the Upper Stoup, and a person who lives here can tell, from the rhythm of the clack, who is weaving and what mood they are in. A steady loom is a person at peace. An irregular loom is a person thinking hard. A loom that has stopped is a person who has stopped, for whatever reason, and the neighborhood will quietly notice.

There is the care of the dogs. The dogs are not pets. They are working animals and they are kin. They are fed before the people are fed, on the days when food is short, which is most days. They are allowed to sleep where they choose, which is usually in the doorways of the houses they have adopted, or in the lee of a particular stone they have decided is theirs. They are spoken to, in a low voice, and they are listened to. They know the weather. They know the approach of strangers. They know, in some way the people do not pretend to understand, when someone in the town is going to die. When this happens, a particular dog will begin to follow that person around, and will sit at their doorway, and will not be coaxed away, and the person will know, and the people around them will know, and there will be a quietness in that house for a few days, and then a sound from the flat stone by the spring.

---

The dying. The people of the Upper Stoup die as they have always died: in their houses, in the low-ceilinged rooms, on the tilted floors, with a family member or two nearby. They are not afraid of dying. They are afraid of specific things — of dying alone, of dying in the open plateau, of dying in a way that requires the flat stone to be struck and the whole town to gather. The flat stone is struck for deaths, and for births, and for the very rare arrival of a stranger. The people gather when it is struck, and they gather quickly, because the sound carries and because they are a small town and can gather quickly. They do not speak at these gatherings unless there is need. They stand, and they look, and they wait to see what is required of them.

The dead are not buried in the ground. The ground is stone. They are laid in the under-town — in the older houses, the ones that are now beneath the current houses, in the low passages that lead nowhere. The bodies are wrapped in woven cloth and placed on a stone shelf in one of these small rooms, and the room is closed with a flat stone, and that is that. The cold preserves them. The damp preserves them. Sometimes, in certain years, when an old wall is taken down for repair, a body is found from a generation ago, dried and dark and perfectly kept, and the people look at it and recognize something of the face and go back to their work.

---

The children. There are not many. A woman of the Upper Stoup will bear two children in her life, if she is fortunate, and sometimes only one, and sometimes none. The cold and the thin air and the difficulty of getting enough to eat take their toll. The children that are born are wanted fiercely. They are watched, in the quiet way that everything is watched here. They are carried in slings of woven wool from the day they are born, because the ground is too cold for a child to be put down, and the air is too thin for a child to be left to cry. They learn to speak by listening. The air carries sound far, and a child in a sling on its mother's back, walking through the town, hears fragments of a dozen conversations, and from these fragments the child learns what the people talk about, which is mostly: the weather, the work, the dogs, the spring, the small grey animals, the absent ones.

A child of four or five knows the lanes of the town by heart, both the above-town and the under-town. A child of six or seven knows the names of the dogs and the names of the people and the names of the stones. A child of ten can fetch water alone. A child of twelve can weave. A child of fourteen is nearly an adult, and is expected to begin the slow work of taking on the tasks of the aging — of fetching water for an old aunt, of mending a roof for an old uncle, of sitting with the dying.

The children play. This is important. They play a game that involves running between the stones and not being seen by the person who is *it*, and this game is played in the fog, and the children learn to be very quiet, and they learn the layout of the town in a way that no instruction could teach. They play a game with pebbles and a line scratched in the dirt, and the rules of this game are complicated and the children argue about the rules in low voices. They make small dolls out of moss and bone and they give them names and they lose them and find them again. They are children. They are also, even at six or seven, already moving at the speed of the place.

---

The oldest person — *the one who was born in the lichen year* — is a small woman with very white hair and very dark eyes and a way of standing very still that makes her seem, for a moment, like one of the stones of the town. She is not the leader. There is no leader. She is the one who remembers the most, and the people go to her when there is a question about how a thing was done before, or how a thing was said before, or what the shape of a particular stone used to be. She sits by her hearth, in a room that smells of dried berries and damp wool, and she answers in a low voice, and the people who come to her sit on the floor and listen, and the dogs lie nearby and do not move.

She does not know how old she is. The people of the Upper Stoup do not count years the way people below do. They count winters, and they count lichen years and fog years and bird years, and the count is approximate, and it is enough. She says she was a young woman when the lichen ran orange. She says her mother remembered a time when the spring ran further, when the water came out of the rock for three hundred paces instead of two hundred. She says the town was larger then. She does not say it was better. She says it was larger, and that is what she says.

---

The dogs. They should be spoken of more fully. There are four. They are grey, and medium-sized, and they have the look of animals that have been bred for a long time in a particular place: the legs are slightly shorter than they should be, the chest is deep, the coat is thick and water-shedding. They are not friendly in the way that dogs below are friendly. They do not wag their tails at a stranger. They do not come up and sniff. They come and they sit at a distance and they watch, and they wait to see what the stranger will do. If the stranger does nothing violent, the dogs will eventually approach, and will allow themselves to be touched, and will then return to their watching.

The dogs have names. The names are not like the names of the people. The names of the dogs are functional — *the one who watches the east*, *the one who barks at the moon*, *the one who sleeps by Fael's door*. The dogs are spoken of with great respect and great familiarity. A dog is the only animal in the Upper Stoup that will look a person in the eye, and the people value this. They say, when a dog and a person truly know each other, that the dog has *seen through* the person, and that this is a kind of blessing.

The dogs know. They know the weather. They know the approach of strangers. They know when someone in the town is going to die. They know, in some way that the people cannot explain, when the lichen is going to run a particular color. They are the most attentive inhabitants of the Upper Stoup, and the people trust them as they trust the spring.

---

The trappers. They are a subset of the people, and they are set apart, gently and without cruelty, by what they do. They are the ones who go out onto the open plateau. The others do not go out, or go out only a little way, to the nearest fold, to gather moss or to check a snare. The trappers go further. They go into the broken country at the rim, where the plateau breaks into something that is almost a landscape, with deeper valleys and higher humps and places where a person can be lost for a day if the fog closes in.

The trappers come back changed. Not visibly. Not in any way that a stranger would notice. But the people of the town notice, and the trappers themselves notice, and there is a quiet understanding that the trappers have seen something that the others have not. They have stood in the silence of the open plateau. They have heard the wind without any buildings to break it up. They have seen, on clear days, the rim of the plateau in every direction, and the folds of the stone going off to the horizon, and the nothing that is out there.

They do not talk about it. When they come back, they bring the small grey animals, and they hand them over to be cleaned and cooked, and they go to their houses and they sleep for a long time, and then they are back at their ordinary work. But there is something in them, after, that was not there before. A stillness. A patience that goes beyond the ordinary patience of the place. The people recognize this and they do not ask. They understand that the trappers have made a trade — a piece of themselves, given to the plateau, in exchange for the meat that the town needs.

---

A stranger, arriving at the Upper Stoup, would see very little at first. The fog would close around them. The wind would find the gaps in their clothing. The dogs would come and sit at a distance. The people would emerge from the low doorways and look, and not say much, and after a while a man or a woman would step forward and offer, by gesture, a seat by a hearth and a bowl of something warm. The stranger would sit. The stranger would drink. The stranger would not understand, at first, what was being offered, because the offer is not a transaction and it is not a welcome and it is not a warning. It is an acknowledgment. *You are here now. You will not stay. While you are here, you will be warm.*

The people of the Upper Stoup do not hate strangers. They do not love strangers. They recognize strangers as the plateau recognizes strangers — as a thing that has appeared and will pass. They have seen very few. Once every few years, perhaps, someone comes up the goat trail. A trader. A runaway. A person fleeing something below. A person seeking something above. The strangers come, and they stay for a few days or a few weeks, and then they go back down the goat trail, or they die, or they go out onto the plateau and do not come back. The people remember them for a while. The dogs remember them longer.

---

What the people believe. This is hard to say, because the people do not speak about it. They have no priests, no rituals, no prayers that a visitor would recognize as such. But there are certain habits of thought that are shared, and that amount to a kind of belief. The people believe that the plateau is alive, in a way. Not in the way that a dog or a bird is alive. In the way that a place can be alive, when a place has been lived in long enough and paid attention to long enough. They believe that the plateau notices things. They believe that the spring is not just water but a kind of gift, and that it could be taken away if the people were not careful. They believe that the lichen is the plateau's skin, and that to take lichen from a stone without need is to hurt the plateau, though they do take it, with care, when they need it for mortar. They believe that the dead go into the under-town and become part of it, and that the under-town is not less real than the above-town, only quieter.

They do not believe in any god. They do not have a word for god. They have a word for *the patience of the stone*, which is the closest they come, and it is not a word that is spoken often. It is spoken, sometimes, when a child is born, and sometimes when a person dies, and sometimes when the fog is so thick that the town disappears into itself and the people must trust that the town is still there.

---

What the people want. Very little. They want the spring to keep running. They want the lichen to grow. They want the dogs to be well. They want the children to survive. They want the roofs to hold through the next winter. They want, in some quiet way that they would not articulate, to be allowed to continue. They are aware, without discussing it, that they are at the end of something. The plateau is wearing down. The town is shrinking. The lichen is taking the stones back. The under-town is growing, and the above-town is settling. They do not fight this. They have never fought it. They move at the speed of the place, and the place is moving toward a flatness that will take them with it.

This is not a sad people. They are not a despairing people. They are a people who have made a particular kind of peace — a peace with the slow wearing of stone, with the patience of moss, with the certainty that they will not be here forever and that this is all right. They go about their work. They speak softly. They listen to the dogs. They drink from the spring. They bury their dead in the under-town. They wait, as the plateau waits, for whatever comes next.

---

No one stays. The people know this. The stranger knows it too, after a while, and the knowing is not bitter. It is the way of the place. The place is patient. The people are patient. The stranger will go back down the goat trail, or will not go back at all, and in either case the town will continue, and the fog will move through it, and the lichen will grow on the stones, and the spring will run its two hundred paces, and the dogs will watch, and the people will speak softly, and the under-town will grow, and the above-town will settle, and the plateau will wear down, and the wind will not stop.
