There are people in the kelp.

This is the thing the naturalist-divers keep having to be reminded of, every time they come down from the drowned cities — from Vorr, from Saltreach, from the long shelf-towns of the continental edge — and hang suspended over the Anrath ridge in their copper-and-glass breathing apparatus, the surface a green-gold dazzle thirty meters above. They have the dossier. They have read the dossier. *Homo descendens*, common name the Anrath, estimated population sixty to eighty, technology organic, cultural index pastoral-sedentary, body plan adapted-submerged. They know, in the careful binomial way of knowing, that the people are there. They have come specifically to see the people. And still, the first thing that surprises them, every time, is that the people are there. That the green-grey water, in its cathedral of kelp, on its drowned mountain, is *inhabited*. That it can be spoken to. That it speaks back.

The Anrath themselves do not find this surprising. They have never found it surprising. They were born inside the kelp, or in a kelp-house very like the one their parents were born inside, and they expect, when they die, to be sealed into a chamber that will become part of the substrate that the next house grows from. To them, the green light is the light. The shrimp-crackle is the sound of having a roof over one's head. The smell of iodine and slow rot is the smell of home. They have no word for *unusual*, because the world they live in is the only world they have ever lived in, and the word would have nothing to mean.

This is the first thing to understand about the people of the Reed-Vale.

---

The second thing is that they are people.

This sounds like the same thing as the first thing. It is not. There are people in the kelp, and there are people in the kelp — the first statement is about the fact of them, the second is about the kind of them. They are not fish. They are not, despite the best efforts of the popular illustrated broadsheets of the lowland cities, mermaids — there is no tail, no scandalous bifurcation of the lower body, no gleaming scale. They are recognizably the same kind of creature as the visitor, in the way that a sea-otter is recognizably the same kind of creature as a weasel: visibly, undeniably related, and visibly, undeniably changed.

The change is slow. The change is in everything.

The chest is deepened, the rib-cage flared into a shallow bell, the lungs still lungs but pressed by generations of submersion into a shape that can take and hold a great breath without strain. The spine is more flexible than the surface-type's — not serpentine, not loose, but capable of a long, easy curl that the visitor cannot match. The muscles are dense and wiry; they do not bulk, because the water does the work of carrying them. The shoulders are narrower than the visitor expects, the hips a little wider; the limbs are long and the forearms especially so, the better to reach across the gap between one stipe and the next without bothering to swim.

The skin is interesting. It is paler than the visitor's, generally — though this varies, family to family, lineage to lineage, and some of the older Anrath are dark as old wood where they have spent their lives in the deep channels of the canopy. In the green light it takes on the color of wet linen, of mushroom flesh, of the inside of a shell. It is cool to the touch, and cool underneath the cool, the way a river-stone is cool underneath the cool of its surface — but it warms, slowly, if you hold your hand there. The body is working. The body is at temperature. The water is the climate, and the body, over centuries, has stopped fighting the climate and started keeping pace with it.

The face is the part the visitor stares at, the first time, because it is the part that is most like them and least like them at once. The eyes are large and dark and set a little deeper than the visitor's, sheltered under a brow-ridge that is heavier than the visitor's, the better to shade them from what passes for sun in this place. There is an inner fold — a slow third lid, almost transparent, that slides across when the water stirs or the light shifts. The mouth is wide and full-lipped. The teeth are even and white but the canines are a little longer than they should be, a little more pointed — a holdover from somewhere, from some ancestor that ate something more contested than fish. The nose is small and flattened, the nostrils set to close at will; an Anrath can hang head-down in a current without snorting, can duck through a gap in the kelp without a flinch. The ears are small, low-set, folded close to the skull; they hear in the water, and hear well, but they hear differently from the way the visitor hears, and there are sounds they can pick out that the visitor, even after a week, cannot.

The hair is usually dark — black, or very deep brown, occasionally the red-brown that is rarer and prized and that runs in certain families along the western flank. It is kept short in most households, though some of the older women wear it longer, weighted with little shell-beads so it does not float into the face when they work overhead. Children are often nearly hairless until puberty, a soft down on the scalp and nothing else; this is normal, and an Anrath parent of a ten-year-old would be confused by the question, *when will the child's hair come in?* The question has the wrong tense. The hair is already coming in. It has been coming in for years. It will arrive when it arrives.

The hands are where the Anrath spend most of their attention, in their conversations with themselves and with each other. The hands are long, narrow-fingered, the first three fingers nearly equal in length, the fourth and fifth a little shorter. There is webbing between them, but only partial — only out to the second knuckle, only along the lower edge of the hand. The webbing is thicker than the skin of the rest of the hand, calloused and ridged from years of binding kelp, and it is warm in a way the rest of the skin is not quite. The nails are flat and wide, almost spade-shaped at the tips. They are used for prying. They are used for scoring the cuticle of a young stipe so that it will thicken in a particular direction. They are used for the careful, slow work of training a plant into a wall.

The feet are not flukes. The visitor always looks for flukes, the first time, and is always disappointed, or relieved, or both. The feet are feet. The toes are long and splayed, webbed out to the second knuckle, the big toe slightly set apart from the rest and slightly opposable. They grip stipe the way a child's hand grips a rope. They grip the woven floor of a kelp-house. They grip the wrist of another Anrath, in greeting, in comfort, in the long grip of a parent who is helping a child learn the rhythms of the current.

---

What they wear.

The water is the climate, the kelp is the architecture, and so the body is neither hidden nor displayed in any of the ways the visitor is used to. Heavy clothing is impractical — it drags, it holds cold, it gets in the way of the slow precise work of tending the town. But the Anrath are not unadorned. They wear harnesses and belts of twisted reed-fiber, slung across the chest and around the waist, from which hang the small tools of their days: a shell knife in its sheath; a spool of fresh binding; a bone hook on a cord; a small woven basket for the morning's gathering; a whistle made from a hollowed fish-bone, used for signalling across the canopy. They wear bands of shell around the upper arm and the wrist, and necklaces of the iridescent scales of the cleaning-wrens, gently harvested with a small spoken apology and a quick stroke of the finger. The older women sometimes wear a kind of cap or hood, woven close, that keeps the chill of the deeper channels off the scalp; the men more often wear nothing on the head at all.

Children, until they are old enough to be trusted with tools, often wear nothing at all except a string around the waist with a single shell on it — a kind of naming-token, given at birth, that identifies them as a member of their family to anyone who knows the family and the shell. After a certain age the string is replaced by a proper harness; the moment is small and unmemorable to the parents, and is remembered by the child for the rest of their life.

Colors in the Anrath world are the colors of the kelp — the browns and yellow-golds of the older stipes, the deep olive of the canopy, the pale lemon of new growth — interrupted by flashes of shell-white and the sudden blue-green fire of a wren-scale catching the light. There is no dye, no pigment. There is no need. The world is already its own color, and the Anrath have learned to wear it.

---

What they do.

The Anrath *tend*. This is the verb the naturalist-divers use, because no other verb fits. They do not build. They do not construct. They do not raise a structure and then leave it; they begin a structure and then continue it, slowly, in partnership with a thing that is also continuing itself. A house is begun by choosing a young kelp — a Thalassia magnifica of good stock, well-anchored, in a position that will suit the family for the next hundred years — and then by beginning, with a shell knife and a coil of fresh binding, the very long process of persuading it to grow into the shape of a room.

This is not metaphor. It is what happens. A cut made at the right place on a young stipe will cause the stipe to thicken and curve in that direction over the next several months; a binding wrapped around a growing point will cause the new tissue to bulge around it; a frond woven into a particular position will, over time, knit itself to the neighboring frond and form a continuous surface. The technique has been in some families for eight generations, or twelve, depending on how you count — depending on whether you count the grandmother who refined the binding-knot, or the grandmother before her who invented it. The Anrath themselves do not count it. They just do it. They do it the way their parents did it. They will do it the way their children do it.

The work is constant but not hurried. A house carved this year will be a slightly different house next year, and the year after that, and the year after that, as the living walls thicken, as new fronds push through the woven roof, as the doorway — that slow slit in living tissue — migrates a finger's width to the left or right as the stipe bends with the current. A storm will tear a roof open and the Anrath will not repair it; they will regrow it, coaxing new fronds across the gap, binding them into the existing weave, waiting for them to take. When a child is born into a family, the family's house is extended — not by adding a new structure, but by cutting a new chamber into the back of an old one, and beginning the long persuasion of another young kelp to become the wall of it. When someone dies, the chamber they lived in is sealed, and the slow process of the kelp reclaiming it begins, and a new chamber is cut for whoever needs it next.

The town accretes. The town accretes the way a coral reef accretes, the way a termite mound accretes, the way a tree-ring accretes — slowly, in the manner of a thing that is alive and that does not understand urgency. A chamber that was a grandmother's bedroom when she was a child is, eighty years later, the floor of her great-granddaughter's bedroom, and is still recognizably the same chamber, only thicker, only darker, only more itself.

The Anrath have a saying, *veth karu, karu veth* — the town is the garden, the garden is the town. They mean it literally. There is no part of the town that is not alive. There is no part of the town that is not tended.

---

Who they live with.

The Anrath are not alone. This is something the visitor registers slowly, over the first day or two, as the layers of the place become audible and then legible and then simply present. The forest is loud, and most of what makes it loud is not human.

The snapping shrimp are the constant. They live in the kelp holdfasts by the tens of thousands, the small pistol-shrimp with their one enlarged claw, and they are loud the way rain is loud, or the way a hearth-fire is loud — a sustained, granular crackling, a field of static, a sound that is not so much heard as inhabited. The Anrath sleep inside it. The Anrath wake inside it. They have never known silence, and they do not particularly want to; when a chamber is too quiet, when a holdfast has been killed by some sickness and the shrimp have moved out, the family who lives there feels the absence the way a visitor would feel the absence of a ceiling. They send to the neighbors for young holdfast-fragments to seed the new growth. They wait for the shrimp to come back. The roof returns.

The eels are the tolerated ones. They live in the hollow chambers of the older houses, coiled in the darker spaces between the woven walls, sliding out at night to hunt the small creatures that would otherwise hunt the Anrath. There is a kind of contract, here, though no Anrath would put it that way. The eels eat the parasites, the lampreys, the small biting things that nest in the holdfasts. The Anrath do not kill the eels. When an eel grows too large for a chamber, it is guided out, gently, with a long bone hook and a soft spoken word, and it finds a new chamber elsewhere in the town, and the family whose house it leaves feels, for a few nights, an undefended sleep. Sometimes a young eel is brought into a house on purpose, introduced as a kitten is introduced into a barn, and the children are told its name and taught not to startle it.

The cleaning-wrens are the small bright helpers. They are small iridescent fish, blue-green and gold, that dart at the parasites on the Anrath's skin the way small birds pick at the teeth of crocodiles. The Anrath hold very still when a wren is working on them. They close their eyes. They let it happen. They consider it a small social grace, a moment of being-taken-care-of, and they return the favor by not eating the wrens, ever, and by harvesting only a few scales at a time, with thanks, for the necklaces that mark a person's passage into adulthood.

The schooling fish are watched, not hunted — or hunted only when there is need, and with restraint. The Anrath know the moods of the schools the way a shepherd knows the moods of a flock. They know the silver-blue cloud that means sardine-analog, the deeper pulse that means anchovy-analog, the long dark shape at the edge of the school that means a predator is in from the deep and the school will tighten and wheel and the Anrath who are out in the canopy should pull back toward the ridge. The children learn to read the schools before they learn to read the woven marks of writing. This is not because the fish are more important than the writing. It is because the fish are more *immediate*, and a child who cannot read the fish will not live long enough to learn the writing.

The brittle-stars are in the holdfasts. The anemones are on the lower fronds. The small darting things that the naturalist-divers have not yet named are everywhere, in every crevice, on every surface that is not kelp and not Anrath. The Anrath have names for all of them, or for most of them, and the names are not in the dossier because the naturalist-divers have not yet learned to ask.

---

What they think.

The naturalist-divers record what the Anrath say. They record the sayings carefully, because sayings are data, and data is what the dossier is for. *Veth karu, karu veth*. They record the words for the parts of a house — *veth*, the structure; *karu*, the growth; the specific terms for stipe, frond, holdfast, chamber, doorway, roof. They record the words for the stages of life: the word for the child-before-naming, the child-after-naming, the person-of-the-harness, the person-of-the-council, the person-of-the-sealed-chamber. They record the words for the eels, for the wrens, for the shrimp — though the shrimp-word is hard to record, because it is more like a sound than a word, a sustained quick trill that the Anrath make when they are referring to the constant noise in the holdfasts.

What they do not record, because they cannot, is the way the saying feels to say.

The saying is not a proverb. The saying is not a piece of wisdom that the Anrath have arrived at after long reflection. The saying is a description of how things are. The town is the garden. The garden is the town. Of course it is. What else would it be? A house is a thing that grows. A room is a thing that is persuaded. A wall is a thing that listens. When the kelp is sick, the family is sick. When the family thrives, the kelp thrives. When a person dies, the chamber seals, and the kelp takes them back, and the next house grows from the place they were.

The Anrath do not think of themselves as living *in* the kelp. They think of themselves as living *with* the kelp, in the way that one lives with a long marriage, or a long inheritance, or a long body. The town is not their home. The town is their *kin*, in a register that the visitor does not quite have a word for, and the Anrath do not have a word for either, because the word would be redundant.

This is what the visitor begins to feel, after a week.

---

How they end.

When an Anrath is dying, the family calls the neighbors. The neighbors come, slowly, and they sit with the dying person in the chamber they have lived in, and they sing to them — a low, sustained, water-borne sound, more felt than heard, a vibration that begins in the chest and travels outward through the water and the kelp walls. The song is not for the dying person, exactly, or not only for them. The song is also for the kelp, which is going to have to take them back, and which the family wants to be ready.

When the person has died, the chamber is sealed. The slit of the doorway is bound shut with fresh binding and weighted with stones from the volcanic ridge. The binding will hold for a year, perhaps two, and then the kelp will begin to grow over it, slowly, the way it grows over everything, and within a decade the doorway will be a thickening in the wall, and within a generation it will be part of the structure of the next house. The person's tools — the shell knife, the bone hook, the spool of binding, the wren-scale necklace — are left inside the chamber, because the person will need them, in whatever way the dead need things, and because the children who come after will want them, eventually, in the way that the dead's things are wanted by the living.

There is no grave. There is no marker. There is the chamber, sealed, becoming part of the kelp, becoming part of the town. The Anrath do not say that the dead are *in* the kelp. They say that the dead are *of* the kelp. There is a difference, and the difference matters, and the visitor who has been here long enough begins to feel it.

When a child is born, the family extends the house. A new chamber is cut into the back of the old one — sometimes into the back of a sealed chamber, if the dead have been there long enough — and the slow persuasion of the new kelp begins. The child is given a name, and a shell on a string, and is shown, in the first days of its life, the chamber that has been grown for it, and the slit in the living tissue that will be its doorway, and the green-grey light that will be its light, and the constant sound of the shrimp in the holdfasts that will be the sound of its sleep.

It will learn to swim before it walks. It will learn to read the schools before it reads the woven marks. It will learn, slowly, the names of all the eels in the house, and the moods of the wrens that come to clean its skin, and the song that the family sings when one of them is dying. It will grow up inside a chamber that is itself growing up, and will not notice that the walls are moving, because the walls have been moving for its whole life, and the movement is the room's way of breathing.

When the child is old enough, it will be given its own tools. It will learn to bind a frond. It will learn the knot that has been in the family for eight generations, or twelve. It will learn to cut a young stipe so that it thickens in the right direction, so that it curves into a wall. It will learn that the kelp listens, and that the listening takes years.

When the child dies, in turn, its chamber will be sealed, and the next child's chamber will be cut, and the town will continue to accrete, slowly, in the manner of a thing that is alive and that does not understand urgency.

---

What the visitor does not write down.

After a week, the visitor stops reaching for the dossier.

After a week, the visitor begins to hear the shrimp the way the Anrath hear the shrimp — not as a phenomenon but as a weather, a constant, a given. The visitor begins to know which eel lives in which chamber, by the sound of its breathing, by the slow thump of its tail against the wall at night. The visitor begins to read the schools — or to think they are reading them — and to feel, when the school tightens and wheels, the small animal alertness of a creature that is not yet prey but is becoming prey. The visitor begins to feel the cold, not as a temperature but as a presence, a thing the body is in conversation with, the way a body is in conversation with gravity on the surface.

After a week, the visitor stops thinking of the town as built. Begins to think of it as *grown*. Begins to think of the Anrath as people who live inside a forest that they are also the gardeners of, in a partnership so old that no one remembers which side started it. Begins to think of the dead not as buried but as *composted*, returned to the substrate, taken up by the kelp, made into the next wall.

The visitor does not write this down.

The visitor cannot write this down. It is not the kind of thing that fits in a column.

---

The water holds it all.

Cool, dense, green-grey, the color of lichen on old glass, the sound of rain on a tin roof, the slow precession of the moon moving the shafts of light across the woven floor of a chamber that has been growing, in one form or another, for as long as anyone can remember.

The kelp listens.

The Anrath listen back.

The eels sleep in the walls. The wrens dart at the parasites. The shrimp crackle in the holdfasts. The schools wheel and tighten and darken the green light when they pass overhead. A child is born into a chamber that has been grown for it, and is shown the doorway, and the light, and the sound. An old woman seals her mother's chamber, and sings to the kelp, and begins the long persuasion of a new young plant. A visitor, after a week, stops reaching for the dossier, and floats in the canopy in the copper-and-glass apparatus with the surface a green-gold dazzle far above, and does not write down what they have begun to feel, because it does not fit in a column.

The town is the garden.

The garden is the town.

The water holds it all, slowly, for a long time.
