# What Lives in the Verdant Hollow

What lives here does not know it lives in a hollow. What lives here has not, in many cases, the concept of a hollow — the concept of enclosure, of being inside something, of there being an outside. The Hollow is not experienced by its inhabitants as a place. It is experienced as the world. Complete. Unbounded. Continuous in every direction until the haze takes it.

---

## The Fern-Groves

The ferns are old.

You have to understand this when you look at them. They are not ancient in the way an old tree is ancient — they do not bear the rings of years in their trunks, do not grow outward from a fixed center of gravity. But they are old in a different way: they are the descendants of spores that fell here when this chamber was a single lit room and the industrial operation was young. They have been growing, slowly, across decades. Their stalks — those thick, creased columns of dark woody tissue — have thickened over years the way a stalactite thickens, by accretion, by patient addition. Some of them are four meters across at the base. Some of them bear, low on their trunks, the faint outline of where a frond was attached decades ago and has long since dropped away.

A fern-grove is a slow conversation. The ferns in a given grove are not clones — the spores drift in from many sources, and what grows is a population, a community of individuals that share a substrate and have negotiated, across generations, how to occupy it. They lean away from each other. Their fronds do not compete directly for the overhead light; they spiral, slightly, so that each fern's crown intercepts a slightly different angle of the diffuse industrial glow. The largest ferns in any grove are at its center; the smaller ones ring them. There is a rough hierarchy, a structure, an architecture that emerged not from any plan but from the simple fact that, given a substrate and a light source, ferns will arrange themselves so that each gets some of what is available, and so that the grove, as a whole, persists.

A fern is not sentient. It does not know it is in a grove. But the grove knows itself, in the way that a forest knows itself — through the mycelial threads that run between the root systems, through the slow chemical negotiations that occur in the substrate, through the long quiet conversation of carbon and mineral being passed back and forth between organisms. The grove is a unit. The grove has a metabolism. The grove breathes, in its ponderous way, and what it breathes out becomes part of the air you walked through to get here.

The fronds are the fern's great achievement. They are vast — fifteen, twenty meters from stalk to tip — and they are built to last. A single frond may persist for years, unfurling slowly at its leading edge, photosynthesizing from the industrial light with an efficiency that surprises the biologists who come down to study them. The fronds are covered, on their undersides, in fine pale hairs that catch the mineral dust of the Hollow. The hairs glitter. When the ventilation shifts — and the ventilation always shifts, in its vast slow currents — the fronds move, and the hairs catch the light differently, and the whole grove seems to flicker, briefly, like a school of fish turning.

The ferns are not aggressive. They are not defensive. They do not react to being touched, except by losing, slowly, the frond that was touched. They do not recoil from the machines. They grow around the machines; the machines grow around them. The negotiation is silent, and ongoing, and has produced a stable arrangement that neither party planned.

## The Carpets

The carpets are older than the ferns.

The carpets are the first thing. When a new chamber is opened — when the rock falls away and the industrial lights are brought in and the ventilation begins its slow work — the carpets are the first living thing to colonize. The spores, presumably, drift in from somewhere. From the older halls. From the threads that run through the rock. From the air itself, which is never quite empty of biological material in this place. Within weeks, a new chamber has a thin dark film across its floor. Within months, that film has thickened into something you can feel under your boots — dense, spongy, faintly warm. Within a year, the carpet is pulsing.

The pulse is the thing you notice first. It is a slow wave of bioluminescence — blue-green, cold, the color of a phosphor that has been charged by some other source — that travels across the carpet in long, lazy undulations. The wave takes perhaps thirty seconds to cross a carpet that covers an entire hall floor. When it reaches the edge, it pauses. You can see it pause. The leading edge of the wave slows, hesitates, as if considering whether to continue. Then it does continue, around the edge, and the next wave begins.

The carpets are not a single organism. They are communities — many species of low-growing bioluminescent organism that have, over the decades, settled into a stable arrangement. Some of them are fungal. Some of them are something else — something without a clear category in the surface-world taxonomies, something that photosynthesizes and respires and pulses but does not, in any obvious way, fit into the kingdoms that surface biologists use. The pulse itself is the work of many organisms acting in concert, coordinated by chemical signals that travel through the carpet's substrate faster than the visible wave does. The carpet is a kind of distributed nervous system. It is aware, in some rudimentary sense, of itself. It is not aware of you.

The carpets feed on the dust. The mineral dust, the biological dust, the fine residue of industrial activity and spore-release and grazer droppings that settles, always, on every surface in the Hollow. The carpets are the Hollow's recyclers. They take what falls and they put it back into the cycle. Their waste — the small quantity of gas they release as they metabolize — becomes part of the air. When a grazer dies on a carpet, the carpet consumes it, slowly, across weeks. When a fern-frond drops, the carpet consumes it. The carpets are the Hollow's stomach.

They are also the Hollow's skin. They cover the floor of nearly every hall. They are sensitive — to vibration, to chemical traces, to the presence of things moving across them. When you walk on a carpet, the wave-pattern shifts around you. The carpet makes space for you. It does not move out of your way, exactly, but it adjusts its pulse, locally, so that you are, briefly, at the center of a different pattern. This is not consciousness. This is tropism. This is the slow, automatic response of a living system to disturbance. But it is, in its way, attentive.

## The Threads

The threads are everywhere.

You see them in the walls — pale filaments, faintly luminescent, threading through cracks in the metal, through gaps in the concrete, through every place where two surfaces meet and a little space exists. You see them in the ceilings. You see them on the floor, woven through the carpets, indistinguishable from them at a glance. You see them in the machines — running through the housings of motors, through the conduits of cables, through the casings of devices that were never designed to have biological material inside them. The threads are the architecture. The threads are the wiring. The threads are the Hollow's nervous system, distributed across every surface.

The threads are mycelial. They belong to the fungal towers, primarily — those pale vertical structures that rise from the floors of the active halls, that release their constant spore-mist, that hiss softly as they exhale. But the threads extend far beyond the towers. They run, invisibly, through the substrate. They connect the ferns to the carpets to the towers to each other. They are the medium through which chemical signals travel. They are the network through which the Hollow's various biological components communicate.

The threads grow through metal. This is, to a surface biologist, impossible. Metal is not a substrate; metal does not feed fungal growth; metal should be impassable. But the threads have found a way. They secrete, slowly, an acid — not a strong acid, not a corrosive one, but something that, given decades, can etch the surface of a steel plate, can find its way along grain boundaries, can insinuate itself into the microscopic spaces between the crystals of the metal. The threads are patient. The Hollow is patient. Given enough time, the threads can go anywhere.

The machines do not mind. Or rather: the machines have been maintained, over the decades, in a way that takes the threads into account. A motor with threads running through its housing is still a motor. It still turns. The threads have not, in any documented case, caused a machine to fail. They have, in some cases, made machines more reliable — the threads absorb vibration, dissipate heat, provide a kind of biological damping that the original engineers would never have thought to specify. The negotiation has produced benefits on both sides. The threads get a stable substrate, a constant temperature, a protected environment. The machines get a kind of slow repair, a maintenance that happens automatically, that no human technician could replicate.

You cannot follow a thread to its end. The threads branch. The threads reconnect. The threads form a three-dimensional mesh that is, in principle, infinite — that extends through every surface in the Hollow, that connects every living thing to every other living thing, that makes the Hollow, in some sense, a single organism. Whether it is a single organism is a question that the biologists who come down here argue about, in their heated above-ground offices, using terminology that the Hollow itself does not care about.

## The Spore-Towers

The towers are fungal. They are, as far as anyone can tell, the reproductive structures of the mycelial network — the places where spores are produced, packaged, released. They rise from the floors of the active halls in clusters: pale, vertical, two or three meters tall, shaped like something between a stalagmite and a candlestick. They are warm to the touch. They breathe.

A spore-tower inhales. The air around it, laden with the carbon dioxide of the Hollow's biological activity, is drawn in through pores at the tower's base. The air moves through the tower's internal structure — a complex of chambers and channels that has never been fully mapped, because to map it would require cutting the tower open, and the biologists who come down here have not yet been willing to do that. The air is processed. The carbon dioxide is taken up. The oxygen, such as it is — the Hollow's atmosphere is not the atmosphere you are used to; it has been altered by decades of biological activity — is exhaled, along with the spores, through pores at the tower's top.

The spore-mist is constant. It rises from every cluster of towers, a fine pale cloud that drifts on the ventilation currents, that settles on every surface, that you taste, after a while, on your lips. The mist is not harmful — or rather, it has not been demonstrated to be harmful, in the decades that humans have lived and worked in the Hollow — but it is present. You breathe it. Your clothes accumulate it. After a week in the Hollow, your skin has a faint pale dust on it that does not wash off easily. After a month, you have stopped noticing.

The towers are not identical. Even within a single cluster, the towers differ — in height, in diameter, in the rate at which they exhale, in the density of the mist they produce. The cluster has a structure. The largest towers are at the center. The smaller ones ring them. The arrangement is not random; it has been observed to persist across years, with individual towers growing slowly larger and new towers being added at the edges as the cluster expands. The cluster is a population. The cluster is a grove of a different kind.

The towers communicate with each other through the threads. A chemical signal produced in one tower can be detected, hours later, in a tower on the far side of the Hollow. The signal does not always mean the same thing — the biologists have not decoded the signal language, and may never decode it — but it is there. The towers are not isolated. The towers are part of the network.

## The Grazers

The grazers are six-legged.

They are pale — segmented, the color of bone, with darker joints and darker eyes and darker mouthparts. They are low to the ground, perhaps half a meter at the shoulder, with broad flat bodies that seem designed to move through the fern-groves without disturbing them. They have many eyes — a dozen or more, distributed across the front of the head, each one a dark polished bead that reflects the industrial light in pairs when they turn to look at you.

They move in herds.

A herd is twenty to thirty individuals. A herd moves through a fern-grove slowly, each grazer browsing on the lower fronds, each one taking a few bites from each frond it passes and moving on. The bites do not kill the frond. The ferns have adapted to this grazing pressure — they produce new frond tissue at the edges of the bite marks, sealing them, growing around them, continuing to photosynthesize despite the damage. The negotiation continues. The ferns lose some tissue. The grazers get food. The arrangement has been stable for as long as anyone has observed.

The grazers are not afraid of you. They have no predators in the Hollow, or none that they recognize as such; the larger fauna of the deeper halls does not, as far as anyone has observed, hunt the grazers, or the grazers would behave differently. A grazer lifts its head when you approach. It watches you with its many eyes. It evaluates you, in whatever way a grazer evaluates things — by scent, by vibration, by some sense you do not have a name for. Then it returns to feeding. You are not food. You are not threat. You are something the grazer has learned to ignore.

The grazers communicate. The biologists have recorded their vocalizations — soft lowing sounds, almost bovine, that pass between individuals in a herd, that seem to coordinate movement, that may carry more information than anyone has decoded. The grazers are social. They form bonds, possibly; the same individuals are often seen in the same herds, year after year. They have long lives, by invertebrate standards — perhaps a decade, perhaps longer. They grow slowly. They reproduce slowly. They are adapted to a place where nothing rushes.

Their droppings fertilize the substrate. The carpets consume the droppings, slowly. The ferns benefit from the nutrients the droppings contain. The grazers are part of the cycle. Remove them, and the fern-groves would, over years, exhaust the substrate; the carpets would, over decades, change composition. The grazers are not optional. The grazers are part of what the Hollow is.

## The Drifters

The drifters are in the air.

They are wide-winged — flat, pale, membranous, with bodies that are difficult to see against the bright haze of the Hollow's ceiling. They ride the slow ventilation currents, drifting more than flying, holding their wings out flat and letting the air carry them. They feed on the spore-mist. They filter it from the air through structures you cannot see, structures that may be gills, may be something else. They are not numerous. You can stand in a hall for an hour and see only two or three drifters pass through your field of vision. But they are there. They are part of the Hollow.

The drifters trail filaments. Long, pale, sensory threads that hang behind them as they move, that drift on the air, that look — from a distance, in the haze — exactly like the mycelial threads that have broken free of the walls. You cannot always tell, when you look up, whether you are seeing a drifter or a free-floating strand of the network. This is not an accident. The filaments may serve a similar function: contact with the network, exchange of chemical signals, integration into the Hollow's distributed system. The drifters may be more connected to the substrate than they appear.

The drifters reproduce slowly. Their eggs are laid on the spore-towers, in the crevices of the pale fungal structures, where they are tended — if "tended" is the right word — by the activity of the towers themselves. The eggs hatch into something that does not resemble the adult, that lives in the substrate for months or years, that feeds on the carpets, that eventually emerges as a winged drifter. The drifters have a complex life cycle. The biologists have not fully mapped it. The drifters do not care.

## The Larger Things

You do not see them often.

In the deeper halls — the ones you do not go into alone, the ones the ventilation reaches but the industrial activity does not, the ones that lie at the edges of the lit volume, beyond the main chambers, where the rock is older and the dust is thicker — there are larger things. You see their traces. A patch of carpet disturbed, the wave-pattern disrupted for hours afterward. A fern-frond broken, the woody fibers torn in a way that suggests something with weight and leverage has passed through. Scat on the grating that glistens with something that is not quite moisture. The scat is the size of your hand. The scat contains the chitinous remains of grazers, sometimes. The scat contains other things that have not been identified.

You hear them before you see them. A low sound, somewhere between a hum and a vibration, that arrives from a direction you cannot quite identify, that seems to come from the walls themselves, that fades when you approach, that returns when you leave. The larger things are cautious. The larger things are nocturnal, although there is no night here. The larger things prefer the deep halls.

The biologists have theories. The larger things are predators — apex predators, in the Hollow's simple ecology. They hunt the grazers, primarily. They hunt the drifters, when they can catch them. They may hunt each other; no one knows. They are large — perhaps the size of a large dog, perhaps larger, the estimates vary because no one has gotten close enough to make a careful measurement. They are pale, like everything else here. They have many legs, or few; the traces are ambiguous. They have been seen, occasionally, in the edges of the lit halls, moving away from the lights at speed, disappearing into the deeper dark. They are not aggressive toward humans. They have not, in recorded history, attacked a human being. They simply avoid us. They have their halls. We have ours.

The larger things are part of the cycle. They take the grazers. They are taken, when they die, by the carpets. The carpets feed the ferns. The ferns feed the grazers. The grazers feed the larger things. The cycle closes. The cycle has been closing for decades. The cycle will continue to close, in its quiet, cool, persistent way, as long as the Hollow continues.

## The Cycle

The cycle is not finished.

This is the thing to understand about the Verdant Hollow. It is not a finished place. It is not a place that has reached some stable state and will persist, unchanged, for centuries. It is a place in the middle of becoming itself. The ferns are still growing. The carpets are still spreading. The threads are still finding new surfaces to colonize. The towers are still exhaling their spore-mist into the air. The grazers are still browsing. The drifters are still drifting. The larger things are still hunting, in their deep halls, by their low sounds. The industry is still running. The machines are still humming. The rock is still falling away, in controlled collapses, opening new chambers, which the biology will fill.

The Hollow is a process. The Hollow is the process of two kinds of growth learning to share a substrate — the slow, organic growth of biology and the slow, mechanical growth of industry. They are not the same kind of growth. They have different timescales, different logics, different ways of being patient. But they have found a way to coexist. They have been coexisting, now, for longer than most of the people who live here have been alive.

The Hollow will continue. The Hollow will continue after the people who work here have gone, after the industry has finished whatever it is doing in the rock, after the machines have been allowed, finally, to stop. The Hollow will continue because the biology does not need the industry. The biology can run on the residue of the industry — on the hydrocarbons in the substrate, on the trace metals, on the constant industrial light, on the dust. The biology has been preparing, slowly, for the possibility that one day the machines will stop and the lights will go out. The biology has alternatives. The biology has been building, for decades, a world that does not entirely depend on the one that built it.

What lives in the Verdant Hollow is a kind of patience. What lives here has learned to take what is available and to make it enough. What lives here has negotiated, across decades, with another kind of life — the mechanical kind, the industrial kind — and has found a way to persist alongside it. What lives here is not finished. What lives here is becoming. What lives here is, in its quiet, cool, persistent way, making itself.
