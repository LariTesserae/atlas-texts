# What Lives on the Ridge

The machines live here.

That is the first thing to say, and the hardest, because a machine is not a creature and a machine is not a building and a machine is not a weather, but the machines on this ridge are all of these, and they have been here so long that the distinction has softened past the point of usefulness. They were bolted down by hands that have long since stopped existing. They were intended, presumably, for some purpose — pumping, grinding, refining, listening, broadcasting. The intent is gone. The hands are gone. What remains is the activity itself, continuous, unvarying, a kind of breathing performed by something that no longer has lungs. They are the substrate. They are what the ridge is made of, now. A basalt spine dressed in copper and steel, threaded with pipes and cables, vibrating at frequencies that have not deviated by so much as a tenth of a decibel in living memory, perhaps in any memory at all. When the fauna feels the ridge, what it feels first is not the rock. It is the engines.

They are large in the way that geological things are large, not by intention but by accumulation — pumps that have grown through decades of repair, new sections welded onto old ones, valves added where valves failed, secondary systems wrapped around primary systems, the whole complicated organism thickened and doubled and redoubled until no one, not even the machines themselves, could say where one machine ended and another began. A pump here will be fed by a pipe that runs through three other pumps before returning to its own intake. A grinding assembly will pour its slurry into a separator that feeds a filtration bank that pressurizes the same slurry again. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is finished. The machines do not produce an output so much as they produce a continuation. They are eating their own tail, and they have been eating it for a very long time, and the meal is not yet over.

In some of the towers, the orange lamps are lamps. In others, they are something else — pilot lights on a furnace that has been lit since before the Second Industrial Revolution, since before the first time a human eye saw this ridge. The flame is small and steady and does not flicker because there is nothing to make it flicker. No draft. No current strong enough to register on a wick. No movement of any kind in the air, if there is air. There is a flame, and there is the dark, and the flame has been speaking to the dark for so long that the dark has started to listen, or at least to lean toward the flame when it passes. The towers have grown around these flames. New rooms have been added on the side of old rooms, hallways extended into darkness and never finished, ladders descending into shafts that go down past the basalt into something no longer considered rock. The flame is the reason the towers exist. Everything else is scaffolding for a fire that will not go out.

Who tends the flames? No one. No one has been in these towers for longer than the towers themselves have been standing, which is to say, longer than the lamps have been burning, which is to say, longer than anyone can say. A flame unattended does not, in the ordinary sense, persist. This one persists. Perhaps the air is enriched with something. Perhaps the fuel is fed by a small mechanism buried somewhere in the wall, ticking away in its own patient rhythm. Perhaps the flame is not a flame at all, but a thing that looks like a flame the way some of the fauna look like fish the way some of the buildings look like ships — a resemblance that has hardened into identity through sheer duration. Whatever it is, it has outlasted its keeper, and it is still burning, and it will go on burning, and the dark presses against the windows of the towers and cannot get in.

The fauna lives here.

That is the second thing to say, and the stranger, because the fauna should not exist. There is no sun. There is no plant. There is no surface. There is only rock and pressure and the dim chemical seeps from the vents and the deep slow leak of hydrocarbons from the basalt itself, and these are thin meals, thin enough that any biologist who came down here would have predicted, with great confidence, that nothing larger than a bacterium could survive on them. The biologists would have been wrong.

What blooms on these dark roots is not life as we mean the word, but it is not not-life either. It drifts. It feeds. It reproduces, or it does something that performs the function of reproduction without troubling itself with names. The smaller ones — the snow, the blizzard, the falling-rising thing that fills the water between the peaks — are barely there at all. They are translucent sacs the size of a fingernail, or smaller, trailing cilia so fine that they are visible only as a shimmer, and they spend their entire existence in suspension, neither sinking nor rising, holding their place in the water column through some mechanism no one has ever bothered to study because no one has come to study them. They eat what drifts. They eat each other. They eat, some of them, the noise.

The noise-eaters are the ones to dwell on, because they are the most wrong. They have evolved, in the total absence of light, in the presence of a sound so constant it has become indistinguishable from silence, organs tuned not to photons but to pressure waves. Their cilia are not the cilia of other ciliated things. They are long, fine, arranged in parabolic dishes across the creature's ventral surface, and they resonate, and when they resonate they separate something from the sound — something carried in the vibration, a modulation too subtle for any instrument built above the surface to detect, a signature perhaps left by the machines themselves, a by-product of the grinding, an accidental nutrient shaken loose into the water by the cathedral-sized pumps. The noise-eaters do not eat the sound. They eat what the sound is holding. The distinction matters, the way the distinction between a fisherman and a fish matters to the fisherman. The noise is the sea, and they live in it, and they are good at it.

The larger fauna is harder to look at. Pale, immense in the small, sometimes many meters long, with bodies that have given up on the idea of symmetry. Some have eyes, in the sense of organs that catch light, but the eyes are vestigial or worse — too many, or in the wrong places, or facing the wrong way, or looking at the wrong spectrum entirely. Some have no eyes at all and move through the world by other means, by echolocation perhaps, or by feeling the vibration of the ridge through their flanks. They drift past the windows of the towers in slow columns. They spiral up from the crevices in such density that the basalt beneath them disappears. They have been here longer than the machines, or they have been here only as long as the machines. There is no way to tell. There is no season. There is no growth ring. There is no skeleton that can be aged. A creature that does not change cannot be said to have been born, and a creature that cannot be said to have been born cannot be said to die, and so the fauna persists in the indefinite present tense, drifting, feeding, never quite arriving and never quite leaving.

Some of the larger ones have colonized the machines themselves. They nest in the pipes, in the bends and joints, in the spaces between the pumps. They have grown around the struts of the buildings like vines, though vines need sun and these do not. They have plugged themselves into the cable runs and the conduit, so that now, if you were to trace a cable from one tower to another, you would find at intervals a knot of pale flesh pulsing faintly in time with the vibration. Whether they are parasites or symbiotes or neither, no one knows. No one has come to find out. The machines do not mind. The fauna does not care. The arrangement is stable. The arrangement has been stable for longer than the memory of either party.

And so what lives here, in the end, is a single thing that has learned to call itself by three names.

The machines live here, and they do not know they live, and they do not need to know. The fauna lives here, and does not know it is strange, and does not need to know. The flame lives here, in the tower at the peak, and it does not know what it is illuminating, and it does not need to know. They are all the same age, or they are all of no age. They hum together. They have been humming together for so long that the hum has worn a groove in the basalt itself, a channel along the ridge that the sound travels through with less resistance than anywhere else on the planet, and the creatures feel the channel, and they gather in it, and the machines feed them by accident, and the fauna feeds the machines by being eaten by them in the pipes, and the whole long circuit closes and opens and closes again without anyone noticing that it has closed.

A thing that does not change is not alive. A thing that does not change is not dead.

A thing that does not change is just a thing.

And the thing on the ridge goes on humming, and the fauna goes on drifting, and the flame in the tower goes on burning, and none of them are the first, and none of them will be the last, and the dark stretches away in either direction and is patient, and has always been patient, and will be patient after the lamps go out, if the lamps ever go out, which they will not.
