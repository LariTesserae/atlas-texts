The basin is inhabited most densely by the columns, and they have been there longest. It is difficult to say where one of them ends and the system around it begins, because the system *is* them — they made the dust finer by dropping their skins, they shaded the ground until other things could root, they invited the beetles into the grooves of their spines and the lizards into the creases of their bark, and in return the beetles carried pollen from one pale flower-pulse to the next and the lizards dropped their white dry droppings into the dust at the base of each trunk, where the roots drank them. The columns do not move. They do not need to. Their argument with the climate is conducted in the slow language of chemistry and patience, and they have been winning it for so long that they have begun to forget the terms of the original negotiation. A column does not remember water. It remembers only the angle of the sun and the particular way the wind sounds when it comes through its own ribs.

The lizards are the next layer of citizenry, and they are the most visible of the animals because they have arranged themselves to be warm. They are small, drab, four-legged things with the watchful posture of creatures that have agreed, by long evolutionary compromise, to be eaten sometimes. They live on the walls of the settlements and on the south-facing faces of the columns; in the morning they are stacked like tiles, every body oriented to the rising warmth, and by the time the shadows have sharpened at noon they have gone to ground. They know the cracks. They are made, in some sense, for the cracks — long-bodied, loose-skinned, capable of flattening themselves into a fissure that looks, from a step away, like nothing at all. They eat the beetles. They eat the smaller wriggling things that turn over the dust. They are, in turn, eaten by the predators, and they accept this with the resignation of a class that has done the math. They have many young. The young are eaten faster than the old. The species persists anyway, in the way that a stream persists — by being always itself, never the same.

The beetles are older than the cities. They came up out of the dust when the first columns took root, and they have been in the grooves between the spines ever since. They are the color of the substrate — pale where the salt is, rust where the iron is, blue-grey where the minerals have mixed into something in between — and they navigate by the geometry of the columns the way water navigates a riverbed, following the ridges downward, branching where the trunk branches, climbing again toward the flowers when the flowers come. They are not numerous in any visible pile. They are numerous in the way that dust is numerous: always there, total in their thinness, never seen in their mass. They are the pollinators. Without them, the columns would flower in vain, and the basin would become, slowly, a forest of the dead.

The burrowers are a quieter order. They live in the dust itself, in the tunnels they make and the tunnels their ancestors made, and they surface only at the cool hours — the deep morning before the sun has climbed, the deep evening after it has dropped. They are pale, soft, almost eyeless, and they eat the dead: the fallen spines, the shed skins, the droppings of the lizards, the bodies of the beetles that did not make it back to a crevice. They are the undertakers. The basin depends on them the way a city depends on its sanitation, which is to say it does not think about them until they are gone.

The predators are the rarest and the most patient. There are perhaps a dozen kinds, and most of them are the size of a forearm or smaller, and they move between the columns with the careful deliberation of things that have calculated the distance between themselves and their prey a thousand times. They are not fast. They do not need to be fast. The lizards are slow. The beetles are slower. The burrowers are slower still. A predator can afford to be a thinker in this basin, because its prey has nowhere to run. They hunt by sound. They hunt by the displacement of dust. They hunt, in some cases, by sitting very still in a place they have sat before and waiting for the geometry of the columns to deliver something living into their reach. They are not numerous. Their prey is so plentiful that they have never had to be. They are the quietest of the inhabitants, and the most deliberate, and they are the reason the lizards sleep lightly.

The people are the most recent settlers, and they have arrived, by every measurable sign, very recently — within the last few hundred generations, which is nothing in the life of a column. They are tall, narrow-shouldered, dark-skinned in the way that anyone becomes dark-skinned who has lived in this much sun for this long. They are plant-people the way all desert peoples are plant-people: they have learned to read the basin the way a sailor reads the wind, to know which column is thirsty and which is full, which dust is safe to walk on and which is a salt crust that will not hold a wall. They are not numerous. They are not meant to be. The basin does not have the resources to support many, and they have known this for as long as they have been here. They are arranged in settlements that are dense in the way that fish are dense in a reef — high local concentration, low overall population, every individual within shouting distance of every other. They are quiet, even among themselves. The basin has taught them to be quiet. The walls of their houses are made of compressed dust and they are the color of the basin itself, and the roofs are stalks lashed with fiber, and the whole arrangement has the look of something that has been grown as much as built. They are not, in any sense, dominating the basin. They are living inside it. They have learned to live inside it the way the beetles have learned to live inside the columns — by being small, by being careful, by being willing to eat what the basin provides and to ask nothing of it that it cannot give.

They are the only inhabitants who have noticed, and remember, that the basin is young. The columns have no opinion about this. The lizards have no opinion about this. The beetles are too small to have opinions. The people, though, have stories about the mountains rising at the edges, and about the water that used to be in the deepest parts where the salt crust is now, and about the first of their ancestors who walked in from the rim and saw the columns and decided, against all evidence, that the place could be lived in. They tell these stories at the cool hours, inside the settlements, in voices that are kept low because the basin is a place where voices do not carry well and where you do not need to raise one to be heard. They are the youngest of the inhabitants, and the most aware of it, and they are the only ones who use the word *home* for the place. The columns do not call it anything. The basin simply is, and they are in it, and they have been in it longer than anyone else, and they will be in it longer than anyone else, and this is the only argument they make about the matter.
