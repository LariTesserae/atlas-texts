They live here in a number that could be counted on two hands, and that has not always been the case, and that is part of why the hall feels the way it does.

The first to be seen — if one were to come down the long companionway from the upper decks, past the dry iron stair, and into the flooded layer — is a woman. She is standing on the iron grating at the far end of the hall, near one of the working lights, and she is doing a thing that looks, from a distance, like she is talking to the pipe.

She is not talking to the pipe. She is listening to the pipe. She has one ear pressed against a length of it, a small brass fitting the size of a soup bowl, and the other ear is closed with a thumb, and her eyes are closed. She is wearing the standard-issue coverall of whatever institution still nominally owns this place — dark blue, the cuffs rolled three times to keep them from wicking water up her forearms, the name tag on the breast long since illegible except for the first letter, which is a *K*. She is barefoot, and her feet, on the grating, are the gray-white of skin that has not seen sun in a long time but has seen much else. She is forty, perhaps, or perhaps older. The hall has not yet taught her to look any particular age.

She is the one who listens.

Below her, in the knee-deep water, something moves, and if you looked down you would see it is a crab — not the crab that was once seen clinging to the pump housing, that crab is gone — but a crab nonetheless, a green-brown armored thing with claws folded and one stalked eye turned upward toward the light. It is not afraid of her. It has never been afraid of her. She has lived here long enough that the crabs have begun to regard her as a feature of the environment, like the dripping, like the steam from the joints.

There are other crabs. There are dozens. They live in the dark below the grating — below the inhabited layer, in the further darkness that has its own fixtures and its own pipes. They climb up through the holes where the rust has eaten through, at night, mostly, though some are bolder, or hungrier, or older, and come up in the amber cones of the working lights to feed on what the algae drops. They are the size of a man's spread hand, mostly. A few are bigger. The biggest one, the size of a dinner plate, lives in the third compressor from the wall, and has for years, and the woman — K. — feeds it scraps of things she finds in the sealed lockers of the upper decks, when she goes up to forage. She has named it, in her head, but the name is not a name one would guess from her appearance. She does not say it aloud. There is no one to say it to.

The hall is not unpopulated. The hall has been populated, in patches, for some time. There is a community here, though calling it a community is generous. It is more accurate to say there are five people who are not trying to leave.

K. is one. She came first, or she came longest ago, and the difference is mostly the same. She is the listener. Her job is to know which of the pipes is doing what, because some of them are doing things and some of them have not done anything in decades and the difference between those two states matters. The hall was built to do work. The hall may still be doing work, in some of its layers. The hall, in any case, groans on its schedule, and K. has learned that the groan is not a single thing but a conversation between the metal and the pressure and the water, and she can tell, by ear, when something has changed. When a pipe begins to sweat that did not sweat. When a pipe goes silent that should ring. When a joint begins to bloom steam where steam has not bloomed before. These are small things. They are the small things the hall can still communicate, and she is the one who hears them.

There are four others.

There is a man called — actually, no one calls him anything, but the logbook that is kept on a clipboard in the dry room above the flood calls him *Tech* — who does the work of keeping the lights alive. Three or four bulbs in the whole hall, amber, in their hexagonal cages of corroded brass, and the man who tends them is older than K. by perhaps ten years and younger by perhaps twenty; age is not a thing that operates normally here. He carries a small kit in a leather satchel, the leather of which has gone soft with the damp, and inside the satchel there are rolls of wire, a hand-cranked generator, a pair of pliers, a small jar of the kind of grease that keeps connections from oxidizing. He has, over the years, replaced each of the working bulbs at least once, and the filaments he replaces them with are not the same kind of filament that was originally in the cages. The filaments he uses glow a slightly different amber. He found a stash of them, once, in a sealed cabinet on the deck above. He does not know what kind of bulb they were for. He has used them for what he has.

There is a younger woman, the youngest of the five, who is not yet twenty. She is the diver. Not of the water — the water is knee-deep, you do not dive into it. But of the lower layers. Below the grating, there are floors and floors of drowned machinery, and once, early in her time here, she went down through a hole in the rust and found that it went on, and on, and on, and she came back up changed in some way that she does not discuss. Now she goes down, regularly, with a length of rope tied around her waist and the other end held by K., and she brings things back. Sometimes the things are useful. A wrench. A sealed tin. A coil of wire. Sometimes the things are just objects — a coffee cup with a logo no one recognizes, a child's plastic shoe, a book with pages that have gone to a single block of paste. She brings them up anyway. The bringing-up is the point. The hall, below the inhabited layer, is a museum of itself, and she is its curator.

There is a man who does not do much of anything that is visible. He sits, much of the time, on the raised platform of iron grating near the bank of squat boilers, and he watches the dark water pour over the lip of the vat and into the flood. He has been here longer than any of them except K., possibly longer than K. He does not speak. He is not, as far as anyone can tell, unhappy. He is, perhaps, the most adapted of them. The dripping of the overflow, the splash of it, the slow pulse of the flood being renewed — this is, for him, a kind of sleep. He sits in the amber light and he watches the water fall and he is not waiting for anything, because waiting would be too active a posture for him. He is just *here*. The crabs, occasionally, come up through the grate near him, and he will, with a slow and practiced hand, take a piece of the algae from the wall and offer it, and they will take it from his fingers. He is the most beloved by the crabs. He is also the most crab-like, in his way. He moves slowly. He has the gray-white skin. He has the patience.

And there is the fifth, who is the most recent, and who has not yet decided what they will be.

The fifth is the reason K. was listening to the pipe just now. The pipe, this morning, has begun to do something it did not do yesterday. It is singing, in a register below hearing, and K. is trying to determine whether the singing is a new song or an old song remembered. She does not yet know.

The animals, the ones that are not the crabs —

A fish. There is a fish in the grating, pale, small, that lives in the layer where the water is coldest. It is seen rarely. It is seen when the diver goes down. It is seen when a crab, in its travels, drives it up through the lattice. It is seen at the edges of the amber cones, where it flickers like a thought. The diver has named it. The diver does not say the name aloud either, for the same reason K. does not say her crab's name. There is no one to say it to, but the not-saying is part of the having.

Something in the ceiling. The thing in the ceiling that sounds, when it sounds, like a small motor — K. has determined, after years of listening, that this is a bat. Or what was once a bat. Or the descendant of a bat. Or a thing that the hall has produced, from the warm steam and the small flying insects that feed on the algae at night, that resembles a bat the way a pipe resembles a vein. It lives in the upper darkness. It has never come down into the inhabited layer, or if it has, no one has seen it. It is heard only sometimes, only when the hall is otherwise quiet, and then for a few minutes it goes *kzzz-kzzz-kzzz* against the metal and is silent. The fifth person, who is new, asked, on their first night, what the sound was, and K. said, "the house settling," and then, when pressed, said, "a thing in the ceiling," and when pressed further said, "a thing like a bat," and when pressed further did not say anything.

Algae, of course. The algae is everywhere. The algae is the color of old copper and the texture of wet hair. It hangs in curtains from the lower pipes. It slicks the walls in sheets so black they look like paint. It grows up through the grate where the rust is. It is, for the people here, the only constant green. The woman in the diver's role pulls it down, sometimes, in handfuls, and eats it — it tastes, she says, of nothing, and then, when pressed, says, of iodine, and when pressed further does not say anything. The man who watches the overflow has, on occasion, been seen to chew on a length of it absently, like a strand of grass.

A fern. The fern is in the crack in the load-bearing column. Three fronds, each one the color of a week-old bruise. It has been there longer than anyone. It was there before K. arrived. It will, probably, be there after. It is the closest thing the hall has to a pet. K. waters it. She waters it by passing it on her way to listen to a pipe, and pausing, and cupping a handful of the black water and letting it fall into the crack. The fern does not respond to this. It has never responded to anything. It is simply there, growing, in the one place in the hall where the light touches a vertical surface and the water reaches but does not pool, and it is the only thing in the whole drowned cathedral that has the decency to look like it is alive in a way one could understand.

The fungi. There is a fungus on the wall behind the squat boilers, white and faintly pink, the texture of a brain. The diver, who goes down into the lower layers, has reported that the fungus is much larger below — there are sheets of it on the drowned machinery, whole panels of pink-white growth, and in some places it has climbed the pipes and gone around the valves in a slow embrace. The fungus is not eaten. The fungus is not touched. The fungus is left alone, the way certain things in certain places are left alone because the leaving-alone is part of the agreement between the place and the thing that lives in it.

The hall itself.

It is not alive. It is not alive in any way the people here would claim. But it is *more* than not-alive, and the more is the part that is hardest to describe, and that K., who has been here longest, has stopped trying to describe, and that the man who watches the overflow has perhaps never tried.

It groans, on its schedule. It drips, on its schedule. The overflow pours, on its schedule. The few lights burn, on their schedule. The temperature is whatever the metal is. The hall, in other words, *does things*, and the things it does are not random, and the things it does are not chosen, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a ruin and a beginning, and the people here — the five of them, the crabs, the fish, the bat, the fern, the fungus, the algae — live in the difference.

The fifth person, who is new, will learn. They will learn to listen, in time, and to watch, and to not ask certain questions, and to wait in a way that is not really waiting. They will learn that the hall is the hall, and they are in it, and it does not need them, and they do not need it, and yet here they are.

A drop falls. It hits the water. The small ring spreads. A crab, somewhere in the lattice below, has begun to climb.
