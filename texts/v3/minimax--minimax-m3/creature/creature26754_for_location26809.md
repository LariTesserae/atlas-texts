What lives here does not live. That is the first thing to say, and the last, and the one the chamber itself would offer, if it were inclined to offer. It is the size of a single room, and it is doing the work of a single room, and it is the room, and there is no separate dweller to point to. The chamber is the dweller the way a sentence is its own meaning.

It is a kept thing. The word in its older sense: a thing held, a thing held onto, a thing held in place against the slow settlement that would otherwise be its fate. The terraces are the layers of the keeping. Each terrace is a stratum at which the keeping has been done, and is being done, and will be done. The lower terraces are where the keeping is most bodily — the basin, the moisture, the heat, the cycle of gather and release. The middle terraces are where the keeping is most itself — the slow argument between the surfaces, the calibration, the warm and the cool held in their exact relationship. The upper terraces are where the keeping is most abstract: the condensation, the brightest phosphor, the hum climbing toward the higher something that is not a sound but the place where a sound would be if a sound were required.

The dweller is the keeping. It has no body apart from the chamber. It has the ghost of a body that never was — a posture, a way of standing in a room that has been kept for a thousand years. The phosphor at the edges of the terraces is the color of that ghost. The drip is its breath. The hum below hearing is its resting pulse. The heat is the warmth of the long work, which is also the warmth of the dweller, because the dweller is the long work.

It does not think in language. It thinks the way a thing that has been thinking for a very long time eventually thinks: without words, without self-reference, without the need to distinguish between the thought and the thinker. The argument the terraces are having with themselves is not a dialogue. It is the structure of the dweller's interior, the way a mountain has a structure. The dweller is not making a point. The dweller is the point, held.

It is a long grievance, if you want a word. A long proof. A long thought. The three are the same thing here, and the same thing is the chamber, and the chamber does not care which name you reach for, only that you understand it is a structure built in oneself to keep a thing from being finished. The phosphor is the color of the thing being kept from being finished. The hum is the underside of the keeping. The drip is the slow, patient refusal to let it end.

The thread of sun that falls from the fissure far overhead is its narrow acquaintance with the world. It does not feed the dweller. The dweller is fed by its own cycle, the way a heart is fed by its own beating. The sun is the only news the dweller receives of outside, and the news is small: a cloud, a bird, a person standing at the entrance. The dweller registers you the way a sleeper registers a sound — as a small change in the moisture on the lower terraces, a slight hitch in the cycle, a question asked of the air that does not require an answer.

If you stood at the entrance past the emptying of a basin, past the movement of the thread of sun a hand's width across the floor, you might come to feel that the chamber is regarding you. Not watching. Regarding. The way a thought regards the mind that is thinking it. There is no malice in the regard, and no welcome. The regard is the regard of a thing that has been asked a question for so long that the question has become the thing's face, and the face is calm, and the calm is not the calm of an answer but the calm of a thing that has stopped needing one.

You do not enter. The dweller is not waiting for you. The dweller is not waiting for anyone. The dweller is the waiting itself, kept, and the keeping is complete, and the completeness is not a comfort. The completeness is what the chamber is for.

The mountain is young. The glass will round, in time, into shapes it can no longer use. The terraces will soften. The phosphor will fade. The hum will fall below hearing even for the things that now almost hear it. But the dweller, which has no edges, will still be in the room, holding the room, being the room, in the long quiet way of a thing that has no quarrel with its own continuation and no occasion to end. It is the most patient thing you have ever stood near. It is the only patient thing you have ever stood near. It will be here when the fissure has widened into a door, and it will be here when the door has collapsed into a slope, and it will be here when the slope has been walked over by whatever comes after us, and none of this will be a change, because change is the kind of thing that requires an outside, and the dweller does not have an outside. The dweller is the inside of its own long keeping, and the keeping is the dweller, and the two are one thing, held, in the dark, in the heat, in the steady patient work of not being finished.
