There is no single word for the things that live here. The plateau does not invite that kind of language. The plateau invites you to slow down until your categories give up, and then to keep going.

Begin small. Begin with the stone-coloured.

They are everywhere, and you cannot see them. A square metre of the warm grey-brown rock here is not rock in the way you mean. It is a city. It is a tenancy that has been worked out over a span of time you would have to invent a new unit to count. The creatures that live in that square metre live in the hairline cracks that the eye slides over, in the loose flakes that have not yet decided to fall, in the thin felt of moss that is more architecture than plant. They are the colour of the stone. They are the colour of the lichen. They are so much the colour of the stone that when they stop moving you cannot find them again, and after a while you stop trying, and after a while longer you realise that finding them was never the point. They are not hiding from you. They are simply not performing for you. They were here before you arrived and they will be here after, and the shape of their lives has nothing to do with the shape of your visit.

Most of them are very small. Some of them are smaller than very small. They live in the spaces between the spaces. They breathe, if they breathe, in rhythms that are too slow to call respiration. They eat the lichen, or they eat what eats the lichen, or they are the lichen's way of eating the stone, or some part of this cycle that has not been named in any book you have brought with you. When the light is low — the small hours, the cooler hours, the brief sweet window after the mist has gathered and before the air has warmed — they come out and move across the surface in patterns that are too patient to call a swarm. A swarm implies urgency. These things do not have urgency. They have direction, but it is the direction of something reading a long document at a careful pace, turning pages only when the previous one has been properly understood.

If you kneel — and you must kneel, because to see them properly you have to give up the height you came in with — you can watch one of them for a long time and not see it do anything. And then, after you have given up watching, you can look at a patch of stone a few inches away and see one of them doing something extremely slow that you would have missed if you had been a second earlier or a second later. They are not slow the way a slug is slow. They are slow the way the weathering of stone is slow. The difference is that they are alive, or whatever word the plateau uses for what they are.

Under the overhangs, on the lee sides of the great knuckles, in the shallow caves that are not quite caves but only the spaces where the rock has decided not to fall, there are other inhabitants. These are not stone-coloured. These are pale — a kind of ash-white, or the colour of the inside of a shell, or the colour of nothing at all. They hang from the upper surfaces in clusters, like fruit, like grapes, like something you would not want to put in your mouth. They are quiet in the way that a held breath is quiet. When you pass under them, you hear them shift — a sound like the inside of a paper bag being crumpled very slowly, or like the page of a thick book being turned in a room where no one is breathing. They are not alarmed by you. They are not unalarmed. They are in some state that does not have a name in your language, a state of having been noticed and continuing anyway. If you hold up a hand, very slowly, toward the cluster, they do not flee. They simply adjust. They make a little more room for you in their idea of the world. The hand passes through the empty air where they were a moment ago, and they are now an inch to the side, and you are not sure if they moved or if you did.

Across the open stone, in the cooler hours, there are the things that look like the dropped husks of seeds. They are about that size, and that shape — a kind of teardrop, papery, the colour of dead bracken. If you walk past them, you walk past them. If you stand still and look at one for a long time, you will see it move. It walks on legs that are too thin to see, and when it walks it rocks slightly, the way a dry leaf rocks when it is blown across a path, except there is no wind to blame. It walks with purpose, but the purpose is its own. It walks toward a crevice, or away from a crevice, or in a long curve that you would have to follow for an hour to see the end of. If you pick one up — and you might, because they look so much like a husk that the hand reaches before the mind has finished consenting — it is heavier than you expected, and warm, and the warmth is not the warmth of the sun. It is the warmth of a thing that has been making its own heat, or that has been holding the heat of the day in some way that stones usually do not. You put it down. It waits. It walks on.

And there are the things that flit. Across the surface of the stone, in low arcs, pale as ash. They do not fly so much as throw themselves, and then land, and then throw themselves again. They land without sound. They are gone before you can focus on them. You will not catch one. You will not even see one clearly. You will see the arc, the pale suggestion of a body, the landing that is not a landing but a fading, and you will be left holding the shape of an absence in your hand. They are most active in the half-light, when the sun has gone but the sky has not yet given up its pearl, and they move in patterns that cross your path and recross it and recross it again as if they are reading something written on the ground that you are not tall enough to see.

The maybe-birds are the hardest. You cannot tell if they are birds. They hang at the edges of the thermals in the middle of the day, when the air has finally remembered how to move, and they ride those thermals in wide circles without ever quite beating a wing. Their shadows pass across the rock and the shadows have no sound. The shadows are long and thin and they move at a speed that does not match the speed of anything casting them. If you look up, you cannot find the thing that is casting the shadow. If you look down, the shadow is gone, or the shadow has moved to a different part of the rock, or there are several shadows now, crossing each other, and you have the strange feeling that the rock itself is being read by something overhead. They do not call. They do not cry. They make no sound at all, except that sometimes, when the wind is right, you can hear a faint hiss — not from them, exactly, but from the air moving around them, as if the air is slightly surprised by their shape.

There are tussocks of a coarse grey grass in the hollows, planted as if by someone with a careful eye, and low shrubs with small hard leaves and a smell like sage but darker, and things that look like plants and may not be — pale fronds that wave without any wind you can feel, and seem to pull back when your shadow crosses them. There are the liver-coloured slimes that colonise the places where the mist gathers longest, felts of moss and lichen and something older. None of it taller than your knee. None of it making a sound when you push through it, because there is almost nothing to push through. All of it, in its withdrawn way, alive.

And there are the things that hunt.

You hear them before you see them, if you hear them at all — a thin rising note, not quite a scream, not quite a call, more like the question the air asks itself when it has been still too long. It comes from somewhere you cannot locate. It comes from a direction that has no opposite. You never see the thing that made the sound, and you never see the thing the sound was made at, and you are left with the impression of an exchange that was completed long before you arrived and will continue long after you have decided you did not hear it. Whatever hunts here hunts in a register that has very little to do with urgency, or with appetite in the way you mean appetite. It hunts the way the stone weathers, or the way the lichen eats, or the way the patience of the small creatures in the cracks becomes, after enough time, the patience of the rock itself. The prey is patience. The predator is patience. The exchange is conducted at a scale your eyes cannot resolve.

Then there is the hum.

You feel it before you hear it, in the soles of your feet, in the bones of your legs, in the place where your body meets the ground and the ground meets the rock and the rock meets whatever the rock is resting on. It is not mechanical. It is not biological. It is not the hum of a hive or a generator or a wire. It is the hum of a thing that is holding still. It is the sound the plateau makes of being the plateau. It has been making this sound for a long time — longer than the shelf has been worn, longer than the half-circle has been losing its slabs, longer than the lichen has been eating the lower stones. It is the plateau's version of breathing, or of thinking, or of being asleep with one eye open.

When you stop walking, the silence comes back, but it is not empty. It is dense with all the things you are not hearing. The hum is still there, underneath, but the not-hearing has its own texture. It is the texture of a place that has finished happening.

And then there are the marks.

The half-circle of dark slabs on the lee side of one of the knuckles is not a ruin in the way you usually mean. A ruin is something that was whole and is now broken. This was never whole. It is the suggestion of a wall. It is the memory of a wall held by the stone that was not used. Whoever set the slabs there did not build so much as place. They did not construct. They indicated. They used what was already here, and they used as little as possible, and what they made was so close to nothing that the wind has been able to take it apart at its leisure, and the lichen has been eating it for a long time, and the sand is finishing the job. In a few hundred years it will not be a mark at all. It will just be stones.

The single standing stone is taller than the rest, and there is a shelf cut into its face near the top. The shelf is worn now. It is worn the way a word is worn when it has been said too many times and has started to mean less. You cannot say what it was for. It could have been a place to set something. It could have been a step. It could have been a marker for something only the people who cut it would have recognised, and those people are not here, and may never have been here in the way you mean here. The cut is old. Older than the lichen, almost. Older than the things that hang in the clusters under the overhangs, or older than their lineage, anyway. It was made before any of the living things you can see were born. It was made in a different silence, when the wind was different, when the mist was different, when the plateau was still in the middle of becoming the plateau.
