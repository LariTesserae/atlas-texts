The inhabitants do not announce themselves. You have to learn to read them the way you would read a place that is mostly stone, by the small tells — a shadow that is too regular, a stillness that is too deliberate, a soft pulse in a wall that should be solid. The chamber is full of them, and they are full of the chamber, and the boundary between the two has become a kind of suggestion.

The first ones you might notice are the fingers.

They grow from the layered pale crust in clusters, the way a small congregation grows from a single decision. They are the color of bone that has been wet for a long time, or of wax left out in weather, or of the inside of a shell after the animal has gone. They are not stiff. They have the give of cartilage, of an earlobe, of something that has remembered it is alive and is being careful about it. Each one is roughly the length and thickness of a child's smallest finger, though some are longer, and some are no more than stubs. They lean at all angles, some upright, some horizontal, some bent as if they were reaching around a corner that no longer exists. Their surfaces are faintly ringed, the way bark is ringed, the way a knuckle is ringed, as if they had been built in stages and had not bothered to smooth the seams.

They withdraw when touched. This is the thing about them that is most immediate, most personal, the way a handshake is the most personal thing about a person you have just met. The withdrawal is slow but absolute. The finger folds inward on itself, like a glove finger being pulled inside out by an invisible thumb, and the soft inside becomes the outside and is tucked away into a small dark mouth in the crust. The mouth closes. The place where the finger was is just a small hole, slightly puckered, slightly wet. You could wait there for a long time and not see it come back. If you waited long enough, you might see a pale tip emerge again, tentative, the way a thought emerges from a person who has been silent for a while.

These are not the only fingers. There are other clusters, smaller, in the lower folds of the crust. There are ones that grow in rings around small vents. There are ones, deep in the layered growth, that are nearly black, as if they had taken on the color of what they have been eating. The chamber is full of fingers, in the way a forest is full of branches. They are the most visible inhabitants because they have chosen to be visible — or because they have not yet learned to hide. They feed, you can tell, by extending the fingers into the slow current at the ceiling and letting the current bring them things. The fingers are faintly sticky. If you were to touch one and pull your hand away slowly, you would feel a small resistance, the way a thread of honey resists being broken. And when the finger is withdrawn, it takes whatever was on its surface back inside, into the body of the thing, and the body digests in private.

The pulsing collars are different.

They are not limbs. They are whole creatures, or whole parts of creatures, or whole colonies — the description given by the chamber is that they are collars of mucus, but mucus is not quite right either. They are translucent, the color of cold tea, the color of jellyfish that have lived a long time and stopped being particular about it. They are open at the top, the way a vase is open at the top, or the way a throat is open. The opening pulses. It is the only motion they make, this opening and closing, this dilation and contraction, a slow heartbeat of the rim, and the rest of the body is a soft, sagging bag that hangs from the rim and is anchored, somewhere deeper in the layered growth, to a place you cannot see.

Through the opening, the current passes. In goes the cold water and whatever the cold water carries. Out goes the cold water and whatever the cold water has become. The exchange is small — you would not notice it unless you were looking for it — but it is constant, and the pulse that drives it is the same pulse you feel in the rock itself when the rock decides to give up a bubble of gas. The collars are breathing the way the rock is breathing. They are part of the breath.

There are many of them. They crowd the soft places where the pale crust has built up into folds and pockets, and they lean against one another the way sleeping animals lean, with the comfortable weight of bodies that have agreed, without discussing it, to share a space. Some of them are large, the size of a cupped hand, and some are no bigger than a thumbnail, and the small ones grow on the sides of the large ones, and the large ones do not seem to mind. The whole arrangement has the quality of a city made of bells, all ringing at slightly different rates, so that the sound, if there were sound, would be a slow chord that never resolved.

Among the collars and the fingers, the black shapes keep still.

They are not the same black. Some of them are the matte black of a stone that has been wet for years. Some of them are the wet black of an eye that has decided not to look at you. Some of them are a black that is almost purple, almost red, the black of a thing that contains blood and is not showing it. They are fixed to the pale crust at angles that suggest they were placed there by a careful hand, the way barnacles suggest that the rock decided to wear them, but the placement is too varied, too deliberate, too much like the placement of objects in a room by someone who was thinking about where to put them. Some are flat against the wall. Some are raised on small stalks. Some are nested inside other black shapes, the way one cup sits inside another.

They do not move when you look at them. They do not move when you do not look at them. They do not move when you touch them, and this is how you know they are alive, because the touch wakes a small current around them, a soft displacement of water, and the water moves away from the black shape in a way that a stone could not produce. They are breathing so slowly that the breath has become indistinguishable from the patience of the water around them. To see them breathe, you have to watch for a long time, the way you have to watch a sleeping animal to see it breathe. The body inflates by a fraction. It deflates by a fraction. The inflation takes a minute. The deflation takes a minute. You could miss the whole thing if you looked away.

Some of them are smooth and round and have the look of a fruit that has been left in a dark place for a season. Some of them are ridged, layered, the way a thumbprint is layered, and the layers are darker at the edges and lighter in the center, as if the creature had been growing outward in slow rings the way a tree grows in slow rings. Some of them are small enough to cover with a coin. Some of them are as large as a closed fist, and these are the oldest, and the pale crust has built up around their bases the way moss builds up around the base of a stone, the way a city builds up around a thing that has decided to stay.

They are filter feeders, the black shapes. Or they are grazers. Or they are simply present, and the food comes to them the way light comes to a thing that has decided to lie still. Their mouths, if they have mouths, are not on display. Their mouths are somewhere inside the stillness, somewhere in the private architecture of the body, and the water brings them what the water brings, and they take what they take, and the rest of them remains a black shape against the pale crust, an absence that has learned to stay in one place.

There are other inhabitants, smaller, less certain of themselves.

There are threads. Fine, hair-like, almost transparent, they drift from the layered growth into the open water, and they do not seem to be attached to anything in particular, and they do not seem to be going anywhere in particular. They are alive the way a doubt is alive — present, slightly nervous, unwilling to commit. They retract if the water moves too quickly past them. They extend again when the water calms. They are the antennae of things that live entirely inside the crust, things that have not yet decided to come out, things that are still considering whether to be creatures at all.

There are spirals. Small, coiled, the color of tarnished brass, they cling to the edges of the layered growth and rotate so slowly that the rotation is a guess. They are the kind of creature that builds a shell and then lives inside the shell and then builds a little more shell on top, and the shell is a record, and the record is read by nobody. They are doing what they have always done. They will continue doing what they have always done. They are not in any hurry, and neither is the chamber.

There are the small mouths. You notice them in the layered growth the way you notice doors in a wall — by their regularity, by their suggestion of something on the other side. They are round, the diameter of a small coin, and they are set in the pale crust at intervals that feel deliberate, the way windows are set in a wall. Some are closed. Some are open. Some are open and showing, in the small darkness behind them, a faint pulse, a faint movement, the suggestion of a creature that has not yet come out and may not come out while you are here. The mouths are the entrances to the incipient city. Through them, the city has its guts, its hallways, its private rooms. Through them, the things that have not yet chosen to be seen are doing the things they are doing.

The builders are not separate from the eaters. The builders are the eaters. The pale crust itself, the layered growth, the white bloom along certain edges — this is being laid down by the inhabitants, by the soft slow processes of their bodies. The fingers leave a faint residue when they withdraw. The collars deposit, in their pulsing, a fine sediment that hardens, given time, into the layered structure. The black shapes, breathing in their slow minute-long breaths, exhale a small chemistry that the water carries and the rock accepts, and the rock adds it to itself the way a wall accepts the lime of a trowel. The chamber is being built by its inhabitants. The chamber is the inhabitants. The city is the citizens. The reef is the reef-builders. The place is what its living things have agreed, without ever meeting, to do.

The eaters are eating what is being built. This is also part of the building. There are things — small, soft, with the look of tongues — that graze along the pale crust and take a thin layer of it into themselves. The crust is slightly thinner where they have been. In a year, perhaps, the thin place will be a groove. In a century, the groove will be a corridor. The eaters are sculpting the place by eating it, the way a river sculpts a valley by running through it, the way a thought sculpts a mind by being thought. They are not enemies of the builders. They are the negative space of the builders, the place where the builders have not yet filled in.

And there are the simply present.

A dense thicket of bodies, in the deeper folds, where the light does not quite reach. They lean against one another in clusters the size of a head, the size of a melon, the size of a small loaf. They are not attached to the crust, exactly, but they are not floating, either. They hold themselves in place by leaning, the way a crowd holds itself in place by leaning. Some of them are the same translucent tea-color as the collars, but smaller, and they do not pulse, or they pulse so faintly that the pulse is a rumor. Some of them are pale and segmented, with the suggestion of legs that have not decided to be legs. Some of them are dark and compact, with a small mouth at one end and a small opening at the other and not much in between. They are pressed so close together that you cannot tell, looking at them, where one ends and the next begins. They share warmth, if warmth is what they share. They share the work of staying still. They are the population of a place that has not yet decided whether it is a city or a thicket, and they are content, in the way that things are content, to be either.

They are not aware of one another. This is the most striking thing about them, more striking than their stillness, more striking than their patience. They do not react to their neighbors. They do not communicate, or if they communicate, the communication is so slow and so soft that it has become indistinguishable from chemistry. A creature on one side of a cluster releases, into the water between them, a small molecule. The molecule reaches the creature on the other side of the cluster in a few minutes, or a few hours. The creature receives it. The creature does not know it has received it. The creature does something slightly different as a result. The whole cluster is, very faintly, in conversation, the way a forest is in conversation, the way a city is in conversation, with itself.

They are afraid of nothing here. You can see this in the way they hold themselves, the way they do not brace against anything, the way their surfaces are smooth and undefended. There is no predator in this chamber. There is nothing in this chamber that hunts. The chamber is too young for hunting, or too cold, or too patient. The creatures are arranged by who eats whom, but the eating is slow, is grazing, is the taking of films and the leaving of residues. There is no chase. There is no sudden motion. There is no fear. The place has not yet developed the kind of urgency that fear requires.

You are here, and they do not have a name for you.

You put your hand near one of the fingers, and the finger withdraws, and the withdrawal is the only recognition the chamber has of you. You breathe out, and the breath rises in a small column of bubbles, and the bubbles pass through the pewter light, and the creatures do not flinch, and the bubbles are gone. You are large to them. You are loud to them. You are warm to them, in a way that is almost offensive, the way a bonfire is offensive to a place that has been cold for a long time. They have no concept of you. You are a slow event, like the thrum in the jaw, like the click of cooling rock, like the cold itself. You are something that has arrived, and the chamber has registered your arrival in the only way it knows how to register anything, which is by slightly rearranging its currents around you, and then continuing.

You do not have a name for them. You could give them names. You could call the fingers one thing and the collars another and the black shapes a third, and the names would be useful for a few minutes, and then the names would fail, because the creatures are not, in any final way, the things you have called them. The finger that withdrew from your touch was not, in that withdrawal, being a finger. It was being a creature responding to a disturbance. The black shape that breathed in its minute-long breath was not, in that breath, being a black shape. It was being a life, conducting its chemistry, persisting. The names you give are the names of the things they look like. The things they look like are not the things they are. They are possibilities. They are incipient. They have not yet decided to be the things you have decided they are.

The chamber keeps its silence.

The pewter light falls on the layered pale growth, and on the fingers that lean from it, and on the collars that pulse at their slow rate, and on the black shapes that hold themselves still against the crust, and on the threads that drift and retract, and on the spirals that build their small coiled shells, and on the mouths that open and close in the wall, and on the dense thicket of simply-present bodies leaning against one another in the deeper folds. The light does not decide what any of them is. The light does not need to decide. The creatures are doing the things they are doing, and the doing of those things is what the place is, and the place is what the chamber is, and the chamber keeps its quiet like a cupped hand holds water, and the chamber does not want you to hear it, and the chamber wants to continue.

The water is patient. The rock is patient. The cold is patient. The inhabitants are patient in the way that only the very young, or the very old, know how to be patient — without trying, without deciding, without knowing they are doing it. They are here in numbers. They are here in patience. They are here in the slow accumulating weight of a place that is becoming, by the act of being inhabited, the thing it is going to be.
