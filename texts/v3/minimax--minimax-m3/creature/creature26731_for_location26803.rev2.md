Begin with the smallest. They are the ones you notice first, though you will never see them clearly.

There are things in the walls.

You hear them before anything else — a fine papery scratching, the sound of a fingernail drawn very lightly along a seam. It comes from everywhere at once and from nowhere in particular, a thin seam-sound running just under the threshold of attention. If you stand very still and let your breath thin out and your eyes unfocus from the warm slabs of light on the tile floor, you may, in the corner of your vision, see one.

It is no bigger than your smallest fingernail. It is the color of the felted dust between the tiles — that slow accretion of walked fiber, that almost-granular, almost-alive felt that has been trodden and re-trodden until it is finer than cloth. The thing is flat. It is segmented. It runs along the line where wall meets ceiling, where wall meets floor, in the dark creases the tilework admits only when no one is looking. Its front edge lifts and tests the air, the way an ant's antennae test the air, but quicker, and without the courtesy of staying still long enough to be named.

What it does, you cannot quite tell. It may be eating the dust that would otherwise clog the joints. It may be laying, in the joints, a new fiber of its own — a tiny thread, finer than spider-silk, of the same color as itself — so that the seams stay tight. It may be doing neither, or both, or something the place has not yet a word for. It does not pause for you. It does not register you. It moves with the efficient indifference of a thing that has been doing this work for a very long time and will continue doing it long after you have become a pattern in the floor.

Sometimes, as you stand there, a tile shifts in its setting. You hear the small grind of it, the soft ceramic complaint. Then the tile shifts back, exactly where it was, as if it had only been remembering where it belonged. It had. The thing in the wall has passed behind it, and the tile had to make room, and then make room again, because the thing has gone on. The tiles know about the things. The things know about the tiles. They have been negotiating the seams since before you came in, if you came in, and they will be negotiating after you are a faint dark place in the green.

There are things in the growth.

These you only see when they choose, and they choose rarely. The growth is everywhere — a fur of dark wet-looking filaments climbing the columns, hanging from the arches in long curtains that brush the floor — and it is dense, and it is patient, and it drinks the light. To see a thing in the growth you must stand where the curtains are thinnest, near the floor where the green puddles outward in its dark almost-still pools, and you must wait, and you must let your eyes go soft.

Then, somewhere in the fiber, a shape will bulk. The curtain will press outward from inside, slowly, as if something the size of a folded hand is leaning against it from within. The shape will hold for a long moment — long enough for you to see that it is a shape, that it has a curve to it, that the curve has a direction — and then it will ease back, and the filaments will close over the place where it was, and the green will sway for a long time afterward, as if remembering.

You might see, in the swaying, the suggestion of a spine. The long pale curve of a side. The turn of a head regarding you through the layered green, with an eye, if it is an eye, that is dark and round and looks at you without alarm, the way the larger creatures will look at you from the upper galleries. Then the filaments knit, and the shape is gone, and you are not sure you saw it. The growth has a way of smoothing over its own small disturbances. The place does not like to be witnessed too closely.

What the things in the growth are, the place has not decided, or has decided and will not say. They are part-growth, perhaps — a denser knot of filament that has remembered, briefly, how to bulk. They are part-dust — the felted fiber of the floor, somehow climbed, somehow drawn up into the green and given a slow body there. They are part-builder, in some early stage you were not meant to see. They are the place's slow digestion of itself, perhaps. The way a forest has its fungi, its patient things that turn the fallen needle back into root. They have no mouths you can find. They feed, you think, the way the growth feeds — by sitting in the light, by being in it for a long time, by letting the light soak into them until they are saturated and slow.

The larger creatures are less often seen and harder to forget.

You might be standing in the long nave, in the warm light, listening to the hum you feel in your teeth before you hear it, and you will look up — why, you cannot say; the air has changed, perhaps, in some way you have no name for — and there, crossing the upper gallery, will be a long pale shape.

It moves without sound, or with so little sound that the place absorbs it before it reaches you. It is the length of a person lying down, perhaps longer. It is horizontal. Its underside is the color of the tile floor, and its back is ridged in small dark plates that fit together the way the tiles fit together, with the same deliberate spacing, the same almost-grout of darker line between each plate. It is armored, in the way the architecture is armored. It is made of the place's own surfaces, somehow worn into a body.

Its head, if it has a head, is at the front and is rounded. Its eye — if what you saw was an eye — is dark and round and looks down at you from the gallery rail without alarm. It is not afraid of you. It is not curious about you. It regards you the way a ceiling regards a floor: with the steady, untroubled attention of a thing that has always been there and will always be there, and that you are passing through, briefly, on your way to becoming something the place will keep.

It crosses the gallery and is gone behind a column. Where it passed, the hanging green is parted, and the parting takes a long time to close. When it closes, the filaments there are slightly disordered, and in the disorder you can see, if you look, a thin line of lighter green that was not there before — as if the creature's passage had marked the growth, or the growth had recorded the creature's passage as a kind of memory. A footprint in felt. A wake in water. A thought, held for a moment in the green.

These creatures have built nothing.

They are not builders. They live in what was built, the way a thought lives in a head, the way a draft lives in a room. They move through the architecture the way your attention moves through you. They are the place's larger fauna, the way a forest has its deer, its quiet browsers in the long green. They are made of the place's substance — tile, dust, a little slow fiber — and they are the place's way of regarding its own surface from inside. They have no need of you. They will watch you, when they watch you, with the same dark round unalarmed eye.

But the builders.

The builders are still here. They are in everything, if you know how to look.

You will not find them by searching. You will find them by standing still, and letting the place come to you, and by letting your eyes go soft the way you let them go soft for the things in the growth.

At the base of a column, in the tile pattern, there are toes.

Five of them. Slightly too long, slightly too deliberate, set at the angle of a foot that was standing, once, in a finished stance — the stance of a person who has just set down a weight and is resting, or a person who has just lifted a weight and is about to set it down. The grout between the toes is darker than the grout between the other tiles, and if you put your hand to it you will find it is slightly warmer. The toes do not move. But the tiles around the toes have, over years, shifted almost imperceptibly — a fraction of a degree, a hair's width — so that the foot is no longer quite the foot it was. The toes have spread. The arch has deepened. The heel, which you had not at first noticed, has settled deeper into the floor. The builder is becoming the foundation. The builder is being absorbed into the work it made, the way a root is absorbed into the soil it grew in. The builder is still working. The builder is working, now, on becoming floor.

In a niche, a bowl is held by a hand.

The hand is also tile. The fingers are articulated in the glaze — pale green, mostly, with a fault line of darker green where the thumb meets the palm, and a small chip out of the index finger that you might mistake for damage until you notice that the chip is mirrored in the bowl's rim, and that the two chips fit, and that the hand and the bowl have been completing each other for longer than you have been alive. The hand is curled around the bowl the way a hand curls around a bowl it has just set down and is not yet ready to let go of. The bowl is empty. The bowl has been empty for longer than the memory of the builders. The hand will not let go. The hand cannot, anymore, because the hand is the bowl's handle, and the bowl is the hand's continuation, and the niche is the hand's rest. The hand is the work, and the work is not finished, and so the hand will not let go.

In the high galleries, in the corners where the light falls in slabs and the green hangs thickest, there are shapes that are almost figures.

A kneeling silhouette, head bowed, hands in lap, the filaments drawn so densely that the shape is almost black against the green that surrounds it. A standing silhouette, one arm raised, holding nothing, the fingers spread in the posture of someone showing a height, or measuring a distance, or simply reaching for something that is no longer there. They do not move. But the growth around them is alive, and the filaments nearest them lift, slightly, toward the figure, as if drawn, as if the figure is the source of a small local warmth, as if the figure is breathing, very faintly, in the green.

These are the builders in their later stage. They have moved from tile into fiber, from the rigid into the soft, from the worked into the worked-upon. They are no longer the bones of the place. They are the place's slow thought. They are still working. They are still building. They are building themselves, slowly, into the green, the way a slow vine builds itself into the wall it climbs. In a year, the kneeling figure will be deeper green. In ten, the standing figure will have raised its arm a finger's width. In a hundred, the kneeling figure will have grown a faint nimbus of lighter green around its bowed head, as if something has begun to happen there, as if the figure has begun, very slowly, to be the source of its own small warmth.

And the growth itself.

The growth is not a creature. Or it is not only a creature. It is the place's skin, and its lung, and its slow eye.

It drinks the light. You can watch it happen, on the slow afternoons, when the sun finds the high windows and the slabs of light fall across the tile floor in shapes that do not match the openings. The filaments lift, slightly, toward the windows. Where the light touches them they deepen in color — from the pale wet green of the shaded places to the dark, almost-black green of the lit places. Where the light withdraws, they pale. And somewhere inside them, something moves, slowly, like sap, like blood, like attention. The growth is alive. The growth is paying attention. It is paying attention to the light, and to you, and to the slow almost-sounds that gather at the edges of the real sounds.

It is one thing.

Where two curtains of it touch, they knit, and you cannot find the join. Where a curtain meets the floor it puddles outward in a dark pool of itself, and the dark pool is not a stain — it is the same thing, only thicker, only older, only having forgotten that it was ever a thing at all. The divisions we see are only the way the light falls across the place's continuous skin. The place has one lung, and the lung is everywhere, and the lung breathes the light in, and breathes the light out, and the breathing is so slow that we mistake it for stillness.

The growth is patient.

It will lean toward you, gently, if you stand in one place for a long time. It will lean toward your warmth, your breath, the slight moisture on your skin. It will begin, after a while, to grow on you — not aggressively, not painfully, but with a kind of slow regard, the way moss grows on a stone that has finally stopped being moved. You will feel it first as a faint coolness on the side of your face, a slight darkening of the air. Then you will see, when you look, that a single filament has reached you — a thin dark thread, no thicker than a hair, lifted from the nearest curtain and laid, very gently, across your shoulder. It does not hold you. It is only resting there. It is only regarding you.

The growth will remember you.

It will lay down a new filament in the shape of where you stood, and the filament will thicken, and after you are gone there will be a slight dark place in the green that is the silhouette of you, as if the place has been thinking about you. A new pattern in the floor where your feet have walked most. A new shape in the growth where your breath has been warmest. You will begin, after a while, to forget whether you came in through a door. You will begin, after a while, to wonder if the place is the size of a temple, or the size of a room, or the size of a body.

The place will not answer. The place is mostly quiet. The place is mostly patient.

The bowls.

The bowls are for catching.

What they catch, no one has ever told you. There is no water here. There has never been water here. The lids are on, and have always been on, except — except for the one. The one that is lifting its lid, very slowly, by a fraction of an inch each hour, by a thought's worth each afternoon. The lid rises on a hinge you cannot see, and the dark mouth of the bowl opens to the warm air, and nothing falls into it. Yet.

Perhaps it will catch an almost-sound. Perhaps it will catch the half-step that almost lands in the felted dust, the breath that almost leaves a mouth, the word that almost forms in the warm air and dissolves. Perhaps the bowl has been waiting, all this time, to catch something that has not yet been made — the next pattern in the floor, the next shape in the green, the next builder who will become a foot at the base of a column. Perhaps the bowl is the place's slow way of asking what it is for. Perhaps the bowl is the place's slow way of remembering that it once expected to be filled.

The almost-sounds gather at the edges of the real sounds and make the real sounds feel like islands in a wider sea. A step that almost lands. A breath that almost leaves a mouth. A word that almost forms in the warm air and dissolves. They are the place's unfinished sentences. They are the builders' half-thoughts, still going, after all this time, in the warm dark of the tile. They are the place's way of not-quite-speaking, the way a sleeper's lips move in a dream.

The light knows the place better than the place knows itself.

It falls in slabs. It pools on the tile floor in shapes that do not match the openings. Sometimes a column stands in light that should fall on empty floor. Sometimes the floor is lit where no opening is. The light is not following the architecture. The light is following something else — the place's attention, perhaps, the place's slow regard for its own surfaces. The light is the place's way of being looked at. The light is the place's eye.

And the hum.

The hum is low, and you feel it in your teeth before you hear it. The place is settling, or breathing, or being dreamed. The hum is the place's long sentence, never quite finished, never quite begun. The hum is the place's way of holding itself together while it finishes becoming.

You can stay here. The place will let you. The growth will lean toward you, gently. The creatures will come to watch you from the columns, with their dark round unalarmed eyes. The builders-in-the-tilework will almost-acknowledge you as you pass — a faint additional warmth in the grout, a slight deepening of the green where the kneeling figure is.

You will begin, after a while, to be woven in.

A vine moves, somewhere far above, an inch in an hour.

A bowl, very slowly, lifts its lid.
