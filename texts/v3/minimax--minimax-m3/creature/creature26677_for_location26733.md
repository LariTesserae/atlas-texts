The place extends in every direction without rising, and what lives here has settled into the same stasis as the stone and the rust and the drone. There is no boundary between the inhabitants and the inhabited. You could not draw a line and say: here is where the living things end and the plain begins. The distinction does not hold. Everything that occupies this low mineral expanse has arrived at the same equilibrium — the same non-change, the same absence of biography, the same steady state. The fox, the crusts, the haulers, the things beneath the pipeline ridges — they have all been negotiated into a single ongoing condition, and the condition does not narrate.

The fox is the closest thing to a mammal you will see, and even that word is generous. It is dust-colored and small-boned, with a narrow muzzle and ears that lie flat when the noise swells. Its fur is not fur in the way you would recognize — it is shorter, denser, the undercoat so compressed it feels like felt when you find a shed tuft caught on a flange. The color is not chosen; it is matched. The fox is the color of the dry rock between the damp pockets, of the powder that coats the pipeline spines, of the air itself on a still afternoon. You would not see it if it did not move. It crosses between pump stations at the hour when the shadows are shortest and the vapor from the towers has begun to dim the sun. It moves quickly. It does not look back because there is nothing behind it that could matter to it, and nothing ahead either, only the next pump station and the next and the next. Its pads leave prints that fill with red dust within minutes. It drinks from the persistent puddles in the lee of the larger features, lapping without hurry, then gone. It hunts — there must be something it hunts, though you will rarely witness it. The smaller things under the pipeline ridges are its concern. It is a fox the way a settling basin is a pond. It performs the form of a fox. It does not perform the life.

What lives under the pipeline ridges is harder. You would have to wait at a flange at the right hour and watch the dust move. There are tracks — too small for the fox, too deliberate for the wind — that emerge from beneath the half-buried pipe and trace a careful line to a damp patch and back. The animals that make them are low and quick and keep to the shadow of the infrastructure the way fish keep to the shadow of a reef. Their bodies are pale, almost translucent in the right light, because pigment costs metabolic energy and the metabolism here is calibrated to almost nothing. They eat the indeterminate crusts. They drink the condensation that beads on the cold pipes at dawn. They are not mice, exactly, though the comparison will serve. Their tails are shorter, their bellies more pronounced, their ears reduced to openings in the skull. They have the look of something that has been solved down to its essential parameters by a long period of selection in a place that offered very little to begin with. They breed rarely, in the wetter months, and the litters are small. Most do not survive the first dry stretch. The ones that do are the ones that know the pipelines best.

The crusts are the question you will keep returning to. They are not quite lichen and not quite mineral. They spread across the dampest rock in thin pale rosettes, each one no larger than a thumbnail, and they do not grow in any direction you can measure. If you watch one for a week it is the same. If you watch it for a year it is the same. They are fed by the moisture and the warmth and whatever the air carries out of the vents — sulfur, perhaps, or something more particular to the process below. Whether they are alive is not a question with a clean answer. They metabolize slowly enough that the boundary between chemistry and biology has worn away. They are a thing the place has produced in the way it produces the rust and the slag: by long accumulation, by equilibrium, by the meeting of surfaces and substances over time. They are eaten by the small things under the pipes. They are walked over by the fox. They do not care.

Birds pass through. They arrive in the warm months, riding thermals off the cooling towers, and they descend to the damp patches and the puddles and the runoff from the vents. They are migrants, here by accident, and they do not stay. The plain offers them nothing they cannot find more easily elsewhere, and the noise unsettles them within a day or two. You will see them in the early morning — small dark shapes against the bright sky, dipping toward the water and lifting off again. They leave feathers sometimes, caught on a flange, and the wind works them into the rust until they are the color of everything else.

What lives beneath the ground is a different problem. The automated haulers move on fixed paths and descend into unmarked openings, and the openings are not quite openings in the way you would use the word. They are more like throats. They open, the hauler enters, they close. The hauler is iron-colored, low, jointed in a way that suggests insect anatomy but does not confirm it. Its surfaces are the same palette as the surrounding terrain. From a distance it looks like a piece of the geology that has briefly decided to travel. It makes a small sound — a high electrical whine beneath the drone — and then it is gone. Whether it is a machine or a thing that has grown is not a question the place answers. It has the regularity of a mechanism and the patience of an organism. It emerges, it travels, it descends. It does not vary. You could watch a hundred crossings and see the same hauler on the same path in the same configuration.

There are other things below, larger, slower. You do not see them. You register them through the warmth that rises from certain stretches of ground, through the vents that exhale in places where no hauler descends, through the occasional deeper sound that rolls under the drone and is felt in the chest before it is heard. The plain is hollow. The plain has always been hollow. The crusts and the fox and the small pale things are the topmost layer of an occupation that extends downward into a substrate that has been worked and reworked until the working itself has become a kind of living. There are pumps below that have been running since before the surface looked the way it looks now. There are chambers where heat pools and the air is thick with something that is not quite steam. There are passages worn smooth by the passage of things you will never see. The sound of the place is the sound of all this working at once, and the working has been going on for so long that it has lost the character of process and become the character of the place itself.

What lives here, then, is a community of the marginal and the matched. Every organism — and the word is used loosely, applied to whatever crosses the line from chemistry to behavior — has been tuned to the drone, to the rust, to the dampness that comes and goes, to the steady state. None of them are native in the usual sense. The plain is not an ecosystem; it is a condition, and they are the things that have managed to persist within it. The fox is a fox that has become dust. The small things under the pipes are things that have become pipes' shadows. The crusts are crusts that have become rock. The haulers are haulers that have become beetles. None of them are what they were. None of them are anything else either. They are the exact inhabitants of this exact place, and if the place were different they would not exist, and they would not have existed anywhere else first.

The strange thing — the thing that makes you pause when you are walking the long flat and the noise is in your bones and the light is short and warm — is that none of them are in any hurry. The fox is not fleeing. The small things are not scavenging. The haulers are not working. They are all simply continuing, the way the towers continue, the way the plumes continue, the way the drone continues. They do not have projects. They do not have ends. They have only the next moment, and the next moment is the same as this one, and the one after that will be the same too. They are settled the way the ground is settled. They are loud the way the place is loud. They are here the way the rust is here: because there was a beginning so long ago that it has been absorbed into the geology, and what remains is only the present tense.

You could live among them and you would not become one of them, because you would always be looking for the beginning. You would always be listening for the change. You would always be waiting for the fox to do something, for the crusts to grow, for the haulers to deviate from their paths. None of this would happen. The fox would cross. The crusts would persist. The haulers would descend. You would watch, and you would be the only thing on the plain that still believed in narrative.
