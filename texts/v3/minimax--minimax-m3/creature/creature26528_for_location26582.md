There is a person who comes here. Not often. Not on any schedule the hollow would recognize as a schedule — the hollow has no use for clocks, and neither, exactly, does this person. They come when there is nothing pressing anywhere else, which is most of the time, because they have made their life such that most of the time there is nothing pressing. They are not old, exactly, but they are no longer young in the way that the word young is usually meant. They are the age of a person who has stopped expecting to be surprised by themselves. Their hands are rough and clean. Their fingernails are short and pale with dust. Their hair is cut close to the skull and is the color of the sediment on the floor, which is to say it is the color of not-being-noticed, which is what they prefer.

They arrive from the south, where the rim is lowest and the easiest way down is a slanted slab that the weather has made into a kind of stair. They come without announcing themselves. The first sign of them, usually, is the faint scrape of a boot-sole on stone, and then a small displacement of the warm air near the floor when their body pushes into the hollow. The plants do not react. The plants have never reacted to anything. The hollow receives them the way it receives the slight warming at noon, or the rare seed-pop on a ledge: as a small event, noted, not remarked upon.

They do not bring much. Sometimes a length of cord, dark and soft, looped at the belt. Sometimes a small knife with a wooden handle, the wood gone the color of the dull-olive plants from years of being held. Sometimes nothing. They are not here to do anything in particular. They are here to be in a place that has finished becoming what it is, and to be in it without trying to add to it. This is, for them, a kind of rest. The hollow is good at this. They know it, and the hollow, if it knew anything, would know them.

When they sit, they sit in the scooped place against the polished wall. The stone there is smooth and slightly concave, shaped to the back of a skull, shaped to the shoulder-blades of a person leaning. They lean into it and the stone receives them without comment, the way a chair receives a person who has sat in it a thousand times. Their knees come up. Their hands rest on their knees, palms down, fingers slightly spread, the way a person holds their hands when they are not about to use them. Their eyes are open. They look at nothing in particular. They look at the pale patch above, or at a plant across the floor, or at the faint scrapes near their feet. They look at these things the way the hollow is itself: settled, continuing, not requiring anything.

They breathe slowly. Their breath does not fog in the air because the air is not cold. It does not rise visibly because the air is not that much cooler than they are. It simply joins the warm layer near the floor and is indistinguishable from it within a breath or two. They are nearly the same temperature as the hollow. They have been here enough times — or have been the kind of person who is the same temperature as places — that this is no longer a thing to remark on.

They do not speak. There is no one to speak to, and they are not a person who speaks to themselves. The hollow is silent and so are they, and the two silences do not contend with each other. Sometimes, in the late part of an afternoon, they will hum, very low, almost below the threshold of hearing, the kind of sound that is more a vibration in the chest than a sound in the air. The hollow does not mind. It cannot mind. It continues.

They eat, when they eat, something small and dry that they bring in a pouch. A few pieces of something the color of the plants, the color of pressed olive, the color of a thing that was once alive and is now provision. They eat it slowly. They do not leave crumbs, or if they do, the crumbs are the same color as the floor and the hollow absorbs them the way it absorbs the rare seeds that blow in from the rim and never sprout. They drink from a flask that is warm in their hand. The water inside tastes, by the time it reaches their mouth, faintly of the flask, which is faintly of leather, which is faintly of nothing. They do not complain about this. They have made their arrangements.

They stay for an hour, or two, or the length of a long afternoon. Sometimes they stay through the slow darkening, when the pale patch above turns from weak milk to grey to something that is not dark so much as the memory of the dim they have been in all day, layered over itself. In this deeper dim they are a shape against the wall, a shape that is mostly the wall. Their eyes are still open. Their breathing has slowed further. They might be asleep, or they might be doing whatever it is they do instead of sleep, which looks similar from the outside.

When they leave, they leave the way they came. They stand, and the stone gives back the warmth their body put into it, slowly, over the next hour. They brush nothing off themselves because there is nothing on them to brush. They do not look back. The hollow does not require them to look back. They step up the slanted slab and the sound of their boot-sole on stone grows fainter and then is gone, and the air settles, and the dim returns to being only the dim, and the silence returns to being only the silence, and the hollow is itself again, except for the faint additional warmth in the scooped place against the wall, which will dissipate by morning.

The plants do not miss them. The hollow does not miss them. They come back. They have come back often enough that the scooped place against the wall is smoother than it would otherwise be, polished by the small repeated pressure of a person leaning into it, year after year. The faint scrapes on the floor near it — those are older. Those are from someone else, or from them before they were themselves, or from water that is no longer here. The new wear is theirs, and it is theirs alone, and it is small, and the hollow holds it without comment.

They have never spoken to another person in this hollow. They have never brought another person here. They have never marked the wall, or left anything behind, or taken anything from the floor except a few small dry things in a pouch that they brought with them empty. They are a visitor, and they are a regular, and they are, in some quiet way that neither they nor the hollow would put into words, a tenant of the dim, the way the plants are tenants of the seams at the wall's base. They have made their arrangements with dryness. They are not waiting for anything to change. They come when there is nothing pressing. They leave when there is, or when the hollow has had enough of them, which is never quite, and which they do not test.

This is who lives here.
