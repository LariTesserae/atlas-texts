# Who Lives in Coalreach Hollow

The town is older than the nations that have owned it, and the people who remain are, in their way, older than the town. Not in years — though many of them are old in years too — but in *kind*. They are a people made for thin air and iron-tasting mist, and there are not many of them left.

---

## The Miners

There is only the one crew now, and they number seven.

They are men between forty-five and seventy-one, and they share, between them, a vocabulary of about three hundred words, most of which concern the seam, the weather, and what their wives have asked them to bring home. They are called, collectively, *the seam* by the rest of the town — *the seam is down*, *the seam is up*, *the seam is taking a late dinner* — the way a river is called *the river*, as if the men were a single body that descended each morning into the earth and rose again each evening.

Their names are:

- **Brannick**, who is seventy-one and who has been underground since he was fourteen and who can no longer, technically, see. His eyes gave out years ago. He goes anyway. He says he can feel the way.
- **Ovid**, who is the foreman and the only one of them under sixty. He keeps the books. He has a ledger at home, leather-bound, in which he records, by hand, the daily tonnage. The ledger is the third such ledger his family has kept.
- **Petros**, who is missing the smallest finger of his left hand and who claims, without irony, that he lost it to a dragon when he was a boy. There are no dragons. He will not be moved on this.
- **The Dúnns** — there are two of them, brothers, **Cal** and **Davy**, who do not speak to one another and have not spoken to one another since 1994, the cause of which no living person can remember, though several have theories.
- **Younger Petros** (no relation, everyone agrees), who is forty-eight and is called *younger* only because he is younger than Petros. His actual name is something else. No one remembers what.
- **An old man** whose name no one uses. Everyone calls him *the quiet one*, and he is the quietest of them. He is said, by the children, to be a hundred and twelve. He is probably sixty-three.

They go down together in the morning in a small cage that has been patched so often it is mostly patch. They wear the same helmets their fathers wore, refitted. They use, for light, lamps that are electric but which were originally designed for carbide, and the fittings have been adapted with electrician's tape and with prayer.

They do not, on the whole, talk about the seam when they are above ground. When they come up in the evening they go to the pub — there is the one pub, *The Standing Stone*, which is mostly standing — and they drink a dark local ale called *brown* and they speak about the weather, the footie, the price of wool. They are, on the surface, indistinguishable from any other working men anywhere.

Below, they are something else. They are a crew. They are a body. They move through the old galleries with the ease of men walking through their own house in the dark, and they know, every one of them, the precise sound of the seam in any given season, the way a sailor knows the sea, and they can tell, from the creak of a timber three galleries away, whether the timber is going to hold or whether it is going to *ask* to be replaced.

## The Wool Men

There is a small building at the lower end of the seam, beneath the second chimney, and inside it there are four vats of stone and brick, and beside the vats there are two men whose whole working life is the transformation of dirty fleece into clean fleece.

**Thevas** is the senior scourer. He is fifty-eight, Slovenian originally, came up to Coalreach thirty-one years ago to visit a cousin and never went down. He speaks four languages badly and the local dialect fluently. He has the largest hands of any man in town and uses them to plunge armfuls of raw fleece into the first vat, where the lanolin floats off the wool in a pale yellow skin that he skims with a wooden ladle his father made.

**Maren** is the junior scourer, though she is forty-four and has done the work for nineteen years and has, in that time, learned to do everything Thevas does except talk while he does it. She is the only woman who works in the upper works, and she is the only woman, in Coalreach, who wears trousers in public, and the two facts are not unrelated. She is from the lowlands. She does not explain herself.

The fleece comes down from the high pastures in autumn, packed on the backs of small ponies whose hooves have been fitted, generations ago, with a kind of soft shoe that does not damage the moss. The ponies are brought up by the shepherds, who come down only in fleece-season and who live, otherwise, in the cloud above.

---

## The Shepherds

The shepherds live above the cloud.

This is, technically, a manner of speaking — they live in the high meadows on the upper shoulders of the saddle, in stone bothies that are older than the terraces, and the cloud, which sits in the saddle, comes up around them only in winter. In summer they are above it. They look down at Coalreach the way a man on a roof looks down at his own kitchen.

There are six working shepherds at any one time, though the number fluctuates the way the number of miners does, and there are perhaps a dozen older ones who come down in bad years to help, and a few apprentice boys who go up for a season to learn and then, mostly, do not come back.

The senior shepherd is **Iagan**, who is eighty and who has been on the high pastures since he was seven. He has not been inside the town itself, he says, in eleven years. He comes down only for the fleece market, and even then only to the upper works, and he does not stay overnight. He does not sleep below the cloud. He says it is too quiet for him.

The shepherds are almost a different ethnicity from the townspeople, though they share a language and a grandparent or two. They are darker, smaller, more weatherproof. They speak a dialect that the Coalreach people can understand but find strange, the way you find strange the speech of a cousin you have not seen since childhood. They are Catholics, all of them, or were, before the denomination withdrew; now they are something else, something not quite named, that meets in the upper bothy on feast days and sings psalms that have been passed down, mother to child, since before the Reformation came up the valley and died, of exhaustion, somewhere around the third bothy.

They keep dogs. The dogs are enormous and white and are of a breed that has no name in any lowland kennel club. They are working dogs and they do not, as a rule, come down to the town. When they do, by accident or in the company of a stranger, the town dogs bark at them and they ignore the town dogs and this is, in miniature, the whole relationship between the shepherds and the town.

---

## Havel

Havel prints the *Almanac*.

He is sixty-seven. He has a beard the color of old iron and he has, since his wife died in 2009, lived alone in a small house at the edge of the terrace that backs onto the foundry wall, and the heat from the foundry pipes runs along the inside of his bedroom wall and keeps him warm in winter, which is why he took the house in the first place and why he has stayed.

His real name is **Havel Marek**, and he came up to Coalreach in 1979 from a flatbed press in Prague and he has not left. He prints the *Almanac* alone, on a hand-fed press that was installed in 1887 and that he maintains with the devoted attention of a priest tending an altar. He sets the type by hand. He pulls the bed himself. He folds the sheets himself. The *Almanac* is sixteen pages, twice yearly, and it contains:

- The dates of the high feasts.
- The market days.
- A weather almanac derived, by long tradition, from the behavior of a particular raven who nests on the foundry chimney and whose comings and goings Havel has, for forty years, recorded in a small leather notebook.
- The obituaries.
- A long serial poem, in progress since 1903, called *The Lay of the Hollow*, which no one reads but which is, by custom, included.
- Births.
- Marriages.
- The price of wool.
- The price of bronze.
- The price of paper.
- Small advertisements for things that cannot be bought anywhere else.

Havel is, by general agreement, the most educated man in Coalreach, and also the most private. He reads, in three languages, voraciously. He will lend a book but will not, as a rule, discuss it. He is the only man in town who knows what year it is, in the larger sense — the rest of the town knows the year, of course, but does not, in Havel's view, *inhabit* it.

He is the closest thing the town has to a historian, and the closest thing the town has to a public intellectual, and the closest thing the town has to a man who might, in a lowland city, be called a writer. He has, in a drawer in the press-room, a manuscript of his own — a memoir, perhaps, or a novel, it is not clear — that he has been working on for thirty years and that he has not shown to anyone.

## The Curator of the Chapel-Museum

The chapel-museum is run by a woman named **Yeva**, who is fifty-two and who came up to Coalreach in 2014 from a curatorial post in a lowland city and who is the only person in town who holds a postgraduate degree, which is in the history of religion, which is to say the history of the denomination that built the chapel and then withdrew from it and left, in the pews and the vestry and the crypt, a small mountain of paperwork and vestments and communion plate that Yeva has spent the last decade trying to interpret.

The chapel-museum is open three days a week, in summer, and one day a week, in winter. Attendance is thin. The visitors are mostly lowlanders who have come up the pass on the new tourist railway that runs, twice daily, from the lowland city to the station at the lower end of the seam. They come for the day. They look at the vestments. They ask Yeva questions about the denomination, and Yeva answers them at length, and they thank her and go down again.

Yeva lives, alone, in the chapel house, which is the only detached dwelling in town. She grows tomatoes in a small greenhouse that she has built against the south wall, and the tomatoes are, in late summer, the only red things in Coalreach. She has tried, several times, to interest the town in the chapel-museum as a cultural institution. She has proposed exhibitions, lectures, residencies. The town has been polite about it.

She is not unhappy. She has come to understand, over the decade, that Coalreach does not want to be interpreted. It wants to be left alone, in the damp, with the moss, with the tick in the seam. She has made her peace with this. She tends the fern. She writes, in a small office behind the vestry, a long monograph on the denomination that no one will ever publish.

## The Young

There are children.

Not many. Perhaps a dozen, all told, of school age, and another half-dozen younger. They go to a school that is held in the old company reading-room, which has been, for ninety years, the school, and which is heated by a stove that the children are responsible for stoking, under the supervision of **the teacher**, who is a woman named **Senna** and who is thirty-six and who is the youngest adult in town.

Senna came up four years ago, from the lowlands, on what she described at the time as *a posting* and what the town, privately, described as *a penance* — there had been some kind of trouble, in a lowland school, that no one will speak of and that Senna will not, either. She teaches the children reading, writing, the four rules, the history of the nation (lowland, official, mostly unknown to her), the geography of the highlands (which she is learning as she teaches it), and the names of the plants and birds and small mechanical things of the seam, which the children already know better than she does.

The children are strange, in the way that highland children are strange: they are quiet, observant, weatherproof, given to long silences and to games whose rules are not fully explicable to outsiders. They play, in the wet yards between the terraces, a game called *the fox*, which involves one child being the fox and the rest being the hens and which can last, by some accounts, for an entire afternoon without anyone moving very much.

They call the cloud *the blanket*. They call the town *the inside*. They call the lowlands *down below*, with the slight distaste of children describing a country they have only heard of.

Most of them will leave. This is known. The young leave. They go down to the lowland cities for school, at sixteen, and they do not come back, except in summer, to visit, and they marry lowlanders, and their children do not know the name of the seam and have never heard the tick. This is the way of it. Senna knows this. She teaches them anyway.

A few will stay. There is always a few. **Younger Petros** stayed. **Maren** stayed. There is a boy in Senna's class now, eleven years old, name of **Cael**, who is the son of Ovid the foreman, and who has announced, with the calm finality of the highland-born, that he intends to take the seam when Ovid retires, and that no one is to speak to him about any other future, and that is that.

---

## The Donkey

There is a donkey in the upper works, kept by the foundry for the carts. Her name is **Mab**. She is twenty-six, which is old for a donkey. She is grey and patient and she knows every cobble of the upper works by feel. She pulls the ingot cart, mornings, from the foundry door to the loading platform at the end of the seam, and she stands, afternoons, in a small stone pen behind the foundry, and she is visited, in a rotation no one has ever formalized, by the children of the town, who bring her crusts and pull her ears and tell her things.

The children tell Mab things they do not tell their parents or their teachers or each other. This is known. Mab is, in the unspoken economy of Coalreach, a confessor. She does not break the seal.

---

## The Ravens

The ravens are not, technically, *people*. But they are present, and they are loud, and they are known, individually, to the town.

The raven on the foundry chimney is the weather raven, and is called, by Havel, **the Baron**. The raven on the chapel roof is called **the Dean**, by Yeva, and is held responsible, by her, for the slow theft of small pieces of the communion plate. There is a pair of ravens, mated for life, who nest on the upper terrace and who are called, by the children, **the King and the Queen**, though which is which is debated annually.

The ravens have been in Coalreach longer than any human. They will be in Coalreach longer than any human. They are, in some quiet way that no one articulates, the town's true permanent residents. Everyone else is just passing through, on a geological scale. The ravens know this. They do not mind.

---

## The One Who Left and Came Back

There is also a man named **Roan**, who is thirty-nine, who grew up in Coalreach and who left at sixteen and who came back, three years ago, with a lowland wife and two small children and a quantity of explaining to do.

Roan is the prodigal son, though no one in Coalreach would use that phrase, because it is the phrase of the denomination that withdrew. He is also the only man in town under forty, except for Ovid, who is just over, and except for the apprentice shepherds, who do not properly count.

He works, now, at the foundry. He casts fittings. He is learning the press from Havel, in the evenings, with a view to — eventually — taking over the *Almanac*, though neither he nor Havel has said this out loud.

His wife is called **Liss**. She is from the lowland city. She is thirty-four. She did not, when she married Roan, expect to live in a cloud. She has been, for three years, the subject of a quiet, sustained effort by the town to make her welcome, an effort that consists mostly of being brought small things — a jar of honey, a cut of wool, a chicken — and of being spoken to, very gently, about the weather.

Liss is, by all reports, beginning to come around.

Their children are four and two. The elder, a girl called **Fen**, has already learned to call the cloud *the blanket*. The younger, a boy whose name is not yet fixed, was born in Coalreach and has never seen the sun, except in the one afternoon last winter when the wind came down off the pass and the cloud tore for an hour and Liss carried him out into the weak-tea light and held him up, and he squinted, and did not cry, and went back inside.

He will be a Coalreach man. Liss has accepted this. The town is, quietly, pleased.

---

## The Rest

There are others. There is a man who mends shoes in a small shop at the lower end of the terrace and who has done so for fifty-three years and who is the grandson of the man who did it before him and whose name is **Pavel**, a different Pavel, and there is a woman who keeps the post office, which is also the only shop, and who is called **Onna**, and who knows, by some instinct, what every person in town is short of and will have it on the shelf before they ask, and there is a young man — young, by Coalreach standards, meaning under fifty — who has come up in the last two years to help with the paper mill and who is called **Stefan** and who is, Onna suspects, falling in love with Yeva but will not say so, and there is an old woman called **the Granny**, though she is the grandmother of no one currently living, who sits in a window of the upper terrace and who watches the seam and who is said, by the children, to be able to tell the future from the way the smoke comes out of the foundry chimney, and there are a number of other people, mostly old, mostly quiet, mostly unnamed here, who make up the rest of the population of Coalreach, which is, in total, somewhere between eighty and ninety souls, though the *Almanac* lists, each year, the names of the households, and Havel counts them, and the count is, each year, slightly smaller than the year before.

This is the shape of the town. A small diminishing thing. A thing that is being slowly, gently, overgrown. A place where the moss is winning, and the people know it, and the people do not, on the whole, mind.

The seam ticks. The ravens call. The cloud rests. The town goes on being itself, in the dim, in the damp, in the long quiet of a high place that is, by all the measures of the lowlands, finished, but which has not, yet, got round to stopping.
