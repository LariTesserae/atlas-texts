# Inhabitants of the Dry Cathedral

What lives here is small, and what lives here is mostly rumor. If you entered and stood perfectly still — stood until the pulse in your ears gave up trying to be a sound and became only a feeling — you might, eventually, see one. Then another. Then you would understand why you had not seen them sooner. They are made of the same dimness that the rest of the place is made of. They are the color of the calcium, the color of the growths, the color of a wall two rooms over. They were not hiding from you. They were simply not a thing the eye could pick out against the architecture they were living in. You were the loud one. You were the bright one. You were the warm one, leaking heat into a place that had no interest in it.

## The Wanderer

The one you are most likely to see, if you are patient, is not the most common. It is only the most visible, because it is the largest of the small ones, and because it has a habit of crossing open ground. The locals — the creatures that actually live in the cracks — call it nothing. They have no calls. They have no concept of names. The wanderer is simply the thing that crosses.

It is the size of two hands held flat and stacked, though it is not flat. Its body is layered. There is a lower portion, close to the ground, jointed and segmented, bristling with the pale fibrous growth that the towers also wear — not because it ate them, but because the fibrous growth has colonized the wanderer's older plates the way lichen colonizes a stone. The wanderer carries its own small garden of the place. It is being slowly encrusted by the architecture it walks on. Some of the wanderers you might glimpse are so thickly bearded with the growths that they look like a moving piece of wall that has decided, on a whim, to go somewhere else.

Above the lower portion, the body rises in a stack of overlapping plates — chitin, or calcium, or whatever this place uses when it wants to make something harder than itself but not as hard as itself. The plates are not hinged in the way an insect's plates are hinged. They slide. They telescope. The wanderer can make itself shorter, taller, narrower, wider, in a slow mechanical adjustment that suggests it is not entirely committed to having any particular shape. Some individuals are tall and narrow and walk on four legs. Others are low and broad and walk on eight. The same individual can be both of these, over the course of a season, as its plates resettle around the slow growth of its own internal scaffolding.

The head, if it is a head, is mostly palps. Two of them, long as the wanderer's body is wide, jointed at three or four points, and ending in fine fanned brushes that are never still. The brushes sweep the air as the wanderer moves, sweeping low when it walks the floor, sweeping high when it climbs a wall, sweeping side to side in a constant blind tasting. The wanderer does not see you. The wanderer does not see anything. Where the eyes would be — and you have to look, the plates there are smooth and featureless and the same bone-white as the rest — there is only a faint deepening, a shallow cup, lined with something dark and wet. Whether that cup can form an image is a question the place has not answered. The cups do not track. The cups do not dilate. They may be vestigial. They may be sensing something the wanderer cannot describe to anything, because the wanderer cannot describe anything to anything. The cups may simply be a memory of eyes, kept because evolution here is slow and has not been embarrassed into giving them up.

The wanderer eats the growths. This is the most you can say with any certainty. Its mouthparts, beneath the palps, are small and precise, and you can sometimes tell where one has fed because the stalk it nipped at has grown back at an angle — the way a tree branch grows back crooked after a deer chews it. The wanderer is a grazer. It moves through the cathedral in long slow arcs, clipping as it goes, leaving the architecture with a kind of accidental topiary that the architecture does not seem to mind. The wanderer is the closest thing the place has to a gardener, and it does not know it is a gardener, and the garden does not know it is being gardened.

The wanderer lives for a long time. You cannot tell how long. Its plates thicken with age. Its growths thicken with age. Some of the oldest wanderers are nearly stationary, anchored to a particular wall by the cumulative weight of what has grown on them, and they feed now only on the stalks that push out within reach of their slow mouths. They are, in a sense, becoming architecture. The cathedral is reclaiming them, gently, in its own time.

## The Chasers

The prey is smaller than the wanderer and faster, and it is almost never seen, and it is the thing the wanderer is mostly made of, in some sense — the wanderer is what grows around the chasers' lives.

There are many kinds. You will not see more than two. They live in the cracks, in the seams, in the cool dark hollows inside the towers where the air rises and where the phosphorescence has not quite reached. They are the size of a finger, the size of a thumb. They are pale as paper. They have many legs — six, eight, twelve, the number varies, and within a single individual the number can vary, because their legs are not legs in the strict sense, they are ventral outpushings of the body that they use as legs, and which they can retract or extend as needed, and which sometimes an individual will lose one of and not bother to replace.

They eat the same thing the wanderer eats, but they eat it from the inside. They bore into the growths. They bore into the seams. They bore, occasionally, into the wandering calcium itself, and the calcium accretes around them as they bore, so that a chaser that has lived in one place long enough becomes a thread of darker mineral running through a wall — a kind of vein, a kind of memory the architecture has absorbed. When the chaser dies, the wall is left with a small hollow tube where its body was, and these tubes are everywhere, if you look closely, and they are how you know the chasers are there at all.

The chasers have no eyes that you could call eyes. Their palps are short and constantly in motion, sweeping the wall in front of them. They navigate by some gradient — chemical, thermal, vibrational — that lets them find the cracks and the seams and the places where the cathedral is thinnest. They move in fast deliberate bursts. They pause. They consider. They move again. They look, when you catch one out in the open — and you rarely will — like a small pale thought crossing the floor.

They are eaten by the predators.

## The Waiters

The predators are larger than the chasers and smaller than the wanderers, and they are slower than both, and they are almost never seen, and they are the reason the chasers are almost never seen.

A predator waits. This is its nature. It wedges itself into a crack — head-out, body-deep, plates flush with the wall — and it waits for hours, for what would feel to you like hours, for the slow convective currents to bring it the chemical signature of a chaser that has passed nearby. When the signature comes, the predator does not lunge. It does not pounce. It extends. It unfolds out of the crack in a single fluid lengthening, palps reaching, palps finding, palps closing around whatever was passing, and then it retracts, and the wall is just a wall again, and the chaser is gone, and the silence is the same silence it was a moment ago.

The predators are not many. The cathedral cannot support many of them. Each predator needs a hunting crack and a stretch of wall where chasers pass, and the stretches of wall are long, and the cracks are particular, and the predators that do well are the ones that have been in the same crack for a long time, and the predators that do poorly are the ones that have had to move. A moved predator is, for a time, visible. It crosses the open floor the way the wanderer does, but with less deliberation, and with a slightly different gait, and it does not graze. It is looking, in its eyeless way, for somewhere to wait.

The predators have plates that are smoother than the wanderer's plates, and they have fewer growths on them, and they are darker — not dark, but the color of the shadows that the towers cast, the color of the deeper calcium, the color of a place the phosphorescence has only just reached. You would not see one against a wall. You might see one against a floor, if you were lucky and quiet and had been standing still for a very long time. Then it would be gone.

## The Other Inhabitants

There are others. There are the things that live inside the tubes of the towers, in the rising air, that you will never see at all because they live entirely in the breath. They are very small. They are filaments. They are made of the same material as the fibrous growths, but they have detached, and they drift, and they feed on the dust of calcium that the air carries upward, and they are very few, and they are the only things in the cathedral that are not, in some way, fixed to a surface, and this makes them the only things that are, in some way, free.

There are the things that live in the calcium itself, the things that bore the hollows the air rises through, that no one has ever seen because they are smaller than a grain of the place's sand, if the place had sand, and because they are made of the calcium they are eating, and because they are, possibly, what made the calcium hollow in the first place, and possibly what made the towers in the first place, and possibly what made the cathedral, in the first place. These are rumors. These are old ideas. The cathedral does not confirm them.

There are the dead. The dead are the most numerous inhabitants. The calcium is full of them. Every plate, every seam, every hollow has, somewhere in it, the silent folded shape of a creature that lived here once, and whose mineral went back into the mineral it was made of, and whose mineral is now part of a wall, or a tower, or a plaza floor, or a thread running through the dark. They are not ghosts. They are not ancestors. They are simply what is left when something stops, in a place that does not stop.

## How They Live Together

The system is old. The system is not in balance, because balance implies something that is being held, and nothing here is being held. The system is in a kind of slow negotiation. The chasers bore. The walls thicken around the bore. The chasers bore further. The wanderers graze the growths. The growths grow back. The predators wait. The chasers pass. The predators eat some of them. The chasers are many. The predators are few. The wanderers are few but large and long-lived and slow. The filaments drift. The things-in-the-calcium, if there are things-in-the-calcium, do what they have always done, which is to be the calcium, and to be the towers, and to be the cathedral.

There is no day here. There is no night. There is the slow convective breath, rising and falling, and the slow change of the light as the phosphorescence brightens and dims by fractions over the course of a year that is not a year. There is no mating you could recognize as mating. There is no death you could recognize as death. There is only the small pale business of continuing, conducted at the scale of the cathedral, which is the scale of patience, which is the scale of things that do not know they are alive and would not care if they did.

## What They Are Not

They are not intelligent. They are not wise. They do not know they live in a cathedral. They do not know they live in a city. They do not know that what surrounds them has an arrangement. The arrangement is not for them. The arrangement was not for anything. It happened, and they happened inside it, and the happening is ongoing, and none of the inhabitants are aware of being inhabited.

They are not watching you. If you enter, they will react — the chasers will pause in their bursts, the predators will settle deeper into their cracks, the wanderers will lift their palps and taste the air you are leaking into the room — but they will not watch. They will not be curious. They will simply take you in, the way the room takes in your warmth, and decide, by some gradient or other, that you are not part of the cathedral, and go on with what they were doing, which is what they have always been doing, which is what the cathedral has always been doing, which is the slow patient business of being made of something that is older, and drier, and sharper, and not for you.
