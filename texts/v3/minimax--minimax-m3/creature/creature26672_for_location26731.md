# The Inhabitants of the Engine Cathedral

There is no one on the catwalk. There is no one tending the gauges, no one shoveling into the firebox, no one reading the brass dials that glow in their amber pilot-light patience. You have already noticed this. You have already noticed the deeper version of it — that there are no rats skating the lower platforms, no spiders in the corners of the indicator panels, no slugs leaving their silver calligraphy across the fleshy leaves of the wall-creepers. What you have not yet noticed, perhaps, is that this is not an emptiness. It is a community. The absence of everything else is the signature of what is here. The cathedral is closed to the lung-breathers, and what remains inside it has made itself at home in their absence the way a pond, after the fish are gone, fills with the things that do not need them.

Start with the moss. There is more moss here than you can take in. It is on the railing under your hand, the catwalk grating under your boots, every flange and valve-wheel and the soft slopes of every pipe. It is a black-cored velvet, so old at the heart of each cushion that the color has gone the color of peat, and only the outermost filaments are still the green that we would call green. It drinks. That is its whole life. It drinks the saturated air, holds what it takes against the iron, and very slowly, over years, thickens itself into a small black loam that has begun, in the older patches along the north wall, to remember it was once a soil. It does not flower. It does not move. It does not need to. It is paid in water and warmth, and the engine cathedral pays both without ceasing, and the moss has learned to live on the income.

Bolthead ferns have taken the bolts. Every visible head of iron on every joint and bracket wears its small green crown, a perfect rosette of fronds oriented with botanical precision toward the faint warmth that bleeds out of the metal even through the rust. They are the same fern, or descendants of the same fern, that began life on a single bolt somewhere on the central engine's upperworks decades ago and has since spread, spore by spore, drip by drip, to every warm head of iron in the chamber. They are listeners. They know where the heat is. Their fronds lean.

The climbing ferns are the I-beam tenants. They have sheathed the great ceiling beams in a lumpy, breathing hide that, in the skylight dim far above, has the look of something sleeping. Their roots are pale and travel along the iron in shallow ridges. Their fronds depend. They are the canopy of this forest that has no sky.

The aerial roots are the strangest of the plants and the most numerous. From every pipe that runs the length of the chamber — and there are hundreds of pipes — descend curtains of roots so fine they are like hair, two meters, three meters, beaded with water that does not fall but hangs, deciding, in the warm saturated air. Each one is a drinking straw pointed at the floor. Each one is a slow negotiation between the pipe above, which is wet, and the pool below, which is wetter. They do not let the water fall because they were here to catch it. They are the place's capillaries. They are how the cathedral drinks.

At the flanges, where one pipe meets another, there is always a fountainhead of broad leaves. The joint is warmer than the pipe. The joint leaks, faintly, and the leak is hot. The leaves orient toward it with the seriousness of compass needles. These are not ferns. They are something leafier, something with a body to them, a fleshy herb that has learned to read the engine's plumbing as a map of meals. Where the joint is hottest, the leaves are widest. They are, in their quiet way, the engineers of the place. They know where the work is being done.

And then there is the fig.

The fig is the inheritor. If you stand on the gallery at half-height long enough, you will see it: a young fig, or its descendant, that has taken root inside the open firebox of the central engine, where the banked clinker breathes its low orange pulse. Its pale leaves unfurl in the warmth. Its pale roots have begun, somewhere up along the boiler's upper curve, to pry the brick housing open one course at a time. The fig is the only inhabitant that is visibly winning. Everything else here has come to a kind of standstill, a held breath; the fig is the one still growing toward something. It is the only creature in the cathedral with a direction.

Now. The water.

The water is not, properly, an inhabitant. But it has been here long enough, and cycled enough times, that to describe the community without it would be to describe a body without its blood. It rises as steam from the boilers, drifts against the vaulted ceiling in white plumes that are caught by the hanging roots and turned back into liquid, slides down the glazed bricks in dark wandering tracks that look almost deliberate, pools on the lower platforms where the drains have been claimed by roots and can no longer carry their load, is drunk by the moss, transpired by the leaves, condensed on every iron surface, dripped from every aerial root, gathered again, drawn up again by the feedwater pump, returned to the boilers. It has forgotten, by now, where it came from. It is only the cycle. It is the medium in which everything else is suspended. It does not belong to the iron and it does not belong to the plants. It belongs to the arrangement.

The fire is the oldest inhabitant.

Look into the open firebox of the central engine. There is no flame. There has not been a flame for longer than you have been alive. What there is, on the iron grate, is a low pulsing orange glow from a bed of clinker and ash, breathing. It is coal on the edge of going out, fed by the slow trickle of unburned fuel from a hopper above that has rusted open at its base and now, on its own schedule, drips coal-dust into the bed below at the precise rate the bed needs to keep itself alight at its lowest possible combustion. The fire does not burn. The fire *holds*. It is the pilot-light of the entire system. It is the match that was never blown out. It has been here longer than any of the plants. It was here before the moss remembered it was soil. It will be here after the fig has finished breaking the boiler open.

The fire is patient the way deep things are patient. It does not flicker. It does not reach. It breathes, and the steam that rises from the boilers is the breath it has been exhaling, in and out, in and out, for as long as the chamber has been sealed.

The machines.

You have been told they are still running. Now you must understand what that means. The pistons lift and fall once every four or five seconds, slow enough that you can watch a single stroke begin, peak, descend, and end without losing your place. The flywheels turn. The valves open and close. The pressure on the great brass dial by the stair holds at a number that has not varied by more than a needle's width. There is no operator. There has been no operator for longer than the fire has held its breath. And yet the system is stable. The system has settled into itself the way a river settles into its bed.

The machines have become, in the long years, something other than machines. Not alive — not in the way the lung-breathers are alive — but behaving, now, with the slow automatic patience of an organism. The pistons are the heart. The firebox is the breath. The feedwater pump is the swallow. The pipework is the vasculature. The plants are the skin. None of it was designed this way. All of it has drifted, one small accommodation at a time, into the shape of a body that breathes and circulates and does not need to be told.

You can stand on the catwalk and feel this. The slow *thunk-ssshh-thunk* of the pistons enters your chest before it enters your ears. The whole chamber has a pulse. The pulse is not fast. The pulse is the pulse of something asleep, something that has been asleep for a long time, something that is not dreaming but is also not quite awake.

So. The community.

It is small. It is bounded. It is closed. It has been closed, very likely, since before you were born, and it will be closed, very likely, after you have gone. It consists of moss and fern and fig, of banked coal and cycling water and iron that has stopped pretending to be anything but bone. It has excluded everything else. Nothing that breathes with lungs has been inside it for years; nothing that grazes, nothing that flies, nothing that spins a web in a corner. The agreement among its members is total. The fig breaks the boiler open. The boiler holds the fig in the warmth. The fire gives the fig heat. The fig does not let the fire go out. The roots drink what the engine condenses. The engine condenses what the roots give back. The moss grows faster than the flywheel wears it. The flywheel turns slow enough that the moss can keep up. Every member of the community has given up something it would have wanted — the fig has given up the sun, the moss has given up the soil, the fire has given up burning, the engine has given up being useful — and in return has been given something it would not otherwise have had, which is the company of the others.

They do not notice you. They will not notice you. They have arranged themselves into an equilibrium that does not have a place in it for a visitor. If you come back in a season, the creeper on the north wall will be half a meter further along the ceiling beam, and you will not have been missed. If you come back in a decade, the fig will have finished opening the boiler housing, and you will not have been missed. If you never come back, the pistons will lift and fall, and the water will cycle, and the moss will thicken by one more black-cored millimeter, and the fire will hold its breath, and nothing at all will be waiting for you, because nothing in the engine cathedral has ever waited for anything.

This is what lives there.
