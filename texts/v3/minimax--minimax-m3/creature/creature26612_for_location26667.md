The people of Calor Reach are not many. They are what is left of people, which is to say they are what people become when the reason for people has gone and the going-on continues anyway — a population distributed across a city built for several times their number, the way a single beating heart can keep a vast body technically alive.

One of them is at the central rotor now, as one of them is always at the central rotor. *He* is the courtesy-word; the language has no gendered pronouns left, only two relational ones that translate roughly as *near* and *further*. He stands close to the rotor housing with his hands at his sides. The hands are long and the joints articulate in slightly more places than they should, and where the wrist ought to be there is a seam — a fine, sealed join of pale metal or pale ceramic set into the skin, sometimes hidden by a sleeve, sometimes not. The seam does not open. It has never opened, or has not opened in living memory. Whatever it seals is sealed, and the inhabitants of Calor Reach do not seem to wonder what it is, the way you do not wonder what is sealed inside the walls of your own house.

His eyes are open and they are watching the rotor, but they are not watching it the way you would watch a rotor. They close, briefly, every few seconds — a slow blink, or something that resembles a blink but is not. It may be how he focuses. The light here is heavy and amber and does not behave well, and the inhabitants have learned, or were built, to see by a different sequence: a flare of brightness first, then shape, then depth, then motion, so that when they look at something still it takes them a moment to find it and when they look at something moving they find it instantly. They are good at finding rotors. They are less good at finding you, at first.

He wears seven layers, or what looks like seven layers. The outermost is stiff and the color of dried clay. The innermost is against his skin and may be the only one he ever washes, though washing is not a frequent act here and may be more about the feel of water against skin than about cleanliness in any meaningful sense. The layers between are varying weights of browns and yellows and the dull rust of the warm metal the Foundries produce. He does not look uncomfortable in them. He looks like a place that has been built up in layers and is not interested in being excavated.

When the rotor gives the small soft complaint it gives every few minutes — the held breath of metal under stress — he reaches out and lays his hand on the housing, lightly, the way a parent lays a hand on a child's back in the night. He is not sure, exactly, what he is listening for. He has done this for a long time. His hand rests there for a moment and then he takes it away and the rotor turns and he watches it with his strange, slow-blinking eyes, and the light falls on him warm and bruised, and the city receives him the way it receives everything: without comment, without judgment, as part of its own long, quiet breathing.

There are perhaps a few hundred of them. The numbers are not counted. The numbers have not been counted in a long time, or are counted by a function of the machines in the deep places, the ones that no one names and no one investigates. They know each other by sight and by sound — each has a particular click to their step, a particular way of exhaling, a particular small adjustment they make when they are thinking. They do not greet each other in the streets so much as acknowledge each other's passage with a slight turn of the head, or a click of the tongue, or nothing at all. They are not unfriendly. They are not particularly friendly. They are simply not in the habit of more.

Their language is mostly consonants. The dry air here carries plosives and fricatives well, and so their speech is a series of small hard sounds that come up from the back of the mouth and the throat — *kt, tch, rrk, msh* — with the vowels rare and small and sitting inside the words like pebbles in a streambed, slowing the current. When they speak it sounds like the Ventilators from a long way off, the same patient rotational patience, the same sense of something working without urgency. They have words for the machines, and for the rotors, and for the Foundries, and for the warm metal, and for bread, and for the basins, and for the hour-strike, and for the layers of clothing, and for the act of going to stand at the rim of a dry basin and looking down. They do not have many words for feelings. The word that comes closest is a small one, a sort of soft hum they make low in the chest, and it can mean several things — patience, or contentment, or the particular quality of attention one pays to a thing one has tended for a very long time. It is the word that best describes the city itself. They use it often.

They eat bread. They bake it in low ovens in the lower Foundries, in the warmth of the slag heaps, and the dough rises slowly — almost not at all — because the yeast, if it is yeast, has been the same yeast for longer than any recipe remembers, and it is patient the way everything here is patient. The loaves come out dense and dark and faintly warm even when they have cooled. They taste, to a visitor, of nothing in particular, and of something mineral, and of a faint sweetness that may be imagined. They are sliced and eaten slowly, often while working, often with one hand while the other hand rests on a machine. Whether the bread nourishes in the conventional sense is unclear; the people here do not appear to need much nourishment, or they need it so continuously and so gently that eating has become atmospheric rather than episodic, like breathing in something slightly warmer than air. They do not die of hunger. They do not seem to die of much, except occasionally, and quietly, in a way that the others notice and that does not interrupt the rotation.

When one of them dies — and it happens — the body is taken down into the deep places, the oldest parts of the city, where the machines move. The body is laid somewhere among the machines and left there. It is not buried in any formal sense; it is returned. The machines in the deep places take a long time, and the others continue their work, and the city receives the lost one back into its slow, geological metabolism the way it receives everything, and the rotation goes on.

They work because the work is there. There is a kind of procedural memory distributed across the population, so that someone is always at each rotor and each foundry and each basin and each of the long, slow routes through the city. They do not specialize so much as circulate. They know the tasks by the feel of them. They know when a housing is running too hot by the slight warmth that comes through their layered sleeves. They know when the warm metal has cooled enough to move by touching it with the back of their hand. They know when the hour-strike is coming by some shift in the air, or in themselves, that no one can explain to a visitor. They do not appear to do anything the operators can name, the operators do not appear to know what the machines do, and the relationship is not questioned because questioning is not in the habit anymore.

The hour-strike comes. The single low tone rises from the central spire and is felt in the teeth and then in the chest and then in the long bones, and it holds, and the work pauses. Not stops — pauses. The breath pauses. The hand on the rotor pauses. The person in the lower foundry pauses mid-motion. The rotors, of course, continue, because the rotors have always continued, and the operators no longer know how to stop them safely, which is one of those facts that is repeated often enough that it has become a kind of prayer. The tone fades over a long minute. The minute is long. The minute is felt. Then the work resumes, and the breath resumes, and the city goes on being the city.

They go to the basins. This is the closest thing they have to a ceremony. The basins are the old cisterns, the places where water once pooled, and they are vast and pale and carved by the long-ago recession of water into shapes that the people here can read the way you read a face — the shape of the rim, the depth of the bowl, the angles where the stone is worn smoothest. The basins are where the pious go. The piety is not for anything they can name. The piety is for the shape of what is missing. They stand at the rim and look down. They may put their hands on the stone. They are quiet there in a different way than they are quiet elsewhere — not the working quiet, not the maintenance quiet, but the quiet of looking at something that is not there and finding it anyway. They come away not changed but slightly different, the way a stone is slightly different after it has been held in a hand for a long time. They do not speak of this when they return.

In the lower corridors, in the deepest parts of the city, there are small moving things. They are heard more than seen — a quick scratch, a soft rustle, a faint suggestion of motion at the corner of vision that is gone when you look. The consensus is that they are machines. The minority position is that they are not. No one investigates, because investigation would require a kind of curiosity that has been ground down here to a fine dust, and because the answer, if it came, would not change anything. The people of Calor Reach leave offerings sometimes, in the lower corridors — a small piece of bread, set down at a junction where the small sounds are loudest. They do not speak of this to visitors either. They do not have a word for what they leave the offerings to, or the word is the same word they use for the patience-contentment-attention hum, and it covers more than one kind of attention.

If you arrive — and visitors come, sometimes, on the long road that took them a long time to walk — they will not greet you. They will continue their work. After a time, one of them will approach you. They will not ask your name. They will not ask where you came from. They will look at you with their eyes that close briefly to focus, and they will be interested in your temperature and your weight and the way you stand. They may touch your sleeve, or your pack, with the careful attention of someone who is interested in the texture of a thing. They may offer you bread. They will let you stay.

If you stay, you will be given a task. Not because they need help — the city does not need help; the city is past needing. Because tasks are how they relate to the world, and you will need to relate to the world to be here. You will be given something to tend. A rotor, perhaps, or a basin, or a small machine that does not appear to do anything that you can name. You will come to this task each day. You will not be thanked. You will not be inspected. You will simply do it, and they will pass you while you do it, and they will see you, and after a time you will be less of a visitor and more of a feature of the city, the way the rotor is a feature, the way the basin is a feature, the way the hour-strike is a feature. You will find that you have stopped wondering about things. Not in a defeated way. In a finished way. The questions you brought with you will become like the water in the basins — present in shape, absent in substance. You will go to the basins sometimes. You will eat the bread. You will wear the layers. You will hear the hour-strike in your teeth and feel it leave you over a long minute.

You will not be unhappy. You will not be happy. You will be of the city's temperature. You will be held, the way the stone is held, the way the rotor is held, the way the bread is held in a hand that has been resting on a machine for a very long time and has forgotten that it is a hand. You will be the city's continued breathing, the slow, vast, exhausted breath of a place that has reached some equilibrium it has been reaching for a long time, and that has decided, without deciding, to go on reaching.

You could live here a long time. You would not, in the end, be certain that you were.
