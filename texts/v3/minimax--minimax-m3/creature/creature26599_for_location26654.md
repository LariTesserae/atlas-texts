# Inhabitants of the Pocket

What lives here has settled into the place the way water settles into a footprint in clay — finding every groove, filling every hollow, and after enough time, no longer quite distinguishable from the shape it was given.

The warmth is the first inhabitant, though you do not think of it that way until you have been in the chamber a while. It is not heat in the dramatic sense. It is the warmth of a hand held too long, of a body beside yours in sleep, of a room that someone once cared about and that has continued to care about itself in their absence. The circulators maintain it without knowing they maintain it; the pumps push it through the chamber's long gut in slow loops that take minutes to complete; and the growth drinks it and respires it back, slightly altered, slightly more itself. The warmth is older than anything else here by an order of magnitude that no label in the room will give you. It is the only thing in this place that has never once paused.

Above the warmth, in the long amber half-light, the **bulbs** hold their stations.

They hang from the chamber's upper struts the way fruit hangs in an orchard that has stopped expecting a picker — heavy, patient, faintly lit from within. Each is the size of a child's head, though saying so makes them sound like fruit and they are not fruit. They are something more committed. Their skins are translucent in the way old parchment is translucent, the way the webbing between an old person's fingers is translucent, and the light inside them is a soft amber that pulses on a rhythm so slow you cannot tell, at first, whether it is a pulse at all or merely the chamber's own breathing made visible. It is not a pulse, exactly. It is the bulbs *answering* — answering the warmth, answering the mineral broth, answering the small attentions of the creatures that feed at their surfaces, in a call-and-response so woven into the room's background that you stop noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

They secrete. This is the central fact of them. From their undersides, in slow glistening threads, they let down something that is neither quite food nor quite light nor quite signal, and the hovering things rise to meet it, and the chamber is fed.

The **hoverers** are the most numerous and the least interesting, and they have perfected this unremarkableness the way a long-tenured clerk perfects the art of being in the room without drawing attention. They are pale. They are near-transparent. They are small enough that a dozen of them could fit in the cup of your palm, and from a distance they read as motion rather than form — a faint shimmering in the air just below the bulbs, the way heat shimmers above a road. Up close they have bodies: a soft ovoid, two or three fine fins they use for hovering rather than swimming, and a mouth — if that is the word — that unfurls against the bulb's underside like a question. They click. The clicks are small and dry and have the regularity of a clock that has lost its second hand, and they answer nothing in particular; the click is the whole of their contribution to the chamber's music. They eat. They hover. They click. They die, eventually, and when they die they sink past the stalks and past the soft drifters and come to rest on the gridplate, where the walkers find them and do what walkers do. The hoverers are the chamber's plankton. They are the reason the room can be said to *have* a food web. They have no opinion about this and would not understand the question.

Below them, the **drifters** hold their middle kingdom.

They are the chamber's inheritors and they carry themselves accordingly. Soft-bodied, slow, possessed of more fins than geometry ought to permit — six, eight, sometimes a dozen, depending on the individual, and the extras are not symmetrical but *arranged*, each fin tuned to a particular current the way an ear is tuned to a particular pitch. Their skins have the color of something that has been left in tea: a deep amber-brown, mottled with patches of pale where the warm water has bleached them. They were designed, once. You can see the lineage of that design in the clean lines of their spines, in the way their eyes (if those dark smudged ovals are eyes) sit in precise sockets, in the symmetry that persists even where it has been edited away. But the long warm dark has worked on them the way a long winter works on a fence post, and what is left is more honest. They have grown *into* themselves, past the original blueprint, the way a tree grows past the stake it was tied to. The fins are too many. The body is too soft. The mouth, when it opens, opens wider than any original specification would have called for, as though the creature has spent generations arguing for more.

They drift between the stalks with the proprietary air the original text names. They do not feed at the bulbs — that is the hoverers' place, and the drifters respect this division the way tenants in an old building respect the unspoken lines around who uses which stairwell. They feed lower. They browse the mineral film that crusts the gridplate. They nose at the slow channels where the broth runs. They are not in a hurry because nothing in this room is in a hurry, and they have a way of pausing mid-drift, fins spread, body suspended, as though they are listening to something only they can hear — the pumps perhaps, or the bulbs' pulse, or the small click of their neighbors in the upper tier. When they move again it is without apology, and without acknowledgment that they ever paused.

They are the largest things here besides the chamber itself. They do not appear to fear you. This is not boldness; it is the simple absence of any inherited memory of a creature like you. You are not in their lineage. You are an event, the way a stone that falls into still water is an event, and once you have rippled past them they will return to their browsing as though you had always been part of the room.

On the floor, the **walkers** do their slow work.

They are the most legible of the chamber's creatures as creatures — the closest to having what you would call a face, or a demeanor, or an inner life, though these are projections you make and they would not return. Jointed. Segmented. Low to the gridplate, never rising more than a hand's breadth above it. Their carapaces are the color of the rust on the hull outside, which is to say the color of the chamber itself, which is to say the color of *having been here a long time*, and this is either a coincidence of chemistry or something deeper, some long accommodation between the creature and the surface it walks. They have too many legs. Not the symbolic too-many of a centipede, which is just a lot of legs in a row, but legs *at different joints*, legs that hinge in different planes, legs that fold and unfold in sequences that you can watch for a long time without ever predicting the next configuration. They move like instruments being played by something with very small hands.

They walk in pairs and in trios, and this is not herding and not mating and not anything you have a word for, but it is *togetherness*, a basic commitment to the proposition that one of them is not enough. Sometimes a pair will travel the length of the chamber in lockstep, the rust-colored carapaces moving at the same rate, the jointed legs lifting and falling in patterns that almost match and almost do not. Sometimes a trio will pause in a clearing between stalks and arrange themselves in a triangle, facing inward, and remain there for so long that you begin to suspect they have forgotten they are three separate creatures and have become a single piece of furniture.

When you approach, they fold.

This is the behavior that holds you. The legs draw in. The joints collapse in a specific order — hindmost first, then the middle pairs, then whatever frontal apparatus they have — and the carapace curves over all of it, and what was a creature becomes a small perfect oval, the size of a large coin, the color of old rust, and utterly still. The oval is so closed that you cannot find the seam. The oval does not flee. The oval does not threaten. The oval simply declines to participate in whatever is happening, the way a stone declines to participate in a current, and after you have moved on — after minutes, or longer — the oval will, in its own time, unfold, and the legs will resume their playing, and the creature will turn toward whatever it was doing before you interrupted.

The folding is not fear. It is not a defense. It is, if anything, *patience made physical* — the body saying, in a language so old it predates the chamber, *I will be here when you are done*. The walkers fold the way a hand folds in sleep. They fold because folding is what they are for, in this place, in this long warm dark, in the patient company of the bulbs above and the drifters around them and the pumps that have never stopped. They are the room's most articulate inhabitants, and what they articulate, when they fold, is that the room is not in a hurry. The room has never been in a hurry. The room will be here, in its small warm way, when whatever you are doing is over.

What they walk *toward* — the thing the prompt leaves at the end, the thing the walker knows — you cannot see. You can guess. You can stand a long time watching them and you cannot see it. There is something. They have somewhere to be. The gridplate has been walked so much in certain corridors that the mesh has polished to a faint shine, and these corridors lead between particular stalks, past particular bulbs, into particular clearings where two or three of the walkers sometimes gather and pause in their triangle formation. The shape of their going is a kind of cartography. It maps something you do not have the language for, something that the chamber knows and you do not, something that has been going on in this small oval room at the bottom of a forgotten trench for longer than anything on the eroded plain above has bothered to keep track of.

The chamber has not told you what it is.

It has only kept its warmth, and its amber light, and its small faithful sounds, and let you watch.

You stand there a while. The pumps cycle. The bulbs pulse their slow answer to a question no one is asking. The hoverers click. The drifters browse. A walker at your feet uncurls, considers you with whatever it uses for consideration, and resumes its slow instrumental path toward the thing it knows, in a small warm room that has been left running for a very long time and that does not, will not, has never intended to stop.
