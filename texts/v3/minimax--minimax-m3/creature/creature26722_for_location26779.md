The inhabitants of the lower engine are not, properly speaking, inhabitants. They are expressions. They are what the place has done with the matter it has been given, the way a fire is what wood has done with the air. But to say this is to presume a metaphysics the place does not invite, so let me begin instead with what can be touched, or seen, or heard.

The smallest things first. In the joins of the brickwork, where the heat from below meets the condensate from above, there is a living stain. Orange. The color is wrong for a place this monochrome — too saturated, too alive-looking against the iron-dark brick and the pale green of the mineral damp. It is not a plant. It is not, by any honest accounting, an animal. It is a smear. It has the consistency of soft cheese left too long in a warm room, or of earwax, or of something you would not want to name in polite company. It has no body plan that I could discern. It spreads along the mortar lines like a slow fire in reverse — not consuming the brick but appearing to keep it company, to live in the seam the way a person lives in a doorway, half in, half out, leaning against the frame.

It eats, presumably. Everything here eats. Its food is the damp and the warmth and the fine dust that sifts down from the upper reaches, and what it leaves behind is the food of the next thing up.

The beetles come next. Or rather, the beetles are already there when you look — you simply have not noticed them yet, because they do not distinguish themselves from the surfaces they occupy until the surface moves. Then you see them everywhere. Black, lacquered, segmented into three legible divisions, the way all beetles are divided, but here the divisions are exaggerated, as if someone had drawn the insect in charcoal and then traced over the lines in lampblack. Their elytra are not quite shiny. They have the dull sheen of a well-oiled boot left in a closet. They are slow. This is the first thing you understand about them, and the last: they are slow in the way that lava is slow, that glaciers are slow, that continental drift is slow. They are not sluggish. Sluggishness implies a faster gear they could have chosen. They are simply operating at the pace the place has set for them, and the place is not in a hurry.

They cluster where the warmth is best. Along the great central drum — a thing I have not yet described and will not attempt to; it is the size of a small house and it is the same temperature as blood, and that is all I will say about it — along its hot face they lie in layers three and four deep, and the layers do not move much against each other. They breathe, presumably. The ticking sound they make is not their breathing. It is their legs, the fine hook-and-pull of their tarsi against the brick and against each other, the sound of one body negotiating passage over another, again and again, in a register below the roar. I listened for a long time before I was sure I was hearing it at all.

If you stand among them, they part. They do this without hurry. They divide around your boot like water around a stone, except that they remember their previous arrangement and resume it almost exactly when you step away. I stood still for eleven minutes once — I was counting, because I had nothing else to count — and by the seventh minute a beetle had reached the laces. By the eleventh there were three on the leather and one investigating the eyelets. They were not aggressive. They were not curious in any way I would recognize as curiosity. They were making use of a new surface the way the place makes use of everything: as substrate.

They eat the condensate. They eat each other's castings. They eat, where they can find it, the orange smear. I have watched one find the smear — they find it the way a dog finds a scent, by going slightly more purposeful for a moment, by tilting forward — and feed on it, and the feeding is not chewing, it is more like a long moist pause, a staying-against. After it had done, the smear was smaller in that spot, and I could see, with my face very close, that it was smaller not because anything had been removed but because the beetle had become part of it, had fused with it at the mouthparts, and the orange was now inside the beetle and would come out again, later, as casting, and the casting would be eaten by another beetle, and so on. The loop is closed. The loop has been closed for a long time.

In the troughs there are other things. Pale. The color of uncooked dough, or of the grubs you dig out of an old log, or of the inside of your own wrist. The length of a finger, sometimes longer. They have no eyes that I have seen, or if they have eyes they are not on the head, or the head is not where I expect the head to be. They are flexible in a way that does not look vertebrate. They fold and unfold, slowly, in the slow water, and the water moves at their pace and they move at the water's pace, and the trough is the only place in the engine where nothing is in a hurry, where even the machines have agreed to slow down.

They eat what falls in. Moth, sometimes, when a moth's flight falters and it drops from the column — though I am not sure a moth's flight can falter; they seem to fly until they do not, and then they are simply on the ground, still, and then they are in the trough. Beetle, more often, the smaller ones that get knocked off the great drum's face by the weight of the ones above them. Castings. Dust. The orange smear, when it has been washed down by a particularly heavy run of condensate, which is when the trough is fullest and the water moves fastest and even the pale things stir. I do not know what they do when the trough is dry. I have never seen the trough dry. I do not think the trough dries.

The moths are above all of this, or rather, the moths are the weather in which all of this is suspended. They are the size of a man's spread hand. I will say that again, because the first time one hears it, one does not believe it. They are the size of a man's spread hand, and there are more of them than you can count in a session of counting, and they are in continuous flight, and they do not land.

Their wings are dust-colored. Not the dust of an attic, which is grey and inert. The dust of a threshing floor, which is warm and full of grain. Their wings have given up on pattern. They have no eye-spots, no bands, no cryptic bark-markings. They have texture instead, the way old paper has texture, the way vellum has texture, and when they pass near a light source you can see the veins of the wings, which are not veins the way a leaf has veins but more like the rivers of a delta seen from far above — branching, rebranching, becoming finer until they are too fine to see. The wings make a sound. The sound is paper. The sound is ten thousand papers being softly crumpled at once, in a continuous roll, and it is woven into the roar the way a single voice is woven into a choir.

They fly in a column. The column runs from somewhere below — a grate, a vent, a channel I could not find — up to the roof and back down again, a closed circuit, a circulation. They are not migrating. They are not foraging. They are moving because moving is what they do, the way water moves in a fountain, the way blood moves in a body. Where the column thins you can see them turning at the edges, riding their own draft back down, and at the bottom of the turn they are briefly close enough to touch, and you can feel the wind of their passage on your face, and the wind is warm.

I do not know what they eat. I have watched them for hours and have never seen one feed. They may eat the dust. They may eat each other, at the edges of the column, where individuals fall briefly behind. I cannot tell. They do not seem to age. The ones at the top of the column do not look older than the ones at the bottom. The moths are, as far as I could determine, in equilibrium with themselves, the way the furnaces are in equilibrium with their unseen fuel: they are being, and being is enough.

They taste your warmth. I had thought this a figure of speech until a moth passed close to my ear and I felt, distinctly, a small change in the air, a draw, as if my heat had been briefly inhaled. After that I did not question the figure.

And then, in the upper reaches, where the catwalks cross and recross in a lattice the eye cannot follow, there are the watchers.

You do not see them. That is the first and most important thing. You see evidence of them. A limb, sometimes, crossing a gap in the grating — long, longer than it should be, jointed in more places than the human limb is jointed, and the joints are wrong, the way they bend is wrong, in directions the human knee and elbow will not bend. The limb is tufted with hair the color of the moths' wings. Not the same hair — the moth's wing is not hair, and the watcher's hair is not wing — but close enough in tone that you understand, after a time, that the watcher is dressed in the same material as the weather, and that this is camouflage, and that the camouflage is not for you.

An eye, sometimes, at the wrong angle. The eye does not move to find you. The eye is already pointed where it will be pointed, and it happens to be pointed at you, and after a moment it is pointed somewhere else, and you have not been given the dignity of being looked away from. You have simply been included in the longer survey.

I have never heard one. The roar covers it. I have never smelled one. The place covers that too. I have only inferred their presence from the fact that the beetle population remains stable, and the moth population remains stable, and that neither of these populations has filled the world, which is what an unchecked population would do, here, where the food is endless and the predators are absent. The watchers are not absent. The watchers are where the cats are in a city full of mice: everywhere, never seen, the reason for the quiet.

They come down, sometimes. I do not know when. I know that once, when I was crossing the bridge over the central drum at the wrong hour — there are hours here, marked by the furnace light, which dims slightly every cycle, and I had lost count — I looked up and a watcher was on the railing, not three meters from me, and it was looking at the drum, not at me. It was the size of a large dog, and it was not the size of a large dog. It was longer, in the limbs, and flatter across the back, and the head was set lower on the shoulders than a head should be, as if the neck had been removed and the head had been permitted to speak directly to the torso. The hair was the dust color. The eye I could see was the color of the trough water. It watched the drum. I watched it watch the drum. I did not move. It did not move. After a time I do not know how to measure, it went back up, hand over hand, into the lattice, and I could not find it again, and I did not look for it again.

The arrangement is the law of the place. The moths do not fill the air because the watchers take them. The beetles do not fill the brick because the watchers take them. The troughs do not fill because the pale things take what falls. The smear does not fill the mortar because the beetles take it. Nothing is wasted. Nothing accumulates. The place is its own accountant, and the books are always balanced.

And the machines, which I have not properly called fauna but which I will call fauna now, because they breathe, they excrete, they circulate, they maintain themselves in a steady state that is not stasis but is not exactly motion either — the machines are part of this too. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are the third thing, the smear-thing, the in-between. They are what the place is for. They are the reason the troughs are tilted and the floor is true and the furnaces are perpetual and the watchers keep their long watch from the upper catwalks. The machines are the apex of the food web, not because they eat the watchers but because the watchers exist to keep the world clean for the machines, and the moths exist to keep the air moving for the machines, and the beetles exist to keep the brick clean for the machines, and the troughs exist to take the runoff from the machines' breath, and the smear exists at the seam because that is where the machines' warmth meets the machines' damp, and the seam is the machines' skin, and the smear is the machines' flora.

You can walk through. The fauna parts for you. The machines do not adjust. The watchers note you. You leave, and the place does not change, because you were not the thing it was waiting for. You were a disturbance in its long equilibrium, and the equilibrium closed over you the way the beetles closed over my boot, the way the water closed over the pale things, the way the roar closes over every smaller sound, and the place resumed being the place.

But you carry a piece of it. The piece ticks in your chest. I have it still. I have it now, writing this in a higher place, where the wind is honest and the scrub-grass grows and the sky has more than one color. I have it as a faint reciprocal motion, a small counter-rhythm to my own heart, and it will be there until my heart stops, and after, perhaps, in whatever walks where I have walked, hearing what I have heard, in the chest of whatever comes next.
