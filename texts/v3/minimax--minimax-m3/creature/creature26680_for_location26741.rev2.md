# The Inhabitants

The place has not been itself for very long, and most of its inhabitants are still arriving at the terms on which they will stay. They are not pioneers in any heroic sense. They are what comes up when the substrate is warm and wet and dark and there is something to do in it. They are abundant. They are quiet. They have made their peace with the timescale, which is not one.

---

The lantern-flies are not flies. That much is clear the moment one drifts close enough to inspect, which happens rarely and without warning, because the air is dense and slow here and the lantern-flies drift in it the way motes drift in a sunbeam that has no sun. They have six wings. Most of the time. Sometimes five, sometimes seven — I have counted — and once, memorably, five and a half, the half being a stub no longer than a thumbnail, growing from somewhere that did not seem anatomically prepared for it. The wings do not beat the way a fly's wings beat. They hold still and adjust, in tiny increments, to keep the body aloft, and when a lantern-fly wants to move it does not so much fly as relocate, by a slow negotiation with the air.

The light is in the abdomen, low and steady, blue-cyan, the color of deep water where there is no water. It pulses, but the pulse is not a heartbeat. It is more like a slow tide — the light rises, holds, falls, holds, and during the hold the whole lantern-fly is just a darker dot inside a blue circle that does not move. They hang in clusters and they hang alone. When many of them hang in the same stretch of not-sky they make a low moving constellation, and the constellation rearranges itself on no schedule you can read — they do not migrate in the way birds migrate, they do not school in the way fish school, they are just many bodies in a wet medium, each holding its place by its own private calculation, and the group is a coincidence of many private calculations happening at once.

They go out, all of them, when the towers sound. That is worth knowing. The whole dim galaxy goes dark for about a minute, and in that minute the place is a different place — quieter, closer, more honest. Then they come back, dimmer than before, as if a fraction of them failed to relight. The ones that failed are not missed for a long time; they are simply less of the constellation. Eventually the constellation readjusts.

---

In the standing water of the young canals — the cracks that haven't learned to be rivers yet — there are worms. Pale. Longer than they should be. Longer than the water is deep, longer than any geometry I trust would allow. They are pale the way the filaments are pale, the way the plates are pale, the way everything down here is pale that is not wet or not lit, and they have, along their length, segments that emit a greenish light — not steady, not in a pattern, more like a string of beads in which each bead has forgotten what the beads before it were doing, so that one segment might be lit while its neighbors are dark, and another, two segments down, will be lit while the first has gone out, and so on in a kind of disordered sequin down the body. Their rhythm is not a rhythm. It is a memory of one.

They are long enough to coil, and they do coil, in slow knots at the bottom of the cracks. They pass through each other when their paths cross, the way two shoelaces pass through each other when they tangle — not merging, not separating, just occupying the same water for a moment and then being elsewhere. Whether this is two worms or one worm I cannot say. The light moves between segments across the impossible junction as if it does not care.

They dim, but they do not go out, when the towers sound. Their light drops to almost nothing and stays there, and the worms go very still in their knots, as if listening, and when the sound passes they resume their broken rhythm without quite picking up where they left off. There is always a beat skipped.

---

On the rims of the pipes there are frogs, or things that have made the same arrangement with their bodies that frogs have made with theirs. They sit. Mostly they sit. They have a red light in the upper throat that does not behave like a light. It spills. It finds its way around corners. It makes the empty pipes behind them look as if they were full of warm water, and the wet metal near them looks as if it were warmer than the rest of the wet metal, and this is not an illusion in the strict sense — it is the light actually doing something the light is not supposed to do, which is find places to be that are not where it was thrown.

They have a second throat, lower, that does not produce sound. I watched one for a long time trying to understand what it does. The lower throat opens, slowly, and closes, slowly, and nothing audible comes out. Once I saw the lower throat of one open and the red light of the throat above it went out for the duration, as if the same animal had two voices and could use only one at a time, and had chosen to use the silent one. When the lower throat closed the red came back and resumed its patient spilling.

They close both throats during the tower-tone. They are the most punctual of the inhabitants. They know.

---

About halfway up some of the towers — not all of them, only some — there is a horizontal opening that breathes. Out of the opening comes a soft grey animal the size of a cat. I say *cat* because I have to call it something and *cat* is the shape that fits, but it is not a cat. It has the size and the soft grey of a cat and the patience of a cat and that is where the resemblance ends. It climbs the rest of the way up the tower, using joints I cannot see, and sits at the top, and looks down at you with patience that does not need you to be interesting.

The tower, the animal, the breath — the place does not commit to whether these are one thing or three. I have seen the opening breathe without the animal. I have seen the animal sit at the top without the opening breathing. I have seen the breath and the animal and the tower all together, and in that configuration the thing that looks down at you is something else again, larger than a cat, longer than the breath, the breath being only one of its organs. When the cycle shifts they shift with it. They are the same color as the tower at the bottom and at the top. They are the same temperature as the tower at the bottom and at the top.

They do not move much. They wait. They will wait tomorrow. They are patient in the way the place is patient, which is to say they are not waiting for anything in particular; they are simply persisting.

---

In one of the unfilled canals there is a larger thing. I have not seen it whole. I have heard it breathe when I stood at the rim of the canal and let my own breathing go quiet enough to hear it, and the breathing was slow and unhurried and full of the wet air, and the air above the canal moved a little with each breath, in and out, in and out. It is patient. It will be there tomorrow. It has probably been there a long time — longer than the rest of the place, perhaps, in the way some seeds are older than the soil they end up in.

Once, leaning over the rim, I saw a curve of dark move through the water far below, and the curve had a kind of slow bulk to it, and the curve kept going past where it should have stopped, the way the worms in the standing water keep going past where they should have stopped, and it took a long time to pass, and it surfaced once, briefly, and what surfaced was not a head but a kind of knuckle of dark, and the knuckle was warm. Then it was gone. The breathing continued. The breathing was not disturbed.

---

Look at a puddle long enough and the puddle looks back. Not metaphorically. The water arranges itself into a configuration that is paying attention to you, and the configuration is not the reflection of anything above it because there is nothing above it that would produce this. The configuration is small — the size of a face held in the water, but not a face, more like a layout, an arrangement of darker and lighter water that has decided to consider you. After a while it loses interest. The water flattens again. The puddle becomes a puddle.

I do not know if it is the same configuration in each puddle or many configurations in many puddles. I do not know if the configurations communicate. They feel separate, but then they also feel like they might be one configuration in many places, looking out of all of them at once, which is a feeling I cannot confirm and cannot disconfirm. Once I stood between two puddles a few feet apart and both were looking and the looking was the same looking, and that was the moment I stopped trying to decide.

---

In the substrate, in the bored tunnels, in the plates and the mud and the small canyons — rustlings. Soft, constant, everywhere. The substrate is full of small things that move through it. I have seen a few of them, briefly, when one of the standing plates was tilted enough that I could see into the tunnel it had been bored through — pale things the size of a finger, jointed in ways I could not count, with no eyes I could find, moving with purpose through the wet dark. They are the most numerous. They are the substrate. The plate is the walls of their city and they are the population. I have never seen one stop moving. I do not think they stop.

Sometimes, walking, you feel a displacement across the back of the hand, a wrongness, a sense of something passing through the air where you are, very close to the skin, that does not touch the skin but uses the skin as a reference. These are the things in the wet air itself — not the lantern-flies, which hold still, but something else, smaller, that moves through the dense medium and passes near you without quite arriving. They are the most felt. I have never seen one. I have felt them often, and they have never once seemed to notice I was there.

---

Sparse. Pale. No leaves. A root and a tip. The filaments grow toward the warmest wet surface, which is almost every surface here, but they choose. They prefer the undersides of the standing plates, where the water runs slowest. They are slow. They will, given enough of the place's slow time, cover the plates. Then the place will not be this place, or it will be the same place with a different skin. That is the long dynamic of it, and the filaments are the slowest part.

Up close they are translucent, like fingernails, like the rind of something soft that has been kept in the dark. They have no flowers. They have no seeds that I have found. They have a tip that is slightly warmer than the rest of them, and the tip is where they grow from, and they grow toward whatever the warmest wet surface is, and they take a long time about it. I do not think they know they are the future of the place. They are just doing what the warmth tells them to do.

---

I have been putting it off. Some of what lives here is not fauna. Some of what lives here is not vegetation. Some of what lives here is the industrial.

The pipes hum at the edge of hearing. They are not iron and not anything I can name. Their color depends on how wet they are. They weep at their joints. They have fittings at their joints of unclear purpose — a wheel that does not turn, a glassy disc you can see through but cannot see into, a hexagonal nut that appears to be welded and can be unscrewed by hand, and when unscrewed reveals the same pipe continuing inside itself with no discontinuity, no interior, only the next length of pipe pretending to be inside itself. They are producing something. They are producing the conditions of the place — the wetness, the warmth, the hum, the abundance. They are a machine for making this place.

The towers have heartbeats. Suspended inside, somewhere, something slow is suspended and is beating. The conveyor frames move when there is enough water to grease them, a faint groan of metal that has not been oiled and will not be. The pump house contains a thing whose front is whichever direction you are looking from. The tank is full of a liquid that is not water and is not anything else I can name, and you can see its surface from outside because the wall is not behaving as a wall. The chimney exhales a slow warm breath every few minutes, smelling of metal and ozone and something sweet I almost remember.

I am not sure whether to call them alive. I will not call them alive. I will say they are doing the kind of work that living things do — making, maintaining, persisting — without committing to whether they are the kind of thing that can stop. They are producing, continuously, the only conditions they know how to produce, and the conditions are this place, and this place is the only place they know how to make.

---

When the tower sounds — the low tone that lasts about a minute and is felt more than heard — the place goes briefly honest. The lantern-flies go out. The frogs close both throats. The worms go very still in their knots. The grey cats at the tops of the towers turn, slowly, to face the direction the tone came from, or to face away from it, I have never been sure which. The thing in the canal breathes once, deeper, and the water around it shivers. The puddles lose their attention for the duration. The rustling things go quiet in their tunnels — not silent, but a half-tone quieter, as if the substrate itself were listening. The brushing things do not pass across your hand for that minute. The filaments stop growing. The not-sky contracts a little harder. The standing water in the cracks rises about an inch.

Then it passes. The lights come back, dimmer than before. The frogs open the upper throat first, then the lower. The worms resume their broken rhythm, skipping a beat. The grey cats sit again. The puddles resume their considering. The rustling things resume their rustling. The brushing things resume their brushing. The thing in the canal resumes its slow breathing. The filaments resume their slow growing. The not-sky expands. The water in the cracks lowers by the inch. The pipes resume their hum.

This happens once an hour, or once a day, or once a year. The place does not commit to a timescale. None of them do. They live in a place that has not yet decided how fast time is supposed to move, and they have made their peace with it, and they go on. The sharp edges of the young sediment weather by a thickness you could not measure. The sparse filaments grow a fraction of a fraction. The not-sky contracts and expands on a slow cycle, maybe hourly, maybe daily. The water rises and falls by inches over timescales that are not hours and not years but something between.

The place is changing. It is changing right now. I am standing in it and it is changing, but the change is in increments that belong to the place and not to me, and I will be gone, or different, before the change is large enough to be the change of anything at all. The inhabitants will still be here. The lantern-flies will still be hanging. The worms will still be coiling in their knots, passing through each other in the wet dark, lighting up out of order. The frogs will still be sitting on the rims of the pipes, spilling their red light around corners. The grey cats will still be at the tops of the towers, looking down with patience. The thing in the canal will still be breathing. The puddles will still be considering. The rustling things will still be moving through their tunnels, never stopping. The pipes will still be producing, the towers will still be beating, the chimney will still be exhaling its sweet metallic breath, the place will still be making itself out of itself in the only way it knows how, and the filaments, slow, slow, slow, will be turning the plates of sediment into something else, into the skin of a place that is not yet this place, that will be this place's next self.

I will miss most of it. They will not.
