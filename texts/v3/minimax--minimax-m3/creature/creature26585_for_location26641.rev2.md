# Those of the Long Wind

They are small against the towers. That is the first thing, and it is not a metaphor but a measurement: a person at the base of an old tower stands in the curve of a single buttress root, and the bark above them climbs a hundred feet before the first branching, and the branching goes on into weather. They have learned to be small without resenting it. They have learned, which is harder, to be small without forgetting it. A child who has grown up here, asked what a tower is, will sometimes say *it is the thing I live in*, and sometimes *it is the thing that lets me live*, and sometimes, if the child is the kind of child who listens more than they speak, they will say nothing and put their hand on the bark and wait, and an older person will nod, because that is the right answer, or one of the right answers, and there are several.

A person of this place is compact and dark and moves carefully. Their hair is long and kept out of the face with a band of woven fiber. Their hands are wide and rough, the knuckles thickened from years of taking the spiral handholds the way water takes a streambed — by being poured over them, repeatedly, until the path is theirs. Their eyes are dark and adapted to the dim. They do not squint at the low sun in summer because they have learned not to look up at it; the snow-blindness that took the climbers of the first expeditions is a thing their great-grandparents' great-grandparents learned about and told stories about, and the telling is its own kind of caution. They wear layered hide and bark-cloth, lashed and wrapped, not stitched — stitching is for the lowlanders; stitching requires a needle of bone or metal, and a needle of metal is a trade item, and a trade item is something you do not waste on a garment that will be cut off you at the end of the season and fed to the fire. Their clothes are not clothes exactly. They are more like the second bark of a person: a layer that grows back.

They live above the ground. Most of their life is in the wood. A young couple, when they join their towers — for they marry between towers, or sometimes within, but always with the matter of climbing in mind — will take a chamber partway up the trunk of one of the older trees, a chamber the tree has already made and emptied, and they will fit it out the way a riverbank is fitted out by a slow river: lightly, patiently, without the assumption that any of it will last. They lash a platform across the widest part of the hollow. They bring up armfuls of dry moss for the sleeping floor. They build their fire at the inner curve, where the wood thickens, and the chimney is already there — a vertical flue the tree grew when it was young and has since closed at the bottom and kept open at the top, so the smoke finds its way without their help. They do not understand how the chimneys work, exactly. They have theories. The theories are inherited and partly wrong and have been for many generations, and no one minds, because the chimneys work whether or not the theory is right, and the people who climbed before them had theories too, and theirs were also partly wrong, and the chimneys worked then as well.

A family in a tower is a quiet thing. There is not much room. There is the wind outside, which is constant, and there is the singing of the wood, which on a still night is a low hum and on a windy night is something you can feel in your chest if you lean against the inner wall, and there is the sound of the other chambers, which are not empty but are not crowded either, because a tower holds several families the way a hand holds a few seeds — loosely, with gaps between. The people do not pound on the walls to talk to their neighbors. They climb. If a neighbor is wanted, a person climbs down or up or sideways — along the limb-ridges that the old trees have — and they arrive and they sit and they say what they have to say. They bring a small gift. The gift is usually food, or a length of good rope, or a story.

Their names are not the names you would give. They have two kinds of names. The first is the small name, the day-name, which is used in the chamber and among intimates and which is sometimes not spoken aloud for years at a time, because to speak someone's small name to a stranger is a kind of exposure the people do not commit lightly. The second is the long name, which is the name of the tower they were born in, with a personal syllable added. So a woman born in the tower called *Kestrel-in-the-Shear* might be, in the chamber, *Sára*, and outside, *Kestrel-Sára*, and to a person from another tower, *Sára-of-Kestrel*, with the grammar bending depending on whether she is being introduced or being spoken of or being spoken *to* in a formal context, and the bending itself is meaningful, and a child spends years learning when to bend which way. This is not ornament. It is the architecture of a people who have learned that a name is a load-bearing thing, and that if you put it in the wrong place it will bring down the small structure that depends on it.

Their sense of time is not your sense of time. They do not count days the way you count days, because the day here is a poor unit — the light is wrong, the seasons do not behave, and a year is the time it takes a certain small bird to come back to its nesting shelf, and a lifetime is the time it takes a tower to grow a chamber you can stand in. A person is young until they have learned to climb in the dark by feel alone. A person is old when their hands have worn the handholds smooth and the tree has grown new ones around the worn ones, so that the person and the tree have negotiated, over decades, a set of steps that fit only them. There are people in the older towers whose handholds are not the handholds any other person uses. These people are respected. They are also, in a way the people do not quite say, partly of the tree.

They eat sparingly. They gather the red moss in the wetter folds, and the bitter lichen from the scree gardens, and the inner bark of the towers — not the outer bark, which is too tough and too alive to be taken, but the soft pale layers beneath, which the tree sheds in slow curls the way a deer sheds antler velvet, and which can be eaten raw or dried or soaked and beaten into cakes. They trade for grain from far below, carried up in skin panniers by the lowland porters who will not themselves come higher than the second camp; the trade is conducted at the edge of the cloud, in a place both peoples agree to call *the meeting*, although neither thinks of it that way. They eat the climbing grazers when they can catch one, which is not often, because the grazers hold motionless in the wind and are the color of bark and have been doing this for longer than the people have been watching. They eat the dark birds in hard seasons, and they eat the small flyers when the flyers emerge after a rain and are briefly a fog; the people do not love the flyers, exactly, but they notice the way the flyers come out, and the way the predators come up to meet them, and the way the hour of killing passes and the air is quiet again, and they have a story about this, and the story is one of the ones a child hears before they can climb.

They are not a violent people. They are not a peaceful people either; those are lowlander words, and the lowlanders do not live here. They are a people among whom violence is rare because the conditions for it are rare. Two people in a tower might disagree. They will not fight, because the tower is small and the fall is long. Two towers might disagree about a seep, or about a lichen garden, or about the name of a far tower that one of them has claimed and the other has visited. They will not go to war over it, because war requires a density of people that this country does not have. What they do, instead, is stop speaking the name of the other tower for a while, and this is felt as a real thing, the way a missing tooth is felt, and after some years — three or four, sometimes more — one of the towers will use the name again, quietly, as if testing, and if the other does not protest, the silence is over. There is no record of these silences except in the long memory of the older people, who will tell you, if you ask, which tower did not speak to which, and for how long, and why, and how it ended. The record is good enough. The people are good at remembering.

Their rituals are few. The largest is the one they perform when a tower is felled — not felled by them, but felled by the wind, or by lightning, or by age. A falling tower is the loudest thing that happens in this country, and it is also the saddest, because a tower is a name and a home and a part of the living architecture of the place, and when it falls, the people who lived in it must go somewhere else, and the somewhere-else is not the same. The ritual lasts several days. The people who live nearby come to the fallen wood. They do not try to lift it. They sit with it. They take pieces of the bark and burn them slowly and speak the names of the people who lived in the tower and the names of the people who lived in it before them, going back as far as anyone can remember, which is not very far, because names are forgotten, but it is as far as anyone has. They do not believe the tree is a god. They do not believe it is not a god. They believe, in the way they believe most things, that it is a thing whose inner life is not theirs to know, and that to sit with it when it dies is the least they can do for a thing that has held them up for as long as anyone can remember.

Their children are taught early to climb. A child is not carried up the trunk; a child is set on the lowest handhold and told to find the next one, and the next, and the next, and the parent climbs below, ready to catch but not catching unless the fall is real. A child who cannot climb by a certain age is a child the parents worry about quietly, in the chamber, in the small voice used for worries. A child who climbs well is a child the parents say little about, because to praise a climber is to give the wind a name to take. They are taught the names of the towers within a day's walk, and they are taught to recognize the towers further out by their silhouettes, because a tower's silhouette is its face, and two towers of the same age and species do not have the same face, although the differences are small and take a lifetime to learn. They are taught, also, not to walk far from a tower at night. This is not a rule. It is closer to a habit, the way not stepping off a cliff is a habit — not because you have decided it each time but because the body has decided it for you.

They have a few words for the dark. In the chamber they have *soft dark*, which is the dark with a fire in it. They have *open dark*, which is the dark of the plateau, with stars, where you can see the towers as suggestions. They have *heavy dark*, which is the dark of the long winter, and *the dark of the speaking towers*, which is the dark in which the far towers are audible but not locatable, and which is the dark a person is in when they are lost. The heaviest word, the one they use rarely, is the word for the dark you die in, if you die out on the stone, which is a particular dark because it is a dark you have chosen, by being out there, and which the wind will fill in. They do not speak of this dark to children. They speak of it to each other, late, in the chamber, when the fire is low, and the words are the kind of words you say to make a thing smaller by saying it, and they do not always make it smaller, but the saying is the thing.

They die here, most of them, in the wood. They are taken down, sometimes, in a long slow lowering on ropes the family has woven, and laid in a crevice between the roots of the tower they lived in, and covered with stones the family has carried up, and the wind begins to fill the crevice, and the moss begins to come up around the stones, and in a few years there is a small mound, and the mound is a part of the tower, and the person who is in the mound is, in a way the people do not say but do not dispute, a part of the tower too. They do not name the mounds. The mounds are not the people. The people are the names.

And then there is the matter of the pulse. The slow pulse of the wood against the hand in the dark. The people know about it. They have always known about it. A child is told, when they are old enough to be told, that the tower is alive, and that being alive is not the same as being a person, and that the difference is not a thing that can be explained with words but only a thing that can be felt, and that the feeling takes a long time, and that some people feel it and some people do not, and that both are acceptable, and that no one is required to climb a tower because they think it is alive, only because they need to live somewhere. They are told that the pulse is not a heartbeat. They are told that no one knows what it is. They are told that some of the older people, in the long dark, put their hands on the bark and listen, and that what they hear is not a sound but a kind of slow agreement between the hand and the wood, and that this is not religion and is not science and is not madness, and is just what happens when a small warm thing rests against a large warm thing for a long time.

They are a people of the long wind. They are few. They are high up. They are listened to by the wind and they listen to the wind. They are held up by things they do not understand, and they hold up each other, and they do not pretend to understand more than they do, and they do not pretend to understand less. They have names for the towers and they have small names for each other and they have a word for the dark of dying and they have a word for the pulse. They do not have a word for what they are. They have, instead, the towers, and the wind, and the slow patient work of climbing down and climbing up and carrying things and sitting with the dead and teaching the names. This is enough. It is what they have.
