The moths come first, because they are what you see, and because seeing them is how the place introduces itself. They are the color of paper that has been left in a drawer for a long time — a brownish yellow, with darker freckling along the forewings where some old chemical in their bodies has oxidized unevenly, and a dusting of pale scales across the hindwings that comes off on your fingers if you brush one. They are fatter than the moths you find in houses above. They have had, generation after generation, no reason to be lean; nothing here chases them through open air, nothing snaps them out of zigzagging flight except the occasional involuntary collision with a wall or a pipe or another moth. Their bodies are soft and slightly translucent at the join between thorax and abdomen, and if you held one up to the bluish Engine-glow you could see the dark line of the gut moving inside, working slowly at whatever the moth last ate. They rest in stacked layers on the lamps, so many of them that the glass of each bulb is hidden behind a trembling curtain of wings and bodies, and the light that does escape has been filtered through them into something warmer and less directional, a glow that seems to belong to the moths themselves rather than to the filament behind them. Their droppings fall in a constant fine rain onto the floor of the workshops, pale and powdery, and form a thin pale soil in the seams of the brick and in the gutters where the water has not yet reached to wash them away. The larvae of these moths live in the same seams — small pale grubs with hard dark heads, almost invisible against the mortar, working through the efflorescence and the crumbly brick at their own unhurried pace. They pupate in the same cracks, in chrysalises so thin that you can sometimes see the forming wing inside, and the adults emerge directly into the Sump's damp air, climb a short way up the nearest vertical surface, and sit for an hour vibrating their wings dry before they lift off toward the lamps. A moth that escapes the lizards, that does not fall into a basin, that does not lose its footing on the iron and tumble down into the brickwork, can live here for weeks. Most do not.

The lizards are next, because the lizards are what makes the moths careful. They are small — the length of a finger, or a little longer — and they sit very still on the walls and on the crossbeams and along the railings of the bent catwalk, and they watch the lamps with the patient attention of creatures that have learned to wait because waiting is what their food does. Their skin is a pale gray-green with a faint bluish cast along the underside of the jaw and the belly, the color of mineral-stained water, and this coloration is not quite camouflage against the iron and the mossy brick but is close enough that a lizard at rest on a wall reads as a small irregularity in the surface, a chip in the paint, a knot in the iron, until it moves and you realize it has been there all along. Their eyes are large for the size of the head, round, and black all the way through, and the black catches what little light there is and gives it back as a tiny wet flash — pinpricks of greenish white in the dimness, which is how you know there are more of them in a workshop than you thought. They hunt from perches. A lizard on a beam will fix its attention on a moth that has settled too far from its neighbors, and it will hold that attention without moving for so long that the watcher begins to doubt the lizard is alive, and then there is a single quick movement — a short drop, a snap of the small pale mouth, a flutter of disturbed wings around the empty place where the moth had been — and the lizard is back on its perch, working its jaw in slow sideways motions, and the other moths on the lamp have not reacted, because this, too, is what they expect. The lizards drink. In the late hours, when the phosphorescence in the rock has faded to its dimmest and the Engine-hum has dropped half a tone, they descend from the walls and walk along the rims of the brick basins and the gutters with a careful, high-stepping gait, and they lower their heads and take the water in small lapping motions, and their throats move. They are not afraid of the water, the way some surface lizards are. They have been drinking this water since they were hatched.

The rats are not what they were, and what they were is no longer quite the right name for them. There are rats here — long-bodied, with fur so pale it is almost white, and tails that are bare and pink and scaled like a small snake's — and they live in the deeper workshops and in the channels cut behind the brick of the walls, in the spaces where the iron plate has rusted through and the rubble behind has stayed loose. They are bigger than the rats you find in cities. They have been here long enough to fill a niche that has no name, eating the moth larvae in the brick seams, eating the things that are not rats, eating the scraps of whatever organic matter falls through the gratings and is not carried away by the water. They are cautious in a particular way — they move along the edges of things, in the strip of wall just above the floor where the iron and the brick meet, and they will not cross an open gutter if there is another route, even a longer one. They nest in the lost corners of workshops, in piles of shredded paper and rusted cloth that no one has disturbed in decades, and their young are born blind and pink and indistinguishable, at first, from the young of the things they live alongside.

And those things — the things that are not rats but live in the same places — are harder. They live in the deeper workshops, in the channels, in the spaces behind the iron plate where the rubble has piled up and the water has not yet reached. You see them rarely, and when you do you are not always certain of what you have seen. They have too many joints. A leg that bends where a leg should not bend, that folds back on itself in a way that is closer to an insect's than a mammal's, but the body to which it is attached is not an insect's body, is furred, is warm-blooded in the way you can feel when one darts across your foot in the dark. They move in a gait that is not quite walking — a series of small hesitations, as if each step has to be relearned at the moment it is taken. They are the color of wet stone. Their eyes, if they have eyes, do not reflect. They eat what the rats eat, and sometimes they eat the rats, and sometimes the rats eat them, and the population of each keeps the other in a balance that no one has ever had to adjust. No one has ever named them. No one has tried. They are part of what the Sump has made of itself.

In the water, the ecology is older and quieter. The pale worms live in the silt that has gathered in the basins — a fine gray sediment that is mostly calcium washed down from the ceiling and the brick, with a small admixture of moth scales and the soft bodies of things that have died and settled. The worms are thin and white and almost transparent, and they lie head-down in the silt with their tails just at the surface of the water, taking what oxygen the slow current brings. They are not many, but they are persistent; if you watch a basin for an hour you can see the fine pale hairs along their bodies moving, and the tiny disturbances they make in the silt as they feed. The soft-bodied things that cling to the underside of the brick — these are smaller, almost flat, with feet that are really more like fine gripping hairs, and they hold themselves against the current in places where the water runs thinly over the brick, in the shallow gutters that feed the basins. They feed on what the water brings, which is to say on whatever has been washed down from above: fine particles of calcium, the dissolved minerals of the rock, the occasional drowned moth. They are so still, and so much the color of the brick, that you cannot tell them from the brick without looking for a long time, and after a long time you realize that what you thought was brick has small openings in it, and from the openings a slow current of waste water emerges, and the soft-bodied things are stationed around these openings the way the lizards are stationed around the lamps. The eyeless fish are further out, in the deeper channels, in the gutters that run under the buildings and behind them, where the light does not reach. They are pale pink, almost white, and their heads are blunt and their mouths turn slightly downward, and they swim in loose shoals that are not quite coordinated but not quite random either, with a slow fanning of their small fins that is more like drifting than swimming. They do not see. They find each other by a faint trail of something they release into the water, and they find their way through the brick channels by following the current, by pressing against the walls with their sensitive bellies, by knowing the shape of the place in some way that is not memory because it is not personal but is, instead, the memory of the species. They nudge along the gutters and sometimes into the basins, where the pale worms are, and they eat the worms they find. The frogs — or the frog-things, the things like frogs with too much webbing — sit on the rims of the deeper basins and sing. They sing a single note, each of them, and they do not synchronize, and the effect is not a chorus but a sustained wash of sound at a single pitch, with small irregularities that drift across it the way ripples drift across still water. They sing more in the hours after the lamps have been at their brightest, as if the light were what set them off. They sing into the Engine's hum, and the two sounds — the biological note and the mechanical one — do not quite match and do not quite clash, and the air of the Sump is full of this faint unresolved chord that you do not consciously hear after a while but that you feel, in the teeth and the lungs, as a pressure, as a presence.

The slow things live here too, in their way. The mosses and the lichens and the ferns that have come up through the rusted iron and are taking the walls back into the rock, layer by layer. The stalactites of rust that grow down from broken pipes, which are not really stalactites but are doing the same work, building downward by slow accretion, the iron giving itself back to the water drop by drop. The calcium that the water lays down on the brick, on the iron, on the concrete pad of the Engine, in thin pale coats that thicken over the years into something that is no longer the original surface but is also not yet the stone it will become. The ferns that have rooted in the joints of the pipes and unfurl their fronds into the air of the workshops, pale green at first and then darker, then brown at the edges, then dead, then another frond from the same root, in a cycle that has been running since before anyone alive remembers. The whole place is alive, in this slow way, and the slowness is not the same as stillness. The slowness is the rate at which the iron learns to be stone, at which the stone learns to be moss, at which the moss learns to be the wall. The Sump is a place that has been made by one set of hands and is being unmade, or remade, by another set of hands that are not hands at all but are the patient work of water and of time.

And the Engine hums. The Engine hums into all of this, and the things that live here do not mind it, the way you do not mind the sound of your own blood in your ears. It is the oldest sound in the Sump, older than the moss, older than the eyeless fish, and it is the sound that everything else has arranged itself around. The moths fly toward it in the absence of other light, and the lizards hunt them where they cluster on its surface, and the rats nest in the warm strip of wall along its base, and the soft-bodied things cling to the condensate that forms on its pipes, and the frogs sing against it, into it, as if they were trying, over the long slow course of their generations, to tune themselves to it, to make their single note and the Engine's note match. They have not yet succeeded. They may never succeed. But the trying is part of the place now, and the place would not be the place without it.

To stand here is to be considered. The moths, briefly, in their small compound way. The lizards, more carefully, with their black wet eyes. The rats, from the safety of their channels, with the quick attention of animals that have learned what a footstep is. The fish, in their slow drift through the dark water, without seeing you but feeling the displacement of your body in the current. The frogs, not at all, because the frogs have not yet evolved the concept of a thing that is not water. To be considered, and then to be returned to the long damp dreaming of the place, the way a forest returns to its own silence after a sound has passed through it. The Sump does not want anything from you. The Sump has wanted nothing for a long time. It has only the hum, and the drip, and the small wet lives that move through it in their cycles, and the slow green return of the iron to the stone, and the slow pale return of the stone to the water, and the water to the rock, and the rock to the silence that is underneath everything, that holds everything up, that has been here longer than any of it and will be here after.
