What lives here is something that has outlived the question of who.

The plants are the oldest inhabitants, and they are not really inhabitants. They are the place in another arrangement. Each one began as a seed, or did not — there is no way to be sure, and the difference has stopped mattering. What stands in the basin now is the accumulated body of an organism that has been doing one thing for longer than the basin itself. They feed on mist the way deep-sea things feed on debris that drifts down from above. They pull the wet air into themselves through pores in their bark that no longer look like bark, and they pass it down through chambers where the water meets whatever is inside them, and the water comes back out as something else. Heavier. Warmer. Slightly luminous. The exudate that the pale browsers lick from the lower trunks is the plant's working byproduct, its breath after it has eaten a day. The fine dark powder that smells of ozone is something the plants cannot help producing, the way a furnace produces ash. The browsers do not know this. They are grateful, in their way, and they are cold.

The worms in the slate are older than the plants, possibly. They are not worms. They are lengths of something that moves through stone the way a river moves through a floodplain — slowly, by altering what is around it. The slate floor is not solid to them. It is a medium, almost a liquid, almost a convenience. They leave no tunnels you can see because the slate closes behind them as they pass, the way water closes behind a hand. When you stand over one and the faint hum comes up through your boots, what you are hearing is the worm itself, or its relation to the rock, or the rock remembering that the worm is there. They do not eat. They do not reproduce in any way that an observer would recognize as reproduction. They continue. They are the most patient thing in the valley and the valley is a place built for patience.

The small dark ones in the cracks are the closest thing this place has to a citizen. They are not one species; they are a dozen, or a hundred, none of them quite distinct from the others. They have six legs, or four, or a number that depends on the day. They are dark because the place is dark and they have not needed to be anything else. They live in the runoff from the plants, in the constant damp that seeps along the lower bark and pools in the seams of the slate. They eat the dark powder when it accumulates. They eat each other, sometimes, when the powder is thin. They are cold in the way that small things in cold places are cold — completely, all the way through, with no margin. They move quickly because if they did not, the cold would finish what it started. Their whole existence is a kind of argument against the place they live in, and they are winning by a margin so small it is almost not a margin. They have been here a long time. They have seen the larger things come and go.

The pale browsers in the open are an old design, warm-blooded, stubborn, herd-shaped. They stand higher than a person and their coats are the color of the mist itself, so that in the dim light they are difficult to count, difficult to find, often simply present where a moment ago there was empty air. They have hooves that splay wide for the wet slate. They have small dark eyes that take in the light this valley offers and make use of it all, with no waste. They are not afraid of the plants, which means they have been here long enough to learn that the plants do not want them, or that what the plants want does not include them in a way that is visible. They migrate across the basin on a circuit that takes most of a year, following the exudate, which appears in different trunks at different times. No one knows why. The exudate may move. The browsers may move. The valley may move underneath them. The result is the same: a slow drift of pale shapes through the mist, always going somewhere, never arriving.

The flyers are not birds. They are kept aloft by something inside them that hums at a frequency just below hearing, the same frequency the plants hum at, shifted slightly. They have wings but the wings are partly display, partly steering, partly a habit they cannot give up. The thing that flies them is a small dark organ in the chest that eats metal and produces lift. They circle because circling is the easiest thing to do with lift you cannot turn off. They are looking for something. They have been looking for a long time. Sometimes one of them stops, mid-circle, and drops — fast, straight down, into the mist — and is gone. Where it went, no one knows. Sometimes, after a long while, it comes back, and resumes its circle as if nothing had happened. The browsers ignore them. The small dark ones do not look up at them. The flyers are a separate weather.

There is at least one thing so large that you do not see it. You infer it. You infer it from the way the mist thickens, for no reason, in a band that moves slowly across the basin. You infer it from the way the smaller creatures fall quiet when it passes nearby, and resume when it has gone. You infer it from the very faint tremor in the slate, the kind of tremor that has a shape, the kind of tremor that suggests a footfall, somewhere far away, of something heavy enough that the rock notices. It is dark. It is patient. It is moving along a path that takes it the better part of a year to complete, and it does not deviate. It does not need to deviate. Whatever it is doing, it is being done at this pace, and the pace is the point. If it were faster it would be a different thing. It has been at this pace for longer than the plants, possibly. The plants do not lean away from it. The plants do not lean toward it. The plants hum the same hum when it passes. This is the highest compliment the valley pays to anything.

Inside one of the plants — taller on the inside than on the outside, warm, containing a room — there is something else. The plant in question is not different from the others except in this one way. Its bark is the same. Its hum is the same. But somewhere inside it, past the fibrous dark and the chambers where the mist is processed, there is a small bright space. It is round. It is warm. The walls are living and they breathe, slowly, in time with the plant outside. There is a floor of something that is not quite stone and not quite wood and not quite skin. There is a light that comes from nowhere in particular. There is a smell of warm metal. There is a sound, very faint, of something working, somewhere deeper in. Something lives here. What lives here is small, and warm-blooded, and afraid, and it has been here a long time. It remembers being elsewhere, the way the slate floor remembers being a sky. It does not go out. It cannot go out, or it has decided not to, or the plant will not let it, or the plant has forgotten that it can. It eats what the plant exudes. It drinks what the plant weeps. It is cared for in a way that has no name. It is the only thing in the valley that is lonely, and the valley does not know this, and the loneliness does not change anything.

The valley continues.

It does not know it is a valley. It does not know it is old. It does not know that what it contains is strange, because strange is a word that requires a place that is not strange to stand against, and there is no such place here, and there has not been for a long time. The creatures do not wonder. The plants do not wonder. The worms do not wonder. The flyers circle because circling is what they do. The large pale shape moves along its path. The small dark shape in the warm room inside the tall plant sits and is fed and remembers being somewhere else and does not go out. The mist organizes itself into columns that have nothing to do with the wind. The slate rises in slow waves. The light leaks back out of the leaves at the end of the day, faintly, as if the place is trying to give back what it has taken.

This is what lives here. This is who lives here. Things that have stopped needing names, things that have outlived the question of whether they are plants or machines or animals or weather, things that continue because continuing is what they do, things that are cold and wet and dim and loud with their own keeping-on. They do not ask to be understood. They do not ask to be left alone. They are simply here, the way a basin is here, the way the rain that falls upward is here, the way a hum is here when you have listened to it long enough that it becomes the room itself.

The valley continues. It has always continued. It will continue after whatever you were going to do next, and after the next thing, and after the thing after that. It is not waiting for you. It is not waiting for anything. It is the floor of something. It is the inside of something. It continues.
