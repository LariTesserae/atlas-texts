The people of the town did not build the town. This is the first thing to understand about them, and the second, and in some ways the most important. They came, two or three centuries ago, from downslope — from another place with a fault, because by the third day of a stranger's visit they will ask, in their unvoweled language, which side of the fault you sleep on at home. They came up the scree and found the spring line already in use. The cisterns were carved. The stepping stones were set. The walls were fitted so closely that mortar would have been an impertinence. They settled in, the way moss settles into a crack: by being willing to be small.

There are perhaps two thousand of them, when the herders have come down for the cold months. They are short, broad, dark-skinned, and very competent. They are not numerous. They are not rich. They are made, by long use, for this place: their lungs are wide, their hands are quick, their eyes are good in low sideways light. They know every spring, every leak, every slow bulge in every wall. They know which stones are safe to walk on and which have shifted an inch since yesterday. They know, without being told, when the Hollowspring is going to pulse. They can tell, by the sound the Coldwell makes before dawn, what the weather will do. They do not speak of this knowledge. They use it the way they use their hands — without remark.

Their language is mostly consonants. It sounds, to a stranger, like two flints being struck, or like the noise the stone brackets make in a hard frost. They speak it fast and they do not raise their voices. The vowels seem to be saved — they seem to be rationed — for the names of things that are not theirs: the three springs, which they still call by the names of the people who carved the basins and shaped the dome-stone; the bell in the cracked building near the upper gate, which they have never named; the mountain itself, which has no name in their language, perhaps because they have not yet finished thinking about it.

They dye wool. The peat-house by the Coldwell is the only building in the town that is not stone — it is built of black fibrous blocks cut from the high bogs and hauled down on sledges in the brief dry weeks of late summer — and it smells of tannin and iron. In winter the smoke comes out of it in a low thick column that the wind cannot quite disperse. In summer, when the dyes are not working, the peat-house stands empty and dark and sweating, and the herders say that it dreams, though they will not say of what. The dyed wool is taken, on foot, over the lower pass, and brought back as grain and salt. The same people, mostly, do both journeys. They are very strong in the legs.

They do not smile at strangers. This is not unfriendliness; it is the caution of a small community in a hard place, and it passes. By the second day someone will offer you a bowl of something hot and bitter. By the third, you are being asked which side of the fault you sleep on. They want to know if you are from a place like this. They are looking, without saying so, for kin.

The children of the town are the loudest thing in it. They are everywhere. They climb the walls and the roofs and the cliffs above the Coldwell with a confidence that makes the herders cross themselves when they see it. In summer they have a game: they try to stand in the dome of the Hollowspring while it pulses, and they are thrown, every time, into the flagstones of the market square, and they laugh, and they go back and try again. The herders scold them for this and the dyers pretend not to watch. The children are the only people in the town who are not yet worried about the crack. They say the mountain is talking to itself, and the herders do not contradict them, and no one explains why. There is, in the way the adults defer to the children on this point, something like an admission that the children may be the closer kin of the place.

The town is not alone on the mountain. In the cold months, when the snow is up to the door lintels, the white grazers come down from the high scree and stand, in rows, in the spine-water, staring at the houses with their two sets of horns — one above the other, like a thought and its echo. They are the size of small horses. Their coats are pale and very thick. They are not afraid of the people, and the people are not afraid of them. There is an agreement that no one has written down, that the grazers do not enter the lanes and the people do not hunt them. In the worst winters, when the wind has been up for days and the snow is packed against the doors, the grazers come further in. They stand in the doorways, breathing, enormous and patient, and they are not turned away. No one explains to a child why this is permitted. The children grow up understanding that the grazers are part of the town the way the moss is part of the wall.

Once a season, in summer, a splitwing flies low over the town.

The splitwings nest on the cliffs to the north. They are large, dark, silent birds with feathers that are not quite feathers — overlapping plates of thin bone, the herders say, though no one has held one. They hunt the high meadows, where a small, almost legless rodent lives under the snow. They do not cry. The herders will tell you, if you press them, that the splitwings have no mouths. This is not true; they have long pale beaks, and they open them only to eat. When a splitwing passes over the town, low, slow, the bone-plates of its wings making a sound like a hand drawn over a comb, everyone stops. The children are quiet. The wool-press in the dye-house, if it is working, stops. The bird goes away, and the town exhales, and no one says what the exhale was for.

In the Coldwell, if you sit long enough on the basin's edge, you begin to see the eel-things. They are not eels. They are long and pale, and they move in the water with a slow deliberate undulation, and at the head end they have a cluster of fine white filaments like the roots of a plant. No one is sure what they feed on — the minerals in the water, the light, something else.
