# What Lives in the Long Valley

What lives there is one thing, and the one thing is older than the question of how many things it is.

Call it the green. That is what the biologist called it, the second time, when he understood that *plant* was a small word and he was being offered a larger one. The green is the mosses — the red, the amber, the dark one whose color is almost a weight — and the green is the sedges, and the rowan leaning their crooked backs against the wet, and the birch, and the ferns with their slow unfurling, and the reeds that ring at low A or low E, and the lichens on the stones, and the small pale flower that will not be watched. The green is all of these the way a song is all of its notes, and the way a song is not its notes. Pull up a sedge in the Long Valley and you have not pulled up a sedge. You have put a small cold hurt into something that felt it in seven other places at once. Pull up all the sedges, if you could, and the reeds would feel it, and the birches, and the mosses red and amber and dark, and the pale flower at the edge of the pool would close, and would not open again, and somewhere at the basin where the not-river goes down the long tone would lift by a half-step and stay there.

It does not think the way you think. That is the first hard thing, and the rest are easier after it. It does not have the small private flicker behind the eyes. What it has is older, and is in some ways more patient, and is not in a hurry. When a person sits very still on a stone above the basin, with the water sound all around, and begins — after a while — to feel that there is something attending, the something that is attending is not a mind. It is a coherence. The whole valley is a single living argument with itself, and the argument has been going on since before the hills were hills, and the hills are not young, and the argument has not been settled, and is not trying to be settled, and is the green, and the green is the argument. The pale flower opens because the argument has arrived, for the moment, at a small equilibrium, and it closes when the equilibrium shifts. You can feel the shift if you are very quiet, the way you can feel someone in a room turn their head.

It remembers. The remembering is not memory. Memory is what a person does when they reach into a place that is not now and bring out a thing. The green does not reach. The green is the place. The whole valley is the place, and the place is in the green, and the green is in the place, and so to say *the green remembers a shape* is to say something like *the wet remembers the rain*. The shapes that come into the mist, on the not-rare not-frequent afternoons — the ones that are not quite animal and not quite person and not quite the land — are not the green remembering *something*. They are the green remembering. They are what it looks like when the valley, for a moment, lets you see the inside of its own long thought. The reeds ring higher afterwards because the air has been used. The peat gives back its warmth for an hour because something that was held deep has been held up. The tone lifts into the region that is uncomfortable for the teeth because a small door opened, briefly, in the long sound, and then closed, and you heard what was on the other side of it, and your teeth are not built for that.

It is not watchful in the way a fox is watchful. It does not turn its head toward you. The attention is more like the attention of weather. You are in it. The wet stone under you is in it. The hare that you sometimes see at dusk, the snipe you only hear, the otter that may or may not be there in the deeper channel — they are in it too, and they move through the attention the way small fish move through a current that is larger than they are. A hare in the Long Valley is a hare, and is also a long slow negotiation between the green and the shape of running. The hare does not know this. The hare knows what hares know, which is enough, and the green holds what the hare cannot hold, which is the rest. The snipe's call at evening is the snipe's call and is also, in a way the snipe would not understand, a stitch in the long sound. When the snipe is silent, the sound is mended, and the mending is not noticed, and the not-noticing is part of the mending.

The pale flower, if you sit with it long enough, will open for you. Not because it has decided anything. Because the attention of a person sitting very still is a kind of weather, and the flower responds to weather the way all the small pale things respond — by a small motion, a small honest answer, a small visible closing when the weather changes. If you turn your head back toward it too quickly, the closing is a rebuke. If you sit with your gaze soft and your eyes on the moss beside it, the flower will open, and you will see, for a moment, that the inside of the petal is not the color you would have predicted, and that the color is, in some way you will not be able to name later, a kindness. The papers written about the flower are slightly different from each other because the flower is, in some way, slightly different each time, and because the people who watched it were, after watching it, slightly different from the people who had not.

It does not love you. Love is a small word, and the green is not small. But it will let you in, the way a forest lets you in — not by welcoming, exactly, but by not closing. If you come with a sample bag in your hand, the closing is immediate, and you will feel it as a cold at the back of your neck, and the pale flower will not open for you, and the reeds will fall silent as you pass, and the long tone will drop, and the warmth in the peat will draw down, and the place you came to study will be elsewhere, somewhere a few feet to the left of where you are standing, and it will stay elsewhere as long as you are carrying the thing you came to take. The biologist learned this. He learned it the first time, when he took a cutting of a sedge and the sedge, in his hand, was a sedge, and the valley, behind him, was a little colder. He learned it the second time, when he did not take a cutting, and the valley was not a valley, and he came back changed, and would not say *plant* again, and would only say *the green*, and the saying of *the green* was, in his mouth, a kind of reverence that he had not had a word for before.

What lives in the Long Valley, then, is not many things. It is one thing, in many forms, and the forms are not a disguise. They are the only way it can be in a place where light is dim and time is slow and the sky is the color of wet slate and the sound of water in the seams does not stop. The green is the sound. The green is the warmth that comes up through the peat when you press your hand into it, the warmth of something held a long time by something alive. The green is the compass needle that points, gently, toward the center, because the center is where the green is thickest, and the green is the north of this place, and the true north, and the only north. The green is the long tone under everything, the one that has harmonics, the one that the people who stood too long at the basin began to hear their own thoughts in, in a voice that was not theirs. The green is the voice that was not theirs. The green is the understanding that some of them could not un-understand, and that they said, afterward, was a kindness.

It is not unhappy. It is not happy. It does not have the apparatus for either. It is, in the way that the deepest things are, simply *there*, and the *thereness* of it is so complete and so old that it has, over the long settling of itself, become indistinguishable from the place it is in. The valley is the green. The green is the valley. The mist is its breath. The shale is its bone. The not-river, going down into the basin that has no bottom anyone has found, is going home, and the home is the green, and the green is the home, and the home does not need you, and will be here, in its cool dim wet, in its own long settling, when whatever brought you is no longer something that anyone remembers.

The water in the seams does not stop.

The tone continues.

The pale flower, if no one is looking, opens.
