This drowned country has its people. They are not numerous, but they are tenacious, and they have been conducting their business here for longer than any of them can remember. To stand in the Garden is to be a guest in a slow commonwealth, and the commonwealth does not perform for guests. It simply continues, the way a heart continues, and you must join it where it is.

What lives here is alive in a tense that the words you have do not quite cover.

---

**The light-bearers, or the tenders.**

They are the smallest of the visible inhabitants, and the most active, though "active" is a word that must be stretched almost to breaking to fit them. Watch the trunk of any bone-column. Wait. After a time — an hour, a day, you cannot say which — a faint glow will resolve against the brown bark. A pale blue-green, the color of a cold ember, the color of a thought you are trying to remember. It is moving. It has been moving for some while; you simply did not see it.

It is roughly the size of your spread hand, though its outline is hard to fix. It has a soft body underneath the light — pale, almost translucent, with the glow diffused through it the way weak tea is diffused through glass. It has no legs. It has no eyes. It moves by laying the underside of its body against the bark and pulling itself upward with the slow muscular patience of a tongue. Its leading edge is slightly brighter, as if a head were simply a place where the light gathered.

It is bringing light to the flowers.

The flowers of this place cannot catch the sun — the surface is too far, the water too thick, the patience of the sky too intermittent. They have given up on photons from above the way a person in a deep cellar gives up on windows. They have made other arrangements. They have entered into a quiet contract with the tenders: a packet of light, delivered to the chalice of the bloom, in exchange for something the flower offers — a chemical warmth, a faint sweetness that drifts in the water, some receipt of gratitude that neither party speaks aloud.

The tender ascends. Its glow dims slightly with each length of trunk it covers, because light is a thing that must be spent — to climb, to think (if it thinks), to be a body that glows. When it arrives at last at a flower, the flower opens. The chalice receives the courier. There is a small pulse of brightness — a kind of *thank you* that is also a *received* — and the tender, now paler, sets off across the dim water toward the next column, where another flower is waiting.

It will fade. It will rest somewhere — in the moss, in a crevice of a column, in the soft floor — and slowly, over days or weeks, gather brightness again. Bioluminescence is its metabolism. It eats light the way a cow eats grass, the way a tree eats sun. It hoards what it can.

They are solitary. They do not swarm or aggregate or form any society you would recognize. Each one is conducting a private postal service, with the letter being a packet of glow and the recipient being a flower that will not open for anyone else.

---

**The pale wanderers.**

Rarer still. You will see one — if you are lucky, or unlucky, depending on how you feel about being noticed by something that has no mechanism for noticing — slide between two of the bone-trees, perhaps a hundred feet away. It is the color of milk-glass, of old paper, of the underside of a mushroom that has been left in a damp room for years. It is long. It is as long as a city bus, perhaps, or as long as a small house, but its thickness is modest — the thickness of a man's thigh, or of a rolled carpet standing on its end. It tapers at both ends. You cannot tell which end is the front, because it has no head that you would recognize. It has no eyes. It has no mouth you can see. It is just a long pale smooth body, moving through the warm water with a slow undulation that barely qualifies as motion.

It moves from one silence to another. The place is made of silences, and the wanderer moves among them the way you might move among rooms in a house you have lived in for a long time. It has a destination, but the destination is private. It does not greet. It does not pause to graze on the moss in any way you would call grazing. It slides. It is going.

What it does, no one can tell you. Its underside may brush the moss and take in some slow nutrient. It may be one of the things that processes the falling debris — the spent flowers, the dimmed tenders, the faint rain of organic matter that sifts down from above. It may eat only water. It may eat nothing, sustained by something more patient than food. It is in no conversation with the others of its kind. It is not in conversation with anything. Each wanderer is a loner with a long body and a closed face, and they cross paths only by accident.

You will not see it again. It has moved on. It is somewhere else, in another part of the forest, pursuing the errand you cannot articulate.

---

**The large things.**

Once, you will feel one.

You will be standing among the columns, or drifting in a clearing, and the water will shift. Not much. A slow pressure against your body, the kind of pressure that says *something has displaced me, and it was bigger than I am*. You will look. You will see, at the very edge of seeing, a darker patch in the dim — not a shape, exactly, but a region where the dim is dimmer. A shadow inside the shadow. It will be moving. It will be moving slowly, but slowly here means something different from the wanderers' slow; it means a slow that takes a long time to cross even a clearing.

It will be gone before you have formed the question of what it was.

You will not know whether it was animal or current or passing cloud of darker sediment. You will not know whether it had a face. It will not have looked at you. It will not have known you were there. It was on its own errand, and the errand was larger than yours.

These come and go. There is no schedule. You can only stand and wait for the water to shift, and feel the displacement, and wonder.

---

**The ferns, the moss, the lichen.**

These are the ground of the place.

The ferns grow from the columns and from the floor in vast fronds, each one the size of a small room, each one unfurling in a slow spiral that may take a generation to complete one turn. They are not green the way you remember green. They are the green of deep wells, of moss that has been everywhere for a long time, of lichen on gravestones that have been there since before you were born. They are soft. They ripple in the slow currents. They smell, faintly, of *being* — of having been here for a long time, of being on the way to becoming stone.

The moss is everywhere. It grows over the columns. It grows over the floor. It grows over the half-buried wall and the steps that disappear into the column and the ring of stones that may be a foundation or a well. It is spongy. It is the texture of velvet that has been wet for years. It is thick, in places, in feet. If you put your hand into it, it gives. It accepts your hand and holds it for a moment and lets it go.

The moss is not silent. If you put your ear close — and here you must put your ear close, because nothing in this place speaks at any volume that travels — you can hear it. A faint hydraulic whisper. A slow digestion. The moss is the great recycler of the drowned forest. It takes the slow falling debris and the spent flowers and the dimmed tenders and it incorporates them. It is patient work. It has been going on longer than the columns have been columns.

The lichen is older still. It grows on the stones of the wall, on the floor of the ring, on the surfaces that have been here longest. It is grey-green, almost silver. It is dry-looking, although nothing here is dry. It is crusty. If you touch it, it flakes slightly, like the skin of something that has been in the bath too long. It is a thing that has nearly finished being alive. It is on its way to becoming the stone it sits on.

---

**The flowers.**

These are the destinations.

They grow on the columns, often near the crown, often where two columns have grown together and made an arch. They are pale — the color of milk, of bone, of wedding dresses that have been in storage for a century. They are heavy in the water; they do not bob, they sink slightly into their own weight, as if gravity still mattered here even though they are underwater.

They open and close on a rhythm no insect has ever set. It is their own rhythm. Perhaps it is the rhythm of a tender's arrival. Perhaps it is the rhythm of a pressure that travels through the water. Perhaps it is a slow chemical accumulation inside the chalice that finally demands release. You cannot tell. You can only stand near one and watch it open, slowly, petal by petal, and watch it close, slowly, petal by petal, with no hurry about either.

They smell of nothing. They smell of *old*. Underneath the nothing and the old, there is something faint and sweet — a memory of a smell, perhaps, the smell of a flower that once grew in the sun and has now been carried down here in spirit only.

They receive the tenders. They receive the slow debris. They receive time. They bloom. They go to seed — the seeds drift down through the warm water and lodge somewhere in the moss, and there, slowly, a new fern begins, or a new flower.

---

**The very small things.**

If you could shrink yourself — and the place has a way of making you want to — you would find that the moss is a city.

There are mites in it. There are slow worms in it, white and almost transparent, that pulse with their own hydraulic systems. There are larvae in the cracks of the half-buried wall, in the crevices where the steps meet the column, in the seams where the floor meets itself. They are too small for you to see at your standing scale. But they are there. They are doing their work. They are the next layer of the slow digestion — the layer that breaks things down before the moss does, or alongside the moss, or in concert with it. They eat. They are eaten. They process. They are processed.

They do not know about the large things. They do not know about the tenders. They are too small to perceive anything except the moss, the immediate moss, the moss they are sitting in. They have their own concerns. They have their own private economies. They are, in their way, the busiest things in the Garden — though even their busy is a slow pulse, a slow chewing, a slow being.

---

**The columns themselves.**

Are they alive? The question is reasonable. They have grown together at the crown. They have fused into arches, into vaults, into the suggestion of architecture that no one designed. They have spread roots into the soft floor, if there are still roots. They are warm to the touch, the warmth of skin, the warmth of a hand held for a long time.

They grow, a millimeter a century.

This is too slow to witness. This is the speed of stone. But it is growth. They are adding to themselves, layer by patient layer, the way a coral adds to itself, the way a stalactite adds to itself, the way a wound adds to itself when it heals. They are alive in a tense that humans do not have a word for. They are alive the way a cathedral is alive — present, ongoing, accumulating, never finished, indifferent to whether anyone is watching.

They are the bones of the drowned forest. They are the substrate on which everything else lives. They are the oldest things in the place, older than the moss, older than the lichen, possibly older than the water. If you put your hand against one, it will be warm. The column does not respond. It does not need to.

It has been here longer than your hand. It will be here longer than your hand.

---

And so the Garden has its commonwealth. The tenders carry light to the flowers. The flowers bloom and seed and become moss. The moss digests the spent flowers and the dimmed tenders. The wanderers slide through, taking some slow part in the cycle, or none. The large things pass through, indifferent. The columns grow. The lichen settles. The very small things chew their small chewing in the moss.

No one here is in a hurry. No one here remembers the word for *improve*. Each thing is conducting its private errand through a country that no longer requires errands, and the country holds them all, warm and patient, in its long bath.

You are standing in it. You are breathing it. You are one more strange shape in a country of strange shapes. You are being slowly, gently, very patiently, made part of the community.

Not today. Not for many todays. But the place has time.

The place has nothing but time, and warm water, and its slow, slow people.
