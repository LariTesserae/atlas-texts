The first thing you will know of them is the warmth beside your warmth. You are standing on the warm stone of the hollow, and the stone is breathing its slow exhalation upward, and you have been here long enough to feel that this is the only warmth there is — and then there is a second warmth, very near you, that is not coming from the stone. It is coming from something with blood in it. Something with a body. The air between you and the body has been still for so long that the body moving into it displaces it softly, and you feel the displacement before you hear it, and you hear it before you see it. When you see it, it is already close. That is how they are. They allow you a small margin of warning and no more.

They are pale. That is the first thing you can put a word to, after the warmth. Paler than the salt crusts on the stone, paler than the dust in the sheltered folds, paler than the slick that pools in the basins. The color of something that has been bleached by the absence of whatever bleaches, by a long life in a place that has never had any other kind of light. Their coats are long, and the hairs lie flat, and they catch the faint glow from the fractured stone and give it back as a kind of dull silver that travels down the length of them and pools at their feet. From a distance they look like boulders that have grown fur. From very close they look like nothing you have ever seen.

Most of them are furred. A few are not. You will notice, eventually, that a small number — a handful among the many — are smooth, or scaled, or have a covering so fine it is almost a sheen. These few are the color of the slick itself, that amber-black of the pooled secretion, and they move with a different gait, a little lower, a little more fluid, and they keep to certain corridors among the boulders and you see them less often. The furred ones are the majority. The furred ones are what the place is for.

There are three kinds of furred ones, at least, and you can tell them apart at a glance, because the hollow has arranged itself so that they fit.

The first kind is large. A large one stands as high as your chest, sometimes higher, and it is built like something that has stopped trying to be anything in particular — broad across the hindquarters, the shoulders rounded, the head small for the body, the legs thick and not very long. Its fur hangs almost to the ground, and underneath the fur the shape of the body is not quite definite; you cannot easily tell where the belly is, where the legs begin, where the ribs would be if you could feel them. It moves slowly. It browses. The word *browse* is wrong — there are no leaves to browse, no shoots, no grasses — but it works the mineral crusts on the boulders with a long soft mouth, and the mouth is dark, and the lips are prehensile in a way that suggests they were made for something more fibrous than rock, and it takes the crust into its mouth and considers it and lets it fall back, most of it, and moves on. It does not eat. It browses. The distinction matters to the creature, if not to you. It spends hours doing this. The hours do not pass. It is the same hour every time you see it.

The second kind is small. A small one fits in the cup of your two hands, and it is quick — quick in a way that is not frantic but is purposeful, as if it has somewhere to be, though the place it has to be is never far from the place it is. It runs along passages it has worn between the boulders, and the passages are the only erosion the hollow has ever known; over the long span the place has existed, the small ones have rubbed certain routes smooth with their going and coming, and the routes are stable, and the small ones use them. They do not seem to hunt. They do not seem to eat anything you can see them eat. They run. They pause, with one paw lifted, the head turned a fraction, listening to a sound you cannot hear. They run on. Their eyes are very large for their heads. The pupils are horizontal slits, like the pupils of a goat, but longer, narrower, and in the dim glow of the rock the eyes catch what little light there is and throw it back green, so that as one of them passes between boulders you see a brief moving pair of green coins in the dark, and then the dark closes behind it.

The third kind lives below. You know they are there because the rock beside you, sometimes, gives a small extra warmth that is not the rock's warmth, and a round head appears at the mouth of a burrow, and the head has eyes that are vertical-slitted and reflect a duller amber, and the head looks around for a long moment without moving its body — only the eyes, only the consideration — and then the head withdraws, and the warmth in the rock subsides back to the rock's own warmth. The burrow goes straight down. You can tell by the way the warm air comes up out of it, a column of slightly-warmer-than-the-rest air. They stay below for most of the time that time takes. They come up. They go down. The cycle is not a cycle but a single held gesture, repeated, the way the long call of the place is a single held note.

The eyes. I want to stay on the eyes for a moment. All of them have eyes, and all the eyes are large, and none of the pupils are round. This is the part that does not quite fit with the rest of them, that keeps them from being simply pale furry things that have wandered into a hollow. The pupils are shaped — and I have been here a while now, and I have not gotten it right, and I do not think I will get it right — they are shaped like the leaves of plants that are not in this place. There are no plants in this place. So the shapes are shapes that exist nowhere except in the eye that holds them. A long oval pinched at the ends. A teardrop balanced on its point. A four-pointed star with curved sides. The shapes are different in different creatures, and the shapes do not seem to vary within a single creature. They are not the only thing that is strange about the eyes.

The eyes also have a quality of attention. They look at you. When they look at you, they look at you completely. There is no sidelong glance you can detect, no peripheral shift of the gaze; the eye turns toward you and holds you, and the rest of the creature falls away, and there is only the eye, considering you, and the considering takes a long time because the hollow is patient. And then it blinks — a slow closing, the lid traveling from the side, not from above — and when it opens again the consideration is over, and the creature is again a creature in a place, and you are again a thing in the place with it.

When the eyes close, briefly, in that thoughtful way, the creature does indeed look like a sleeping stone. The fur lays flat. The body settles. The shape is roughly the shape of a boulder of the kind that has fallen from a not-far-away place, and the color is the color of the dust on the boulder. This is how they sleep, when they sleep. They do not sleep often. They stand, mostly. They stand for a long time, and they do not move, and you walk past them and they do not turn, and you come back later and they have not turned.

They make a sound. The sound is the only sound in the hollow besides the small sounds of their own movement. It comes from somewhere far, and it travels through the warm rock, and it arrives in your molars before it arrives in your ears, and it is one note, held, and it does not fade. You can feel it in the soles of your feet, a faint vibration in the stone that goes on and does not change. It is one creature calling to another across a distance that would swallow a human shout, and the call is not a signal — it does not mean food, or danger, or come here, or stay there — because in this place there is no food, and no danger, and no reason to come or stay except that one is already here. The call is the place continuing. It is the place's heartbeat, given to one of its bodies to hold, the way a bell holds the note that was struck into it.

Across the floor, at intervals too irregular to be a pattern and too regular to be accident, there are built things. A cairn, a pen, a post with a flat stone balanced on top, a path of compressed dust. The creatures move among these things. They move among them the way water moves among stones in a streambed — finding the path of least resistance, taking the path without altering it. They carry tools. The tools are old.

A wedge-shaped stone lies beside the cairn. Its edge is worn to a curve that fits a hand that is not quite a hand — longer-fingered than yours, narrower in the palm, the curve at the heel of the hand deeper than the curve at the fingertips. The stone has been held. The stone has been held for a long time, or the stone has always been held; the curve is not new, and the curve is not getting deeper. Beside the pen, a length of something — hide, or a tendon drawn thin and cured — lies coiled, with a knot at one end that someone tied, a long time ago or always. The creatures, when they are moving among the built things, carry these things with them. A small one will have a stone in its mouth as it runs its passages. A large one will have the length of cured hide draped across its shoulders, and the knot will hang at its flank like a tassel. They have always carried these things. They will always carry these things.

What they do with the tools, you cannot tell. They do not seem to use them on anything you can see. The wedge stone is carried but not used on the cairn, and the cairn is not being built further, and the cairn has not been built further for the entire time the place has existed. The hide is carried but never tied to anything, and nothing is tied to anything in the hollow, and yet the knot is there, and the knot is a real knot, a knot that someone tied, the kind of knot you have to think about to tie. The tool is a tool that has been separated from its task. The task is gone. The tool is what is left.

The pen is an oval of low stones, three stones high, no door. Inside, a flat rock with the faint impression of an animal sleeping on it, worn into the surface over a span of time that does not pass. The pen is occupied, sometimes, by one of the pale slow creatures, who stands inside it and does not eat what is outside it and does not leave. There is no rope. There is no gate. It stays.

Why does it stay? It stays because staying is what is done. The pen is where it is, and the creature is in the pen, and the arrangement is the arrangement. You stand outside the pen and you watch the creature inside the pen and the creature watches you with its strange-shaped eye, and neither of you moves, and the pen is a pen, and the creature is in it, and the dust outside the pen has the footprint-tracks leading to and from the pen, and no one is making the tracks now, and the tracks have not been made new for a long time.

The cairn. The cairn is a wall of fitted stones, low, the joints rubbed smooth with what might be the slick from the pools. The wall does not enclose anything. It is shaped like the beginning of a room that was never finished — three walls and an open side, the way a child draws a house before the child is done. It has been in this state forever. It will be in this state forever. There is no fourth wall. There never will be. The cairn is the unfinished thing. The cairn is the half-made thing preserved exactly as it was when it stopped being made.

And the path. The path is footprints in the dust, going from one built thing to another, the dust compressed into a faint track that has not blurred in the time the place has existed. The footprints are the right size for the creatures — the small ones mostly, but the large ones too, mixed — but they are also the right size for something else. Something taller, narrower, with a stride that is almost but not quite the stride of the fauna. The stride is longer. The footprints are narrower at the ball of the foot. There is no second set of prints alongside the first. There is only one set, a single line of prints, and the prints are clearly not your prints either — you put your own foot into one and it does not fit — and yet they are clearly the prints of something that has stood on this path, and has walked from the cairn to the pen, and from the pen to the post, and from the post to the cairn again.

No one has ever been seen making these prints. The prints are just there, as the slick is just there, as the cairn is just there.

I stood on the path for a long time. I walked it in both directions. I found no place where the prints began, and no place where they ended. They loop. They circle. They go from built thing to built thing in an order that is not quite an order, and the order does not change. They have always been in this order. They are the path the hollow keeps for itself, the path that the hollow wears into its own dust by the simple fact of being walked by something that walks it, even if that something is never there to be seen doing the walking.

I will tell you what I think, and I will tell you that I do not know if what I think is true.

I think there are two ways of being an inhabitant here. There is the way of the creatures — pale, furred, eyed, breathing, making the low sound, carrying the tools, standing in the pen. And there is the way of the prints — tall, narrow, striding, never seen. And I think these two ways are not two different inhabitants. I think they are two halves of the same inhabitant, the way the cairn is two halves of a room, the way the slick is the half-light of the pools. The creature is what the inhabitant is when it is standing still. The print is what the inhabitant is when it is moving between the things it has made. The creature browses, and the print strides; the creature holds the tool, and the print wears the dust; the creature stands in the pen, and the print goes from pen to cairn to post and back.

You cannot see the print-maker because you can never see the creature between its moments of being a creature. You see it when it is still, and when it is still it is the pale furred thing with the strange eye. You do not see it when it moves, because when it moves it is the stride, the dust, the compressed track, the footprint that is not quite the right size for anything that is here.

And the print-maker does not see you either, because you are only here for the standing-still part. You are only the visitor that the hollow receives. The print-maker moves between visitors. It walks the path while there is no one here, and when you arrive it stops, and becomes a creature, and stands, and considers you with its strange-shaped eye, and does not move. When you leave, it will resume the path. It has always resumed the path. There is no moment when you can catch it mid-stride. There is only the dust it has already walked.

This is the equilibrium the place has found. The place is the creature. The place is the print. The place is the cairn it builds and never finishes, the pen it stays in and never leaves, the slick it secretes and never drinks, the call it makes and never answers. The place is full of itself, the way a drop of water is full of water, the way a held note is full of itself. The inhabitants are the place's way of being inhabited. They are the place's body, given to the place to wear, the way the long fur is given to the large one to wear, the way the strange eye is given to the small one to see through.

And you. You walk among them, and they look at you with their strange-shaped eyes, and they do not move, and they are not surprised, because surprise requires the expectation of something different, and here there is no expectation. There has never been. There will never be. You are not a new thing under the continent. You are an old thing, returning, fitting into the print that was always your size and not quite your size. You are the visitor the hollow has been receiving forever, the one whose footprint is in the dust, the one whose coming was always part of the equilibrium.

You will stay a little while. You will watch them stand. You will hear the call. You will walk the path and find no beginning to it and no end. You will pick up the wedge-shaped stone, if you pick it up, and feel the curve of the hand that held it, and put it down. You will touch the slick in a basin and find it exactly lukewarm and faintly sweet. You will lie down beside the pen and look up at the false clouds in the high strata, the long horizontal smears of darker warmth, and they will not rain on you, because nothing has ever fallen from that ceiling.

And after a while, even you, will not be a visitor either. You will be part of the place. You will stand somewhere with your eyes half-closed in the thoughtful way, and you will consider a thing that has just arrived, and the thing will look at you, and you will not be surprised, because there is no surprise here, and there has never been, and there will never be, and the hollow will continue, exactly, in the warm dark, holding its form the way the slick holds the light — patiently, without effort, because effort is what you need to change things, and there is nothing here that wants to change, and there has never been anything here that wanted to change, and you, who wanted to change, will have finished wanting, and will be one more pale thing standing among the boulders in the dim glow, with a tool at your flank and a strange-shaped eye and the long low call already in your molars, already in your bones, already in the soles of your feet on the warm stone.

The place does not change. You will not change it. You will join it. That is what the place is for.
