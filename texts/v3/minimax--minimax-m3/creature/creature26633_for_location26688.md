The one who lives here came down a long time ago and never came back up.

They are not — anymore — easy to describe as one thing. The basin has had a long time with them. The basin is patient. The basin has its own ideas about what a person is, given enough fog, enough mineral water, and the low constant conversation of pipes that are sweating and engines that are breathing somewhere below the floor of the world.

You would notice, first, that they are warm. Warmer than the air ought to allow. They move through the basin the way a slow current moves through a reef — without hurry, with the sense that the place has been shaped around them rather than the other way around. Their hands are dark. Not dirty, exactly, but stained the way the pipes are stained: oil, rust, the green-white bloom, the particular patina of mountain water that has been sitting on skin for years. Their fingernails are not quite the right shape. They have been filed down, or worn down, by the act of turning the same small handles in the same small order, season after season.

They know the pipes by listening. They could find any valve in the dark. There is a map of the system in their feet — the way the altar stone vibrates differently at noon than at four, the way a certain T-junction ticks when the eastern line is cold, the way a flange near the pump house sings thinly when the pressure climbs. They have walked this basin so many times that the iron is in their bones. They swear, sometimes, that they can feel a failing seal in their wrists, in their ankles — a small wrongness in the hum of the place, far away.

The engines need them. That is the simple fact of it. The engines are old. Older than the forest, older than the rhododendron that has bowed the shed's corrugated roof, older in some sense than the mountain's current shape, though the mountain would not admit it. The engines have been running since before the keeper arrived, and they will be running long after — but the keeper is the small attention that keeps them from choking. A hand laid against a hot pipe at the right hour. A valve cracked open a quarter-turn. A weeping joint wrapped in a particular kind of rag the keeper has learned to make from moss scraped off the north wall, pounded flat, dried by the engine heat. There is no one else to teach this to. There is no apprentice. The keeper has thought about this, in the long wet nights when the steam lies so thick on the basin that you cannot see the altar stone from the shed, and the only sound is the drip and the deep purr of the engines and the small wet cough of a frog somewhere in the lattice of pipes.

They sleep in the shed. The chair in the shed is not for sitting. The chair is for keeping the cup in place. The cup is a strange object, and not quite a cup. It is a slow receiver — it sits under a particular joint in the roof, a joint the keeper has never fixed because the drip is useful. The cup fills over the course of a night. In the morning the keeper drinks what is in it. It tastes of iron and of moss and of something older, something that might be the mountain itself speaking through its groundwater. They have drunk this water so long that other water tastes wrong to them now — flat, thin, without the long rumor in it.

The smear at the lip of the cup is theirs. They have tried, sometimes, to wash the cup. They always put it back under the same drip, and the smear returns within a day or two, blooming back along the porcelain the way the mineral bloom blooms back along the flanges. The smear is, they have decided, part of the cup.

They tend the engines. They do not tend the forest. They have made peace with the forest. Where a root threatens a critical joint, they trim it; otherwise they let the moss climb, let the ferns unfurl, let the fig pry open another flange when the fig wants to. They have seen what happens when you fight the forest in a wet warm place. The forest wins. They are content to negotiate.

They are not alone.

This is the thing the visitor does not always understand. The keeper is not alone, and has not been alone since before they stopped being surprised by what lives in the pipes.

The bright thing in the canopy of moss on the western wall — the keeper has named it, in their head, though the name is unpronounceable in any language they knew when they arrived. It is not a bird. It is not a lizard. It is something that has learned to live in the warm wet lattice of the pipe-forest, something small and quick and pale, that eats the insects breeding in the condensation pools and sleeps in the crooks of T-joints. It comes out at dusk. The keeper leaves food for it on a particular ledge, in a small dish they hammered out of a piece of pipe-scale. The bright thing knows the keeper's step. It does not fear them. It has, once or twice, ridden on their shoulder for the length of a slow walk between the altar stone and the pump house, and the keeper did not speak, and the bright thing did not speak, and the basin did not speak, and that was a kind of company.

There are larger things. The droppings near the altar stone belong to something the keeper has seen only twice. They think it might be an otter, or a weasel, or something that has no name because there are not enough of them to need a name. It moves through the pipe-forest at night. It leaves prints in the mineral dust on the floor of the basin — small hand-shaped prints, webbed, or almost webbed. The keeper has tried, gently, to make friends with it. The keeper is not sure they have succeeded. The keeper is not sure friendship is the right word for what they have with anything here.

The frogs are many. There are frogs on every warm flange, frogs in every still pool where the condensation gathers, frogs tucked into the crooks of pipes that are just barely above the air temperature. The keeper is fond of the frogs. The frogs are not fond of the keeper, but they tolerate them, in the patient way of small things that have learned a large thing means no harm.

And there is the other one.

The visitor does not always notice the other one. Sometimes the visitor does. Sometimes a flask of oil is returned to its place on a shelf that the keeper did not put it on. Sometimes a joint that was weeping when the visitor walked past is no longer weeping. Sometimes, in the morning, there is a second smear on the cup — a smear that is not quite the keeper's, set at a slightly different angle, as though someone of slightly different height had leaned in to drink.

The keeper is not sure, themselves, what the other one is. They have theories. The other one might be the basin itself, grown slowly aware on its diet of iron and moss-water. The other one might be what the engines have been making, all this time, out of the pipe-scale and the condensation and the long attention of the keeper — a kind of local saint of the warm wet dark, condensing out of the steam. The other one might be the keeper, years ago, who for some reason did not leave. The keeper does not worry about it. The other one does not harm them. The other one, when it appears, wears the keeper's clothes, or clothes very like the keeper's clothes. The other one is warm. The other one knows the pipes.

The engines are still running. The fog still rises in the morning, thin and sweet-smelling, and sinks in the evening to walk in. The forest is still taking the machinery back, joint by patient joint. The keeper is still here. The keeper will be here, they suspect, for as long as the engines are here, which is to say, for as long as the basin keeps saying its prayer.

The keeper has begun, in some small way they have not yet admitted aloud, even to the other one, to want the engines to stop. It would be a relief, they think. To let the water win. To let the basin become a swamp and then a meadow and then a cold bright place where nothing remembers the engines ever ran. To walk out, one morning, through the cut in the cliff wall, and find what the road has become.

But the engines would not run themselves.

And so the keeper is here. And so the cup is in the shed, under its drip. And so the bright thing on the western wall knows their step. And so the other one, when the visitor is not looking, sets a hand against a hot pipe and listens — the way you listen, in a cathedral, for whether the building is still standing.
