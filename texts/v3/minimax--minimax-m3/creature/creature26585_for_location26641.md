# The Inhabitants of the High Forest

What lives here is two things, and the two things are bound, and you cannot really speak of one without the other. The first is the people, who are few. The second is the trees, which are not called trees but are called towers, and which are older than the people and will be here after. Between them, and under them, and in the long slow air around them, there are smaller things — birds, grazers, flyers, the pale low things, the mosses, the lichens, the cold. The place is mostly cold. The place is mostly silence, except for the long singing that is not silence. We will begin with the people, because they are the ones who name, and to name is to be a kind of resident.

The people are small. This is the first thing to say, and the most important. The air is thin, and a tall body in thin air is a body that is always a little hungry, always a little cold, and the body of a person here has learned to be less of these things by being less in the world. A grown man of the high forest stands about as high as a child of warmer countries. He is wide through the chest, and his ribs are deep, and the muscles of his shoulders and his back are thick, because the work of climbing is the work of pulling, not the work of pushing, and a body that climbs all day is a body whose arms are larger than its legs. His hands are wide, and the fingers long, and the joints are thickened in a way that looks almost arthritic to a person from below, but is not — it is the way the hand has grown around the work, the way a tree grows around a stone. The nails are hard, and the pads of the fingers are callused, and a person can grip a knob of bark and hold there for an hour if the wind is not too bad. The feet are narrower than you would expect, and the toes are long, and the big toe is set forward in a way that lets the foot hook around a branch or a handhold. A baby of the high forest, in its first weeks, is carried in a sling of bark-rope against the mother's chest, and so it learns the rhythm of her climbing before it learns to walk. By the time it can walk it has been up and down a hundred trunks. By the time it can talk it has begun to learn the spiral of the handholds itself, the slow way up and the faster way down, the places where the bark is worn smooth and the places where it is still rough. A child who grows up in a tower and not on the ground learns to read the wood the way a child of a coast learns to read the water. It learns where the chambers are. It learns where the wood is sound and where it has gone soft. It learns to hear, before it can speak, the difference between the sound of a living trunk and the sound of one that has been hollowed through.

The people are brown. The brown is the brown of bark that has been wet a long time, a deep reddish brown that is not the brown of any warmer country. Their eyes are dark, and the iris is large, and the white of the eye is barely there, because the light is mostly low, and the eye that must work in the long dark of winter is an eye that has let the pupil grow. In the short summer, when the sun comes and is high and is white on the stone, they squint and shade their faces with their hands and pull strips of bark-cloth across their brows. The hair is black and thick and worn long, tied back in a knot at the nape, because a loose hair in a wind is a thing that will catch and pull. The men do not grow beards, or grow them thin, and the women do not shave their heads, and the children go bare until the cold bites.

The people are quiet. This is not a politeness. It is a way of being. A baby, in the first weeks, hears the mother's heartbeat, and the wind in the bark, and the low creak of the wood, and only after these does it hear a voice. The voice is the third thing, and it is soft. The people speak softly because the air is thin and carries sound far, and also because a loud voice in a tower is a voice that bounces off the inner walls and comes back at you strange. They speak into the hand, sometimes, when they want to be heard only by the person beside them, and a person who is out of earshot will see only the shape of the mouth and the tilt of the head and know that something has been said. They listen much. They listen the way a person of a warmer country looks — with the whole body, with the stilling. A person who is listening in the high forest stands a certain way, with the head a little forward and the hands open at the sides, and the eyes half-closed, and the breath held, and in that posture is a kind of courtesy to the sound, as if to say, I am making room for you.

A child learns the names. The names are the curriculum. There is no other. A child is taught, in its first years, the name of the tower it was born in, and the name is a story, and the story is the tower. The name might be the name of the person who first climbed it, if that person can be remembered, and the person is usually the great-grandmother of someone, and the climbing is usually a long-ago story of a sickness in a lower camp and a woman who went up alone and came back with a baby in her arms and said the trees were warm. The name might be the name of a bird that nested there in some remembered season, and the story is of a year of many birds and a young man who climbed to the top of the tower and found a feather the length of his forearm and brought it down. The name might be the name of a wind, and the story is of a storm that broke an upper limb and the limb came down and a person was killed and the tower was named for the wind that did it. The names are not arbitrary. The names are the place.

A child is taught the names of the towers within a day's walk, which is a small list, because the towers are far apart. A child is taught the names of the towers where its mother's mother lived, and its father's father, and where the cousins are, and where the dead are kept. The dead are kept in the trees, in the inner chambers that the people only enter when they have to. The dead are wrapped in bark-cloth and laid on a shelf of wood in a chamber near the heart of the tree, and the chamber is closed with a plug of lashed poles, and the tree is allowed to do what it does, which is to grow. The tree grows around the dead, the way a tree in a warmer country grows around a fence-wire, slowly, patiently, in the long patience of wood. After a few generations the bones are part of the wood, and a person who knows where to look can see them in a cross-section of a chamber floor — a rib there, a finger-bone there, the curve of a skull. The people do not fear this. The people do not think of it as a desecration. The tree is the tree, and the tree is alive, and the dead have gone back to the wood, and the wood is warm, and the wood pulses, and the pulse is slower than breath and more regular and it is the closest thing to a constant that the place has.

The relationship between the people and the trees is not worship and is not friendship and is not ownership. It is something for which the language of warmer countries does not have a word. The people live in the trees, and the trees allow this. The people do not understand the allowing, and do not try to. The people are careful with the trees, and the trees are patient with the people, and this is the arrangement, and the arrangement is old. A person will not cut into living wood for a platform, but will lash poles to the natural crooks of branches and let the lashing rot and be replaced. A person will not light a fire near the inner wall of a chamber, but will build the fire in a stone ring at the outer edge, where the heat will rise and find the chimney the tree has already made for itself, and the bark will not be scorched. A person will not fell a living tree except in certain rituals, and the rituals are rare, and the consequences are said to be bad, and the bad is not a punishment from a god but is a thing that happens to the wood and to the people and to the weather for a season after. A person will speak to a tree sometimes, in a low voice, in a way that is not prayer and is not gossip and is just speech, and the tree does not answer, and the person does not expect it to, and the speaking is the point. The tree is there. The person is there. The speaking is what two things that share a place do.

The trees. The trees are the other great inhabitant, and they are more strange. A tree is not a tree in the way of warmer countries. A tree here is wider at the base than a house, and at the height of a house its trunk is the width of a doorway, and higher it tapers and does not vanish, and on a clear day you can sometimes see that it goes up farther than the eye accepts, and on a cloudy day you lose it in the cloud and have to take on faith that it is still going. The bark is thick, and warm to the touch even in the cold, and the grain runs in spirals that catch the dim light in a way that looks like the lines of a hand. The bark is not stiff. The wood is not stiff. The trunk flexes a little in the wind, and at certain heights the hollows inside it act as a kind of organ pipe, and a big tree in a strong wind produces a low note that you can feel through the soles of your boots a hundred yards away. A forest of them, which is what this is, although it does not look like one from below, makes a music that goes on through the night. The music is not a melody. The music is a chord that shifts with the wind. A person can lie in a chamber in the dark and listen to the chord and know, by the way the chord changes, that the wind has shifted in the southwest, or that a storm is coming, or that the night will be cold.

A tree grows. A tree grows up and a tree grows in. As a tree ages, the inner wood softens and is reabsorbed, and chambers form. The chambers are not all on the same level. They are stacked, and they are connected by natural chimneys and by the spiral of the handholds on the outside. Some chambers are deep, in the heart of the tree, and the air inside is warm and smells of cedar and wet wool and something else that is not quite either, and the walls are smooth as skin. Some chambers are shallow, in the outer wood, and the light comes through the bark in a green dim way, the way light comes through leaves in a denser forest. A chamber that has been used by people for a long time has a different feel from a chamber that has not, and a person who knows the tree can tell which is which by the warmth and the smell. The tree makes handholds. This is the strangest thing, after the pulse. The handholds are knobbed ridges that spiral the trunk in a regular pattern, and on a young tree they are just bark-thickenings, and on an old tree they are staircases, worn smooth in the centers where the feet have been. The tree grows them. The people do not carve them. The tree grows them in the places where the bark is most often touched, and a person who has climbed a tree for forty years has, by the pressure of his hands and his feet, taught the tree where to thicken, and the tree has obliged. The tree also makes chimneys. A chamber in an old tree has a vent near the top, a natural opening that lets the smoke of a small fire find its way out, and the vent is lined with a harder wood than the chamber walls, and the vent does not soot up, or does not soot up much, and the people do not know why.

The pulse. The pulse is the deepest thing. If you put your hand against the bark of an old tree in the still of a winter night, you would feel it. A slow rise and fall. Slower than breath, slower than heartbeat. Not quite a rhythm you can count to, because it is too slow and too patient. The wood is warm. The wood is alive in a way that is not the way an animal is alive. The tree is not thinking. The tree is not aware the way you are aware. The tree is something else. The people do not have a word for what the tree is. The people do not have a word for the pulse, except maybe the word they also use for breath, because the pulse and the breath are the same thing. The pulse is the tree breathing. The tree is breathing, and the breath is the pulse, and the breath is not air in the way your breath is air, and the breath is not the wind, and the breath is something the tree does with the whole of itself, slowly, in the long dark, in the way the lichens glow.

A tree dies, sometimes, and a dead tree is a different thing. The bark loosens. The wood goes brittle. The chambers open to the weather. The handholds wear down. The pulse stops, or becomes so slow that it is indistinguishable from the cold. The people do not climb into a dead tree. The people do not use a dead tree for a chamber, or for fire, except in certain rituals, and the rituals are about mourning and about the cutting of the dead wood. A dead tree is left standing, mostly, because the wind is patient and the wind will bring it down, and the wind will bring it down in its own time, and the wood will lie on the ground and slowly be covered by the moss and the lichen and the red mat of the scree, and the people will pass it for a generation and then the place will have forgotten it. A dead tree that has fallen is sometimes used for fire, and the wood is good, and the smoke smells different, and the people do not speak of where the wood came from, in the way that you do not speak of a thing that has been a person.

The fauna. There is a lot of fauna, although you might not see it for a long time, because it lives mostly in the upper trees and the cloud. The most often seen are the grazers. A grazer is the size of a housecat, and slow, and thick-furred in a brown that is the color of wet bark, and it has six legs. It eats the red moss and the bark-lichens. It climbs well. It can hold motionless against the wind for so long that you will think it is a swelling of the wood until it moves, and the moving is a small surprise each time, because the body has been so still that the mind had stopped including it. The grazer is not afraid of people. The grazer is not afraid of anything, exactly, because it is so well-camouflaged and so slow that nothing hunts it successfully except the dog-sized thing that pads the ridges at night. The grazer is a comfort to see. If you are climbing a tree and you pass a grazer on the trunk, you put out a hand and the grazer does not move, and you touch its back and the fur is soft and warm, and you go on. The grazer does not look at you. The grazer is busy. The grazer has been on this trunk, in this position, for an hour, and it will be on this trunk, in this position, for another hour, and it does not have anywhere to be, and the not-having-anywhere-to-be is the grazer's whole life, and the life is a good one.

The birds are large and dark. They are the size of a child's arm, each one, with long wings and a long beak and a scream that carries for miles. They nest in colonies on the lee sides of the trunks, in the upper chambers that the people do not use, in the crooks of broken limbs. They are loud. They are unafraid. They ride the wind all day, screaming at each other across the gaps, and at night they come back to the roost and settle, and there is the small sound of many bodies shifting, and the wind in the feathers, and the slow creak of the wood under the weight. The people and the birds have an understanding. The people do not climb into the bird chambers in the breeding season. The birds do not come down into the human chambers, except sometimes a young one falls, and then a person brings it up in a sling of bark-rope and sets it on a high branch and the parent comes for it. The people eat the birds sometimes, but not in the breeding season, and not the old ones, and they have a way of asking first, which is a small ceremony with a twist of red moss and a low word. The birds scream, and the person waits, and if the birds scream a certain way the person takes one, and if they scream a different way the person goes away.

The tiny flyers come out after a rain. They are smaller than your thumb, and they emerge by the thousands from certain chambers, and for an hour the air around a tree is full of them, a living fog that rises and disperses on the wind. They do not sting. They do not bite. They are eaten in midair by predators that come up to meet them — small things with hard wing-cases that snap and zip through the cloud of them. After an hour the swarm is gone. The flyers go where flyers go. The predators go where predators go. The air is quiet again, and a little emptier, and a person who has been watching will sit still for a long time, because the watching was a kind of gift, and you do not move away from a gift immediately.

On the ground at night there are pale low things with too many legs. They are flat, and they come out from under stones, and they are the color of lichen. They eat the lichen. They are eaten by the dog-sized thing that pads the ridges, which leaves the only readable tracks in the morning — a line of prints, four-toed, with a long drag of the tail. The pale low things are not seen often. They are not thought about.
