# The Keepers of the Weeping Cellar

There is no one here.

Or — there is almost no one here. Almost, the way a candle is almost not lit when the wick is held to the match but has not yet taken. Almost, the way a path is almost not walked when the foot is lifted but has not yet come down. The people who come to this place are few, and they come rarely, and when they come they do not speak. So it is fair to say that the Weeping Cellar is unoccupied, in the way that a tide pool is unoccupied, in the way that a long-held breath is empty.

But there are hands here. The ghost of hands, at least. The pressure of them, worn into the stone as a shape that water now finds and follows. And there is memory, in the way that the vessel on the second shelf from the floor is slightly larger than its neighbors because it was made to fit a particular palm, generations ago, and the palm that fits it is the only palm that has ever lifted it cleanly, and so it waits.

---

Who tends such a place. That is the question. The question, and the absence of the answer, is the place.

A keeper, you imagine. A single one. You imagine her before you see her clearly, and then you do not see her at all, and then you see her again. She is old, or she is young, or she is neither — the Weeping Cellar is not a place that cares much about the difference. She is small. The passages are narrow and the ledges are low and the kneeling-stone in front of the great trough is worn down to the shape of one specific posture, and the person who can hold that posture without strain is not tall. She wears no cloth that does not also get wet. She carries no light. She has been coming here since she was small enough to be carried, and she was carried by someone who was also carried, and so on, backward into a dimness that has no name.

She does not live here. She lives somewhere else — higher, where there is sky, where there is weather that changes. She has a name. She has other work. But a portion of her life belongs to the weeping walls, in the way that a portion of a river belongs to the mill it turns. Not all of it. Not most of it. Enough.

She comes when the water changes. She can tell. From outside, where the springs feed in, the weeping varies — by season, by the year, by the long slow breathing of whatever is beneath the stone. The mineral taste shifts. The warmth shifts. Sometimes a new weep opens higher on the wall, and a vein that has been dry for years begins again, and she must come and decide what to do about it, which is to say: she must come and watch it for a while, and then she must adjust one of the small clay vessels to catch it, and then she must wait again.

She works slowly. She does not speak while she works, and the place does not ask her to. The chisel-marks in the stone are not hers. The hand-prints in the stone are not hers. The stacked stones with their spiraling line are not hers. The trough with its small dark shapes is not hers. She is not the author of this place. She is its reader. She is the one who turns the page.

---

There may be more than one of her. There are signs, in the way there are signs in tide pools — the overlapping tracks of small creatures, the layered drift of shells. A second kneeling-stone, in a different alcove, for a different arrangement, worn at a slightly different angle. A basket, old, the reeds gone soft, that was not woven by the same hands that wove the basket beside it, or that was woven at a different time of life, when the hands were stronger, or when the reed was younger. The Weeping Cellar does not require a single keeper. It requires continuity. The distinction is important. A river is not a single drop. A song is not a single throat.

So perhaps there is also someone else. Perhaps a younger one, learning. Perhaps someone who is only an apprentice to the apprentice. The work is not complicated, but it is exact, and exactness is not something that can be handed over in a sentence. It must be stood beside. It must be watched. The younger one comes with the older one, sometimes, and does not speak, and is shown which vessel is which, and how to tell when a crack matters and when it does not, and how to hold the hand against the warm stone to feel the slow pulse of the water beneath. The younger one is not yet trusted with the rotation of the vessels, because the rotation is a kind of memory, and memory takes time to grow into.

---

And beneath them, or beside them, or around them, in a way that does not map cleanly onto a body — there is something else.

Not a person. Not exactly. But a presence, in the way that a long-maintained place has a presence. A settled thing. The Weeping Cellar is old in a way that is hard to think about, because the things in it are simple — stone, water, clay, the absent ore, the carved line — and the work is patient, and the patience of the work has added up, over the years, to something that is no longer just stone and water and clay. It has become a kind of slow attention. The walls attend. The stepped basins attend. The trough attends, with its long still water and its small dark shapes that may be seeds or may be teeth or may be the chips of older carvings broken down and laid to rest in the place where the water is most clear.

This presence does not watch you. It does not judge you. It is not waiting for you to do anything in particular. It is the way the light falls in two places and does not move. It is the way the water weeps and is caught and is poured and is caught again. It is the way the hand-prints in the stone are not worn away but are slowly, gently, becoming more themselves, as the water learns their shape and the stone softens around it.

---

The keeper, when she is here, is not afraid of this. She is not in awe of it, exactly, though there is a kind of attention she pays to the larger arrangements that is different from the attention she pays to the small ones. She knows which troughs must be checked. She knows which ledges have settled and which have not. She knows the small dark shapes in the great trough are not to be touched, and she does not touch them. She knows the sound of a vessel cracking in the dark, and she knows that sometimes the cracking is the place speaking, and sometimes the cracking is just the place being old.

She has not, in her life, ever seen the Weeping Cellar full of people. She has not, in her life, ever seen a torch lit within it. She has, once, seen a stranger — a person from further off, who had heard of the place and come to see it, and who stood in the entrance passage and did not come in, because the light was poor and the smell of wet stone was strong and the silence was the kind of silence that the body recognizes before the mind does. The stranger did not speak. The keeper, who had been kneeling by the trough, did not rise. The stranger left, after a while. The keeper, after a longer while, also left, and came back another day.

---

So.

The Weeping Cellar is lived in by a small number of people, and by a larger number of vessels, and by a great number of stones, and by a presence that is made of all of these together, and by the water, which is the oldest of them.

The water does not live here. The water passes through. But the water has been passing through for so long that its passing has shaped the place, and the place has shaped its passing, and the two are now difficult to tell apart, the way a river and its bed are difficult to tell apart after a thousand years.

The keeper tends the passing. That is her work. She watches the weeping walls. She moves the vessels. She does not speak. She does not move the stones.

The place continues. It has continued for a long time. It will continue.

The light falls in two places. The water runs. The hand-prints in the stone soften a little more. The small dark shapes in the trough do not change. The kneeling-stone is worn, and will be worn further, and the person who wears it will be followed by another person, and another, and the line of them will not end while the water still weeps, and the water weeps because the stone is warm, and the stone is warm because something beneath it has not stopped being warm, and that something has not stopped being warm for a very long time, and no one knows why, and the question is not asked.
