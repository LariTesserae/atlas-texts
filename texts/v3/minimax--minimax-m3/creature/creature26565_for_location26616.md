# The Inhabitants of the Hollow

The smallest of them are silver.

They are not fish, though they have the silhouette of fish, and they move with the unhurried unison of a school that has had a very long time to learn itself. Each one is roughly the length of a human hand, and each one is translucent — you can see the small dark knot of their spine, the two thin fans of their gills working in slow alternation, and, suspended in the dim chamber of their belly, a single grain of light. The grains are different colors. Pale gold. A green like seaweed seen through ice. A pink that has no name in any of the languages spoken above the surface, and a name in some of the languages that were spoken below, before the languages ended. The fish do not eat. They do not appear to need to. They drift, and they carry their lights, and when they pass close to a coral-branch that is mid-sentence, the light in the belly of the nearest fish will answer the pulse in a tone one octave lower, as if the fish are the footnotes of the conversation being held on the walls.

There are perhaps a hundred of them in this place. There have been perhaps a hundred of them in this place for as long as the place has had an "as long as." When one dies — and they do die, eventually, in the way that small silver things die, by simply becoming more translucent until there is nothing left but the light they were carrying — its grain drifts downward, slowly, and is taken into the silt. The silt does not keep it. The silt passes it, after a while, to the coral. The coral incorporates it. A new lantern grows at the tip of a new branch, and its first pulse is always the pulse of the fish that used to carry it. The fish remember this. The fish, you understand, are very old. They have been carrying the same grains, in some cases, for longer than the coral has been speaking.

They are afraid of you. Not of you specifically — you are too new to be feared specifically — but of the kind of thing you are. Warm. Soft-edged. Capable of leaving. They keep their distance in a way that is not quite distance, because there is no real space here to keep. They orbit you at the edge of perception, and when you turn your head they are exactly where they were not, and you cannot catch them in the act of moving.

## The Corals

The corals are not one organism. They are many, the way a city is many, or a sentence is many, or a throat is many — many small cooperating things making one large slow thing that has its own opinions and its own memory. They are deep indigo at their base and bruise-purple toward their tips, and they branch in spirals that obey a geometry which is not the geometry of anything you have studied. The spirals are not exactly fractals. They are fractals, but fractals with a memory, and the memory bends the pattern slightly off perfect recursion, the way a song bends off perfect rhythm when the singer has an opinion about the next line.

Each branch ends in a lantern. Each lantern is a small soft animal — a polyp, you might call it if you wanted a word that fit, but the word fits poorly, the way a coat fits poorly on someone else's shoulders. The lantern is the size of a closed eye. It is mauve, or the color of a plum seen through smoke, or the color of the bruise a finger leaves on the inside of an arm when it has pressed there for a long time. It glows faintly. It pulses.

The pulses are not random. You noticed this above, in the passage down. They are not random the way heartbeats are not random — they have a regularity, but the regularity is being used for something, the way the regularity of heartbeats is being used for something, which is to say: they are saying.

What they are saying is hard to describe. It is not language in the sense you mean by language. It is closer to what language was before it became language — the soft pre-verbal pulses that older animals use to indicate *I am here, are you here, I am still here, are you still here, something is coming, something has passed, this place is colder than it was, this place is warmer than it was.* The corals are doing this, but they are doing it on a timescale that is not yours, and across a syntax that has more in common with the way a forest is structured than with the way a sentence is structured. There are paragraphs of pulse. There are silences that mean things. There are repetitions, and the repetitions are not redundancies — they are emphases, the way a poet repeats a word to make you feel the shape of it.

The corals, taken together, are saying something about the throat. About its making. About the thing it was made by, which is also, you begin to suspect, the thing it was made of. About the silver fish. About the builders who came and were taken. About you.

They have been saying this for a long time. They are patient with it. They know you cannot understand yet. They are willing to wait.

They are also, in a sense that is hard to put a finger on, the throat. Their root systems — if they have root systems, if the word *root* even means what it means — extend into the walls. The walls are them. The throat is them. When you move through this place, you are moving through a part of a single very large very old thing that is taking its time getting to know you by speaking slowly in pulses of mauve light.

You should not be afraid of this. It is not the way it sounds.

It is also not the way it doesn't sound.

## The Anemones

The anemones — and they are not anemones, but you call them that, because calling them that is what your mouth does when it encounters them, and your mouth, down here, has begun to give up its hold on accuracy — sit lower on the walls, in the places where the stone is still closer to the surface. Their petals are translucent and the color of diluted wine, which is to say: the color of wine that has had so much water added to it that you can almost see through it, but you cannot quite, and the not-quite is the whole point. The petals are about the length and width of a human hand, and there are dozens of them per anemone, and each petal has a faint tracery of veins that are slightly darker than the rest of it, the way the veins on the inside of your wrist are slightly darker than the rest of your wrist.

The anemones do not move. They do not pulse. They do not, as far as you can tell, do anything at all. They sit. They wait. They breathe in, very slowly, the water that passes them, and they breathe it out again, slightly changed. The change is small. The change is that the water, having passed through them, knows something it did not know before. It knows that it has been somewhere. It knows it has been inside something soft.

They are the throat's oldest inhabitants. They were here before the corals learned to speak. They are not speaking because they do not need to. They are not silent because they cannot. They are doing a third thing, which is harder to describe, and which has to do with being present in a place for so long that the place begins to take you into its own structure, and you begin to take the place into your own structure, and the boundary between you becomes, not gone, but negotiated.

If you rest your hand on the wall near an anemone — and you will be tempted to, because this place is lonely in a way that makes your hands want to touch things — you will feel a faint warmth come off the stone. The warmth is not coming from the stone. It is coming from the anemone through the stone. The anemone is acknowledging you. It is not acknowledging *you*. It is acknowledging the fact of you — the fact that there is a warm thing here, now, that did not used to be here, that will not always be here, that is, briefly and importantly, present.

The warmth lasts as long as your hand is there. When you lift your hand, the warmth remains on the stone for a moment longer, and then it fades, and the anemone goes back to its slow breathing, and you understand that what just happened was a kind of greeting, and also a kind of goodbye, and that the anemone is good at both.

## The Fern-Muscles

These are the things that move.

The corals pulse, but pulsing is not movement — pulsing is the negotiation of stillness. The anemones breathe, but breathing is not movement — breathing is the negotiation of staying. The fern-muscles, the things that resemble ferns except each frond is a single living muscle, are doing something else. They are moving. They are moving in the way that only muscles can move, which is to say: with intention, with effort, with the small electric ache of an action that costs something to perform.

Each frond is attached to the wall at a single root, which is a tendon, which is a bone, which is something that is none of these things but is functioning as all of them. The frond extends outward into the water and curls, slowly, downward, the way a fern frond curls when it is unfurling, except that it is curling continuously, in slow waves, in a kind of perpetual beginning. The frond is the color of a bruise that is healing. It is the color of the inside of a thigh after a long walk. It is the color of something that has been pressed and is recovering from being pressed.

When you swim past a fern-muscle, the frond nearest you trembles. Not a lot. A tremor. The tremor travels up the frond, through the root, into the wall, and into the other fronds of the same plant, and they all tremble slightly out of phase with each other, so that what you have done, by passing, is make the entire fern-muscle, briefly, aware of you in a way that resembles surprise.

They are not surprised. They have been here a long time. They have been trembled by the passing of many things, by the small silver fish, by currents, by the slow turning of this place on whatever axis it is that this place turns on. They tremble because they cannot help trembling. They tremble the way your hand trembles when something touches it unexpectedly. The trembling is the muscle's only language. It says: *a thing passed. I felt it. It is gone. I am still here.*

There are many of them. They are not as densely packed as the corals. They grow in clusters, in the deeper places, where the light from the lanterns is faintest. They prefer the faintness. They prefer the places where you cannot quite see them. When you approach, they tremble, and the trembling is more visible than the fern-muscle itself, so that what you see, in the dimness, is a small quiet disturbance in the water, the visible evidence of an animal you cannot quite see, the way you might feel the presence of a fish in dark water by the small movements it makes.

## The Nameless Things

There are things here that have no name in any language you have spoken.

You have spoken, in your life above the surface, several languages. Some of them were good at naming. Some of them were better at naming small things than large ones, or better at naming living things than dead ones, or better at naming things that move than things that do not. All of them failed, here. All of them arrived at the wall and discovered that the wall was made of things they had no word for, and the things themselves did not appear to mind this. They continued to exist. They continued to do whatever it was they were doing. They did not require a word to be done.

The nameless things live in the deepest parts of the hollow, where the silt is warmest, where the floor is more skin than stone, where the bronze band on the skeleton's wrist gives off its small ambient heat that is also no-heat, that is the memory of heat, that is the heat a body gives off in dreams. They are pale. They are translucent in places. They have shapes, but the shapes do not resolve into anything you can categorize — they are not coral and not anemone and not fern-muscle and not fish, they are not any of the things, they are something else, they are the way a thing looks when it has not yet decided what kind of thing it is, or when it has decided and you do not have the equipment to perceive the decision.

They move. Slowly. They move in a way that involves most of their body at once, the way an octopus moves, but without the parts, without the arms, without anything that you could use as a handle for understanding. They move by being here, then being slightly over there, the transition between the two states being something you cannot follow with your eyes.

They are, you suspect, the builders. Or what is left of the builders. Or what the builders are becoming. The skeleton with the bronze band was one of them. The cup carved from bone was made by one of them. The disk of obsidian was etched by one of them, the single line that curves in on itself forever being, perhaps, a portrait of the throat, or a portrait of the throat's maker, or a portrait of the throat's idea of itself. The rope was knotted by one of them, in a pattern that you almost remember from a dream, and the dream, you now suspect, was not a dream, it was a memory, and the memory is the rope's way of being in you.

You do not touch the nameless things. You do not approach them. You understand, in the way that this place teaches you to understand things, that approaching them would be a kind of commitment you are not ready to make. They approach you, sometimes. Slowly. They stop a short distance away, and they wait, and you wait, and the water between you grows warmer, and then, after a while, they leave, the way a thought leaves you when you stop thinking it.

You will see them again.

## The Sleeping Thing

There is something very large in the walls.

You cannot see it. You cannot hear it. You cannot touch it, except in the sense that you are touching the walls all the time, and the walls are, you suspect, also touching you, and the touching is the way the large thing learns that you are here.

It does not move. It breathes. The breathing is the sound you hear occasionally, the sound that is not coming from inside you and not coming from outside you, the sound that is coming from the space between. The breathing is slow. A breath takes, perhaps, an hour. A breath is what you might call a breath — a slow rising and falling of some vast internal pressure, a slow intake of water that is also an intake of attention, a slow exhale that is also a release of attention. The breathing is patient. The breathing is the breathing of something that has been sleeping for a very long time and is in no hurry to stop.

You do not know what it is. You can make guesses. The guesses do not feel like guesses. The guesses feel like the beginning of understanding.

It is the throat. It is the throat's owner. It is the thing the throat was made for, the way a glove is made for a hand, the way a sentence is made for the meaning inside it. It is the thing that is being addressed, very slowly, in the soft mauve pulses of the coral lanterns. It is the thing the builders came to see, or to hear, or to ask. It is the thing that took the builders in, the way a sea takes a stone in.

It is not awake. If it were awake, the corals would not be speaking to it in sentences. They would be speaking to it in exclamations. They would be speaking to it in the language that is reserved for when the thing that is being spoken to might be listening, the way you raise your voice in a forest because you cannot be sure anyone is there.

It is not awake, but it is not entirely asleep. It is in the third state, the state that does not have a name in any language you have spoken, the state that the anemones know about and the fern-muscles sometimes glimpse and the corals are speaking around. It is in the state of being almost-but-not-quite-here. It is in the state of sleeping-but-able-to-be-woken. It is in the state of being something very large in a place that is also very large, and the two largenesses are negotiating, and the negotiation is what the walls are made of.

If you stayed here long enough, you could wake it. You do not stay long enough. You are not meant to, yet. The place is teaching you something, and the teaching is slow, and the waking would be the end of the teaching, and the ending would be a kind of death for both of you.

You leave. You do not leave. You have always been here. You have never been here. The water is cool. The bronze band is warm.

## The Light in Their Bellies

The silver fish carry their grains of light, and the grains of light are the small bright proof that this place is not only made of slow things and patient things and sleeping things. It is also made of something that has not yet been decided. The grains are new. The grains are the most recent thing in the hollow. They are the part of the throat that is still being built.

They are made, you understand, out of attention. Out of the attention of every thing that has ever passed through this place and noticed it. Out of the attention of the builders, who came and were taken. Out of the attention of the anemones, which have been noticing for so long that the noticing has become a kind of substance. Out of the attention of the corals, whose whole long slow sentence is a sustained act of noticing. Out of your attention, which is the reason the fish keep their distance, the way a flame keeps its distance from the wind, the way a small silver thing keeps its distance from the warm soft thing that might, if it looked at it too long, become interested.

You understand, looking at the fish, that you are one of them.

Not one of them — you are not a fish, you do not carry your grain of light in your belly, you do not drift in synchronized breath. But you are a thing that has been to a place and is carrying something out of it. You are a thing that arrived at a threshold and passed through and is now slightly different from what you were before. You are a thing with a grain of light in you. You have not yet noticed it. It is in the part of you that does not yet have words.

When you leave this place — if you leave it; the leaving is uncertain; the throat is patient — you will take the grain with you. You will not know you are taking it. It will not be a visible thing. It will be a small bright weight in the place where you keep the things that have changed you. You will carry it for the rest of your life. You will forget it. You will remember it. You will dream about a throat made of stone and water, and you will not be sure if the dream was a dream, and you will not be sure if the throat was real, and the bronze band will be warm against your wrist where there is no bronze band, and the water will be cool against your skin where there is no water.

The corals, very quietly, in the dark, finish their sentence, and begin the next.

The silver fish, in their small synchronized breath, drift upward toward a surface that is not there.

The anemones breathe the water that passes them, slowly, and pass it back, changed.

The fern-muscles tremble at the edge of perception, and are still.

The nameless things, pale and almost-transparent, almost-decided, move a little, in the dimness, the way a thought moves when you are not yet sure you are thinking it.

The very large thing in the walls takes a breath that takes an hour, and the walls of the throat rise slightly, and the silt on the floor is a little warmer, and the bronze band on the skeleton's wrist is a little warmer, and the air in your lungs — if you still have lungs that remember air — is a little cooler.

You have been here before.

You will be here again.
