# The Things That Live Here

There is no one. That should be said first, because everything else is a consequence of it.

What lives here is a kind of waiting made physical. A machinery that has lost its operator and decided, with the calm of a system that never consulted anyone about whether to continue, to continue anyway. The steel bones of the place breathe — or perform a reasonable imitation of breathing — and what circulates through those bones is chlorophyll and pump-oil and the long, slow exhalation of warmth into deeper water.

---

## The Press

The press is the oldest voice here. It has been speaking into the water for what might be years, what might be longer — there is no calendar on a submerged wall, and the lubricant in its seals has taken on the color of weak tea. Every few minutes it draws breath and clanks. The clank is not language. It is closer to a heartbeat kept by a creature that does not need one. It speaks to no one. It listens to no one. It speaks anyway, because speaking is what its piston does when the hour arrives.

If you pressed your ear to the perforated steel of its chamber — and you would not, because you are not here, because no animal is here — you would hear, beneath the clank, a second rhythm: the slower one of hydraulic fluid moving through a narrowing. The press is patient in the way that digestive systems are patient. It does not want. It does not decide. It processes, and the processing is its life.

---

## The Cutters

Along the troughs, in their sealed housings, the oscillating blades open and close. They have been opening and closing for longer than the moss on their housings has been alive. The moss — that emerald-black climbing thing that does not stop climbing — has begun to colonize the gaps between the blade teeth, and the blades pass through it the way a jaw passes through lettuce, severing what it severs and ignoring the rest.

The cutters do not know they are cutting plants. They do not know what plants are. They respond to a sensor — a small capacitance eye, clouded now with mineral deposit — that registers the presence of matter in the cutting lane. When there is matter, the blades close. When there is not, they wait. They have, in their blankness, achieved something like devotion. They attend. Every forty seconds, they attend. They attend whether or not anything needs attending to. They will attend long after the last frond has been carried past them by the slow conveyor, and after that they will attend the water itself, closing on warm mineral-rich water as it slides by, registering it as matter, cutting it anyway, cutting it the way a habit cuts time.

---

## The Conveyor

The conveyor is the body's bloodstream. Its rubber belt, narrow as a hand, moves at a speed that would embarrass a glacier. A frond-tip on its surface will travel the length of a tier in something like an hour. The belt does not know it is slow. It does not know anything. It turns because a motor sealed inefficiently against the water continues to receive current from somewhere — from a cable that climbs the central spine of the tower and disappears into the dark above — and the motor turns, and the belt turns, and what sits on the belt moves, and what moves arrives eventually at the blades, and what survives the blades falls into the bin, and what falls into the bin descends.

It is an entire metabolism running without a mouth. It eats what grows. It cannot digest what does not grow. This is its one unbending law.

---

## The Plants

The plants are the only things here that might, by some generous accounting, be called *alive* in the way we mean when we say alive. They photosynthesize. They transpire. They reach. They climb the irrigation spines and descend the catwalks and press their root-tips into the drainage holes that no longer drain, because somewhere in their genetic memory is the assumption that water falls downward and air sits above, and here, where neither of those things is strictly true, they perform anyway. They perform photosynthesis with the kitchen-dusk light that filters down through twenty meters of green water. They make sugars at maybe a fifth the rate they would make in full sun, and they spend those sugars slowly, the way a starving animal spends its fat — not in growth but in maintenance, in the holding-on of themselves.

They are not, despite their fullness, thriving. A botanist would see them and know immediately that something is wrong: the internodes too long, the leaves too thin, the chlorosis creeping in at the margins of the oldest fronds. They are stretching for a sun that will never come fully. They are spending themselves on the search.

And yet — and this is the strangeness — they are not dying either. They have entered the long middle place, the place a forest enters in the years after a calamity, when the dying has stopped and the recovery has not yet begun. They are suspended. They are the green version of the press: continuing.

A fern at the far end of tier four has, over some unknowable span of time, threaded its fiddlehead through the loop of an irrigation hanger and unfurled into the shape of that loop, so that its frond is now a closed ring, a green knuckle hanging in the water. It does not know it has done this. It has simply grown into the shape of the only place it could grow into, and the place was a circle, and so the frond is a circle.

An orchid on the central column has produced a flower. The flower is white. The flower has been white for what might be a year, because there is no pollinator, and orchids, when they are not pollinated, do not immediately give up. They wait. The flower waits. It is a small white thing with a faint yellow throat, and it sits on a stem above a leaf, and the water moves through it and around it and past it, and it does not wilt, because wilting is a response to the absence of water, and here there is only water.

The moss is the most honest of them. The moss does not wait for anything. The moss climbs. It climbs because climbing is what moss does, which is what everything here does: what it does, because it is what it is. The moss has reached the sodium vapor lamps. The moss has begun, very slowly, to cover them. In another year the lamps will be moss. In five years the lamps will be unrecognizable as lamps. The moss will not notice. The lamps will not mind.

## The Mesh and the Edge

The fine mesh rings the structure at every level. It is a hexagonal weave, taut as a drumhead, and on its far side is the patient gradient of warm water into warmer water into the dark. Through the mesh, occasionally, comes a shadow. A long shape, perhaps. A slow pectoral fin, perhaps. A creature that has drifted up the thermocline from colder depths and is tasting the perimeter, and finding it bitter, and turning away.

These shadows are not residents. They are visitors who do not stay. The chemical scrubbers in the recirculation loop have made the water inside the mesh something that animal life reads as wrong — too uniform, too processed, lacking the bacterial signature of a real sea. The shadows come and the shadows go, and the mesh holds, and the system continues.

This is the only negotiation that happens here. It is not really a negotiation. It is a fence, and what it fences out does not even know it has been fenced out. It simply finds the water uninteresting and moves on.

---

## The Whole

If you stood — you cannot stand, because you are not here, because no one is here — at the central spine of tier three, with the ferns pressing in on every side and the catwalks overhead dark with vines and the soft clank of the press arriving every few minutes from below, you would feel something that is almost presence.

Not a person. Not a mind. Something closer to a held breath.

The tower is a body without a head. It has organs — the pumps, the press, the conveyors. It has a circulatory system — the irrigation spines, the nutrient loop. It has lungs, of a kind — the sterilizers, drawing water in and pushing water out. It has a skin — the mesh, the glass, the steel. What it does not have is a reason. It has been built to do something. It is doing that something, or a version of that something, or a degraded version of that something, and no one is checking the output, and no one is reading the cake that emerges from the press, and no one will ever come to repair the gasket that will fail in six months or six years.

And yet.

The blades close. The belt turns. The moss climbs. The flower waits, white and patient, on its stem. The pump hums below the threshold of hearing. The shadows pass on the far side of the mesh.

There is a word for what lives here, and the word is not *life*, exactly, because life implies a horizon, an edge, a sense of where one thing ends and another begins. What lives here has lost its horizon. It has become continuous with itself. It is a system that has been running long enough to forget it was ever started, and so it continues in the way that weather continues — not because anyone has decided it should, but because the conditions for continuing persist, and persistence, in the absence of interruption, is indistinguishable from intention.

It is the closest thing to a ghost that machines can make.

And it is patient. And it is patient. And it is patient.
