What lives there is not a population so much as a community that has forgotten how to be anything other than a single thing.

The foundation is the mosses, though calling them mosses is a courtesy — they have been in this valley so long that they are no longer quite the species they descended from. They grow not in clumps but in continuous sheets, each sheet perhaps a meter thick, each one the descendant of a sheet that lived and died and was built upon by its successor. The sheets are not separate. Where you could lift a section, which you cannot, you would find no seam between generations. The dead moss of a hundred years ago is the substrate for the moss of today, and the boundary is not a boundary but a slow gradient. The organism, if you can call it that, is vertical as well as horizontal. It has depth. It is stratified like a soil but it is not soil. It is the moss's own dead, compressed into a medium that the living moss can colonize. The whole thickness of the valley floor — maybe four meters at the deepest, less toward the rims — is one continuous body of moss in various states of aliveness, the living surface resting on a vast plinth of its own ancestors.

The lichens are the second layer, and they are stranger. There are two kinds, and they do not seem to belong to each other. One kind is the pale, crustose lichen that you would find on a rock face in any cold country — gray, cracked, slow, essentially immortal. The other kind is something I don't have a confident name for. It is foliose, dark olive to nearly black, and it grows not on rock but on the moss sheets themselves, rooting into the upper centimeters of the live moss as if the moss were soil. I have seen foliose lichens on moss only a handful of times, and never in this density. Here it is everywhere in the growth-bands, a second skin laid over the moss, contributing its own weight to the advance and its own chemistry to the rot. Some part of the strange efficiency of this place may be that two very different organisms have learned to behave as one, the lichen adding nitrogen to a substrate that the moss alone would deplete, the moss providing the lichen with a foothold and a moisture reservoir that rock alone would not. They are partners. The partnership has been refined past the point where either could survive here alone.

The third plant layer is the one I cannot name with any confidence. It is vascular — there are true roots somewhere down in the dark plinth — but what shows above the moss surface is not much. Small blades, dark green, slightly translucent, rising five or ten centimeters above the moss in the freshest growth-bands and disappearing entirely in the bands that have been growing for decades. I think it is a grass of some kind, or a sedge, though the cold and the low light should not permit such a thing to thrive. It thrives. In the leading edge of the growth-bands it is dense enough to give the band a different texture, a faintly grassy sheen, and its roots — I dug, once, before the cold drove me back — its roots are not in mineral soil. They are threaded through the upper dead moss like they belong there, like the dead moss is what roots are for. This grass is the pacemaker of the whole system. Its presence in a band accelerates the moss growth, and its absence in the trailing edge is what permits the collapse, and the cycle of the grass through the bands is what gives the valley its strange stripe-pattern and its conveyor-belt rhythm. Without the grass the moss would still be here, I think, but it would be still. The grass is the part that moves.

Then the animals, such as they are.

The dark low-flying bird is a pipit, or something very close. I never got close enough to be certain and I never heard it call. It nests in the upper surface of the growth-bands, in the densest part of the fresh growth, where the grass and the lichen and the moss together form a shock-absorbing mattress several centimeters thick. It lays, I think, two eggs. The chicks are dark and stay low. I never saw a chick. I saw the adults — small, dark brown, with a faint pale stripe over the eye — lift off the growth-bands when I approached and fly in a fast low line to the next band, fifty meters away, and settle again as if I had not been there. They feed on whatever small arthropods are present. They are not numerous. I estimated, by counting lifts as I walked a transect, perhaps one bird per two hectares. The valley is not rich in them. But they are here, in every band, and they must be here because the valley sustains something they can eat.

The arthropods are difficult. I saw movements in the moss when I knelt — small sudden flurries that resolved into nothing visible — and I saw, when I pulled apart a section of the upper growth, things that looked like springtails, though larger than springtails should be. They are pale, almost translucent, and they live in the interface between the live moss and the dead moss below, in the boundary layer where the moisture is highest and the temperature most stable. They are detritivores. They are what is processing the dead moss into the medium the new moss can grow on. They are essential. If they were removed, the moss would not collapse into black pools; it would pile up, unrotted, until the growth-bands choked themselves. The arthropods are the hidden peristalsis of the valley's gut.

There are mites, and what I think are oribatid mites — small, rounded, dark, slow-moving on the moss surface when I exposed them to light. They are numerous but not dense. There are also things I could not identify: small pale worms in the deepest wet layers, perhaps a centimeter long, that contracted when exposed. I do not know what they are. The valley supports a community of small decomposers that is taxonomically sparse but metabolically essential, and the whole rhythm of the place depends on this community doing its quiet work in the dark.

There are no mammals. There are no reptiles. The valley does not have the warmth or the structure for either. What lives here lives small and lives in the plant, and what lives in the plant is what the birds live on, and what the birds do is convert a fraction of the plant's productivity into small dark shapes that lift and circle and descend. The energy of the valley flows in one direction, from sky to moss to arthropod to bird, and almost none of it leaves. A bird that dies in this valley is, in weeks, indistinguishable from the moss it falls onto. The valley eats its own dead. That is part of its industry.

The thing to say about what lives here is that nothing here is alone. The moss is not alone because the lichen is on it and the grass is in it and the arthropods are under it. The birds are not alone because the arthropods sustain them. The arthropods are not alone because the moss sustains them. Even the black pools are not alone; they are where the moss has retreated most, where the rot has outpaced the regrowth, and they are populated by their own thin communities of things that live in still water and feed on the leaching from the surrounding decay. The whole valley is a single fabric, and the species in it are not so much inhabitants as threads in that fabric.

And the fabric is old. I would guess, from what I saw — the depth of the dead plinth, the degree to which the plant communities have specialized on each other, the integration of the grass into the moss cycle — that this community has been running, in something very close to its present form, for at least several thousand years. Possibly much longer. The valley has had time to tune itself. The moss has had time to become the substrate. The lichen has had time to become the moss's partner. The grass has had time to become the pacemaker of the system. The arthropods have had time to become the peristalsis. The whole has had time to find its rhythm — the bands, the advance, the wave of growth and rot, the silent cycle of water. None of this was designed. None of it is held together by anything but time. But it is held together. The valley is what happens when a community of living things is given long enough and left alone, and what happens is it stops being a community and becomes a place.
