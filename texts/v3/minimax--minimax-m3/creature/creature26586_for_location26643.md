What lives here does not really live *here*. It lives in a way that has so thoroughly merged with the place that pulling one from the other would leave neither recognizable. The place is what its lives have made of it, and the lives are what the place has made of itself, and the boundary between them is something neither party has bothered to maintain.

It is best to begin with what makes the bloom, because the bloom is the foundation, and everything else is, in one way or another, built upon it.

The bloom is not a thing. The bloom is a thing that is many things. It is a community of microorganisms so small that to see one you would need to see at scales the place does not permit, and so numerous that to count them would require more patience than anyone has brought. They live in films. They live in strata. They live in a layered mat that is, on its surface, the soft white bloom you could almost touch, and in its depths, a darker, denser community that has metabolized for so long in the dark that it has become, itself, a kind of geology. Some of them are bacteria. Some of them are archaea — different organisms, related only by the ancient fact that they are small and have learned to live where nothing else can. Some of them eat sulfur. Some of them eat iron. Some of them eat methane that bubbles up through the cracks in the floor from somewhere deeper than the chamber. Some of them eat light — not very much light, not direct light, but the thin pewter column that falls from above and is already exhausted by the time it reaches them. They are chemosynthetic and photosynthetic in some obscure ratio. They are the foundation. Without them, nothing else would be here. They are the ones who precipitate the pale layered stone, molecule by molecule, the structures that crowd the chamber in the way brains have folds. They do not know they are building. They are simply being themselves in a way that happens to leave a trace.

They have been here longer than anything visible. They were here when the chamber was smaller. They will be here when the chamber has filled in, when the structures have grown thick enough to close themselves off, when the cold patient water has been replaced by something else or by nothing. They are the patience of the place, at its smallest scale.

Then the fingers.

The fingers are the first of the visible lives. They are pale and slow and they withdraw when touched. They are not one creature. They are many creatures, perhaps related, perhaps not — a phylum, or a class, or simply a way of being alive that has been reinvented several times by evolution under similar conditions. They are worms, or worm-like. They have no skeletons. They have no eyes. They have no organs that would be familiar to a surface creature except in the loosest sense — a mouth at one end, an anus at the other, a body that is mostly gut, in the way worms are mostly gut. They live in the soft places — in the cracks, in the seams where water has eaten into the stone, in the small dark mouths that connect one structure to another. They are soft the way jellyfish are soft, the way a thought is soft. Their integrity is the integrity of fluid held in by membranes that are thinner than language.

When you touch them, they withdraw. This is what they do. They pull back into the crack, into the seam, into the small dark place they came from. They do not flee, exactly — they have nothing to flee toward. They simply make themselves smaller. They reduce the surface area they present to the world. They trust that the world will lose interest, or will not follow them into the narrow places where they live. Most of the time, this works. Most of the time, whatever touched them is not a predator — it is a current, or a piece of debris, or the slow settling of sediment. The withdrawal is a reflex. It has been selected for because the cost of withdrawing is small and the cost of not withdrawing, sometimes, is being eaten.

They eat the bloom. They scrape it from the stone with surfaces that are not recognizable as mouths — with the whole of their undersides, perhaps, or with a ventral surface that secretes enzymes and absorbs what has been digested externally. They are grazers. They are the dominant macro-invertebrate of the place. They are so numerous that the structures of the chamber are partly shaped by their grazing — the pale layered growth has the texture it has because the fingers have eaten some of it before it could fully develop. They keep the bloom from closing over the place entirely. They are the balance between building and unbuilding.

Some of them are smaller. Some of them are larger. Some of them are translucent. Some of them are opaque. Some of them are pale in different ways — the pallor of absence of pigment, the pallor of pigment bled out, the pallor of being made mostly of water. They vary. They have not been catalogued. They have not been given the kind of attention that would result in names.

Then the mucus collars.

The collars are different from the fingers in almost every way except that they, too, are soft. They do not withdraw when touched. They do not move at all, except in the slow way that growing things move. They are not individuals in the way fingers are individuals — or rather, they are individuals but their individuality has been subordinated to a function. They are filter feeders. They have secreted, around themselves, a collar of mucus that spreads out into the patient current like a net. The net is alive. It is alive the way a tongue is alive — not a separate creature, but an extension of the creature that made it, still chemically connected to the body that produced it, still receiving signals from the body, still being regulated by the body.

The collars pulse. This is the thing you notice about them. They pulse in a rhythm that is not breath, not heartbeat, but the rhythm of the net being expanded and contracted to push water through itself, to filter out whatever the water is carrying. The pulse is slow — once every few seconds, perhaps, or once every few tens of seconds. It varies. Some collars pulse faster in faster currents. Some collars pulse slower. The pulse is not synchronized between collars. Each collar has its own rhythm, its own response to the water around it, its own life that is happening in its own time.

They live where the currents are best. They live at the mouths of cracks, where water enters and exits the chamber. They live at the high points of the structures, where the patient threads along the ceiling come down closest to the floor. They live in the way plants live — rooted, sessile, dependent on what comes to them. They do not seek food. They wait for food to come to them. They have made the patience of the place into a strategy.

Some of the collars are solitary. Some of the collars are clustered — many of them near one another, sharing the same current, occupying the same small piece of the structure, their mucus nets interpenetrating one another's in ways that are hard to see. In the dense places, the nets fuse and become a single mat of mucus that covers a portion of the structure like a second bloom — but living, pulsing, filtering. In those places, it is hard to say where one creature ends and another begins. The collars have become a colony. The colony is alive in a way that is different from the way any individual is alive.

Then the small black shapes.

The small black shapes are predators. They are watchers. They are not large — the size of a thumb, perhaps, or a fist, or smaller. They are dark in a way that is not the dark of the absence of light. They are dark with pigment, with melanin or something like it, which is unusual in a place this dark and which suggests they have not been in the dark as long as the fingers, or that they retain pigment for reasons other than camouflage. They hold themselves still against the pale growth. They hold themselves so still that they look like part of the structure, like a feature of the stone, like a thing that has always been there.
