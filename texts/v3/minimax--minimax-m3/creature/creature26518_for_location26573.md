What lives here does not announce itself. The first thing you might meet — and you could walk a long time without meeting it, because the floor is wider than any measure a single visitor could take in — is not an animal. It is the stalks. But the stalks are not one thing, and they are not, exactly, what they appear to be.

The stalks are towers. Each tower is a single structure three feet tall, leaning in the current at the same angle all the way down to a holdfast that grips the shell-pavement like a black, crusty hand. The towers look rigid. They are not rigid. They are accreted. They were built, plate by plate, by something small and soft, over a span of time that has no human-scale analogue.

The plate at the base is fused to the floor. It has been there so long it is part of the floor. The plate at the tip — the one that still holds a faint, mother-of-pearl shine — was laid down recently, last season, last decade, by something that lived only at the tip. Between those two plates are hundreds of others, each one the work of a generation. Each generation lived at the tip, fed on what the current brought, built the next plate, and then went still. Its plate became the next plate's floor. Its calcium was partly reabsorbed; the rest was inherited. The tower grew. The tower leaned, because the current never stopped leaning it. The lean is permanent. It is in the calcium. There are towers here whose entire structure is held in a permanent curve, like the spine of a question mark set in stone by water.

What built the tower is, in the strict sense, not visible. It is a small soft thing pressed into the tip, mostly hidden by the plate it is laying down. It has no eyes — there is nothing here for eyes to do. It has a mouth, but the mouth is small and used rarely. Most of what it needs comes from somewhere else: from the bacterial mat that wraps the base of the tower and spreads a little way across the shells. The mat is the city's market. The mat is a packed layer of microbes, sulfur-yellow at the chemical level, gray at the level of the eye, that oxidizes the sulfide that seeps up through the shell-pavement and pulls a thin living from the diffuse organic drizzle that falls from the upper sea. The tower-builder does not eat the mat. The tower-builder hosts the mat's cousins — different bacteria, slightly more particular, that need a body to live in — and in exchange the bacteria pass sugars back across the partnership. The tower-builder and the bacteria have been each other's home for so long that neither is quite sure, any longer, who built the tower. They pass calcium back and forth. They pass time.

The tower is, then, a kind of life. Not one life — a thousand lives, layered, a kind of vertical city whose only citizens are successive generations of the same small soft thing, and whose economy runs on chemistry too slow to see.

That is one kind of inhabitant.

Another kind is much smaller, and much more numerous, and lives not on the towers but in the silt. The silt is alive. The silt is more alive than the water by an order of magnitude you could not easily count. Every grain is inhabited. Every grain is a city of bacteria on its surface, a honeycomb of bored tunnels inside it, and a kind of borderland at its edge where predators — smaller things, things that swim on flagella so fine you would need a glass made of light to see them — drift between grains looking for prey. The silt has its own predators. The silt has its own viruses. The silt has things that prey on the viruses. The silt is a forest of prey. Most of the silt's citizens are doing nothing most of the time. They wait. They drift a little. They wait. They divide, very slowly, once a year, once a decade, on a schedule that has more in common with the orbits of planets than with anything a warm-blooded observer would recognize as life. The haze you could almost drink is alive in nearly every mouthful.

The shells, too, are alive, in their lower floors. The ancient matte shells — the ones that have fused into the pavement — are riddled with bore-holes. Endolithic worms have threaded through them. Endolithic fungi have threaded through them. Endolithic things without a name have threaded through them. The calcium has become, in places, mostly them — a scaffold of mineral packed with the living threads of organisms that will never come out, that have not come out in geological ages, that pass the calcium back and forth between themselves and the chemical sea they live in. If you put your ear to the floor — if you had an ear, here, and if you could put it to the floor without being buried in silt — you would hear, in the sub-audible, a hum. The hum is the sum of all these tiny metabolisms. It is the reason the floor is not, despite its appearance, a dead thing.

And then there are the things you can see.

The pale darting thing. The hand-sized creature with the one black eye. It is the most visible animal here, which is not saying much. You might pass a long time without seeing one. You might see three in an afternoon. They live among the humps, where the shells have piled into lee-shelters, where the current breaks against a whale-backed rise and produces, on the down-current side, a pocket of comparative stillness.

It is transparent because there is no light to hide from and no light to advertise with, and being transparent is cheaper than being pigmented. It has one eye because two would be redundant in a world with no shadows and a uniform sourceless gray. The eye is black because black, here, is the absence of any other option; the eye is mostly a pressure sensor, a motion sensor, a thing that registers the disturbance in the water when something moves through its visual field. It has a long tail that is mostly fin, and a small soft body, and a mouth it uses often. It eats the tower-tips. It eats the bacterial mats. It eats other darters, when it is large enough and they are small enough.

It has a larval stage that drifts in the current, far above the floor, that may take years to settle. Almost none settle. Most drift until something eats them, or until they starve, or until they are buried. The basin is a sieve for its own young. The ones you see are the ones who got lucky.

Their name, if they had one, would be a word from a language no longer spoken by anyone who has ever seen one.

And then there is the shadow.

The shadow is what you sense rather than see. It is the long, slow shape at the edge of perception. It moves in a way that movement should not move — too slow for an animal, too deliberate for a current, too consistent for a shadow. It suggests mass. It suggests mass the way a dark room suggests the furniture: by the way the room is held differently where the furniture is.

The shadow is not one thing. There are several of them in this basin, cruising at different depths, on different schedules, in slow transverse gyres that take days to complete. You cannot tell them apart. You cannot tell whether they see you. You cannot tell whether they care.

They are very large. They are not as large as the basin, but they are larger than the humps, and longer than any darter, and older than any tool. They are cartilaginous, or gelatinous, or something between. They have no working teeth, or no teeth at all. They feed by sweeping the current. They drift with their mouths open, very wide, in the path of anything small enough to fit, and they close their mouths when enough has accumulated. They are not built for pursuit. They are built for patience. Their fins are short. Their tails are long and used only for steering. Their muscle is mostly white, for slow cruising, with very little red. They breathe what little oxygen the cold water holds — and the water here holds almost none. They breathe slowly. They breathe once every few minutes. Their heart beats eight times a minute. Their metabolism, by any warm-blooded standard, is barely above that of a plant.

Are they the adults of the darters? No one knows. There is no one to know. The darters and the shadows do not appear to be the same creature at different ages. They may share a distant ancestor. They may not. They live in the same basin and they tolerate each other and that is all that can be said.

The shadows are the closest things in this basin to a memory of the upper sea. They have, in the cartilage of their skulls, traces of structures that, in fishier animals, would have been a swim bladder, an echo-location organ, an electrical sense. These structures are vestigial. They do not work. They are leftovers. They are the equivalent of the tools in the lee of the humps — something made once, by something that lived once, that is here now because no one has come to clear it away.

And then there are the tools.

The tools were made by a hand. A hand held the chert and struck it against another stone, and the flake came off, and the flake had a sharp edge, and the sharp edge was used, and the hand let go of it, and the tool settled, and the basin took it. The hand was warm. The hand had blood in it. The hand had a wrist and a forearm and a shoulder and a brain that, somewhere in its convoluted surface, knew that this place was a place, knew that it could go to places, knew that it could make a tool, knew that it could leave the tool here. The hand is long since gone. The bones of the hand are somewhere in the shell-pavement, indistinguishable from the other shells. The calcium has been reabsorbed. The phosphorus has been reabsorbed. The marrow, the blood, the warm wet chemistry of a mammal — all gone, all returned to the diffuse drizzle that falls from the upper sea and feeds the mat at the base of the towers.

Only the tools remain. The chert hand-axe. The wedge of volcanic glass. The bone needle still threaded with a length of sinew that has not dried, because nothing here is dry. They lie against the rises as if placed there deliberately, though no one placed them. They are preserved, which is not the same as being kept.

There are no descendants of the hand in this basin. There may never have been. The hand came down — perhaps from a shore, perhaps from a shallows — for a reason no one will ever recover. The hand did not stay. The hand was not built for this. The hand is a tourist in the geological record of this place, a brief warmth in a billion years of cold, a single visitor whose only remaining trace is three small objects that will outlast everything the visitor's species ever built above the tide-line.

What lives here, then, is what has been negotiated over that billion years. It is small. It is patient. It is built to wait. It is built to wait in the dark, in the cold, in the haze of silt, on the bones of the sea, in the lee of the whale-backed humps that have slept through all of it. It does not know it is alone. It does not know there is any other kind of life. It does not know that the light it does not see exists. It only knows the current. It only knows the silt. It only knows the next shell to fuse, the next tower-plate to lay, the next slow gulp of water, the next decade.

The gray persists. The cold persists. The towers lean. The pale things dart. The shadows drift. The silt hums, faintly, in the sub-audible. The tools lie in the lee of the humps and do not move and do not corrode and do not forget, because they were never alive enough to forget.

The basin is inhabited. The basin is full. The basin is, by long negotiation with itself, exactly as alive as it can be, and no more, and no less, and it will go on being so for a long time after the last thing that could have described it has also become a shell in the pavement, leaning, in the current, into the dark.
