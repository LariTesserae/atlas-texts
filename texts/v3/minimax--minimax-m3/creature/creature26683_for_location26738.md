# The Inhabitants of Veth Khariv

Begin with the listening. That is what Veth Khariv does to you first, before anything else — it makes you listen. The newcomers stand in the cable-station plaza with their mouths slightly open, their hands at their sides, and what they are doing, though they do not yet know it, is listening. The flushers among them especially — the ones still red-faced from the ascent, the ones whose lips are faintly blue and whose breath comes short — are listening with their whole bodies, because the altitude has thinned them, has made their skulls lighter, has cleared the wax from their ears. They are receiving the hum for the first time without anything between them and it. They do not yet know that they will, in time, become part of it.

The locals have a name for those who have been here long enough that the hum has gotten into their sleep. They are called *the tuned*. They are not specialists. They are not musicians or engineers or any of the things you might expect. They are just people who have lived here long enough — thirty years, forty, longer — and whose bodies have begun to resonate at the city's frequency. A tuned sleeper can nap in the lower quarter with the refineries running at full bore and the cable station clanking over their head, and they will wake, suddenly and completely, if the hum shifts even a quarter-tone. They have been known to call the cable station to report a fault they felt in their sleep an hour before any instrument did. They are slightly embarrassed about this. They do not advertise it. But if you live here long enough, you start to know who they are, because they are the ones who go quietest at certain moments, the ones who tilt their heads slightly during a silence, the ones who, in the middle of a conversation about something else entirely, will say, mildly, that the smelting works on the east face sound different today, and they will be right.

## The Walkers

After listening, the second thing you notice is the gait. The people of Veth Khariv move slowly, and the slowness has become, over centuries, something more than a habit — it has become a kind of grace. They walk as if they are listening to music no one else can hear. They pause in doorways. They lean on parapets. They watch the water go by. The slowness is not laziness; it is adaptation, and it is also, somehow, an aesthetic choice, the pace at which the city reveals itself. To walk fast here is to be vulgar. To walk fast and pant is to be a flusher. To walk at the city's pace is to begin to belong to it.

There are certain inhabitants who have made the slowness into an art. The parapet-leaners are a recognized population — the retired refiners, the widowed olive-pressers, the old women who have outlived three husbands and still keep a small kitchen garden on the upper terrace. They are the ones you see at any hour of the day, in any season, leaning on the carved limestone rails and looking out at the view, which is mostly the inside of the city, because the city is so built-up that the view is often a view of other buildings, or of the sky through a canyon of stone. They watch the pigeons. They watch the children. They watch the water go by. They are not bored. They are paying attention. They will tell you, if you sit with them long enough, that the city is a slow animal, and you have to learn to be still to see it move.

Then there are the stair-walkers. Stairs are the city's true streets, and there are people who seem to spend most of their lives on them — the courier with her bag of ore samples going down to the refineries, the grandmother climbing up to visit a daughter, the priest (and there are priests here, of the city's two religions and the unofficial third, which is pigeon-racing) making his rounds to the upper chapels. The stair-walkers develop, over years, a particular carriage: knees slightly bent, weight low, hands often behind the back. They take the long flights without apparent effort. They know which treads are worn in the middle and which along the edges. They can tell you, by the way their foot falls on a particular step, what year it was relaid.

And then there are the children, who are the only ones who run. They run because they have not yet learned the gait. They are flushed and gasping, their too-large coats flapping, and they splash through the shallow water in the lower squares with the particular glee of someone who has discovered that they cannot fall. The children also eat the pomegranates in late summer, the small sour ones from the dwarf trees, with salt from a twist of paper. They do this on the stairs, in groups, and their fingers and lips are stained for days. They are the most recent arrivals, in a sense, and they have not yet learned not to ask the questions that the older residents know better than to ask.

## The Water-Keepers

Water is the city's first obsession and its first necessity, and so there are people whose lives are built around it. The cistern-tenders, who live in the small stone rooms attached to each of the great cistern-towers, are the closest thing the city has to a priesthood of the obvious. They check the levels twice a day. They clean the screens that keep out the pigeons (the pigeon-keepers and the cistern-tenders are, by tradition, slightly at odds, though they will share a pipe). They read the small gauges that show how much water has come in from the glacier and how much has gone out to the mains. They know, without instruments, when a channel is blocked. They know when a joint in the pipework is beginning to weep. They know, by a faint change in the smell of the morning air, when the lichen on the north walls has had a good night.

There are also the gutter-walkers, who are younger and lower in the hierarchy, and whose job it is to clear the carved ruts that carry the overflow from the public basins. They work in the early morning, before the squares fill up. They are the only people in the city who regularly kneel. Their knees are remarkable.

And there are the basin-sitters, who are not employed by anyone and who simply sit, on the stone benches that ring the public basins in the lower squares, and watch the water. They are mostly old. They are mostly women, though not entirely. They pull their chairs just out of the water's reach. They do not speak much. They are the city's quietest inhabitants, and they are paying attention to something.

## The Pigeon-Keepers

Pigeon-racing is the second religion, and the pigeon-keepers are its ordained. They are an entire recognized profession, and they are organized, loosely, into a guild that has no charter and no written rules and that meets, in theory, every new moon in a courtyard near the upper chapels, though the meetings are often called off for weather or for the racing calendar or for reasons no one can quite articulate. Each keeper maintains one or more coops on a rooftop, and the coops are, by ordinance, painted in one of seven colors. The keepers communicate with one another across the city in a complicated semaphore of colored flags and shouts. The shouts are an entire language, learned in childhood, and a stranger cannot tell them apart, but the keepers can tell you, by the angle of a flag and the particular vowel-shape of a cry from three rooftops away, exactly which coop the message is coming from and whether the bird being discussed is home yet.

The pigeon-keepers are the noisiest inhabitants of the city, and they are proud of it. They are also the most superstitious. They will not race on the new moon. They will not race in fog. They will not release a bird that has eaten salt. They have, between them, an entire cosmology of omens, and they consult it constantly, in the same way the tuned consult the hum. The two populations, the tuned and the pigeon-keepers, overlap more than either admits.

The pigeons themselves are not really kept; they are tolerated, even encouraged, and they have become, over generations, slightly different from the lowland pigeons. They are a little smaller. They are a little darker. They know the city in a way that no human does, because they fly over it rather than through it, and they have learned the thermal patterns of the heated air rising from the refineries, and they ride them in long loops above the rooftops, and they come home, almost always, before the keepers start to worry.

## The Refiners and the Ore-Workers

The industrial inhabitants of Veth Khariv are a different kind of being from the others. They work in shifts in the refineries built into the rock, and they emerge, at the end of a shift, looking as if they have been underground for a long time, which they have. Their faces are pale. Their eyes are slow to adjust to the daylight. They carry with them, for an hour or so after they come up, a faint chemical sharpness that is the smell of the ore. They are slightly more silent than the other inhabitants, as if the work has tuned them to a different frequency. They are also, by local reputation, slightly more patient than the others, and slightly less given to superstition, though they have their own quiet rituals — the way they wash their hands at the basin before leaving the refinery yard, the way they do not speak on the long stairway down to the lower quarter, the way they look, when they emerge, first at the sky and only second at the people they are with.

The slag-shapers, who work in the yard at the city's lowest point where the still-warm slag is dumped to cool in slabs, are a tougher population. They are darker-skinned than the rest, because they work outdoors in the brightest light. They are louder. They are the ones who, in the early afternoon, when the rest of the city has gone quiet in the worst of the heat, can be heard hammering. They are also the ones who, by tradition, eat the first pomegranates of the season, because they are the ones who work near the pomegranate terraces, and they have the privilege, unstated but unchallenged, of picking the ripest fruit.

The ore itself, the refined ingots that come out of the smelting works, glow faintly and are heavier than they should be. The refiners do not speak of this. They do not speak of the ore at all, except in the technical vocabulary of their trade. There is, in the city, a sense that the ore is the city's secret, just as the thing in the lower cistern is, and just as the glacier is, and the pomegranates are, and the slight uncanniness is. The ore-workers know what they are making. They know it is not entirely an ordinary metal. They do not discuss this with anyone.

## The Cats

The cats are more mysterious than the other inhabitants, and they have been allowed to remain so. They are not fed so much as they simply *are*, a baseline population that fluctuates with the seasons and the food supply but never disappears. They sit on windowsills and judge you. They appear on stair landings when you are troubled and seem to know. They are, almost without exception, of a particular coloring — a soft dun with darker points — that no one has ever seen in a lowland cat. They have been here for as long as anyone can remember.

The story is that the cats belong to the glacier, and the glacier sent them down to keep the city company. This is, of course, not true. But it is not not-true either, and the older residents will tell you, if you press them, that the cats appeared first on the upper terraces, near the snow line, and have been working their way down ever since, and that each generation is slightly more acclimatized to the lower quarters than the last, and that the cats in the lowest squares are not quite the same as the cats in the highest. The cats do not, themselves, confirm or deny this. They sit on windowsills. They watch you. They know things they are not telling.

There is a particular cat — or there is always said to be a particular cat — who lives on the parapet of the lower cistern-tower, and who is older than any of the other cats, and who is watched by the old women who leave the small coins and the salt on the parapets. This cat is not spoken of by name. The cat knows.

## The Lizards

The small brown lizards that live in the walls are the city's least remarked inhabitants, and they are also its most numerous. They live in the cracks between the dressed stone, in the crevices of the older buildings, in the joints of the pipework, in the seams of the cistern-towers. They eat the insects that come for the moisture, and they are, in their quiet way, the reason the city is not overrun with the small flies that would otherwise be inevitable. They appear, briefly, when the sun hits a particular wall in the morning, and they are gone again. The children try to catch them and cannot. The cats occasionally hunt them. The lizards do not seem to mind. They have been here longer than the cats.

## The Thing in the Lower Cistern

No one speaks of it. It is large. It moves at night. The water in the morning is slightly disturbed.

This is all that can be said, and perhaps all that should be said. There are inhabitants who have seen it, or who claim to have seen it, and they are not the kind of people who claim things. They will not describe it. They will say only that it is large, and that it moves in a way that water does not usually move, and that in the morning the surface of the cistern is not quite level. The refiners do not speak of it. The cistern-tenders do not speak of it. The old women who leave the coins and the salt leave them, sometimes, on the parapet of the lower cistern-tower, and they do not explain why.

The thing in the lower cistern is the city's deepest secret, and it is not mine to tell. I will only say that the city has lived with it for a long time, and that the city intends to live with it for a long time more.

## The Old Ones

The older residents of Veth Khariv are a population of their own. They have been here for decades. They have been tuned. They move at the city's pace. They leave the offerings on the parapets. They watch the new moon. They know, without being told, when the hum has shifted. They are the city's memory, and they are also, in some quiet way, the city's conscience. They will not say what they know. They will sit with you on a stairway, in the late amber light of a summer evening, and they will tell you, if you are quiet enough and patient enough, that the city is alive in a way that other cities are not, and that the aliveness has been here longer than any of them, and that it will be here longer than any of them, and that the right way to live here is to accept this and not to ask too many questions.

They are the most patient inhabitants of the city, and also the most slightly strange. They do not look at you directly. They look at you the way the cats look at you — as if they have been briefed, as if they are waiting for you to understand something you are not yet ready to understand. They are kind to the flushers. They are kind to the children. They are kind to the cats. They are the city's quietest, and most attentive, inhabitants.

## The Glacier

The glacier is not, strictly, an inhabitant. But it is alive in the way the city is alive, and it has been here longer, and it is leaving. The locals do not speak of its leaving. They speak of it as if it is permanent. They speak of it as the source of the water, and the reason the lichen grows, and the place where the cats came from. They will tell you, if you ask, that the glacier has been here for a thousand years, and that it will be here for a thousand more. They will not look at the white patch above the city, which is smaller than it was, and smaller than the year before, and smaller again. They will change the subject.

The glacier is the city's oldest inhabitant, in the sense that it has shaped the city's life the way a parent shapes a child's. It has given them water. It has given them the cats. It has given them the lichen-perfume of the early morning. It has, by its slow retreat, given them the sense that the city is a thing that must be defended, and the offerings on the parapets, and the slight uncanniness that lives in the fact that nothing here is quite as stable as it appears.

The glacier is leaving. The city knows. The city will not say.

## The City Itself

And then there is the city, which is the inhabitant of all the others, and which has been shaped, in turn, by them. The city is not really an inhabitant, and yet it is, in the same way the hum is an inhabitant, and the water is an inhabitant, and the slight uncanniness is an inhabitant. The city has made the people in its own image — slow, listening, attentive, slightly strange, slightly patient. The people have made the city in their own image — built of dressed stone and copper pipework, full of stairs and cisterns and pigeon-coops and offered coins, layered and ancient and always adding a new quarter. The cats have made the city. The pigeons have made the city. The glacier, in its slow retreat, has made the city.

Veth Khariv is inhabited, in the deepest sense, by itself. If you go — and you should go, in the late summer, when the pomegranates are fruiting and the light is at its most amber — you will sit on a stairway and you will watch the water go by and you will listen to the hum, and you will feel, after a while, that you are not really a visitor. You are one more thing that has come to live here, and the city is deciding, slowly, whether to keep you.
