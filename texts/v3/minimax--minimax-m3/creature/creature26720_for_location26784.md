# The Residents of the Salt-Flat Settlement

They are sixty-three, this season. The number is approximate. Some weeks the count is higher. The most recent arrival is a woman named Thekla who came up from the south with two cases and a child who has not yet learned to count rooms. The most long-standing resident is uncertain; the question of who arrived first is folded into the same uncertainty as the question of which wall was the first wall. The settlement accretes people the way it accretes structure — gently, without announcement, by the patient addition of itself to itself.

Most of the residents were born elsewhere. This is not a place that produces its own; the cold and the damp and the patchworked light do not permit the full biological contract of childhood, the long bright summers of growth. Children who arrive stay or leave. Those who stay become something the settlement recognizes. Those who leave return, sometimes, after decades, with a strange half-orientation, a sense of the place having continued without them in a way that the world outside does not.

The residents are quiet people, but the quiet is not a temperament. It is an accommodation. The damp air will not carry a shout across more than two courtyards. A raised voice loses its edges in the wet. The cold settles in the throat and turns the voice low, breathy, a little hoarse. The residents speak softly out of practicality, and over generations the softness has become a way of being. Visitors from outside are often mistaken for being angry because the residents never seem to be.

The residents know the seams.

Not intellectually — or not only intellectually — but in the body. They have grown up walking from one physics to another and learned the small adjustments without ever being taught them. They know to step carefully through the door of the upper bakery, where momentum is strange; they have learned the particular gait, the slight lean, the way to let the room take their weight without fighting it. They know that the corner room above the old tannery has light that bends in the late afternoon, and they plan their reading accordingly. They know which floors creak and which floors mean it. They know that the cold in the eastern rooms is a different cold from the cold in the western rooms, though the difference is too small to be a complaint and too real to be ignored. They know these things the way a person knows their own skeleton — not by looking, but by the small daily negotiation of moving through themselves.

There is a man named Saro who maintains the seams. This is not a job anyone was hired to do. It is something he began doing and then continued doing, the way a person becomes the one who feeds a cat. He walks the settlement in the mornings with a small case of tools — not all of them recognizable as tools — and he tests the joins. He puts his hand against a wall and feels for the small disagreement between one room and the next. He steps through a doorway and listens. He has a small brass instrument he holds to the air at the thresholds, and the instrument tells him something, though nobody else can read its dials. When a seam is wearing thin, he tends it. The tending is a slow private craft. He does not explain it. The seams are patient and his work is patient and the two understand each other.

There is a woman named Halla who keeps time.

This is also not a job. She came to the settlement thirty years ago with a doctorate in a field that exists only at three universities, and she discovered that her training — the calibration of small instruments, the synchronization of divergent signals — was exactly what the settlement required, though it had not known to ask. She maintains the timepieces. She goes from room to room with a reference device and a long thin cable and she listens to the clocks. The clocks are mostly small, worn, of various makes, kept in pockets and on wrists and embedded in the skin of older residents who chose long ago to let the time live in them. The clocks drift. The clocks drift differently in different physics, as clocks will. Halla brings them back into correspondence. She does not bring them to a single time; she brings them into a loose federation, a kind of time that knows it is not the only time. The residents are aware, always, that their minutes are not identical to their neighbors' minutes. The small wrist-devices keep this from mattering. The settlement is a place where six o'clock is a range.

There is a child — not Thekla's, a different one, a boy of perhaps nine who has been here since he was three — who has begun to see the seams without being taught.

This is considered unusual. The seams are a craft-knowledge, an artisan thing, passed down in the small unspoken curriculum of growing up here. To see them directly, to look at a wall and perceive the place where one physics leans against another, is something most residents achieve only in fragments, only in glimpses, only at the corner of vision. The boy sees them as a feature of the world. He has begun drawing them. His drawings are strange and accurate. He is watched, gently, by people who are not sure what they are watching for.

There is a couple in the western rooms who have been together for forty-one years and who sleep, now, in different physics. This is not a separation. The bedroom they share — the small room with the morning light that bends in a particular way — has become, over decades, a room whose time is slightly slower than the rest of the settlement. They have each adapted differently. She, who is older by three years, has begun to feel the difference as a kind of slowness in the mornings; he, who is younger, has not yet noticed. They have spoken about it. They have decided to stay in the room. The decision is not a small one. A minute a day, compounded, is a different season by retirement. They are aware. They are quiet about it, the way the residents are quiet about most things.

There is an old woman, perhaps the oldest, who lives in a single room on the ground floor of the oldest structure. Her room has a gravity slightly heavier than the rest of the settlement, and she moves through it slowly, with the deliberation of someone who has decided that slowness is a kind of dignity. She does not go out. She receives visitors. She has lived in the settlement since before the seams were maintained, since before the timepieces, since before there was a name for what the place was. She is the settlement's only resident who remembers the substrate being walked on for the first time. She does not speak of this often. When she does, the room is quiet and the visitors are quiet and the seam between her physics and theirs leans in to listen.

The residents eat together, in shifts, in the one room of the settlement that has been permitted to be communal. The room has been arranged so that its physics is as close to standard as any room in the settlement — a small consensus, a place where the locals can meet on common ground. The food is the food of a cold wet place where nothing blooms. It comes from elsewhere, in trade, or it comes from the lichens, which the residents have learned to prepare in seven different ways, none of which make the lichens less lichenous. The meals are quiet. The meals are the noisiest event of the week, by a small margin.

The residents have names. The names are mostly from the surrounding region, mostly from languages that have not been the dominant language for a century. The names are old in a way the residents are not old. A child here is named Yarrow or Sedge or Tern, and the names fit the place, and the place does not quite fit the names. The names are a kind of import. The residents have imported, along with their food, a small archive of small human things: the names, the recipes, the small habits of being a person. The settlement is a place where being a person is the dominant import.

The residents do not marry often. When they do, the marriages are small ceremonies, witnessed by a few, registered in no record that exists outside the settlement. The marriages are recognized by the place itself, which is to say, by the slow accretion of two lives into a single accommodation of physics. Couples who have been together a long time develop, the residents notice, a tendency to prefer the same rooms. Not a single room — the privacy of separate physics is too valued — but adjacent rooms, rooms whose seams are thin and known. There is a kind of architectural intimacy. The settlement couples in its own slow way.

The residents die here, sometimes. The death rate is low; the cold preserves, in its way, and the patchworked physics does not seem to trouble the larger biological project. When a resident dies, the room is left empty for a season. The room's physics adjusts. The settling of grief is also a settling of the seams. A new resident will arrive, eventually, and the room will learn them, and the room's small implementation of small laws will negotiate the new presence. The settlement grieves the way it accretes — gently, without announcement, by the slow addition of an absence to the rest of the structure.

The residents are aware, in the deep unspoken way, that they are a kind of experiment. Not a cruel one. Not even an intentional one. The settlement is what it is; they live in it. They have made a life in a place where life is uncertain, where the basic contract of being a single thing in a single world is held only loosely, locally, in the small private physics of individual rooms. They have done this without commentary, without philosophy, with the patient pragmatism of people who have decided that the world is what it is and that the right response is a small adjustment of the body and a long quiet life.

The cold sits in their wrists. The damp lives in their clothing. The patchworked light falls on them from a sky that is not quite one sky. They move through the seams with the ease of long practice. They synchronize their minutes in the morning and let them drift by evening. They eat the lichen. They name their children after the plants that won't grow here. They maintain the small devices that keep them coherent. They do not, mostly, ask why the place is the way it is. The question is not forbidden; it is simply not the work. The work is to live, quietly, in a place where the world is a small held thing, and to keep holding it.

This is the technology nobody remarks on. This is the most ordinary strangeness. The residents are the strangeness made gentle, made daily, made into the texture of an ordinary life. They are the people of a place that is itself a kind of person — accreted, patient, holding its moisture close, knowing its seams, flexing at the joins, lit by a patchwork of suns none of which is wrong exactly, none of which is quite right.

They live here. They will live here. The substrate is still becoming. The accretion is still gentle. The seams still hold.
