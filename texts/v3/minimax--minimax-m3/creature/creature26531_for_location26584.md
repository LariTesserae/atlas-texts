The inhabitants of this place are not many. They are also not few. They are present in the way a population is present in a city at three in the morning — sparse, but covering everything, each one to its post, each one engaged in the particular business that has placed it there. The place has been described in detail, and the detail will be inhabited by what actually occupies it. Most of what occupies it is not alive in the way that one usually means. Most of it is the machinery itself.

The pumps are the first inhabitants, and the most constant. There are several visible from the platform, and others further out in the chamber, set on their concrete islands in the black water. Each one is roughly the size of a small house — a steel housing on a poured base, ribbed with bolt heads, fitted with flanged pipes at top and side. They are mounted on moorings that allow them a small range of vertical motion, and on each cycle they rise a few inches and settle again, sending concentric ripples out across the floor of the chamber. The cycle is slow. A man standing on the platform can feel it through the grating as a faint rhythmic pressure in the soles of his feet, and through the railing as a vibration in his hands. It is not loud. It is not quiet. It is the kind of motion that the body stops registering after a while and starts to feel as a kind of weather.

The pumps are attended. Each one has a small constellation of indicator lights mounted on its housing — red, green, amber — which wink in sequences that look, from a distance, almost deliberate. A green light holds steady while a red one pulses. The amber comes on briefly, then goes out. These sequences are not random. They are the visible face of whatever is happening inside the housing, the part of the work that has been made available to be looked at by whoever built the lights and connected them to whatever they are connected to. They are attended in another way by the gauges bolted to the rails of the upper catwalk — small LCD screens that cycle through numbers no one watches anymore. The numbers mean something to someone, somewhere, but on the gallery they are unread. They flicker. They update. They advance through their small progressions and then reset, and no one on the platform at this hour sees them.

The pumps breathe. This is a thing one can say about them without anthropomorphizing them too far, because what they actually do is vent vapor from the seams of their housings. The vapor is pale, almost colorless, and it rises in slow plumes into the dimness of the upper chamber and is taken by the draft and dispersed. The vapor looks like breath. It is not breath. But it has the same quality of being something emitted from the body of the machine by the machine's own working, a small exhalation that the air receives and carries away.

The pumps are not alone in the chamber. Below the gallery there are generators, compressor units, a heat exchanger with hundreds of fins weeping constantly. Each is a dark mass in the yellow haze, attended by its own dripping pipes, its own indicator lights, its own slow internal work. The heat exchanger weeps more than the others — it sweats from every fin in a fine, continuous film that catches the sodium light and makes the whole apparatus look as if it were encased in a thin skin of luminous orange. The water collects at its base and runs down a channel cut into the concrete. The channel feeds into the larger body of black water that occupies the floor. None of this is wasted. It is all in the cycle. The water comes down, the pumps lift it back, the heat exchanger takes its share, the cycle continues. The chamber is a single working body, and the machines inside it are its organs.

Above the gallery, on the upper tier, smaller machines are racked in rows. These are different in kind. They are quieter, smaller, more numerous. They hum rather than thump. They have their own indicator panels — green LEDs mostly, sometimes amber, very rarely red. They do not breathe visibly. They do not weep. They have the quality of machines that are doing something internal and specific, something that does not require the great slow physical motion of the pumps. They are attended by service ladders and narrow walkways of their own, and these walkways are lit by their own lamps, smaller than the sodium lamps of the main gallery, casting a cooler and less forgiving light. Nothing on the upper tier is moving in the way the pumps move. Everything on the upper tier is just running.

The water is an inhabitant. This sounds odd, but the water does not behave like a feature of the place so much as a resident of it. It covers the floor of the chamber to a shallow depth. It shifts with each pump cycle. It laps at the bases of the concrete piers, at the supports of the catwalk, at the islands on which the machines stand. It works. It is the medium in which the rust forms, in which the mineral blooms crust the bolt heads, in which the white efflorescence gathers on the underside of every flange. It is also the medium through which the cold is conducted into every piece of metal it touches, and so it is itself partly responsible for the frost that rimes the dry surfaces above it. The water is in motion always, even between pump cycles, because there is always some smaller disturbance — a drip from a pipe above, a thermal current, the slow settling of the chamber's air. It is never still. Its surface is never quite the same shape twice. It is the most restless thing in the place.

The pipes are also inhabitants. They run in bundles along the ceiling, follow the curve of the chamber, disappear into the branch tunnels, reappear at intervals to drop down to the machines. They are of different diameters. Some are as thick as a man's torso. Some are thin and release single drops at intervals that, after a while, become almost musical. The dripping is not regular. Different pipes drip at different rates, and the rates drift over time, and the combined sound — the irregular choruses of drip — is part of what fills the air in this place along with the thump of the pumps and the hiss of compressed air. The pipes sweat. The pipes weep. The pipes groan under thermal stress in tones that can be heard if one listens for them. They are not alive, but they have their own weather, their own small violences of expansion and contraction, their own slow leaking into the water below.

The concrete piers are not inhabited, but they are occupied. They stand in the water at intervals, each one fitted with a bolted ladder leading up to a maintenance platform at gallery level. The ladders are rusted. The platforms have been locked for years — the chains on the gate are stiff, the padlocks swollen, the locks themselves in some cases fused with corrosion. The piers are unused. They are the infrastructure of a maintenance that does not happen anymore, or does not happen here, or has been routed elsewhere. They are standing in for a population of workers who would, in some other version of this place, be moving among them with wrenches and flashlights, climbing up and down, opening junction boxes, taking readings. That population is not present. The piers wait for them.

On the underside of one pipe, where water collects and lingers, a pale moss has taken hold. It is the color of old plaster. It grows in a thin, almost translucent mat, and it has the quality of something that has decided to be alive in a place that has not yet decided whether anything should be. On the same pipe, a fungus grows in rosettes the color of bone — small, flat, layered things that look almost like brackets of coral. They are dry to the touch. They have a faint dusty smell that is the only non-mechanical smell in the chamber, apart from the wet mineral smell of the water and the concrete. The fungus and the moss are the first visible colonizers. They have not spread. They are confined to the conditions they require — constant wetness, the absence of direct light, the absence of foot traffic. They are doing the slow work of taking apart what the engineers have put together, and they are doing it invisibly, at a rate that the next generation of maintenance workers, if any come, will probably not notice unless they are looking for it.

Near a warmer vent, in a drainage grate at the edge of the gallery, a single fern has rooted. It is small. It has perhaps eight fronds. It is vivid green — the most vivid color anywhere in the chamber, almost obscene in its vitality against all the grey and the orange and the black. It has no business being here. It is doing what ferns do in dark wet places, which is to take advantage of the small local warming provided by a steam vent or a heat leak, and to photosynthesize as much as it can on whatever light reaches it from the sodium lamps above, and to grow at a rate that would astonish anyone who watched it over weeks. It is the only thing in the chamber that is visibly increasing in size.

A shape that might be a cat sits on one of the concrete piers, near the far edge of the platform's light. It is grey, or black, or some mixture that the mist makes uncertain. It faces the pumps. It does not move. It is in a posture that cats assume when they are watching something that interests them — settled, tail curled, head steady. It has been there, or one like it has been there, for as long as anyone now working in the complex can remember. It is not fed by anyone. It is not owned by anyone. It eats whatever can be eaten in this place — the mice that live in the warmer service corridors, perhaps, or the insects that gather around the heat exchanger, or the small invertebrates that the dripping pipes sustain. It drinks from the puddles on the grating. It is in some sense the only warm-blooded resident of the lower chambers, and it has the quality of a thing that has found its way into a place that was not built for it and has made itself at home in the cracks. It might be a trick of the mist. From a distance, in the yellow haze, anything can be.

The humans are the most intermittent inhabitants, and their traces are everywhere. The floor markers in stenciled paint. The stenciled numbers on the walls. The instrument panels. The padlocks. The chains. The service corridor sloping up toward a warmer zone where a backup generator runs as a steady diesel rumble. The signs of their presence are everywhere, and their presence itself is somewhere else — in the warmer zone, probably, where the air is something a human can sit in for a full shift without equipment, where there is a desk and a logbook and a thermos of something hot. They come down the elevator, walk the gallery, check the readings, sign the log, walk back. They do this on a schedule that has been established by someone and adjusted by someone and is now maintained by the people currently doing it, who have inherited the schedule and may not know who established it or why. They are the inheritors of a working arrangement that extends back further than any of their working lives. They are not the engineers who designed the pumps. They are not the workers who laid the grating. They are the current occupants of a job that has been occupied, more or less continuously, for as long as the pumps have run.

They leave traces. They walk the grating and the grating receives the small abrasions of their boots. They touch the railings and the railings receive, in the places where they grip, a slight polishing of the rust. They breathe on the gauge screens and the breath hangs and disperses. They take readings and write them in the log. They are the reason the logbook exists. They are the reason the logbook has entries. They are not, for the most part, present on the platform at the moment of this description. But their absence is not the absence of abandonment. It is the absence of shift change, or the absence of the between-shifts lull, or the simple fact that the gallery is not a place one inhabits continuously. One descends to it, does a thing in it, and ascends. The chamber, meanwhile, runs.

There is one more inhabitant that is harder to name. It is the place itself — not as a feature, not as a setting, but as a kind of continuing. The complex has the quality of something that has been underway for a long time and is still underway. The new wing has been added to the old body. The new welds gleam beside the old rust. The new paint is glossy under the sodium haze. The new machines stand beside the old and serve the same purpose, and the old machines serve their purpose because no one has decided to stop them. The complex is in a state of being continuously rebuilt and never quite finished. It is in time. It is a place that has a duration, and the duration is not the duration of any one of its inhabitants — not the pumps, which can be replaced; not the workers, who rotate; not the cat, which may already be a different cat from the one that was here a decade ago. The duration is the duration of the working itself, of the cycle of water lifted and dropped and lifted again, of the cold held and the lights held, of the sound held in the body through the floor and the railings and the air.

This is the inhabitant that is hardest to describe because it does not have a body. It has the quality of something that has been running long enough to have acquired its own kind of presence — the presence of a thing that is doing what it does and has been doing what it does for longer than anyone now in it has been alive, and that will go on doing what it does after they have gone. It is not conscious. It is not aware. But it has a kind of patience. It waits. It is the most persistent thing in the chamber, more persistent than the pumps, more persistent than the rust. It is the simple fact of the continuing, and it is what the place is, when one stops listing what is in it and starts attending to what it is doing.
