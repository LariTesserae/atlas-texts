The basin does not have residents so much as it has inheritors. The things that live here have been living here so long that the word *living* has come to mean something quieter than it usually means — less like motion and more like a kind of staying. What follows is what is there, insofar as what is there can be said.

**The large insects** are the most visible. They are long and segmented and their shells are the same dark color as the wet stone, and they move across the surface in slow deliberate arcs, the way a hand moves when it is reading something by touch. There are several kinds, or what look like several kinds, but they all do the same work: they go to the stone, they settle, they lower the front of themselves to the surface, and they remain. They remain for a long time. When they lift away there is a faint trace, a shallow etching, a softening of an edge that was not soft before. You could watch one for an hour and see almost nothing happen. You could watch one for a week and see the stone change by a hair's width. They are not eating, exactly, or not only eating. They are doing something to the grain of the place, and the grain of the place is doing something back to them, and the exchange has been going on so long that neither party is in any hurry about it. Some of them are very old. You can tell by the way the pale fern-mist has settled into the grooves of their backs, the way it has made a faint dust of itself there, the way they wear the basin on them like a coat.

**The pale amphibians** sit in the shallow water and do not sit in the shallow water. They are the color of skimmed milk and their skins are so thin you can see the slow dark movement of their blood. They breathe in a rhythm that is slower than yours, slower than you would think a body could sustain, and the breathing rises through the whole body — throat, flank, the soft place behind the eye — so that when one of them draws air you see it arrive, the way you see a wave arrive on a very still shore. They eat, but you never see them eat. Whatever they eat is so small that the act of eating has been folded into the stillness. They are the most still things in the basin. When they move, they move by becoming slightly more present, the way a stone becomes more present when you finally see it — the same body, the same place, but a different relationship to the act of being looked at. They have been here, you feel, longer than almost anything. They have been here since the water was deeper. They are what is left of the time when the basin was something else.

**The small dark flying things** drift through the mist between the ferns. They are slower than you expect. They do not dart. They hang, and they shift, and they let the air take them the way the air takes a leaf. They navigate by the patches of light on the floor — you can see them adjust when the canopy moves — and they seem to know the basin the way a sailor knows a coastline: not by naming it, but by the feel of it under them. They land on the structure, sometimes, and walk along the marks with a deliberation that is different from the deliberation of the insects. The insects are working. The flyers are reading. Or perhaps the flyers are also working, and the work is the same work, and what you are seeing is two different instruments making the same music.

**The water itself** is not a thing that lives here, but it is not not a thing that lives here. It moves so slowly that it does not seem to move. It gathers on every surface and thickens, and from the thickening there comes the slow weep you can hear near the stone — a sound like a single drop falling forever inside a wall. The water is the reason the place smells the way it smells: mineral, vegetal, faintly sweet, the smell of something that has been steeping in itself for a very long time. It is also the reason the amphibians can breathe through their skins, and the reason the insects can work the stone without wearing out their mouthparts, and the reason the ferns above lean so heavily over the rim. The water is the medium. The water is what the basin is made of, in the same way that a language is what a people are made of.

**The ferns** are not inhabitants. They are the rim. They are the leaning world above, and they have been leaning longer than the stone has been flat. Some of their roots are in the basin floor. Some of their roots are in the structure, where a crack has admitted them. They are, along with the pale-barked trees, the part of the basin that is still reaching for something — still growing, still oriented toward a light that is not the basin's light. They belong to two places at once, and this is why the basin feels enclosed without feeling closed. The ferns are the membrane. The basin breathes through them.

**The structure in the middle** has its own small tenancy. The insects cross it. The flyers land on it. The amphibians, when the water rises enough, will press their pale bellies against its lower face and breathe against the cold of it. A thin film of something — not quite moss, not quite lichen, something patient and grey-green — has taken the marks as its own, has grown into the lines and made them slightly more visible, slightly more legible, in the way that a label can make a jar more present without making the contents different. The structure is being read continuously. The structure is being worked on continuously. The marks on it are not being erased. They are being attended to.

There is no way to say who was here first. The amphibians suggest they were here when the water was a different shape of water. The insects suggest they were here when the stone was a different shape of stone. The structure suggests it was here before there was anyone to notice it was here. The basin does not adjudicate. The basin is past the point of caring about priority. The basin is a place where several very long stories have arrived at the same quiet chapter, and are continuing together, and have stopped distinguishing themselves from one another.

What lives here, in the end, is a kind of agreement. The stone agrees to be worked. The water agrees to keep working. The insects agree to do the working slowly. The amphibians agree to witness. The flyers agree to carry the small news of the surface down into the mist. The ferns agree to lean. The structure agrees to remain, marked, older than the marks suggest, patient as the insects, the way a sentence agrees to hold still while it is being read.

You were there for an afternoon. The afternoon is not the unit the basin uses. The basin uses a longer unit, and you happened to pass through a single tick of it. The things that live there did not notice you leave. They will not notice, tomorrow, that you were ever there. But you will carry the basin with you in the way you carry any place that is more complete than the world you came from: as a quiet disagreement, a small weight on the other side of every ordinary place you stand in afterward.
