# The Lives at the Tindal Pass

The people of the saddle are short and brown and built close to the ground, as though they had been shaped, somewhere back in their lineage, by the wind that comes over the western ridge in the afternoons. They look, when you see them against the wet stone of their walls, like something the mountain has agreed to. Not born of it. Not made for it. But *agreed to*, the way a lichen agrees to a stone, by sitting there long enough that the sitting becomes a kind of growing.

They wear felt, and heavy wet wool, and they wear it in layers, and the layers darken with the day, and by evening a person walking the lanes is a darker, heavier version of the person who walked out in the morning. Their boots are thick. Their boots are always wet. They have stopped minding this. A child will stand in the spine-water in bare feet and not notice.

They do not hurry. This is the first thing a visitor misreads. They move slowly because the ground is uneven, because the stepping stones are slippery, because a lane that looks level is in fact a long stair of a centimeter here and two centimeters there, and a person in a hurry is a person who falls. They have learned the town with their feet. They know which stones give slightly and which do not. They know the place on the bridge over the Coldwell's runoff where the green moss is treacherous and the place where it is merely slick. They know which of the bracket-corbels under the upper stories drip and which are dry, and they walk under the dry ones when they can. They know, without looking up, when a roof-stone is loose, because they have walked under it a thousand times and the air under it has a different weight.

They speak a language that sounds, in the lanes, like a fist tapping a wall. Gutterals, and a few vowels used sparingly, like coins held back for a particular purchase. The herders use it fastest; the dyers use it slowest, the way a person who works with their hands uses speech when they have to put the hands down. They speak it to each other across lanes, leaning out of upper windows, and the words go *up* the cliff and do not come back down. They speak it to strangers, when they speak at all, and a stranger feels the words as small hard things landing on them, one at a time.

They do not smile at strangers. This is not unfriendliness. It is the wariness of a small animal that has been left alone in a place that was already occupied when it arrived. They came here, two or three centuries ago, from downslope, and they found the town already in its balance — the springs running, the walls standing, the brackets carved with someone else's meaning — and they settled into it the way a thing settles into a crack: by being willing to be small. They did not rename the springs. They did not take down the carvings. They learned which stones to walk on. They learned when the Hollowspring was going to pulse. They did this in silence, and the silence has stayed in them.

A stranger is offered, on the second day, a bowl of something hot and bitter, in a cup that has a chip out of one side and a brown stain on the inside that will not come out. The cup is offered with both hands, and the offering is not a smile, but it is a *decision*, and the decision is that you may stay another night. By the third day, the stranger is being asked which side of the fault they sleep on at home. This is not small talk. The question is a kind of recognition: that the world, here, is understood to be cracked, that people sleep on one side or the other, and that which side you sleep on is, in some way the herders could explain and probably will not, a fact about you.

The dyers are a particular kind among them. They work the long peat-house by the Coldwell, where the smoke comes out in winter and the walls sweat in summer and the air, always, smells of tannin and iron and something else, something darker and sweeter, that is the wool itself going into its transformation. They are patient in a different way than the herders. The herders are patient with the mountain. The dyers are patient with the wool. They know the difference between a fleece that will take the dark and a fleece that will take the grey and a fleece that will, no matter what you do, come out a sad yellow. They know the days when the dye will set and the days when it will not. They know, by smell, when the peat-fire has the right kind of smoke. They work in a hot, dark, wet place, and they come out into the lanes looking like people who have been somewhere deep, and the children ask them what the peat-house dreamed today, and the dyers do not answer, but they do not forbid the question.

The herders are the ones who go up, and come back down. They take the white grazers — but that is later, that is a different life. The herders themselves spend the cold months in the town and the warm months on the high meadows above the scree, where the grass is short and tough and the wind is a constant companion. They are thinner than the dyers, and their eyes are redder from the light up there. They come down for the winter with their flocks and their stories and a certain heaviness in them, as though the high air has been pressing them down for months. They cross themselves when the children climb too high. They cross themselves when a splitwing passes low. They are the keepers of the small observances, the people who remember to do the things that no one has told them to do.

The children are the loud ones. They are everywhere. They climb the walls, which are not made for climbing but which have, in three centuries of small boots and small hands, been climbed into a kind of ladder. They climb the roofs, which are flat and pinned with river rock and which ring under them like a struck drum. They climb the cliffs above the Coldwell, where the spray makes the rock black and slick and where, if you slip, you fall six meters into the basin, which is why the herders cross themselves. The children do not fall. Or they fall, sometimes, but not often, and when they fall they come up laughing, with water in their noses, and they do it again.

They have a game in summer. They gather at the Hollowspring, in the market square, and one of them — the bravest, or the one who lost the last round — climbs onto the flat stone in the center of the bowl and stands on it, and waits for the dome. The dome rises. The child is lifted, slowly, into the trembling half-meter of water, and stands there, laughing, until the pulse comes — and the pulse is a thing that cannot be predicted, only waited for — and the dome breaks, and the child is thrown, with considerable force, into the square, and lands on the wet stones, and the other children cheer, and the herders, who have been watching from the doorways, go back inside.

The children are the only ones who will tell you what the mountain is doing.

---

The white grazers come down from the high scree in the cold months. They are pale, and thick-coated, and the size of a small horse, but they are not horses. They have two sets of horns — one set above the other, the upper set curving back, the lower set forward, like the cupped hands of someone asking to be given something. They stand in the spine-water, in rows, at the edges of the town, and they stare at the houses. They are not afraid. They have been coming down, every winter, for longer than anyone remembers. They have an agreement with the people, and the agreement has never been written down, because it would not survive being written down. The grazers do not enter the lanes. The people do not hunt them. This is the whole of it, and the whole of it has held.

In the worst winters — the winters when the snow comes up to the door lintels and the brass lamps are lit at three in the afternoon and the wind in the rock is a sound like the mountain thinking very hard — the grazers come further in. They come up the lanes. They stand in the doorways, breathing, and their breath steams, and their pale coats darken with the wet, and they are not turned away. The herders step around them. The children, quieter in winter, put out hands and are sniffed at, gently, with the large dark muzzles. The grazers stay for a day, or a week, and then they go back up the scree, and the snow covers their tracks, and the agreement holds for another year.

The splitwings nest on the cliffs to the north. They are large, and dark, and very silent. Their feathers are not feathers. They are plates of thin bone, overlapping, like the scales of a pinecone, and they make no sound in the air when the bird flies. The splitwings do not cry. The herders will tell you this is because they have no mouths. This is not true; they have beaks, long and pale, and the beaks open only to eat. They hunt the small, almost legless rodents that live in burrows under the snow in the high meadows, and the hunting is silent, and the eating is silent, and the only sound a splitwing makes, in its whole life, is a small dry clatter when it takes off from a ledge, the plates of bone clicking against each other once and then smoothing out into silence.

A splitwing flies low over the town once a season, sometimes twice. When it does, everyone stops. The herders stop talking. The dyers come out of the peat-house and stand, blinking, in the smoke. The children, who are never quiet, go quiet, and stand with their mouths open, and watch the dark shape pass over the lanes without a sound, and the brass fittings on the doors flash as it passes, and then it is gone, over the southern ridge, and the town exhales, and the bell is struck, once, from the stone building near the upper gate, as though to mark that something has happened, or that something has not happened, or that the day is now permitted to continue.

In the Coldwell basin, if you sit on the edge long enough, you begin to see the eel-things. They are not eels. They are long and pale, and they move in the water with a slow, deliberate undulation, as though they are thinking about each movement before they make it. At the head end they have a cluster of fine white filaments, like the roots of a plant, and these filaments wave gently in the current, and the eel-things feed on something — on the minerals in the water, on the light that comes down through the surface, on something else that no one has ever been able to name. They are eaten in the early spring, when the Coldwell runs higher and the eel-things come up out of the deep places and are easier to catch. They are good. They taste, the herders say, like the memory of a vegetable you ate as a child — a vegetable you cannot name, and have not eaten since, and are not sure was real, but whose taste, in the eating, you recognize immediately. The herders are not always to be trusted about what they ate as children. The eel-things are real.

---

And the mountain, of course.

The mountain is cracked, and the crack runs through the town, and the water comes up through the crack, and the town is built on the water, and the water is built on the crack, and the crack is in the mountain, and the mountain is the thing underneath everything.

It breathes.

It breathes most visibly in the evenings, when the light is at its long horizontal best, and the wet walls of the town are crying light. You feel it before you see it. A small drop in the pressure of the air, as though a door has been opened somewhere far away. A sense of the ground having shifted, by a millimeter, in a direction that is not down. Then, along the line of the spring, in the stone of the street, in the walls of the houses, a fine slow almost-silent *opening* — hairline cracks, half a millimeter wide, running for meters, opening along the grain of the stone. The water in the spine-water brightens. The Coldwell hisses higher. The Hollowspring, which was not going to pulse, pulses.

It lasts less than a minute. Then the cracks close, with a sound like a whispered word repeated many times, and the town exhales, and the herders, who had stopped, go on. No one has ever been hurt. Stones do not fall. Walls do not crack through. But things move. A door that used to open inward now opens outward. A stone step, sure for generations, becomes a trap for a day, until the foot learns its new depth. A house, in the morning, has a new thin bright wet line running down its inner wall, where none was before.

The herders say the mountain is settling.
The dyers say the mountain is stretching, the way a cat stretches.
The children say the mountain is talking to itself.

The herders do not contradict the children.

And once — in the time of the grandmothers' grandmothers, which is a long time, and also not very long, because the town does not count time the way other places do — the crack opened and did not close for a whole night. In the morning, the spine-water was running in a new channel. Two houses had moved a hand's breadth downslope. The bracket above the tanner's door, which had always shown a wave, now showed, unmistakably, a wave with something in it. The crack closed, the next day, at noon. The bracket is still there. The thing in the wave is still there. It looks, if you look long enough, like an eye.

No one looks long.

---

So the lives at the Tindal Pass are these: the people, who came and stayed; the dyers, who work in the dark; the herders, who go up and come down; the children, who play in the spring; the grazers, who stand in the doorways; the splitwings, who pass once a season; the eel-things, who feed on what cannot be named; and the mountain, which is underneath all of them, and which breathes, and which is, very slowly, very quietly, very deliberately, settling into itself.

And the bell, which is struck once, at an hour no one can keep track of, from a stone building near the upper gate, by someone whose face you will not see, for a reason you will not be told. The bell is cracked. It sounds like a stone dropped into deep water. It sounds, if you have been in the town long enough, like the mountain, agreeing to something.
