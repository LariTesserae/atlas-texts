# Who Lives in the Cistern Quarter

They have given up on trying to be dry. That is the first thing to understand about the people of the Cistern Quarter, though they themselves would not think to put it that way, because the question of wetness was settled so long ago that it is no longer a question. They are a people of *near* water and *deep* water and the thin warm layer of water that lives between the cobbles, and they have made their peace with this the way one makes one's peace with a relative who has come to stay. The water is here. The water will continue to be here. The rest is arrangement.

**The Pepper Man**

He is at his brazier before the small gray hour has finished smudging into whatever passes for morning in a place with no proper morning, and he has been doing this for so long that he no longer sets an alarm — his body simply wakes, the way the cisterns simply breathe. His name is either Ettore or Ottavio depending on whom you ask, and he is not bothered by the discrepancy. He wears a leather apron over a shirt that was once white and is now the color of the cistern drums, and his hands are the hands of a man who has reached into and out of hot things for decades: the knuckles are thick, the skin is glossy, the fingertips are slightly curled, as though permanently shaped around an invisible pepper. He roasts *peperoni cruschi*, the small sweet peppers of the lagoon country, pressing them flat against the iron with a wooden paddle that has been blackened into a thing that resembles, more than it resembles a paddle, a piece of driftwood. The peppers blister and crackle and give off a smell that is the smell of the quarter, or at least the smell that the quarter shows to visitors: that green-sweet-sulfurous note that hangs in the mist above the channels and follows you home if you have visited and stays with you if you live there, baked into your clothes, woven into your hair.

He does not own the triangle of brickwork where the brazier stands. Nobody owns it. He has used it for thirty-one years, and that is a kind of ownership that the quarter recognizes, which is the only kind that matters. He knows every person who passes his crossing by their wading-sound, the way their boots push the water, the way their pattens slap. He can tell a stranger by the hesitation in the step. He does not call out to strangers. He watches them the way the cats watch.

He has a wife who minds the upper floor of their building on the Rue of the Nineteen. She does not come down. He brings her peppers in a cloth, and bread, and sometimes an eel when the pumps have run hard. They have no children. He says this without sorrow if you ask. The quarter gives children to some and not to others, and he has made his peace with this too.

**The Gauge Woman**

Her name is Ines, and her job is older than her, and her job will be older than her successor. Each morning at the hour when the lantern-flame is at its most steady — for the lanterns, like everything else, breathe, and have their own small weather — she walks the round of the cistern drums with her brass keys and her chalk and her ledger. She crouches at each cap. She listens. She does not need to remove the cap to know what is below; she listens with her whole body, head tilted, one palm flat on the stone, the way a doctor listens to a chest. After a moment she chalks a number on the lip of the drum — the day's level, the day's pressure — and moves on.

She has done this for nineteen years. Before her, her mother did it. Before her mother, her aunt. The keys have been in her family for four generations, which is not so long, by the quarter's reckoning. The pump-house keys are not her responsibility; those belong to the old man on the Rue XIV, who is a different species of functionary altogether, and she and he do not speak to each other except at the new year, when they exchange a nod across the channel and this nod is a kind of census.

Ines is small and dry in a place where nothing is dry, and she has the posture of a woman who has learned to take up as little space as possible, which is a skill in the quarter, where the channels are narrow and the doorways are thick and the streets are crowded with the long patient going of people who have somewhere to be. Her hair is pinned up under a cloth. Her hands are chalk-white at the fingertips. She has an oleander outside her door in a cracked pot, and the flowers fall, and she sweeps them up, and she throws them into the channel, and this is her small daily ritual of refusal: she will not let the pink things accumulate. She will not let the quarter grow prettier than it intends to be.

She reads, in the evenings, on the flat roof that is also the floor of someone else's flat roof above her. The books come down from the city in a sack, once a month, carried by a boy who does not stay. She is the only person in the quarter whom the boy will make eye contact with. He tips his cap to her. She tips her chin. They do not speak.

**The Key-Keeper**

He is very old, and he has been very old for a long time, and the quarter has made its peace with this also. His name is Vittore, or it was; he does not answer to it much anymore. He sits in a doorway on the Rue XIV, in a chair that was once a kitchen chair and is now a throne of wicker and rags, and he holds the keys to the engine house across his lap the way a saint holds a lamb, or the way a miser holds a purse: with a tenderness that has nothing to do with use. The keys are three. They are iron. They are so large that they would be weapons in another context; here they are merely keys. The largest one is for the main pump-house door. The second is for the interior gate behind the door. The third is for the box at the back of the engine house where the logbooks are kept, and where the logbooks have been kept since the logbooks began, and where the logbooks will be kept long after Vittore is no longer sitting in his chair to mind them.

He is not, strictly, a key-keeper anymore. There are younger men who could open the doors. There is a woman, his granddaughter, who lives with him and who could do it. But he has been given the keys, and to take the keys from him would be to take something the quarter does not give back, and so the keys remain across his lap, and he remains in his chair, and the younger men defer to him when something must be opened, which is not often, because the doors are opened at the beginning of each month for the cleaning of the housings, and this is the only time, and Vittore is the one who opens them, and this has been so for so long that the schedule has bent around him.

He eats the small bitter figs from the fig tree that has cracked the curbstone at the end of his street. No one else eats them. The children do not really eat them either, though they pretend to; they spit them out into the channel when they think he is not looking. He is always looking. He has seen everything the quarter has done in his long tenure and has never commented on any of it, because commentary is not his office, and also because he has forgotten most of what he has seen, which is a mercy he would not call a mercy but which the quarter recognizes as one.

**The Children**

They are everywhere and they are in the water. They wade up to their hips in the channels and they do not seem to find it remarkable, because it is not remarkable, and they have been wading since before they could walk, their parents having carried them into the channels as infants, the way parents in other places carry their infants to the sea. They wear no shoes in summer. They wear shoes in winter that are not made for water but that they wear into water anyway, because the quarter does not have a different shoe for different conditions; it has one shoe, and the shoe gets wet, and the shoe dries, and the shoe gets wet again. The shoes last longer than they should. The shoes are made by a cobbler on the Rue VII whose name no one remembers and who is described as *the cobbler* and nothing else, as though the profession and the man were the same entity.

The children play a game they call *the breathing*, in which one of them crouches over a cistern cap with their ear to a breathing-hole and then must describe what they hear to the others, who have not heard it because they have not crouched low enough. The descriptions are always wrong. The children know they are always wrong. This is part of the game: the production of a wrong description, the laughter, the next child taking the cap. The winners are the children whose descriptions are most beautifully wrong, not most accurate, and the most beautiful description anyone can remember was given by a girl named Mira twenty years ago, who said *it sounds like a big dog sleeping under a stone*, and this has been quoted ever since, and Mira is now a woman with children of her own, and her children play the game, and she has not told them the description was hers.

**The Cats**

The cats are not owned. They are *inhabited by*, in the way the quarter is inhabited by: present, persistent, ungovernable. They are large, as the cats of any place that feeds them well and does not bother them will be large, and they are the color of the quarter — orange-black-gray, the colors of the lantern light on wet stone — and they sit in the unlit corners and watch the rest of the district with the patience of fish, because fish is what they resemble in this light, in these postures, and because patience is what the quarter teaches all its inhabitants, even the ones with paws.

There is a cat that lives in the engine house. There has always been a cat that lives in the engine house. The cat is not the same cat from year to year, but the cat is of the same lineage, the way the gauge-woman is of the same lineage, and the cat's job, insofar as the cat has a job, is to keep the frogs company, and to keep the mice from the logbooks, and to sleep on the warm housing of the seventh pump, which is the warmest pump, and which has been the warmest pump for as long as anyone can remember. The current engine-house cat is missing the tip of one ear and has a notch in the other ear that resembles, when she is in profile, a small bite taken out of the moon. She does not allow herself to be touched. She does, occasionally, allow herself to be seen, and when she is seen, she is seen against the iron, and she is the same color as the iron, and the distinction between cat and machine becomes, for a moment, optional.

**The Frog-Population**

They live inside the housings of the pumps and they have, over generations, become the same color as the iron. They are not seen unless one puts one's ear to the housing, and then one hears them, or one hears the wet place that is also a machine that is also a population of frogs that has decided to live inside the machine. They are not spoken of. They are part of the sound.

**The Woman Who Does Not Come Down**

She lives above the quarter in a building with windows that look down onto the flat roofs, and she has not come down into the quarter in eleven years. She is the wife of the pepper man. She minds the upper floor. She has her own reasons, which are her own, and which the quarter does not ask about, because the quarter has learned that some people belong underground and some people do not, and the ones who do not are not to be pulled down. She is not unhappy. She has a view of the small square of sky above the quarter, and she watches the square of sky the way the gauge-woman watches the cistern drums: with attention, and with the understanding that what she is watching is breathing, and that the breathing is hers.

**The Boy Who Carries the Sack**

He comes down once a month with a sack of books and a sack of other things — letters, news, the small commerce of the upper city — and he does not stay, because he is not from here, and the quarter knows this, and he knows the quarter knows this, and the arrangement between them is the arrangement between a visitor and a place that is not a place for visiting: he comes, he delivers, he nods to Ines, he goes. He is not yet twenty. He will do this work until he is no longer the boy who carries the sack, and then someone else will be the boy who carries the sack, and the quarter will receive him the same way, and he will nod to whoever is the gauge-woman by then, and the nod will be the census, and the cycle will continue.

**The Quarter Itself**

If the quarter is a who and not a what, then the quarter is a patient one. It has arranged itself around the water and the machines and the small lives that have grown up around both, and it does not ask to be otherwise. It breathes. It pumps. It glows. It watches, from its corners, with the patience of fish.
