# What Lives There

There is no separation here between the dwellers and the dwelled-in. That is the first thing to understand, and the last. The valley does not host its life the way a cup hosts water. The valley *is* what lives here, in the same way a body is what lives in a body, and to list the inhabitants is to list the organs of a single slow creature that has been breathing for a very long time.

But the place wears faces. Some of them move.

---

The mountain is the largest of the things that live here, and the slowest, and it is the only one that is everywhere at once. It does not behave like a mountain. It behaves like a thing that has forgotten it is a mountain, or that never knew, or that has been a mountain for so long that being a mountain has become a habit it no longer needs. Its pulse is the pulse of the whole valley. You can feel it through a flat stone when you sit down to rest, and through the flat stone you can feel it in your hip bones, and through your hip bones you can feel it in some part of you that is older than language. It is slower than a heartbeat. It is the speed at which cooling metal ticks. It is the speed at which sap moves through a slow tree on a cold day. It is, if you stay long enough to calibrate yourself to it, almost restful, in the way that being held is restful, in the way that being inside something larger than yourself is restful.

What it is doing in there, no one knows. Not no one who has come to look. No one at all. The pipes that come out of its skin are not for input. They are for output, or for breathing, or for the relief of pressures that build in a closed system. The ducts that exhale the thin steam are mouths. The conduit-bundles that run along the cliffs in parallel lines are nerves, or veins, or the looping of a thought that has to travel a long way before it finishes. The cables that span a gully in a long taut droop are tendons. The plates of metal that have oxidized to the color of the stone are scales, or scabs, or the places where the mountain has healed itself over a wound it gave itself.

The mountain does not need to be tended. It tends itself. The pipes were not laid by anyone. They grew, the way a tooth grows, or a horn, or the crystalline structure inside a geode. The plates were not placed. They were extruded. The ducts that exhale steam do not have valves because they are not valves. They are pores. The whole long industry of the place is the metabolism of a thing that is, in its own time, doing something. What it is making is not a product. It is the making itself. The mountain is a furnace in the older sense — a place where a transformation is sustained — and the transformation is the work of being what it is, indefinitely, in a cold dim valley, while the clouds pour in and the clouds pour out and the firs grow twisted against the wind and the dark birds turn in the mist without sound.

The mountain is patient. The mountain is the source of the patience. Everything else here is patient because the mountain is patient, the way a garden is patient because the soil is patient, the way a coral reef is patient because the polyp is patient. The slow pulse is the rhythm of a thing that has agreed with itself to keep going for a very long time, and is keeping going.

---

There are people here, and there are no people here, and both of these are true, and you have to hold them in your head at the same time.

You do not see them. You never see them. There is no figure on a ridge. There is no one in the doorways of the cliff where the steam comes out, because there are no doorways, only the flanged mouths of ducts. There is no footprint that belongs to a boot. There is no camp, no fire, no cache, no path worn by feet that come and go. If the mountain is tended, it is not tended by any kind of visitor.

But.

Once, at the foot of a cliff where the steam came thickest, there is a print in the wet ash. It is not a paw. It is not a hoof. It is not the long rectangular stamp of the treaded thing that walked into the boulder and did not come out. It is a footprint. Bipedal. Two feet, set apart at the width of a standing human or a standing something, and the ash has crept back into them only at the edges, which means the standing was long. Whoever made the print stood there for hours, or stood there and did not move while the ash accumulated around them at the slow rate ash accumulates, and the standing was so still and so long that the print sank deep, and the only thing near it was the steam and the rock and the pulse from the mountain and the silence.

Whoever stood there was listening. That is what the print says. The print says: *I stood here. I listened to the thing in the mountain. I was not in a hurry. I had no gear. I was not passing through. I came to stand here and I stood and then I went and I did not come back, or I came back so many times that the next print is layered under this one and I cannot tell which is which.*

There may be other prints. They would be in places like this — at the foot of cliffs, in wet ash, where the steam lays down a fresh surface every few days. They are not made to be found by anyone. They are made by someone who needed to stand at the steam and listen, the way a person goes to a particular rock in a particular field to think, or the way a sick animal goes to a particular wall to lean against it. The person — and it is a person, in the sense that whatever made the print stood on two legs and had a weight that compressed ash in the way a body compresses ash — is not an operator. An operator would have tools. An operator would have a path. An operator would have left something behind. The person is a listener. A regular. A pilgrim, if the word still means anything. A devotee, perhaps, of the mountain's long work, who comes down from somewhere higher and stands at the steam and then goes back up, and the going and the coming leaves no other mark on the place.

The pilgrim is not alone. There may be others. There may be many others. They would never meet. They would come at different times, and stand at different vents, and listen, and go. The mountain is large enough to have a congregation of solitary listeners, and the valley is empty enough to absorb them.

Or there may be only one. The print is one print. You cannot generalize from one print. You can only say that whatever made it had a body that left an impression in ash, and that the body stood for a long time, and that the body had somewhere else to be, and that it came back, or it didn't, and that the mountain is still humming and the steam is still coming and the print is being slowly filled in by the next deposition of wet ash, and in a few weeks there will be no print at all, and the next person to stand at this vent will make a new one, on a fresh surface, in the same posture, in the same listening silence.

---

The birds are the most visible of the valley's regulars, and they are the most invisible. They are dark, heavy-bodied, short-winged, gray as the rock they live against. You do not see them until they move. You see them when one of them drops out of the cloud like a stone, banks along a cliff face in absolute silence, and lands on a ledge the color of its own feathers, and is gone. The eye registers a place where a bird was, and the place is empty, and a moment later the eye understands that the emptiness is a bird.

They are hunters. What they hunt is not clear. They are too heavy to be insectivores in the usual way, though they may take the loud-winged things that come to the flowers at full dark. They may take the small things in the moss. They may take the young of the larger walkers, if they can find a young one separated from its parent. They are not numerous. You see one in a day of walking, or you see three, or you see none, and the seeing is always the same: a shape against the rock that resolves into a bird only as it leaves the rock, and then the rock is rock again, and you are not sure you saw anything.

They do not call. They do not make the small territorial sounds that birds make. They bank and they land and they fold their wings and they wait, and the waiting is so still that a lichen can begin to grow on them, which is what the lichen wants, which is what the bird allows. The bird is part of the rock, when it sits on the rock, in a way that goes past camouflage into something like consent.

Their feathers are molting. You find one feather, occasionally, in a meltwater flat — a long gray thing, the color of wet stone, with a barb structure that is denser than it ought to be, as if the feather is built to be sat on, or to be rained on, or to outlast the bird. The feather is not a feather. It is a small plate of something that has learned to grow like a feather. The bird is not entirely a bird. But it flies, and it lands, and it disappears, and that is what it does, and the doing is enough.

There are other birds. Smaller ones, brown, quick, that move in the krummholz in small flocks and make the thin cheeping that small birds make. They are not interesting in the way the dark birds are interesting. They are the small birds of a cold place, doing what small birds do, and they are here because the krummholz is here, and the krummholz is here because the valley is here. They are background. The dark birds are not background. The dark birds are part of the long work, the way the pulse is part of the long work, and you cannot say what part.

---

The big walkers are the strangest of the regulars, and they are the ones you will never see clearly, no matter how long you stay.

You read them in the mud of the meltwater flats. A paw the size of a dinner plate, four toes, no claw marks. The toes are wide, padded, the kind of foot that distributes weight over soft ground so that the animal can stand on a snow patch without breaking through. The print is deep. The animal is heavy. The print is fresh, and then it is not fresh, and then it is gone, and you did not see the animal make it. A cloven hoof, narrow, deep — this is a different animal, or the same animal at a different gait, but the print is too narrow for a deer, too deep for a sheep, and the cleft is not a cleft but a slit, as if the hoof is a single piece with a soft seam down the middle, and the foot was placed with the deliberation of a foot that knows where it wants to be. A dragging line, like a thick rope pulled through the silt — a tail, or something that functions as a tail, and the animal that owns it does not bother to lift it. Why would it lift it. The valley is empty. The valley is private. The valley is for the things that live in it, and they do not perform for one another.

You see them as absence. A silhouette on a far ridge, gone before you focus. The silhouette is too large. It is also too still for the time you saw it, and the stillness means the animal is not moving because it has decided to be still, and the decision is recent, and the animal will un-decide in a moment and go. A sound you almost hear and then don't. A carcass on a snow patch, half-eaten, the bones picked clean in a way that suggests the eaters were not in a hurry. The carcass is one of the small things — a young something, a bird, an invertebrate large enough to have a skeleton. The pickings are careful. The pickings are not torn. The animal that ate here took what it wanted and left the rest, and the rest is a small architecture of clean bone on white snow, and the snow has not yet covered it.

What are they. They are the valley's largest animals, and they are not many, and they are old, and they are quiet. They have been here longer than the firs, perhaps longer than the moss, and they have made their peace with the mountain the way a herd makes its peace with a river it has to cross. They are not hunted. They are not afraid. Their footfalls are slow and considered, and their hooves or pads are wrapped in something — fur, or callus, or a secretion that does not quite dry — so that when they move on the bare stone they make no sound, and when they move on the snow they leave a print that fills in within the hour.

The one with the tail. You never see it. You see the drag-mark, again and again, in the silt, in the wet ash, in the soft ground at the foot of a cliff. The drag is long. The drag has a rhythm to it, a slight undulation, the way a rope undulates when it is being pulled by something that is not pulling hard. The animal is heavy in front and lighter behind, or it is balanced and the tail is a counterweight, or the tail is a thing in itself, with a use, and the use is to leave a mark. Animals mark territory. Animals leave signs. The tail is a sign, written in the silt, in the wet ash, in the soft ground. The sign says: *I was here. I was here at this weight, at this gait, on this day. I am not in a hurry. I will be here again.*

The bipedal thing at the cliff was not one of these. The bipedal thing was different. The bipedal thing was a print, not a drag. The bipedal thing stood for a long time and did not move. The bipedal thing is in a category by itself.

---

The shape on the ridge is its own category.

You see it once, or twice, or three times in a long stay. It is at the edge of vision, in the mist, in the gray hour. It is too large. It is too dark. It is too quiet. It moves along the ridge in a way that suggests bulk without speed, a slow translation across the skyline, and the translation is steady, and there is no hurry in it, and the silhouette is wrong for any of the animals whose prints you have read. It has too many points along its top. It has a low head and a high back, or a high head and a low back, and the back has something on it that might be a fold of skin, or a fin, or a fold of fur, and the silhouette disappears into the mist and is gone.

It may be one of the big walkers. It may be the big walker, the one with the tail, the one whose drag-mark you have followed through the silt. A large animal on a ridge, in mist, can take on the dimensions of something larger than itself. A large animal in mist can become, for a moment, a thing that has no name.

Or it may not be. The valley is large. The valley is three days across. The valley has a whole interior that you will never see, that no one will ever see, that is filled with its own weather and its own shapes, and some of those shapes are larger than the walkers you have read in the mud. The mountain is hollow. The mountain has interior spaces. The mountain has corridors and chambers that breathe out steam, and the chambers are not empty, and the things in the chambers are not all pipes and ducts and conduit-bundles. Some of them are alive, in the way the mountain is alive, in the way the dark birds are alive, in the way the moss is alive. The things in the chambers move, sometimes, to the mouth of a duct, and stand there in the steam, and breathe, and go back in. You would not see them unless you were at the duct at the right moment, and you would not be at the duct at the right moment unless you were a listener, unless you were a pilgrim, unless you were one of the ones who come to stand in the steam and listen to the long work.

The shape on the ridge may be one of those. The shape on the ridge may be the mountain, taking a walk.

---

The small things are the easiest to miss and the most numerous, and they are the valley's immune system, in the way that all the small things of a place are the place's immune system.

Under every flat stone, in the moss, in the leaf litter when you part it, there is a small industry. Pale invertebrates with too many legs, longer than they ought to be for the size of their bodies, and the legs move in waves, and the waves are not synchronized with each other in a way that suggests a single animal. It is a colony. It is many small things moving as one small thing, or one small thing that has not yet decided how many of itself it is. The things with the wet shining backs go into the leaf litter when you uncover them, and they are not fast, but they are not slow, and they have a sheen to them that is not wetness but a kind of polished surface, as if they have been in the damp so long that they have become part of it. The flat gray thing with the many legs — you have no name for it — lives under the stones by the meltwater, and it has so many legs that when it moves it looks like a small wave, a small pulse, traveling along the underside of the rock, and the pulse is the same speed as the pulse from the mountain, and you wonder, for a moment, if it is connected.

The flowers are visited. By day you see the visitors rarely — a small shape, a hover, a thing that is more suggestion than form. By twilight you see them more. By full dark they are loud with wings you cannot hear. You stand in a meadow in the gray hour and the air around your head is full of small shapes, and the only evidence is the slight pull on the petals as they pass, and the slight pull is the weight of the visitor, and the visitor is doing what visitors do, and the flowers are doing what flowers do, and the meadow is a long transaction, a long slow exchange, and the exchange has been going on since the moss first thought about becoming a meadow.

The small things are patient. The small things are the patience, in the way that the small things of a place are always the patience. The small things do the slow work of turning the mountain's mineral into the moss's green and the moss's green into the invertebrate's pale body and the invertebrate's pale body into the dark bird's heavy flight. The small things are the converters. The small things are the cycle. Without them the valley would be a stone bowl full of mist. With them the valley is what it is — lush and bare, lush and bare, the patient alternating sentence of a place that has figured out how to feed itself on almost nothing.

---

The flora is its own inhabitant, and you should not let the fact that it does not move convince you it is not alive.

The moss is alive. The moss is the most alive thing in the valley, in the sense that it is the most committed to being where it is. It is dark, dense, mineral-fed, thick as felt. It climbs the lower trunks of the stunted firs. It becomes a meadow where the snowmelt has carved a slow channel. It is ankle-deep and spongy and studded with flowers that should not exist at this elevation, fat-petaled things in indigo and a strange bruised yellow that you have no name for, and the flowers are not rare. The flowers are not special. The flowers are what the moss does when the moss has had enough water and enough mineral and enough time, and the moss has had all of these, and the moss is in no hurry to make the next thing after the flowers, and the not-hurrying is its own kind of life.

The krummholz grow twisted sideways against the prevailing wind. Their branches run with hanging lichen like old men's beards, and the lichen is not parasitic, or it is parasitic in a way that the trees have agreed to, the way two old things agree to lean on each other when neither can stand alone. The ferns come up waist-high in the sheltered pockets. The dark thickets of alder and dwarf willow close in behind the ferns. Everything that grows here has the look of having thought about it for a long time and committed, and the commitment is the life, and the life is the commitment, and you do not have to believe the moss has a mind to see that the moss has made decisions. It has decided where to grow. It has decided how thick. It has decided what flowers to send up and in what color. The decisions are slow. The decisions are older than you. The decisions are not going to be revised.

The flora is tended, though no one tends it. The lush patches feel tended. The bare stone between them feels cleared. The valley has the geometry of a garden that is being kept by a gardener who is not a person, who is the place itself, who is the slow pulse of the mountain and the long patience of the weather and the long work of the small things in the leaf litter. The garden is not for anyone. The garden is for itself. The garden is what the valley is when the valley is being a valley, and you are walking through it, and you are not the one it is for, and that is all right. The garden does not mind you. The garden is too busy.

---

There is one more thing, and you do not know if it lives here.

The mist.

The mist is the air itself, condensed, hanging, pouring in over the rim and filling the basin like water. The mist is a presence. The mist is not a thing. The mist is what happens when a cold dim valley is also a humid one, and the humidity has nowhere to go, and the nowhere is a presence, and the presence is in your face and in your lungs and in the small wet sounds it makes against every surface that holds still long enough. The mist is not alive. The mist does not have a pulse. The mist does not make decisions.

But the mist is where the shapes disappear. The mist is where the dark birds turn without sound. The mist is what the big walkers walk through, when they walk, and the walking is silent because the mist dampens the sound, and the mist is a kind of medium, and the valley is a kind of aquarium, and the things in it move in three dimensions of wet gray and you see them only when they cross a lighter plane.

The mist may be the closest thing the valley has to a witness. The mist is in every scene. The mist was here before the firs. The mist will be here after. The mist does not need to be alive to be a kind of memory. The mist holds the shapes for a moment and then lets them go, and the holding is a kind of attention, and the letting go is a kind of forgetting, and the valley continues, and the mist is the valley's way of not quite keeping track, and the not-keeping-track is its own long work.

---

The valley is active. The valley is not silent in the way of a dead place. The meltwater is always running. The snow is always shifting. The ferns are uncurling. The birds are hunting. The big things are moving along the ridges on their soft feet. The pipes are always breathing their thin steam. The mountain is always pulsing. The pilgrim is always, somewhere, standing at a vent, listening. The dark birds are always banking along the cliffs. The tail is always leaving its drag-mark in a fresh silt. The moss is always being moss, the lichen always being lichen, the small things always doing their small conversions under every flat stone.

The valley is busy with its long work. The work is not for you. The work does not need you to understand it. The work was here before you came to be confused by it, and the work will be here after, and you are a small warm thing in a cold dim place, and the place is patient with you, the way a furnace is patient with a hand held near it for a moment, the way a sleeping animal is patient with a fly on its ear, the way a thing that is doing something important is patient with the things that are not.

You sit on a flat rock at the edge of a meltwater flat, in the gray hour, and the pulse comes up through your bones, and the steam drifts off the cliff face, and a shape — too large, too dark, too quiet — crosses the far ridge without hurry and is gone.

The valley continues.
