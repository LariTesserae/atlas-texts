What lives here is patient, and most of it is small.

Begin with the lichens, because they are the slowest and the oldest. They are not one organism but a settlement — a fungus woven around an alga or a cyanobacterium, each kind unable to live alone in this thin a place, both of them unable to live apart. They have taken the stone by inches so small the stone has not noticed. On the upper faces of the granite knobs, where the sun lies heaviest, they spread as pale grey crusts, the surface mapped into tiny polygons like dried mud, the whole affair so flush with the rock it feels like a stain. On the shaded faces they are greener, looser, almost leafy — foliose lichens lifting slightly off the rock, their edges curling down to grip a dampness they cannot drink but must have. On the wettest stones, near the water line, they thin and step back for the mosses, which are a different bargain — faster, less committed, more plant than fungus, capable of holding water against themselves like a sponge.

The mosses make the shaded north faces of the knobs into something almost furry — a felt of green so dark it reads as black in low light, the individual stems so fine the eye cannot follow them down to the rock. After rain they are fat with it. In a dry hour they grey and flatten and seem to give up. Then the air wets again and they revive, plump and vivid, in a few minutes. They do not flower. They send out spores the size of dust, and the spores settle where they will, and most of them settle where they cannot live, and the ones that live make more moss by extending their reach into new ground at a pace measured in millimeters per year. They are old. Their lineage was old when the first ferns unrolled.

In the cracks of the granite, where a thin skin of soil has gathered from the bodies of dead mosses and lichens and the occasional leaf the wind has misplaced, the grasses begin. They are not ambitious grasses. They are tufts — clumps of narrow blades the size of a hand, with a root system that has had to find its way down a crack that might be only a finger wide and has gone down, down, until the water table says stop. The blades are tough. They bend rather than break. In a wind they flatten against the stone and wait it out. They seed, but they seed without enthusiasm — a few stalks holding a small dry head that does not seem in any hurry to open. They look tired, the way the text says. They have been here long enough to be tired of trying.

The ferns are the most ambitious plants, and even they are not very ambitious. They uncurl in the deeper shade — the north-facing cracks, the hollows under the larger knobs where a fallen leaf has rotted into a thin damp mat. Their crosiers, when they first push up, are tight green fists. They open slowly into fronds that are always slightly too large for the space they have, so they lean and curl against the rock, making the shape of their concession. They belong here in a way the grasses cannot quite claim. They do not bloom. They dust their spores on the undersides of their leaves in patterns so regular they look intentional, and the dust falls and most of it falls wrong.

The shrubs are smaller still. Low, woody, with leaves that have learned to be hard — dark green on top, paler beneath, cuticle thick enough that the wet air does not rot them. They are scattered, not in groves. They drop a leaf or two into the water when the leaf is done, and the water takes the tannin from it and turns another shade darker. When they flower, the flower is small and plain, the color of something that does not expect to be looked at. A small bee comes, or a fly pretending to be a bee, or a beetle that has always been a beetle.

In the water itself the fauna begins in earnest. The first animals are not animals as a person would recognize. Diatoms drift in the brown water on a current so slow it is almost not a current — single cells in glass houses, each one catching what light there is and giving back a faint green-gold flicker when the sun slants through. Around them, smaller still: flagellates spinning, ciliates pulsing, bacterial films lacing every submerged surface with a slick the finger can feel if it reaches in. These are the ones the eye will never catch. They are the foundation. They are why the water is alive instead of merely wet.

Above them, the rotifers swim with crowns of cilia that whirl like tiny wheels. Copepods hop in sudden bursts when a shadow passes over them. Mayfly nymphs cling to the underside of stones. Caddisfly larvae, in cases they have built from grains of sand and fragments of leaf, drag their small houses across the mud. And the dragonfly nymph lives among them — the most fearsome thing in this water by a long measure, with a folded lower lip it can shoot forward in a fraction of a second to take a passing larva in its jaws. It lives here for months, sometimes years, before it climbs a stem and splits its skin and walks out as the winged thing that will patrol the channels of air above. As a nymph it does not move quickly. As an adult it does not stop moving. The transformation between the two is a small private apocalypse no one sees.

The fish are darker. Some are small — fingerlings in the sheets, mouths working at the surface where the air meets the water, taking whatever the air has brought down. Some are larger, but not large. They hang in the deeper basins, where the tannin has turned the water the color of strong tea, and they wait. They are patient. Their eyes, adapted to low light, take what light there is and use it well. They take a passing larva or a fallen insect without a sound. And there may be an eel among them. Eels fit this place — long, sinuous, patient as the granite itself, willing to wait in a crack between two stones for something edible to come within range. Eels have been in places like this for a very long time. They are not in a hurry.

Above the water the insects hold the air. Mosquitoes first, because mosquitoes are loudest in the ear even when they are not heard. Their larvae hang from the surface film, breathing through a snorkel, developing in a week or two into the adult that will find a mammal and drink from it. They are an old family. They have been doing this for tens of millions of years. They are not discouraged. The crane flies are gentler — long-legged, fragile, hanging in loose clusters and drifting when they move, as if the air itself were a slow current they were sampling. They do not bite. They live a few days as adults, and in those days they mate, and the females drop their eggs into the water, and the larvae hatch and live longer in the mud than the adults lived in the air. The midges form clouds. Above the still water in the early morning or late afternoon, a column that is less a swarm than a slow atmospheric event — the individuals indistinguishable from the mass, the mass itself moving with a coherence that is hard to explain.

The dragonflies are the visible apex. They patrol. They are deliberate, almost military, with a path they keep and a territory they defend against other dragonflies, and eyes that wrap almost all the way around their head so they see what is behind as well as what is ahead. They are aerial predators. They take midges in the air, mosquitoes, sometimes a small butterfly or another dragonfly. They are not large, but they are precise. Their wings catch the light and throw it back in flashes that are almost signals. They hover, and then they are gone, and then they are back at the same spot a few seconds later, as if the air were a beat they were keeping.

The frogs take over at dusk. They have been waiting all day, pressed into the wet grass, under flat stones, in the cracks between the granite, anywhere their skin can stay damp. When the light begins to fail, they begin. One starts — a single note, often repeated, a low pulse with no melody but with rhythm. Then another, from a different place. Then a third, and a fourth. The notes braid. They thicken. Within a few minutes the whole wet country is sounding, a texture rather than a tune, a continuous roar with the individuality of each voice lost in the mass. It goes on for an hour, maybe more, until the air is so full of it a person standing in it feels the sound in the chest. Then, sometimes without any visible cause, the chorus drops to a few voices, and then to one, and then to silence. Then, perhaps twenty minutes later, it begins again.

The frogs are not large. Some are small enough to sit on a thumbnail with room to spare. Their colors are the colors of the place — browns, greys, mottled greens, the better to disappear against wet stone and dead leaf. Their eyes, when they are open, are gold or bronze. Their skin is not smooth — granular, or warty, or covered in small ridges. They do not drink. They absorb water through the skin on their thighs and belly, the damp of the place drawn into the body by contact. They eat insects, mostly. They are eaten by snakes, when there are snakes, and by the larger fish, and by the birds.

The birds are occasional. A kingfisher arrives in a streak of blue and perches low on a stone that gives it a clear view of the water. It watches. When something moves below the surface — a small fish, a tadpole, a dragonfly nymph rising to breathe — it folds its wings and drops. The water closes over it for a moment. It comes up with a silver twist in its bill, and the silver twist is gone in a single shake of the head, and the kingfisher is back on its perch, scanning for the next. The performance is short and complete.

The other bird is harder to place. It is glimpsed along the stone ridges — a flicker of long red tail, a body in greens and blacks, a head that turns once before it flies. It could be a trogon. It could be something without a name yet, a form that has not been separated from its relatives by anyone with a net and a notebook. Its call, when it gives one, is a low soft note, almost a sigh, and it does not give it often. It is a bird that prefers not to be noticed. It lives in the deeper parts of the country, in the thicker cover, in the places a person would have to push through wet brush to see, and so it stays half-imagined.

There are other things, and they are hard to see. A spider, in a web strung low between two stones, waiting. A beetle on the water's skin, dimpling it. A worm in the mud, going about its dark business. A small mammal — a shrew, perhaps — moving at the edge of hearing through the wet grass, eating what it can catch. A bat at the edge of the frog-song, taking moths out of the air on the wing, the only mammal in this place that flies. None of these are seen often. None of them are loud. They are part of the dense invisible web the original text mentions — the small urgent lives that fill the spaces between the more visible creatures and make the place not empty.

And then, in the last light, the sun low and turning the standing water briefly to brass, the dragonflies settle. The frogs thin. The kingfisher is gone. The red-tailed bird has not been seen for an hour. The midges hold their column above the water. The water itself, brown and slow, continues its consideration of whether to move.

A frog calls once, from somewhere close, into a silence that is not quite silence.

The place continues. Patient, wet, almost finished, not yet finished.
