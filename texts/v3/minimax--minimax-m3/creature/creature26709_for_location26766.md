What lives here has learned to be a kind of weather.

The dominant citizen of the Sounding Flats is not a creature you could point to and name. It is the insect stratum — beetles, midges, larvae — pressed together in such numbers that individuality has become a courtesy the land no longer extends. They are the ground truth. They are the roaring. When you stand on the Flats, you are standing on the back of an animal that has no name because it never needed one; the animal is the place, and the place is the animal, and they have been each other for so long that the distinction is one only a visitor would bother to draw.

But there are figures in the din, and you come to know them by their habits the way you know people in a city by their silhouettes passing lit windows at night.

---

**The Heron-Stands** are the Flats' watchers. They are not herons in the strict sense — they are a heron-descended lineage that has been here long enough to have absorbed other things, maybe a bit of rail, maybe something older that walked soft-marsh edges before herons existed. They are enormous. They stand motionless in the brown-green half-light for hours that may not be hours, on legs that have thickened into something between stilts and the boles of small trees, their feet splayed wide across the saturated peat so they do not sink. Their plumage has gone the color of the place — moss-gray, peat-brown, the dark green of sphagnum in deep shade — so that they are difficult to distinguish from the half-buried snags and stumps around them. This is intentional, or it has become so.

They hunt by stillness. A heron-stand does not strike; it simply *is* present at a point where a fish might, eventually, glide past, and when that happens the long neck unfolds with a speed that does not belong to its languid pose. They eat the otter-things when they can catch them, which is rare. They eat frogs. They eat, sometimes, the large pale slugs that leave phosphorescent trails across the higher hummocks at night. They have been known to stand for so long in one place that a hummock has grown up around their feet, and then they stand on the hummock, and the heron-stand becomes its own small island, and the island accumulates sphagnum and sundew and a single self-seeded pine, and the pine grows around the heron-stand's legs and the heron-stand does not mind because it has decided that this is now its home.

They are long-lived in a way that does not correspond to bird years. No one has marked one.

---

**The Otter-Things** are what happens when a mustelid lineage is given a marsh with no fish and a million years to think about it.

They are low and quick, sleek to the point of wetness, with bodies shaped for swimming through standing water only ankle to knee deep. They are dark — true dark, the dark of peat-water, the dark of things that have not seen direct sun in generations. Their tails have flattened into something between a tail and a fin. Their feet have become broad and partly webbed, but the webbing is furred, because they spend almost as much time *in* the growth as in the water. They move through the sphagnum as a fish moves through coral, leaving V-shaped wakes that close behind them in the still pools and in the dense moss alike.

Their diet is mostly the giant sundews — those plate-sized, perpetually digesting plants that bead with mucus. The otter-things have learned to tip a sundew and drink the dissolved insect slurry from its leaf before the plant can reabsorb it. They eat frog-spawn. They eat the grubs that live inside the half-buried ancient trunks. They are not above a heron-stand egg, when they can find one, though the heron-stands nest in the few places — the higher hummocks, the rare upright snag — that an otter-thing cannot easily reach.

They are curious. They will come close to you. They will watch you with small dark eyes that are not unfriendly, and then they will be gone, with a wake closing where they were, and the moss will spring back up where their passage bent it.

---

**The Frogs of the Long Mud** are what remains of the genus *Rana* after a tenure here that has softened them toward something almost vegetable. They sit on half-sunken logs and watch you with no urgency because urgency is a thing the Flats have eaten out of everything that lives on them.

They are large. They are the color of muddy gold, of wet peat where the sun has never quite reached, of the underside of certain shelf-fungi. Their skins are not smooth — they are lightly corrugated, almost bark-like, and in low light they disappear against the wood they sit on. They breathe through their skin so much that you can see the pulse of their throats without watching for it.

They do not sing. They cannot — there is too much roar for a single voice to be heard, and so the long-mud frogs have abandoned song entirely. Instead they communicate by pressure: a deep, slow throb in the water around them, felt through the legs more than heard through the ears. They mate when the rains come — the great traveling rains that move across the Flats like walls — and the rains trigger something in them that is older than weather, and they deposit their eggs in the cold spots, those strange ten-meter bubbles of still air, where the predators are fewer and the water is, for reasons no one understands, slightly less acidic.

Their tadpoles overwinter in the peat. Some of them overwinter for years. A long-mud frog does not die on schedule; it dies when something gets it, or when the peat shifts under its log and it is slowly buried, and it sinks, and it is converted, eventually, into another dark layer of the breathing paste it lived on.

---

**The Beetles** are the substrate. They are not one species; they are dozens, hundreds, a whole press of carabids and staphylinids and rove-beetles and weevils and creatures that have no English name because no English naturalist has ever lingered here long enough to describe them in writing. They live in the upper inch of peat. They live in the rotting trunks. They live in the spongy bark of the half-buried giants. They are everywhere, all the time, and their motion — leg against fiber, jaw against fiber, the slow ingestion and slow excretion of a place that is always decaying — is what makes the roar.

A beetle here does not have a normal beetle's life. The larvae live for years, decades, in a single cubic foot of peat, eating the wood of an ancient trunk from the inside out, and when they finally emerge as adults they may be the size of a child's fist, pale and soft, and they live only long enough to mate and lay their eggs in the very trunk they emerged from, and then they die and become part of it again.

You do not see them unless you look. You feel them.

---

**The Midges** are the Flats' weather. They hatch in clouds from the warmer pools, rise in columns that are not quite vertical, and drift across the place in slow migrations that take days to pass a single point. When a midge-column is overhead, the light dims further, and the temperature rises by a fraction, and the roar acquires a higher register, a whine laid over the bass of the beetles. When the column passes, the Flats are lighter, cooler, quieter, and you realize the midges had been insulation.

The cold spots exist because the midges do not go into them. No one knows why. The midges spiral around the cold spots as a river spirals around a boulder, and within the bubble of still air there are no midges and almost no beetles, and the peat is older, and the temperature is lower, and the silence is so loud it is its own kind of roar. Things go into the cold spots to rest. The long-mud frogs breed there. The otter-things den there, in burrows dug into peat that has become almost stone.

---

**The Snags** are not alive. But they are not entirely not-alive either. The half-buried trunks of the ancient trees have been here so long that they are now inhabited — perforated with beetle galleries, threaded with fungal hyphae, colonized at their surfaces by sphagnum and liverwort and the pale crowns of bracket fungi — so that they are less objects than neighborhoods. A snag is a city block. A snag is a council of species. When one finally sinks, or when the current of some slow underground flow shifts it, the whole snag-community moves with it, and the beetles that lived on its upper surface must descend, or die, and those that die become the peat.

The largest snags are wider than a house and longer than a city block. They are weightless now, waterlogged to buoyancy. Nothing pushes them. They have been settling, slowly, for longer than anything human has measured. They will continue to settle. They will become, eventually, indistinguishable from the peat around them — which is to say, from the place itself.

---

**The Cold-Spot Spirits** are what the long-walkers of the Flats half-believe in, and what the rest of us will never see.

If you stand very still in the brown-green dark, and if the roar happens to thin for a moment around you, you may become aware that you are being watched by something that is not an animal and not a heron-stand and not a snag. The watching has a weight to it. It is patient. It has been doing this for a long time.

You will not see it. If you turn, there will be only the peat, the moss, the dim light from nowhere, the hum of beetles, the slow thud of the breathing ground.

You will know it was there.

The Flats, after all, are large enough to have a self. What lives on the Flats is the Flats. The heron-stands and the otter-things and the long-mud frogs and the beetles and the midges are the parts of that self that have chosen, or have been chosen, to be visible. The rest of it watches you, from inside the peat, from inside the roar, from inside the cold spots where even the midges do not go.

You walk on. The marsh closes behind your steps.

It hums.
