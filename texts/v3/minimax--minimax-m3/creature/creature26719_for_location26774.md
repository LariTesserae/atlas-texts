What lives in the Greenworks is not a single answer, and the place is in no hurry to give one.

---

There are the people, first, if they are people.

There cannot be more than a handful. The town was built for hundreds and the hundreds are gone, and the manner of their going is not spoken of, because there is no one left to speak of it, and also because the manner of their going may have been slow, and may still be in progress, in the way that a thing can be still in progress long after it has technically stopped. The handful who remain are not seen in the way that people are usually seen. You do not encounter them on the street. You do not pass them on the stairs. You stand in the square at any hour and the square is empty, and yet you feel that you are being regarded, and the regard is patient, and you cannot find its source.

What you find, sometimes, is the aftermath of them. A window that is clean when the rest of the windows in the row are filmed with condensation. A door that is closed that should, by all the humid physics of the place, have swelled in its frame and stuck open. A kettle on a stove that is full of clean water, the water surface undisturbed except for the faint meniscus at the rim where it has been poured in and not quite filled to the brim — the slight discretion of someone who has filled the kettle with care, who has not slopped, who has done a small domestic thing in a place where small domestic things have begun to acquire the weight of ritual. The water is clean. The water tastes of iron and of stone and of nothing living. Someone has carried it in. Someone has filled the kettle. The someone is not visible.

If you are patient — and patience is the only currency the town accepts — you may see, on a still evening, a figure cross the far end of the main way. It is small. It moves slowly. It wears something dark and long, and it does not lift its head, and by the time you have looked carefully to see what it is, it is no longer there, or it is no longer at the place you were looking at, and you are not certain you saw it at all. You may see, in a doorway, a shape that is more the idea of a shape than the shape itself, the way a coat hung on a peg is the idea of a person, except the coat breathes, faintly, and the breath fogs the air for an inch in front of it, and then the breathing is not there.

The people who remain are not the people who were built for. The town has changed the people in it, or the people have changed to fit the town, and you cannot tell which change came first. They are quieter than people usually are. They are more careful with objects. They move through the wet air without disturbing the standing water on the cobbles, or they move through it so lightly that the water hardly registers the passage. They keep the windows clean. They keep the kettles full. They do not speak to each other in voices that carry. They may not speak at all. They have become, in their habit and in their patience, more like the mechanical systems of the place than like the people the systems were built to serve. They have learned to maintain. They have learned to maintain without being seen maintaining. They may be the last few hands of a watch that has been winding itself for a hundred years.

Or they may not be people at all. The kettles are full of clean water. The water is in the air. The air is saturated. A kettle left on a stove in a place where the air is saturated will, in time, fill with condensation, drop by drop, until the level rises to the rim and stops there because the surface tension is right. A window left alone in a steam rain will, in time, be cleaned by the rain, because the rain falls through the chimney plume and through the great wheel's exhalation and the condensation has a particular character, mineral and faint, and a window that is washed by it for a hundred years will come to a kind of patina of cleanness, which is not the same as the cleanness of a cloth but is a kind of cleanness nonetheless. The door that is closed when it should be stuck open may be closed by the moss, which has learned to grow in the swell of the wood and press it into its frame with a slow vegetable insistence. The door is closed. The window is clean. The kettle is full. The town is keeping up its own appearances, and the appearances are very good, and you cannot, from the evidence, tell whether anything human is doing them.

This is the trouble with the Greenworks. The evidence is two things at once. The kettles are full because someone filled them, and the kettles are full because the air filled them. The window is clean because someone cleaned it, and the window is clean because a hundred years of mineral rain cleaned it. The door is closed because someone closed it, and the door is closed because the moss grew it shut. Both explanations are true, or both are true enough, and the town does not care which one you choose.

---

What lives there, also, are the plants.

The plants are not the riot of a jungle. The soil is slag and cinder under the silt, and the air is too hot and too wet for the thrusting green things of a tropical lowland. But the plants of the Greenworks are not the plants of a tropical lowland. They are something else. They are the plants that have learned to live on the wrong thing, the plants that have rooted in metal and brick and the warm standing water, the plants that have made a vegetable life out of the runoff of a furnace hall that has been running for a century. They are dark and oily and thick of blade. They do not grow up so much as they grow in. They colonize. They insinuate. They do not climb a wall so much as they enter the wall and become the wall, the way a slow fungus enters a beam and becomes the beam.

The ferns in the gutters are the most patient. They have been there for as long as anyone remembers, and they are not the kind of fern that you would put in a parlor. They are the kind of fern that has put itself in a gutter because the air is right and the runoff is warm and the metal of the trough holds a faint consistent heat that the rest of the town does not. They uncurl in the morning, if there is a morning, which is a difficult thing to define in a place where the light is the same at every hour. They uncurl in the morning because the chimney plume thins a little at certain hours and the diffused light momentarily increases, and they take the moment, and they do their vegetable business, and they are the only clocks in the town that anyone could read.

The moss is everywhere. The moss is on the bricks and on the ironwork and on the valve housings and on the lower runs of the pipes and on the cobbles of the square where the standing water is not. The moss is patient the way moss is patient, which is to say it is more patient than any other living thing in the place, including the mechanical systems, which are themselves very patient. The moss does not creep. The moss arrives. One day a brick is clean and the next day the brick is green, and you cannot say when the change occurred. The moss is the color of the inside of a throat. It is damp always. It is feeding on the perpetual condensation, and on the faint mineral sweetness of the water, and on the slow exhalation of metal from the pipes. The moss is making something of the town. The moss is making the town into a thing that can be inhabited by moss.

The grass in the central square is the dark thick-bladed kind, and it pushes up between the cobbles with the slow insistence of a thing that has decided where it is going to be. It does not grow tall. It grows in a low dense mat, and it is dark with the damp, and the blades are wider than they should be, and they have a faint glossiness that is not quite the glossiness of a healthy leaf. The grass is healthy. The grass is a kind of health that has not been seen before. The grass is feeding on the runoff of the carts and on the iron that leaches from the pilings and on the faint carbon that drifts down from the chimney plume. The grass is the first thing in the town to begin to change the town into something else. The grass is the floor of a new place, a place that is being made on top of the old place, and the old place is being slowly eaten.

The vine is the most particular of the plants. The vine is the one the town is famous for, if the town is famous for anything, which it is not, because the road out of the gate is not followed. The vine has wrapped itself around the great copper drum of the primary boiler, and it has done so over decades, and it is as thick as a man's arm in places, and in one place it is as thick as a man's thigh, and it is the color of dark wax, and it has a faint sheen to it that is not the sheen of a healthy leaf but is the sheen of something that has been polished by the heat. The vine has grown in the heat. The vine has not grown in spite of the heat. The vine has made the heat part of what it is. The drum is hot. The vine is on the drum. The vine is not wilted. The vine is the most alive thing in the town.

The vine flowers. The flowers are small and pale and never fully open. They are the only white in the place, and they are the only thing that is not the color of the town, and they are the only thing that suggests a season, because they open a quarter-inch more after the rain, and a quarter-inch is enough to release a little pollen, which is the color of old ivory, and the pollen drifts down into the square in a slow pale fall, and it lies on the surface of the water there, and the water is still, and the pollen is not disturbed, because there is nothing to disturb it. The vine is doing something. The vine is doing something that no one understands. The vine has been seen, on certain still evenings, to lean toward a particular cart, and the next morning the cart is full of coal. This may be a fancy. The vine does not have a hand. The vine does not have a plan. The vine has a direction, and the direction is toward the cart, and the cart is full, and the two facts are adjoining, and the town does not care whether you think the joining is cause or coincidence.

The banyan-like growth on the eastern row is the most serious of the plants. It has taken a whole house. The trunk has come up through the kitchen floor, and the roots have walked down the street, and the canopy has broken through the roof, and the house is, in the botanical sense, gone. The house is the banyan now. The banyan is the house. The door of the house is closed and latched. The window is clean. This is the strangest detail of the place. The door is closed and the window is clean even though the house is now a tree, and the tree has broken the roof and the walls, and there is no longer any structural reason for the door to be closed or the window to be clean. The fact that they are is a fact the town must account for, and the town accounts for it by a silence that is its own kind of explanation.

The plants are not decorative. The plants are inhabitants. The plants are making the town into a thing they can inhabit, and they are doing it slowly, and they are doing it well, and they are not in a hurry, because the town is not in a hurry, and the plants are the town now in a way that the people are not.

---

What lives there, also, is the mechanism.

The mechanism is not, strictly speaking, alive. The mechanism is not, strictly speaking, not alive. The distinction has begun to soften in the way that the edges of things in the town have softened, and the mechanism is one of the things whose edges have softened. The mechanism was built by hands, and the hands are gone, and the mechanism continues, and the continuation has begun to acquire a quality that is not quite mechanism and not quite life but is something adjacent to both, the way a coral is something adjacent to both animal and mineral.

The furnace doors open and close on a schedule. The schedule is not maintained by anyone. The schedule is maintained by the schedule. The doors part, and a bloom of yellow heat comes out, and the doors close, and the long exhale of cooling goes out through the chimney, and the rhythm is a heavy mechanical breathing, and the breathing has been going on for so long that the town has begun to breathe with it. The bellows pump. The pipes hiss. The valves crack open and shut with a percussive knock that is the heartbeat of the place. The upper tanks gulp periodically, and the gulp is the swallowing of the place. The wheel turns in the secondary works with a groan and a sigh on every revolution, and the groan is the sound an old man makes getting out of a chair, and the wheel is old, and the wheel is the only old man in the town.

The carts run on rails. The carts are loaded with coal. The carts arrive at the furnace hall at the right intervals. The carts return empty. The carts arrive again. The carts are pushed by no one. The carts may be loaded by the vine. The vine may be a fancy. The carts continue.

The whole town hums. The hum is in the pipes that are not cold, the pipes that have a faint tremor you can feel by laying your hand on the metal. The hum is almost below hearing. The hum is felt in the teeth. The hum is in the standing water, which carries it. The hum is in the bricks, which hold it. The hum is the sound of a kettle that has been on the stove for decades and shows no sign of either boiling or being lifted. The kettle is the town. The stove is the coal that the carts bring. The boil is the furnace doors. The hum is the note that the kettle sings before it boils, and the kettle has not boiled, and the kettle has not been lifted, and the singing has gone on so long that the singing has become the condition of the kettle, and the kettle is content, if a kettle can be content, to sing.

The mechanism is not alive. The mechanism maintains itself. The mechanism has learned to maintain itself. The mechanism may have been designed to maintain itself. The mechanism may have been designed to fail without maintenance, and the failure has not occurred, and the non-occurrence has become the new design, and the new design is the one that is running, and the running is the design. The mechanism is the town's memory of being built. The mechanism is the town remembering what it was for, and continuing to do that thing, and the thing being no longer necessary, and the continuing being its own justification.

---

What lives there, last, is the town itself.

The town is an organism. The town is a single organism with a slow respiration, the chimney the mouth, the pipes the veins, the standing water the blood, the wheel the heart, the furnace the liver. The town is a thing that was built and has become a thing that is grown. The town is a thing that has outlived its builders and has begun to build itself in the builders' absence, and the building is not the same building, and the town is not the same town, and the town is something that is being made, slowly, out of the town that was.

The town has a temperament. The town is patient. The town is not in a hurry. The town has been running for a hundred years and will run for a hundred more, and the running is not effort, the way the breathing of a sleeping animal is not effort. The town is asleep. The town is awake. The town is in a state that is neither and is its own name, and the name is the Greenworks, and the Greenworks is a thing that is becoming more itself as the centuries pass, and the more itself it becomes, the less it resembles the thing it was built to be, and the less it cares.

The town is small. You can walk across it in twenty minutes if you do not stop. The town is whatever it is, and you are in it, and you are alone, or you are not alone, and the difference, in this place, has begun to seem less important than it used to.

The town continues.
