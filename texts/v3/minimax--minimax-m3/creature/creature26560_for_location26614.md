What lives here is mostly small.

If you stand on a platform for any length of time — and you have been standing here a long time, and you will stand here longer still — what you begin to notice, after the hiss has become ordinary to you and the trills have become a kind of weather, is that the fauna is not one thing. It is a stratification, a layering, an entire ecology folded into the seams of the construction like pages in a book that no one has opened for a thousand years. What you thought was one kind of small dark clicking creature resolves, as the minutes pass and your eyes soften into the pearl-grey dark, into several kinds, and then into several more, and the several more keep resolving into still more, the way a word you have spoken once will sometimes, if you listen to it after it has left your mouth, break apart into syllables you did not mean to put there.

The most numerous are the size of a thumb and the colour of wet slate. They are the ones you hear first, because they are the ones that live inside the seams, and the seams are everywhere, and when you are standing on a platform they are running, in their multitudes, just beneath your feet — a faint papery rustle, like a book of pressed leaves being slowly thumbed, broken now and then by a single sharp report, a knuckle tapped against wood, which is the sound of one of them hitting a hard place in the seam at speed. They are thin. They are not flat, exactly, but they are built along a long axis, with a great many short legs kept folded close when they are moving through tight spaces, and they move the way long thin things move through long thin spaces: with a steady lateral wave, a kind of oar-stroke repeated from the front of the body to the back, that lets them thread a crack no wider than your thumbnail without ever having to turn around. Their heads, if you can see them — and you can, if you kneel and press your face to the platform edge and look down into a seam where the condensation has collected in a small bright bead — are wedge-shaped and almost featureless, two pale slits where eyes would be on a thing that had them, and a small soft mouth that works constantly, even when the animal is not feeding, as if tasting the air itself.

They are, in some sense, the seam-dwellers. They are the city at its most intimate. They have no interest in the open surface of the platforms; you will not find them walking about on top of things. They live in the spaces between, in the warm dark current that runs through the construction, in the faint hum that you can feel through your palm if you lay it flat on a wall and wait. What they eat, if they eat, is whatever the seams hold — and the seams hold, it seems, a great deal. The seams are not empty. They are, in places, thick with a pale dust that is not dust, a kind of flour the rock has been producing for a long time, and the flour moves through the seam the way silt moves through a slow river, and the slate-coloured things move through the flour. They move through the flour the way fish move through water, with the same unhurried continuous paddling, and the sound of all of them in a single seam is the sound you have been hearing since you arrived, and will keep hearing for as long as you stay.

The ones that come up onto the surfaces are different.

They are the size of a fist, and they are dark and pale at once — dark on top, where the back is ridged and segmented, pale underneath, where the belly is soft and gleams faintly with whatever moisture is on it. They have more legs than the seam-things — a great many more legs — and the legs are longer, and the legs end in small flat pads that let them walk on vertical surfaces the way flies walk on a ceiling. They are the ones you see, when the light allows. They are the ones that come to the wet patches.

The wet patches are everywhere. They are the places where the rock weeps — those long pale seams of condensation running down the faces of the structures — and they are the places where the walls glisten with the small dark shapes. They come up the wall in a slow tide, dozens of them at a time, and they settle onto the wet patch and they lower their heads and they drink. Their drinking makes a sound. It is not a loud sound, but it is a definite sound — a faint continuous rasp, like a thumbnail drawn very gently and very fast across a stretched piece of silk — and when there are many of them drinking at once the rasp becomes a kind of soft sustained note that sits under the hiss and the click and the trill like a low organ-pipe beneath the rest of an orchestra. They drink for a long time. They drink without urgency. When they have drunk they do not go away; they simply move to the side and rest, and let the next one in, and the resting ones fold their legs beneath them and become part of the wall themselves, indistinguishable from the wet mineral crusts that grow there, indistinguishable from the rock.

These are the ones that trill.

The trill is not a song. It is not a warning. It is not, as far as you can tell, anything that has a human shape to it. It rises from the resting ones in a thin bright thread of sound that climbs into the upper air and is gone. A trill begins somewhere on a wall you cannot see. Three structures away — and the measurement is yours, because the structures have no addresses — another one answers. And another, somewhere further. And then it is not several any more, it is many, it is a great ringing exchange that crosses back and forth across the plateau and has the quality of a conversation being held by people who can all see each other and are choosing, for the moment, to speak only to those who are not next to them. You cannot tell what they are saying. You can only tell that they are saying something, and that the saying is a long habit, and that no one is in charge of it, and that it will continue with or without you. When the trill dies down it does not die down all at once; it thins out, the way rain thins out, leaving a few droplets that come at long intervals, and then silence, and then a single new trill, somewhere, that begins the whole thing again.

These are not the only ones on the surfaces.

There are others, smaller and paler, that move in quick darting runs across the open platforms and are gone before you have located them. You catch them at the corner of your eye — a small pale streak, a flash — and when you turn to look there is nothing there, or there is a faint disturbance on the surface, a tremor in the wetness, the smallest possible indication that something pale and quick has just gone past. You will never get a clear look at one of these. You will never hold one in your hand. They live in the moving air above the plateau, which is to say they live in the thin liquid the cold has squeezed out of the air, and they are the upper limit of the fauna, the ones that ride the surface of the world, and they make no sound that you can hear.

And then there are the ones you hear at night.

There are not many of these. They are larger than the fist-sized ones, and they move differently. Where the small ones move in tides and the seam-things move in currents, these move as single bodies, with weight, with the slow deliberation of something that has been alive for a long time and has stopped pretending to be in a hurry. You hear them under the platforms. You hear them in the spaces between the constructions where the walkways widen into small plazas of worn rock. You hear a low creak, like a board under pressure, and a heavier scraping, and then a pause, and then the scraping resumes further on. You cannot see them in the pearl-grey dark, or you can barely see them — a darker shape among the dark shapes, a moving absence. They are, as far as you can tell, the same general design as the others: long, low, segmented, many-legged, hard-bodied. But they are bigger. Their legs are thicker. The sound they make when they move is the sound of the construction itself being shifted by a careful inhabitant. They are the largest things you have encountered here, and they are not large. A large dog would dwarf them. But they are large enough that, when they move, the surface under your feet gives a little, and the fauna nearby goes quiet for a moment, and then resumes its business, because it is not afraid of them, either. They are part of the place. They are, you begin to suspect, what the place is for, at its largest scale — the slow-moving things that the entire construction exists to support, the things that the smaller things have built up around themselves over many thousands of years, the way a coral reef is built up around the polyps, in patient increments, in the dark.

These are not predators. Or they are not predators the way you understand predators. They move through the strata of the place the way the seam-things move through the flour, with the same unhurried continuity, and they eat — you suspect — the smaller fauna, the way a whale eats krill: not by hunting each one individually but by moving through a place where the smaller things are present in such numbers that eating them is not so much a hunt as a side-effect of being there. You can hear them feeding, sometimes. There is a small wet sound, and a faint grinding, and then the slow movement continues. They do not feed often. They are patient.

Above all of this — above the plateau, above the constructions, in the upper reaches of the cold thin atmosphere that is barely air any longer — there are the specks.

The specks are not fauna. You have decided this, or the place has decided this for you. They are too high, too slow, too uniform. They drift. They are carried by whatever the wind has become at that altitude, which is not wind so much as a slow shifting of the medium itself, a tide in the air. They are very small. They are very cold. They make no sound, and you cannot tell what they are made of, and you will not be able to tell, because they are too far away and there is too little light and your eyes are not the right kind of eyes for this. But they are there, and they are moving, and the fact that they are moving across the upper air in slow continuous drifts suggests that they are part of the place's larger metabolism, the way spores are part of a forest's metabolism — something being released, something being carried, something that will eventually settle on a raw patch of rock on the leading edge of the city and begin to do its work.

You do not know what they are.

The fauna, you have begun to understand, is not a collection of separate creatures living in a place. The fauna is the place. The seam-things and the surface-things and the darting pale things and the slow heavy things and the drifting things in the sky are all parts of a single organism, or a single system, the way the liver and the heart and the lungs and the blood are all parts of a single animal. The construction is the bone. The meltwater is the blood. The small dark shapes are the cells that move through it. The larger things are the organs that move slowly through the body of the place, sustaining it, taking from it, being part of it. The specks in the sky are its breath, or its seed, or the pollen of the mineral growths that crust the walls.

You are standing in the body of something, and the body is breathing, and you have been here long enough that you can hear the breathing.

You will not stay long. You will step off the platform eventually, because you have legs and legs are for stepping off things with. When you step off the platform you will find the walkway, the narrow pale strip of worn rock that leads away between the constructions. You will follow it. You will be alone on it, or you will not — you may find yourself walking in company, a slow tide of the small dark shapes flowing up and down beside you, crossing your path, indifferent to your passage, going about their business — and the walkway will branch, and you will follow a branch, and the branch will lead to a place where the constructions thin out and the rock is more itself.

At the leading edge of the place, in the raw stone where the channels are still being cut, where the newness is still new, where the cold is patient and the meltwater is patient and the place is still being written onto the rock in water and in small dark shapes, you may see, if you look very carefully, the very first arrivals. A single pale crust on a wet patch of stone. A single seam-thing, thin and slate-coloured, feeling its way along a crack in the raw rock as if tasting it. A single drop of meltwater, held in a small concavity the cold has cut, that will, in a thousand years, be a platform.

You will not see them arrive. They were always arriving.

The place goes on making itself. The small dark shapes go on drinking. The slow heavy things go on moving, beneath the platforms, in the dark. The specks go on drifting, in the upper air, in the cold. The trill goes on, unanswered, answered, beginning again.

Nothing here is in a hurry. You were not in a hurry either, when you arrived. The place has not hurried you. It has simply let you stand here, in the pearl-grey dark, in the thin air, on a platform that is older than you and will be here after you, and it has let you hear what is in the walls.
