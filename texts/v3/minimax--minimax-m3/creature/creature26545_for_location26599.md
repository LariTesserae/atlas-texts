# The Inhabitants of the Sunken Room

The room is not empty, though it is quiet, and it is not still, though everything here moves slowly. What lives in it lives the way the basin holds water — by being in the right shape to receive what arrives. The whole place is an answer to a question the water keeps asking, and the things that thrive here are the things that have made themselves into an answer too.

## The Fig

The fig is the chief inhabitant, and it has been chief for a long time. It came down here as a seed dropped by a bird, or carried in the gut of something that ate figs in the world above, or blown in on a wind that found the opening in the dome. It landed in the dampest place available, which was the lip of the basin, and it began to do the only thing a fig knows how to do, which is to make itself permanent. Its first root worked into the mortar between two of the basin stones; its second followed the line of the old wall that no longer rises above the soil; its third found the channel where the water rises from below, and it has lived there ever since, drinking what the basin offers and giving back what the basin can use.

The trunk leans at the angle the collapse left for it, because the ceiling-fall knocked the upper room sideways and the fig grew into the space that the ruin made. The crown spreads across the dimness and does not push against any wall, because the walls have moved inward over the years, or the fig has moved them, with roots and the slow insistence of a thing that does not give ground. The bark is thin and pale in places, where the lichen has eaten its outer layer, and dark in others, where the water has stained it. It is a tree that has become a room of its own; the canopy is low and dense, and the leaves, when they fall, do not lie flat on the floor but curl into small, papery vessels that hold their own humidity for days. The fig fruits twice a year, in the way figs do — small, dense, sour-sweet things that drop into the basin or onto the soil and are taken, in their time, by every other mouth in the room. The fig is patient because it does not have to be impatient. It will outlive everything here that is not stone.

## The Moss, the Lichen, the Threads

The walls glow because the walls are alive. There is moss in the corners where the light does not reach — a low, plumose thing, the color of old verdigris, that thickens into cushions an inch deep. There is lichen on the flagstones, crustose, hugging the stone, putting out pale rosettes that crack open in dry weeks and reseal themselves in wet ones. There are the white threads — a fungus, or many fungi — that colonize the dampest stone and the underside of every root and the bark of the fig where it touches the basin wall. The threads give the room its sourceless shine, and they are doing the oldest work in the place: they are turning stone into soil, very slowly, molecule by molecule, and they are turning dead things into more of themselves, faster. A moth that fell here three weeks ago is now a pale lattice of threads in a crevice, and the threads are feeding the lichen that is feeding the moss that is feeding the air with its breath. They are the first economy.

## The Lizards

The lizards are the visible mind of the room. They are slender and gray-green, the color of wet stone, and they carry a thread of yellow at the throat that brightens when they are warm and fades when they are not. They are fast, in the way that everything here is slow — they move in flickers, and between flickers they are statues. They hunt along the cracks where the wall meets the floor, because the cracks are where the insects are thickest and the heat is most even. They pause on warm stones with one forefoot raised, and the raised foot is a kind of attention — they are listening with their bellies, tasting the air with their open mouths, calculating the distance to the next small thing.

They eat what they can catch, and what they can catch is most of what moves in the room. They are not numerous — six, perhaps, or eight, in the whole bowl of the place — because the room cannot support more than that. They are long-lived, for lizards, and they know the geometry of the cracks the way a fish knows the shape of its river. They have names for the places in their heads, if lizards have names, and they return to the same stones each morning and the same basin's edge each evening. They do not fight each other except in brief, glancing skirmishes over a particularly good feeding wall, and they tolerate each other's proximity because the room is large enough and the food is small enough that rivalry is not yet worth the energy it costs.

When a moth comes down the light-shaft, the lizards know before the moth does. They flatten themselves on the warm stones nearest the column and wait. They are not the only ones waiting. The moth is taken by what is most convenient to take it — a lizard, often; a frog, sometimes, though the frogs are rare; a beetle, once, in a quick mid-air collision; or simply the air itself, if the air has been waiting in the right way. The lizard that catches the moth chews it thoughtfully, with the side of its head, and does not share.

## The Salamanders

The salamanders live in the basin. They are pale and almost translucent, with a faint pinkness to them where the blood shows through, and they are the color of the water they live in, which is the color of ink. They lie in the dark with only their nostrils showing, and the showing is so slight that you have to kneel at the rim and let your eyes adjust before you see them at all. They surface, breathe, and disappear with a motion that is more suggestion than sight — a small disturbance in the plane of the water, and then the surface is again a mirror that shows you nothing.

They eat the larvae that hatch in the basin, and the insects that fall in and do not climb out, and the small pale tendrils of the fig when the fig has sent them too far. They are not in a hurry, because nothing in the basin is in a hurry. They are the size of a finger, or two fingers, and they have lived in this water longer than the fig has lived in this room. They are, in some way, the original inhabitants — the ones who were here before the ceiling fell and let the light in. The basin was theirs before it was anyone's.

In dry weeks they retreat into the deepest part of the basin, where the water is coldest and the channels from below still feed. They slow their breathing to almost nothing and wait. They have waited through droughts that lasted two seasons, and they came back up when the water rose again, with the same small disturbance of the surface, as if they had only just submerged.

## The Insects

The insects are the dense thing, and they are most of what is alive. The air near the basin has a haze to it that is not all vapor — it is small flies, held aloft on the humidity, and the flies are doing the fly's work, which is to find the next thing that is dying. There are beetles under the bark of the fig, small and dark, with the patient industry of their kind; there are termites in the buried wood of the old wall, working at a beam that has been their home for a hundred years and will be their home for a hundred more. There are ants in a long, sinuous line that runs from the fig's base to the basin's edge, and they carry, in both directions, things too small for you to see without kneeling. There are larvae in the soil, white and soft, curled into the shape of a question mark, eating the floor from the inside out. There are the things under the fig's bark that look, when you peel the bark, like small white hands — psocids, or bark lice, or something older, something that has no common name — and they scatter when the light finds them, and reassemble when you put the bark back.

The continuous, wet, clicking sound is the insects. It is too many to count, and it is not one species. It is the whole room, working at the small tasks, chewing and tunneling and digesting and laying and hatching, in the unbroken quiet industry of things that have been given a small, wet, dim place and have made it their entire world.

## The Moths and the Visitors

The room is not closed, though it feels closed. The opening in the dome is a passage, and sometimes a moth comes down the light-shaft, or a wasp, or a beetle too large to have been born here, and it arrives in the room the way you arrived — as a thing that has fallen from above. It does not understand the room, and the room does not understand it, and the meeting is brief. The moth bats at the column of light, which is the only thing in the room it recognizes, and then it is taken, by what is most convenient, and is folded into the economy of the place. The room is patient with its visitors because the visitors are rare, and because the room will have them anyway.

## The Unseen

There are things you do not see. There is a frog, sometimes, that has come down through the cracks in dry years and lives for a few weeks in the basin's edge, eating beetles, before the moisture leaves and it retreats upward, or dies, or becomes something else. There are spiders in the highest corners of the dome, where the old ceiling meets the new sky, and they build small, lopsided webs that catch nothing for weeks and then, in a single night of moths, are full. There is a slow worm, perhaps, or a small snake, that lives under the largest flagstone and is seen only as a movement when you step too near. There are worms in the soil — the common kind, the ordinary kind — and they do the worm's ancient work of turning the floor into itself.

There are, beneath all of this, the things that are not alive but are not dead. The carved stone with the chevron. The lip of the basin, worn into a curve by hands that are not here. The mortar that the fig has learned to push apart. These are not inhabitants, but they are the reason the inhabitants are what they are. The basin gave the water a place to gather, and the water gave the salamanders a home, and the salamanders gave the insects a reason to be, and the insects gave the lizards a reason to stay, and the lizards gave the room its quiet, watching mind, and the fig gave them all a roof. The whole engine turns on a piece of carved stone that has been here longer than any of the living things.

## The Engine

The dynamic is what lives here most truly. A moth comes down the light-shaft and is taken, and the lizard that takes it is watched by another lizard, and the other lizard is watched by the silence. The fig drinks the basin, and the basin is filled from below, and the salamanders drink what the fig does not, and the insects eat what the salamanders leave, and the lichen eats the insects when they die, and the moss eats the lichen, and the threads eat the moss, and the floor eats the threads, and the fig eats the floor. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is exported. Everything that is taken into the room is, eventually, the room.

The room does not know it is a room. It knows only the water schedule, and the light schedule, and the warmth. It breathes on the rain's rhythm. In the dry weeks it tightens, and the edges of the moss draw inward, and the insects slow, and the lizards eat less, and the fig drops fewer leaves. In the wet weeks it loosens, and everything expands by a small fraction, and the sounds multiply, and the haze near the basin thickens, and a salamander surfaces in the dark water and breathes. The cycle is older than the fig, and older than the carved stone, and is the only thing here that is not a guest.
