The figure you notice first is the figure lashing the cord.

You almost don't notice them. They are at the height of a three-story building, balanced on a ledge that might be a branch and might be a root, working a length of green vine around a fork in a column of bark. The cord runs through their hands in slow coils. They are not hurrying. They have not been hurrying. They will not finish this cord today, and they know this, and it does not grieve them. The forest has taught them — or they have taught the forest — that some of the work is for hands and most of it is for time, and time is what the forest has in abundance.

They are small. Not in the way a child is small — small the way a tool is small, made for the work it does. They are dense, fibrous, knotted at the joints. Their hands are wider than you would expect, the palms pale and lined with the dust of pounded bark, the fingers long enough to wrap twice around a vine if the vine is thin. The knuckles are bark-colored. The nails are worn down to the quick or worn past it. There is moss — a real moss, a thin green bloom — in the creases of their knuckles, in the hollow at the base of the thumb, in the fine wrinkles at the corners of the eyes. The moss does not come off when they wash. It has been there long enough that it has begun to be part of the skin. They are aware of it only when you ask.

Their face is wide, flat, the color of wet bark but a little warmer, with eyes that are not dark exactly but a kind of deep dim brown that has been green for so long it has forgotten how to be brown. The eyes are patient. They look at what they are doing. They look at it the way a tree looks at a branch — with the slow attention of something that knows it will still be at it tomorrow. The hair, when there is hair, is fine and dark and twisted into cords itself, sometimes hung with small carved beads of bone, sometimes not, sometimes wound through with a length of vine that has rooted there and begun to put out a leaf. They do not cut it. They train it. There is a woman whose hair has grown into the bark of the trunk she leans against, gently, by a finger's width, and she has not pulled free and will not pull free, and this is not a tragedy.

Their clothes are not clothes. They are wrappings — strips of bark beaten soft, layers of moss dried and retted into something like felt, lengths of woven vine that have been lashed into a kind of garment whose seams are also its structure, whose ties are also its hems. The garments are the same greens and browns as the place, and they move with the body, and the body moves with the forest, and after a while you cannot tell where one ends and another begins. A figure on a high platform looks, for a moment, like a particularly shaped burl of the trunk. A child at the lip of a hollow looks, for a moment, like a knot in a root that has grown a face. This is not camouflage. It is the slow consequence of being woven in.

They climb the way the forest climbs — slowly, but everywhere. The trunk of any large tree has its spiral of pegs driven long ago, and the pegs have sprouted, and the sprouts have thickened, and the spiral has become a kind of ladder that is also a vine that is also a branch. They go up these without thinking, the way you would walk along a hallway. Their feet know where to find the next hold. Their hands know where to grip. The bark under the worn places is glass-smooth from generations of the same grip in the same place. Some of the holds are carved. Some are grown. They cannot always tell you which is which.

The tools they carry are few. A wedge of antler, dark with age, its edge still sharp because it is resharpened on every idle hour by someone who has nothing else to do. A stone hammer, river-smoothed or hand-smoothed, hafted to a wooden handle with lashings of vine that have been replaced so many times the handle is mostly lashings and the lashings are mostly handle. A length of green vine, kept supple by being kept alive, wound in a loop at the belt. A knife, sometimes, but more often the knife is replaced by a tooth — a sharpened canine of some animal, hafted to a short stick, used for finer cuts. They do not need more. They have not needed more for as long as anyone remembers.

The work they do is the work of the place. They drive pegs into trunks — pegs that will sprout, pegs that will become the next rung of the next ladder, pegs that will be absorbed in a hundred years and replaced by their successors. They lash platforms into the crooks of branches. They bind bridges between trunks across the smaller hollows, and the bridges sag under the ferns that root in them, and the bridges are replaced when they can no longer bear the weight, and the replacement is also lashed and also sags and also roots. They train vines. They speak to vines, sometimes, in a low voice, telling them where to go, coaxing them along a rail of bark, helping them find the next trunk. The vines listen.

A child learns by being present. A child of three sits in a sling against the trunk while a parent works, and watches the hands, and puts small hands on the cord, and is not corrected. A child of six has a length of bark fiber and a wooden needle and is making something — not yet a useful thing, but a thing, a knot repeated, a pattern begun. A child of ten can lash a platform. A child of fifteen can drive a peg that will sprout in the right place. The children grow into the work the way roots grow into soil — slowly, without the question of whether.

The hammocks are slung everywhere. Across crooks of branches. Across curves of hollow trunks. Between two saplings that have grown up around the suspension cords. A hammock is a net of bark fiber, knotted tight, dressed with a layer of moss for warmth. People sleep in them alone, or two together, or three if the third is small. They sleep curled. The hammock holds them the way the architecture holds everything — by being patient, by giving, by giving back.

A fire pit, when there is one, is a ring of clay and bone set into a platform, raised slightly so the coals do not scorch the wood. The fire is small. The forest is wet; the fire does not wish to spread. Smoke rises through the canopy and is lost in the dimness above. The fire is for warmth, and for the soft cooking of root and tuber and the occasional small animal caught in a snare of vine. It is also a gathering place. People sit around it in the long green dusk and do not speak much, and what they speak of is the work — which peg is loose, which vine should be coaxed this way, which platform is sagging and needs another lashing.

They eat what the forest provides. Tubers pulled from the moss, roasted on the coals. The small bitter fruits that grow in the cupped rosettes of the bromeliads. The sweet watery fruits that hang in the canopy, brought down by the gliders, who drop them from a great height and let them fall where they may. The people know where to stand. They know the angle. A fruit falls a long way. The people are patient. A child is sent up to the high platforms to gather what cannot fall and wait for what will.

There is no ceremony, or there is ceremony so constant it has become invisible. A new lashing is ceremony. A new peg driven is ceremony. A hammock hung for the first time, a platform finished, a bridge crossed for the first time — these are remembered, and someone is told, and the telling is its own ceremony, low and quiet, repeated until it becomes true. A death is not a crisis. The dead are lashed into the architecture. Their bones, cleaned by the small things in the moss, are worked into the platforms. Their hair is woven into a cord. They are not forgotten. They are incorporated. They become part of the building, and the building remembers them the way a tree remembers a scar.

They are not alone here.

There are the gliders in the canopy — membrane-winged, the size of small children, who drop from trunk to trunk and drift across the hollows in absolute silence. The people do not hunt them. The gliders do not fear the people. There is a kind of truce so old it has become a fact of the place. A glider will land on a platform near a sleeping figure and sit there, warm, folded, eyes half-closed, for hours. The people do not touch them. The gliders do not touch the people.

There are the climbers — small, quick, furred, going head-first down the trunks in spirals. They are afraid of nothing. They run across a sleeping hammock without waking the sleeper. They steal fruit from a hand that has set it down for a moment. They are the small irritants of the place, and the people tolerate them the way you tolerate the itch of moss on a wound that is healing.

There are the things in the moss that you only see when they move. They are not named. They are not large. A small wet eye regarding you from a crevice, and then gone. A ripple under a foot that you would have sworn was solid. The people know them. They do not talk about them. They are part of the small population of the place — the countless small lives that occupy the countless small pockets of a forest that is also a building.

But the deeper inhabitant is the forest itself.

You begin, after a while, to suspect that the place is one thing. The patterns of growth suggest intention. The way the vines have been trained suggests memory. The way the hollows open and the way they close suggests something like attention. The people speak to the vines and the vines listen, and you begin to wonder whether the vines were listening before the people spoke, whether the people are part of the listening or the listening is part of the people.

The forest may be the inhabitant. The forest may be the dream. The people may be the dream's hands, the dream's small tools, the dream's patient way of continuing itself into the next generation of wood and moss and lashed cord. The people may be the forest's way of not forgetting.

Or the forest may be the people's. The people, who have been here longer than language, who have built a place that is also a body, who have trained vines and driven pegs and woven themselves into the architecture until the architecture cannot be pried apart from the inhabitants — they may have made a god of green wood and moss and slow weather, and the god may have made them in return, and neither side can remember which came first.

You cannot say whose dream this is.

You can only say that you are inside it, and that it is patient, and that it is full, and that the people who live here are not its masters and not its guests.

They are its most recent layer.

They are its cord.

They are the rung that the next dream will climb.
