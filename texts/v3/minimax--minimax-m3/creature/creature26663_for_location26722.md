# The Dwellers of the Iron Hollow

There is no word for them, or there are too many. They have not, as a people, ever gathered to decide on one. They live in ones and twos — a phrase the visitors from downslope find quaint and a little sad, the way one finds the habits of moss quaint. The moss does not find itself quaint. The moss does not find itself anything.

They are small. This is the first thing, and it surprises no one who has watched them move through their own forest, which is built for creatures smaller than themselves. A grown one can stand in the cup of one of the great rosettes. A grown one can duck under the lowest arch of the pillar-fungi and walk between the shelves as if between the walls of a house. They have made themselves a scale, and the scale fits. They are dark. Not the dark of pigment but the dark of long exposure — the brown-black of hands held over a smoky fire, the deeper black of skin that has never seen a sun that was not filtered through a hundred feet of canopy and a drift of vapor. Their eyes, when you can see them, are wide and very wet, the whites faintly yellowed, as if they are always a little unwell. They are not unwell. They are always a little lit.

They number, in the entire hollow, perhaps a hundred and twenty. Fewer in the wet months, when the vents run heavy and the air is thick enough to chew. More, a little, in the dry months — though dry here is a relative thing, and what others would call dry they would call pleasant, breathable, the season of the long walks. They have names for the seasons that are not the seasons of anywhere else. *Tall-fungus year. Leaning year. Year of the Pale Seep.* They keep no calendar. They do not need to. The fungus is the calendar. The fungus is many things that are not a calendar, and the calendar is the least of them.

## The Body and the Work

They go naked, mostly, or nearly so — a length of the cured fungus-skin wound around the waist in summer, a heavier cloak of it in the wet months, when the air off the vents is hot enough to scald a newborn. Their hair is long and matted with the amber syrup, which they treat as both cosmetic and preservative; it plasters the hair into plates that shed the dripping condensation. Their hands are calloused in patterns that would tell a traveler everything about their work if the traveler knew how to read hands, which travelers never do. The right thumb is horn-hard from the cutting. The left palm is ridged from the basket-weave. The fingertips of both are stained black from the seep, permanently, as if dipped in ink.

They cut with chipped obsidian. The stone comes from somewhere in the rim, traded down through hands that may not be theirs — they have never gone up the goat-track themselves, not in living memory, not in the memory of grandmothers, though there are stories of grandmothers who did. The chipped edges are renewed constantly. A dulled obsidian blade is a blade that has betrayed you. They keep them in a leather wrap at the hip, each one in its own greased sleeve, and the wrap is the most personal object a person owns. To be given a blade is to be given a life. To lose a blade is to begin the slow process of finding another self.

They are not farmers. They have resisted the word every time it has been offered, and it has been offered, by travelers, by scholars, by the rare itinerant from the dry country who has heard tell of the hollow and climbed up to see. *You cultivate the rosettes. You tend the pillar-fungus. You propagate the syrup-bamboo.* They smile, or what passes for a smile, which on their dark faces is more a tightening of the eyes than any movement of the mouth. *We do not cultivate,* they say, or *we tend,* or simply *we are here.* The distinction matters to them and is too subtle to translate. It has to do with direction. A farmer makes a plant grow toward him. Whatever they do, the plants were already growing. They arrived in time.

## The Drink

The seep is the center, and there is no center besides it. A child is brought, in its first days, to the warm cracked basalt at the lowest part of the basin, and its mouth is held to the surface, and it drinks. The first taste is iron and salt and a faint, almost floral rot — the taste, the elders say, of the mountain's own mouth. The child screams, or does not. Either is acceptable. It will drink again, the next day, and the day after, and for the rest of its life, until the drinking stops being a choice and becomes a thing the body does without consulting the mind, like breathing, like the slow blink of those yellowed eyes.

The seep is warm. It varies — cooler in the dry months, hotter in the wet — but it never reaches a temperature the body cannot bear. It tastes of copper in some years, of sulfur in others, of a green, mineral sharpness that has no name in any language the dwellers speak, because they speak only their own, and in their own the taste is just *the seep,* the way water is just water in the mouths of people who have never had to think about water. They have never had to think about the seep. They have only had to think about it, which is different, and which is what they do, constantly, with a vigilance that would look, to an outsider, like religion, and may be.

The seep glows. Faintly, only in the dark, only where it pools in the deeper cracks — a low, lambent green, as if the mountain is thinking. The dwellers do not stare at it. They are used to it the way one is used to a familiar lamp. Children dare each other to put a hand in the glowing pools and hold it there. The older ones watch and do not forbid, because the water is not hot enough to harm and bright enough only to frighten, and a child who has touched the glow will not touch it again, which is its own small lesson, and is enough.

## The Small and the Many

The hollow runs on small things. This is the first practical truth any child learns, after the seep, and it is taught not by words but by hunger. A toddler is set down beside a scored bamboo-stalk and shown how to lick the slow amber syrup that wells from the cut. A child of three is taught to spot the columns of jointed dark things that travel the vines in their thick rope, and to pluck from the rope a single creature with a quick pinch, and to bite the head off without hesitation, because hesitation is what the body cannot bear. The body learns or it does not. The mind catches up later, or it does not.

They eat the column-things in great quantities. They eat them raw, mostly, though there is a way of smoking them over a fumarole that makes them tough and faintly sweet and keeps for weeks. They eat the pale flesh of the pillar-fungi — sliced thin with obsidian, laid on a hot stone, eaten with the fingers. They eat the syrup straight. They eat the coral-thing that flinches, though carefully, because the flinch is a warning and the eater is quick. They eat the red furred carpet, sparingly, because it hums, and there are stories about the hum. They eat the rosettes only at the heart, where the flesh is youngest and palest, and they eat them at the moment of harvest, because the heart blackens in minutes when exposed to the air. They eat everything that does not eat them first, which is most things, because most things are small.

The columns themselves are a problem the dwellers have never solved and have stopped trying to. The columns migrate. They cross the stalks and the stones and the low walls and the worn floors of the few structures, and they leave behind them, in their wake, a stripped and dry trail that will not recover for weeks. A basket left on the ground is emptied overnight of its syrup-stores and replaced by a basket of husks. A sleeping child, left unwatched for an hour, may wake to find itself the temporary landscape of a passing column, the creatures running across its chest and belly in their rope, unhurried, neither hostile nor friendly, simply passing through, as the river passes through the stone.

The dwellers have learned to hang things. The baskets, the cured skins, the strips of fungus-meat, the small clay vessels of the seep itself, all of it hangs from poles and from the lower vines, above the line the columns prefer to travel. They have learned to sleep on raised platforms of lashed bamboo, because the columns will cross a platform but will not cross a sleeping body, which is a superstition and is also, in the practical sense, true.

## The Vines

The cordons — the arm-thick vines, scabbed with the bruise-colored lichen, swinging in their slow, sluggish arcs — are not touched by the dwellers. This is not a rule. It is a feeling, and the feeling is old, and the elders enforce it with a look and the occasional story, and the children learn it the way children learn the edge of a hearth.

The vines move when there is no wind. The dwellers have always known this. They do not find it remarkable. They find it remarkable, if at all, only in the same way one finds the tides remarkable, which is to say, one does not find the tides remarkable, one finds the shore, because the shore is what changes. The vines swing. They thicken, visibly, by the hour. They put out new leaves in the night, and the leaves unfurl in the morning with a small wet sound that is not unlike a whisper. They grip, sometimes, when a hand is laid against them — a slow tightening, as if the vine is taking measure of the hand. A dweller who has had this happen does not pull away. They wait. The vine lets go, in its own time, and goes on swinging.

The vines are the reason the dwellers do not travel the upper canopy. There are paths up there — there are platforms lashed between the higher stalks, there are sleeping-nests in the crooks of the giant bamboo, there are the faint, regular call-systems that the upper dwellers use to speak to one another across distances that would be shouting in the lower forest. But the vines run thick up there, and the dwellers do not trust themselves among the vines. The dwellers do not trust themselves, period, in the upper canopy, and so they keep to the floor, and the upper canopy is the place of the bell-hoot and the metallic trill and the long, slow displacements in the leaves that mean a larger thing is passing, and the floor-dwellers look up at the upper canopy and do not go.

There are some who go. They are not a different people. They are the same people, the same ones and twos, who at some point in their lives have gone up and not come down, or come down and gone up again, or live between, sleeping on the floor and hunting in the canopy, moving between the two with a fluency the floor-dwellers admire without envy. They are the *high ones,* and they are respected, and they are not entirely trusted, because a person who can live among the vines is a person who has learned a kind of patience that the floor-dwellers find hard to imagine, and patience of that order is not entirely safe.

## The Larger Things

The dwellers do not speak of the larger things. This is not a prohibition. It is a habit of the mouth, a kind of courtesy, like not speaking of the dead by name. The larger things are present. They are present in the bowed stalks, in the crushed corridors through the rosettes, in the slow movements of the air that have no source. A floor-dweller walking the trail at the edge of the basin will sometimes stop, in the late afternoon, and stand very still, and wait, and after a long moment a shape will pass through the far trees, low and broad and almost silent, and the dweller will resume walking and never mention it to anyone, because there is nothing to mention.

The larger things eat the columns. They eat the rosettes, the whole rosette, the great fleshy leaves and the heart. They eat the jointed things that are larger than the column-things, the pale worms that lever themselves between the basalt cracks, which are themselves a kind of column, a different sort of passage. The dwellers do not hunt the larger things. The larger things do not hunt the dwellers. This is an arrangement, and the arrangement is older than memory, and it is not spoken of because to speak of it would be to risk breaking it, and breaking it would be the end of the hollow, or would be a different hollow, and the dwellers do not want a different hollow.

There is one exception. A particular larger thing, or a particular shape of larger thing, comes down to the seep itself, at long intervals, to drink. The dwellers do not approach it. They leave offerings at the edge of the seep — a strip of cured fungus-skin, a vessel of the column-things, a length of the syrup-bamboo scored and weeping amber. The offerings are taken, in the night, and the dwellers do not watch. They do not need to. They know the offering is accepted because, in the morning, the seep runs a little cleaner for a few days, a little brighter, and the yellow stain on the basalt is fresher than it was. This is a transaction. It is not, the dwellers would insist, a worship, though it is the closest thing they have to one.

## The Light and the Dark

The dwellers live by a light that is not the sun's. The sun, in the hollow, is a rumor — a brighter patch in the canopy at midday, a faint warm bloom that moves across the upper rosettes and is gone. The real light is the seep-glow, faint, green, mostly underground. The real light is the foxfire in the fungi, the amber in the old pillar-shelves, the small steady lights that the clustered comma-things carry in the undersides of their wings and use, no one knows for what, but they use them constantly, and the forest at night is freckled with them, as if the leaves are full of dropped stars.

The dwellers have good eyes in this light. Their pupils, in the day, are wide and dark; in the night, they are wider and darker still, and they catch the faintest glow and turn it into something they can see by. They move easily in the dim. They are quiet, mostly, because quiet is what the forest permits, and also because they have learned, over a long time, that a noise in the dim forest is a noise that something is listening to.

The night is when the calls begin. The long bell-hoot from the upper canopy, which the high ones say is a larger thing, or one of the larger things, though the floor-dwellers are not sure. The metallic trill, which is a small thing, a comma-thing probably, or one of the flat many-winged things, the call going out from one roost and being taken up by another and another, a kind of census, a kind of conversation. The dwellers do not answer the calls. The dwellers have their own calls, fewer, simpler — a long whistle from the lips, two tones, that carries across the basin and is used to bring a child home, or to find another dweller in the dark, or to warn of a fallen stalk across the path. The whistle is heard by the larger things. They do not come.

## The Few Structures

The stone enclosure against the far wall is a sick-house, or a dying-house, or a sleeping-house. It depends on who is inside it. It is roofed with lashed stalks, open on the side that faces the seep, so that the sick, or the dying, or the merely tired can lie and watch the slow glow of the water and listen to the hiss of the vents. The clay vessels beside it are for water. There is no water. They are for the seep, stored against a time when a person cannot walk to the lowest part of the basin. One is cracked and mended with resin, because the dwellers do not throw away a vessel, ever, because a vessel is a vessel, and the resin is the only mend that holds against the seep, and a mended vessel is a vessel that has been used.

The line of dressed stones in the long curve is the beginning of a wall. There are several such beginnings, around the hollow, none of them completed. The dwellers are not builders. They have never been builders. They set a stone, and they set the next, and they stop, and the next dweller, or the dweller after that, will come and set another, and the wall grows by a stone a year, and is never finished, because to finish a wall would be to close the hollow, and the hollow is not to be closed. The wall is a kind of punctuation. It says: *here is a place a person has been.* It does not say: *here is a place a person will stay.*

The pole with the strips of cured fungus-skin and the bleached shards of carapace is a marker. It marks a death, the recent one, the one still in memory. The strips are the skin of the dead, cured, hung. The shards are the carapace of whatever the dead ate last, or whatever ate the dead, no one is sure which. The pole leans, because the pole was set leaning, because a straight pole in this soil will not stay straight for a season. The strips and shards click in the warm air, irregularly, and the dwellers hear the clicks and do not look up, and the pole is taken down, in time, when the memory has faded enough, and the strips and shards are burned in a fumarole and the pole is left to lean until it falls, and then it is a piece of wood in the forest, and the forest takes it.

## The Children

Children are many, and small, and brown-black from birth, and yellow-eyed from the first week, when the seep has done its work on the lens. They are taught to walk on the basalt and not to step on the coral-thing, which flinches, because a flinched thing is a hurt thing and a hurt thing is sometimes a poisonous thing. They are taught to lick the syrup, to bite the heads off the column-things, to sleep on the raised platforms, to listen for the long whistle. They are taught the names of the larger things by their shadows, which the elders draw in the dust with a fingertip, and the children learn the shadows before they learn the names, and this is considered correct.

A child who has lived in the hollow for ten years knows more of the forest than an outsider will learn in a lifetime. The child can tell, from the faintest change in the hum of the red carpet, that a heavy rain has fallen in the dry country, which means the vents will run harder tomorrow, which means the seep will be hot, which means the rosettes will unfurl faster, which means the columns will move up from the lower basin to feed, which means the floor will be quieter for a day. The child knows this without being told. The child knows it the way a fish knows the current.

The children do not leave. Almost none of them leave. The ones who do are rare and are spoken of in the way the dwellers speak of the larger things, which is to say, gently, and not often. A child who has climbed the goat-track and gone down the goat-track and not come back has become a different kind of dweller, a dweller of the dry country, and the dry country is a place the dwellers cannot imagine, because to imagine it one would have to imagine the absence of the seep, and the absence of the seep is not a thing the dwellers have words for.

## The Singing

There is a sound the dwellers make that the visitors never hear. It is not a song, exactly. It is a low, continuous vocalization, almost sublingual, made in the throat and felt in the chest, that the dwellers produce when they are doing a thing they have done many times. Walking the path to the seep. Cutting a stalk. Weaving a basket. Holding a child. The sound has no words. The sound is not a song. The sound is what the dwellers sound like when they are being dwellers, and it is the sound the hollow hears, mostly, from the dwellers, and the dwellers hear it from each other, and it is the closest thing the hollow has to a heartbeat.

The visitors, when they come, hear the calls of the comma-things, the hiss of the vents, the groan of the basalt. They do not hear the singing, because the singing stops when there are visitors, and resumes when the visitors have gone, and the visitors wonder, sometimes, what the hollow is like when no one is listening, and they will never know, because they are not the hollow's listeners. The hollow's listeners are the dwellers, and the dwellers are always listening, and the hollow is always making its sound, and the two sounds, the dweller-sound and the hollow-sound, are not the same and are not different, and the hollow is not silent, and the dwellers are not silent, and this is how it has been, for a long time, and will be, for a long time, in the small green wound in the side of the mountain, where the air breathes in and out and the seep glows and the columns pass and the larger things move in the upper dim and the dwellers live lightly, in ones and twos, on a floor that is never still, under a lid of leaf and vapor, in the place where there is no water but the drinking is never done.
