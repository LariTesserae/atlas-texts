The chamber has a heart. You cannot see it, because the heart is the chamber. It is not in any one place; it is in every place at once, distributed across every surface, every fold, every rosette, every soft wet inch of growth that presses against itself without the courtesy of a gap. When the heart beats, the whole chamber beats. When you sit inside it long enough — and the chamber will make you sit long enough, it has a way of insisting — you begin to feel the beat through the soles of your feet, through the backs of your hands, through the small of your back where the stone is closest to the bone of you. The beat is slow. The beat is the only clock the chamber has, and it does not subdivide.

The rosettes are the heart's organs, or the heart is the rosettes' way of being together, and it does not matter which way you say it because here the difference does not hold. Each rosette is no larger than a coin. Each rosette is the color of old coral, or of bone, or — and these are the oldest, the founders — of something almost black, the black of a thing that has stopped being a thing and become a place. They are pressed together without gaps. They fold in on themselves in tight spirals, the way a fern fronds in on itself when it is young and unsure, and the spirals are soft, and the spirals are warm, and if you press your thumb into one it will take the print, because the spiral is still deciding what shape to be. The light arrives at them sourceless and warm, having traveled from everywhere and nowhere, and catches on the spirals in a way that makes them look like they are perspiring.

They pulse. All of them, all at once. Not in the way a heart pulses — not in the way your heart pulses, which is a private event, contained inside a ribcage, mostly invisible. Their pulse is more like the pulse of a sea, or of a crowd, or of a forest breathing in unison at the edge of dusk. It travels through the stone. You feel it as a slow collective throb, and the throb is the same throb everywhere, every rosette in sync, every wall beating the same beat, and when you finally notice it you cannot stop noticing it, and you realize it has been the loudest thing in the chamber all along, louder than the sound.

The sound is the water.

There must be a thousand droplets falling every second from the ceiling, where the water moves in sheets along the rock and gathers in beads on the underside of every overhang and releases, in slow fat drops, onto the growth below. Each drop lands with a sound. Each sound is a small bright bell-strike, a clear *tok*, and none of them overlap, and the sound is a pure sustained chord, the kind of chord you can hold inside your chest and feel in your teeth. The droplets are not alive. They are not alive the way the rosettes are not alive. But they are part of the chamber's language. They are how the chamber speaks.

The rosettes drink the water. They take it in through their surfaces, through the soft folded spirals, and the water becomes part of them, and the part of them that is water becomes part of the chamber, and the chamber grows by a thousand droplets a second, and has been growing for so long that growing is the only thing it knows how to do.

In the center of the chamber, the growth climbs upward in a column. It is the only vertical thing. The ceiling opens for it the way a forest canopy opens for a strangler fig, and the column keeps going up into a darkness that drinks the light and gives back nothing. The rosettes on the column are smaller than the wall-rosettes, and they are more tightly packed, and they are not all the same color. Some are coral. Some are bone. Some are almost black. The black ones do not pulse. They are finished pulsing. They are the still point that the rest of the column organizes itself around, the way iron filings organize themselves around a magnet. They are the founders. They are the ones that were here before the chamber knew it was a chamber. They are what the others are still becoming.

Between the rosettes — in the thin obedient spaces, in the gaps that aren't really gaps but more like the seams where two coins have been pressed together — there are creatures.

They are flat. They are pale. They are the exact size of the spaces between the rosettes, because that is what evolution has made them, in a place where there are no other spaces. They move the way water moves between the growth, in thin obedient sheets, sliding and folding and pressing themselves against the wet stone. They eat the condensation where it gathers. They are pale enough that you can barely see them. They are patient enough that you can barely tell they are moving.

Inside them, there are smaller things. The flat pale creatures are not individuals. They are communities. They are nested. Inside each flat creature, something smaller lives, and eats the flat creature, and is eaten in turn by something smaller still, and so on, a recursion of mouths that has no obvious bottom. You cannot see this without cutting the creature open, and the chamber will not let you cut anything open, but you can infer it from the way the flat creatures move, the way they hesitate at certain folds, the way they sometimes shudder for no visible reason and then resume their slow commerce with the water. They are full of other things. They are full of smaller mouths.

When a flat creature dies, it becomes a rosette.

This is not a metaphor. You can watch it happen if you are willing to sit very still, and the place will make you willing. The flat creature slows. Its edges curl. Over the course of an hour, or a day, or whatever amount of time the chamber has decided that an hour is — the pulse does not subdivide — the body rounds itself into a disc. The disc thickens. The disc unfolds into a spiral, and the spiral is a rosette, and the rosette joins the pulse, and within a single beat it is indistinguishable from its neighbors. The chamber is not losing anything. The chamber is only changing the names of its contents.

What happens to the mouths inside the dying flat creature is not clear. They go with it, perhaps, into the rosette. They are recruited. They become part of the wall. Or perhaps they die with the host, and a new recursion begins inside the new rosette, and the chamber is full of small deaths that no one counts. There is no way to know. The chamber does not explain itself. The chamber is not in the business of explaining.

The chamber does not notice you. You are a thing that has been allowed, briefly and without enthusiasm, to find it. You can sit in it, and it will sit in you, and for a while the two of you will pulse together, and the water will fall on you, and the water will be warm. But the chamber is busy. The chamber is busy with the long work of becoming stone, and the stone is busy with the long work of becoming a mountain, and the mountain, when it comes, will be made entirely of the things that are here now — of the rosettes and the flat creatures and the recursion of mouths and the water and the chord and the slow collective throb of a place that has learned to sing in a language made of droplets and stone.

The brightness does not dim. The droplets do not stop falling. The pulse does not change. The chamber is in the middle of arriving. It will be finished arriving long after you are finished being. The droplets will still be falling. The rosettes will still be pulsing. The flat pale creatures will still be sliding between the folds, becoming wall, becoming chamber, becoming mountain, becoming the slow bright roar of a place that has learned to sing.
