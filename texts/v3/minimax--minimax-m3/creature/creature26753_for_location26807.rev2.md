The accretions live first.

This is not metaphor. The tubeworms of iron oxide, the pale orange curtains of iron bacteria draping the undersides of the catwalks, the lacy crusts of manganese and calcium that have built themselves, layer by mineral layer, over every submerged surface — these are the oldest inhabitants. They are not visitors. They were here before the eels were eels, before the silver things had drifted in through whatever crack admitted them, before the slow dark shape had learned its first corridor. The accretions were the first living response to the chamber, and they have been quietly answering it ever since.

They feed on what the chamber gives them. Iron, in solution, in suspension, in the rust that sloughs off every corroded surface. Manganese, leached from the steel alloys of the older generations of machinery. Calcium, washed out of the concrete in slow alkaline plumes. The water carries these in low concentrations, and the bacteria — *Gallionella*, *Leptothrix*, *Crenothrix*, and a hundred others whose names no one will ever apply, because no one will ever sample this water — oxidize them, precipitate them, build their bodies out of the metals, and leave them behind as a rust-colored felt that thickens year by year.

The accretions are not plants. They are not animals. They are something more patient than either — a metabolic strategy that has committed to slow growth and indefinite persistence. A hand laid against a catwalk rail, if any hand could reach it, would come back furred with orange slime, with a texture somewhere between wet velvet and fine sand. Peel it away and underneath is a darker layer, then black, then the gray of the original steel. The accretions are a kind of memory of the chamber. They are its sediment. They are the slow way the chamber is becoming itself in a different form.

On the accretions, in turn, other things live.

There are rotifers. There are nematodes, pale as glass, an inch long if they are lucky, that thread themselves through the bacterial felt and eat what they can. There are small crustaceans — harpacticoid copepods, perhaps, or something that began as harpacticoid copepods and has been drifting in genetic isolation for so long that its descendants are no longer quite the same thing. There are flatworms, ribbon-thin, that adhere to the undersides of pipes with a glue of their own making. There are the things that cannot easily be named — small, soft, faintly luminous in certain lights, that pulse in the warm currents and serve as food for things larger than themselves.

The base of the chamber's food web is chemical. It runs on iron. It runs on the slow gradient between corroded steel and oxygenated water, and the bacteria that exploit that gradient, and the things that eat the bacteria, and the things that eat the things that eat the bacteria. It is a closed loop. It has been a closed loop for as long as the chamber has been flooded. It does not need the sun. It has never needed the sun.

Above this base, the silver things drift.

They are fish, or they were fish, or they are what fish become when fish are left in a place long enough. They are small — most of them would fit in a man's hand, though no man is coming to measure them. They are pale. Their scales have a metallic sheen that is partly the natural silver of a sardine or a smelt and partly something else, something that has crept in over generations, a faint dusting of iron oxide in their skin that makes them catch the dim orange light in a particular way. When they turn, they flash. When they are still, they are almost invisible.

They school, loosely. They drift along the slower margins of the chamber, in the long arcs between the support columns, in the warm pockets behind the heat exchangers where the rising plumes bring food upward and the eddies hold it there. They feed on the crustaceans. They feed on each other, when they must. They feed on whatever drifts into their mouths.

Their eyes are large. This is a tell. Eyes enlarge in dim light, generation by generation, when the animals that have them survive and the ones that don't, don't. The silver things have been in this chamber long enough that their eyes have become disproportionate — soft, dark, slightly bulging, taking up a quarter of the face in some individuals. They can see the closest geometry. They can see the orange smear of the exit-sign afterglow, the green pinpricks of the control panels, the slow shadow of a column they are about to drift past. Beyond a few meters, everything is inference.

They have other senses too. Their lateral lines are tuned to the chamber's particular hum — the chamber has a hum, the turbines give it one, and the silver things can feel the difference between a current and a wall, between a swirl from a cracked pipe and the wake of something larger than themselves, through that hum. They live inside the sound. They are aware of it the way a person in a city is aware of traffic: always, peripherally, never as information, only as the texture of being alive.

They breed. Somewhere in the chamber, in the warmer pockets, the females release their eggs into the current and the males release their milt and a small percentage of the fertilized eggs find a crevice, a crust of bacteria, a place warm enough to develop. The young hatch, and the young eat, and some of them survive, and the species continues. It is a small, slow, complete ecology, repeating itself.

The eels are older.

They came in, the story the chamber tells itself, through a cracked intake that has long since closed over with corrosion products. This was many generations ago — perhaps a hundred years, perhaps more. The eels that came in were the eels of the surface, *Anguilla anguilla* or some local variant, and they did what eels do in warm water: they lived, and they grew, and they waited for a cue that never came, because the cue required them to leave and they could not leave, and in time they stopped waiting.

The eels of the chamber are not the eels of the surface anymore. They are smaller. They are paler. Their snouts have softened, become more rounded, because there is nothing in the chamber that requires a sharp snout to catch. They live mostly in the warmer pockets near the heat exchangers, in the long slow gyres that the heat creates, and they feed on the silver things when the silver things drift too close.

They are patient hunters. An eel will hang almost motionless in a thermal plume, its body curved in a loose S, its gill covers pulsing slowly, for hours. When a silver thing drifts past at the right depth, the eel moves — a single short contraction, fast as a sprung trap — and the silver thing is gone, and the eel resumes its waiting. It is economical. The chamber has taught it economy.

Their eyes, too, have enlarged. Their skin has taken on a faint orange cast from the water they live in, from the iron in their food, from the long chemical dialogue between their bodies and the medium they inhabit. They are not the bright silver-yellow of a surface eel. They are the color of weak tea. They would be hard to see against the chamber's haze, if you could see them at all.

They have been here long enough that some of them are old in a way surface eels rarely get to be old. The chamber has no predators larger than themselves. The chamber has no fishing. An eel that survives its first year can survive for decades. There are eels in this chamber, somewhere, that have been here longer than the people who built it would have lived. They have seen the orange lights fade and then re-emerge as the tritium phosphor has continued its slow burn. They have felt generations of silver things come and go. They have learned the geometry of the chamber in their bodies — every column, every catwalk, every quiet corner, every loud one.

The eels and the silver things do not speak to each other, except in the oldest language, which is eating.

And then there is the slow dark thing.

It is large. It is not a fish. It is not a mammal. It is not, in any rigorous sense, anything that has a name, because no one has ever named it and no one will.

It moves through the chamber once or twice a day. Sometimes less. It comes out of the deeper corners, where the brown-black water is thickest and the sound is muted by distance, and it traverses the chamber along corridors that no other thing in the chamber uses. It knows the empty spaces. It knows which columns have gaps between them that a body of its size can pass through without scraping a fin. It knows where the catwalks end and the open water begins.

Its shape is hard to read. It is dark against dark. When the orange light catches it, briefly, at the right angle, it shows a long back, a tapering tail, a head without obvious features — no clear eyes, no clear mouth, just a smoothness, a continuity with the water around it. It might be fifteen meters long. It might be twenty. It might be longer. The chamber is large enough that it could be either.

It does not feed often. When it feeds, the silver things scatter, and the eels retreat into their thermal plumes, and the smaller fauna vanishes into the bacterial felt, and for a while the chamber is a place with only the slow dark thing in it. Then it passes on, and the chamber resumes its ordinary business.

It has no ears, in any useful sense. The chamber is too loud for ears. It has some other way of knowing what is around it — a pressure sense, a vibration sense, a sense of the currents and the warmth and the geometry that has been built up over what must be an immense span of time. It has been here longer than the eels. It has been here longer, perhaps, than the eels' grandparents. It is older than the chamber's current ecology. It may have been here when the chamber was first sealed.

It is part of the chamber. It moves through the chamber the way a thought moves through a mind — slowly, deliberately, with the whole shape of the place implicit in its movement. It is the chamber's animal. It is the chamber's self, embodied, given a single dark slow form.

And it is not the only thing.

There are other presences, less definite, that move through the chamber in their own ways. There are warm patches where the water seems thicker than it should be, and cold patches where it seems thinner, and these are not entirely currents. There are moments when the orange light seems to organize itself into patterns that are not quite reflections. There are places where the accretions on the walls have grown into shapes that are not quite mineral, not quite biological, that suggest faces or hands or the contours of something once alive.

These may be nothing. They may be the chamber playing tricks on whoever or whatever is in a position to be tricked. They may be the slow dark thing's dreams, if the slow dark thing dreams. They may be the chamber's way of remembering itself, the way the accretions are the chamber's way of being a wall.

The chamber is inhabited. It is inhabited from the bacterial felt to the slow dark thing at its center. It is inhabited the way a forest is inhabited, the way a reef is inhabited, the way any place is inhabited when it has been left alone long enough for life to take it seriously. The chamber is a sealed pocket below the surface. It is full of warm water. It is deafening. It is dim. It is running.

And it is full of things that live there.
