What lives here is not the people.

The people come and go. They leave their clipboards and drink their tea and check their vats and go home to somewhere with windows, and when they sleep at night they dream of other places — places with sun, with weather that changes, with sky. They are visitors here. They are tolerated. They are not what the place is *for.*

What lives here is the moss.

The moss lives here in the way that a forest lives in a valley, or a reef lives in a sea. It is not a crop. A crop is something you plant and harvest and eat and sell. The moss is something else. The moss is the *substance* of the place. It is what the place has *become*, the way a ruin becomes a garden, the way a road becomes a hedgerow, except that here the becoming is the point, and the place was *designed* to become.

It lives in the vats first, and in the vats it is at its most comprehensible. Each vat is a small kingdom. A shallow film of water, fed from above by the drip of a pipe that has been calibrated somewhere by someone with a clipboard. A pale fuzz across the water — the new moss, the seeded moss, the moss that does not yet know it is moss. It grows. It thickens. It greens. By the time it is spilling over the rim it has been here for months, or years, or decades. It is dark green. It is cold to the touch. It holds water the way a sponge holds water, the way memory holds a thing — unwillingly, completely. It is harvested, sometimes. Someone comes with a tool and takes some of it and puts it somewhere else. The moss does not protest. The moss does not know it has been taken. The moss continues.

But the moss in the vats is not the moss that *is* the place. The moss that is the place is the moss on the walls, on the ceilings, on the floors, on the pipes, on the catwalks, on the backs of the great slow fans. That moss is not a crop. That moss is a *colonist.* That moss arrived on its own, or was seeded deliberately, no one remembers anymore, and it has taken the place the way moss takes a place — slowly, completely, without permission. It has eaten the corners. It has eaten the shadows. It has eaten the dust. Where the rock was pale with the dust of the blasting, it is now green with the dust of the moss. Where the pipes were black with paint, they are green with growth. The place has been *claimed* by its own product.

And the moss does not stop at the surfaces. The moss is in the air. The fine green pollen — which is not, strictly speaking, pollen, but spores, the spores of mosses and liverworts and the small things that grow alongside them — is everywhere. It is on your clothes. It is on your face. It is in your lungs by the time you have been here an hour. You are breathing the moss. You are becoming part of the moss, in some small way, every minute you stand here. The moss is patient about this too.

The moss has no mouth and no eye and no ear. It has no plan. It does not think about the pipes. It does not think about the fans. It does not think about the people with their clipboards. It does what moss does: it grows where it is cold and wet and dim. It is cold here. It is wet here. It is dim here. Therefore it grows. The place was made for it, or it was made for the place, or the two were made for each other so long ago that the making is no longer distinguishable from the growing.

The moss lives here the way a lichen lives on a stone — completely, slowly, without ambition, without end.

The insects live here too, though you do not see them. They are small. They are not the insects of fields — not the bright things, not the loud things. They are the insects of caves and cellars and deep leaf litter. Springtails, mostly, the kind that uncurl themselves from a fleck of moss when you look closely and are gone before you have registered them. They are pale. They are eyeless, or nearly so. They feed on the moss and the things that feed on the moss. They are not numerous. They do not need to be numerous. The place is not generous with food, but it is patient with hunger. A springtail can wait.

There may be mites. There may be the small flies whose larvae eat decay. There may be worms of some kind in the deepest moss, in the places where the moss has been growing undisturbed for decades and the substrate has built up into something almost like soil. You do not see them. You feel the moss shift under your boot, sometimes. That is them. That is the small fauna of the place, going about its business in the green dark, undisturbed by you, undisturbed by anything, eating and being eaten and turning the moss slowly back into the minerals the moss needs to grow more moss.

The cold lives here.

This is not a poetic thing to say. The cold is not a metaphor. The cold is a substance. It is manufactured, in the depths, by compressors that push it through ducts that exhale it into the caverns. It is the cold of refrigeration. It is the cold of a morgue. It is the cold that has been *maintained*, at cost, for years, so that the moss can live here in conditions it could not find for itself, because the moss is the product, and the product requires this cold, and the cold is therefore the foundation of the place.

The cold does not move. The cold does not think. The cold is simply here, in the way that gravity is here, in the way that time is here. You feel it on your face and in your lungs and on the backs of your hands. It is the medium in which the place exists. It is what makes the place a *place* and not just a hole in a rock.

The damp lives here.

The damp is the breath of the cold. Where the cold meets the warmer air — at the joints of the pipes, at the edges of the ducts, in the wake of the fans — the damp condenses out of the air and becomes visible. It hangs in lazy coils. It trails down the pipes in sinuous lines. It sits on every surface as a faint sheen. It is in your hair. It is in your clothes. By the time you leave the place you will be carrying some of its dampness out with you, in the weave of your jacket, in the dampness of your boots. The damp is patient. It does not need to leave. It can stay here forever, cycling from vapor to condensation to vapor, breathing with the fans, exhaling with the compressors, holding the moss in its perpetual wet.

The fans live here.

This is the strangest thing to say, but it is the truest. The fans are alive in the way that the moss is alive — not conscious, not aware, but *ongoing.* They have been turning for years. They will turn for years more. They are the breath of the place. They are the lungs of the place. They are what makes the air move across your face in the slow mechanical exhalation that is the signature of the place, the first thing you feel when you enter, the last thing you remember when you leave. They do not rest. They do not slow. They turn at their different speeds, and the sound of them turning is the sound of the place being *a place*, the way the sound of waves is the sound of a sea being a sea.

The fans are not alive in the way the moss is alive. But they are alive in the way that a heartbeat is alive — in the way that a thing that has been doing its thing without interruption for longer than anyone can remember becomes indistinguishable from the place itself. If the fans stopped, the place would die. Not immediately. Not dramatically. But slowly. The damp would stop cycling. The cold would stop moving. The moss would stop growing. The springtails would go. The pollen would fall and not be replenished. The place would become, over decades, a cold dark hole in the rock, visited sometimes by people with clipboards who would find less and less to write down.

So the fans live here too.

The place lives here.

This is the deepest truth of it. The place itself — this chamber of black iron and living green, this cold damp vast industrial cathedral of moss and pipe and turning blade — has become, over the years, something that is more than the sum of its parts. It is not a building. Buildings have purposes, and purposes can be completed, and completed buildings become ruins. It is not a machine. Machines have operators, and operators can leave, and abandoned machines become scrap. It is a *place.* It is the kind of place that, once established, maintains itself, the way a forest maintains itself, the way a reef maintains itself, the way a city maintains itself, by the constant slow interaction of its parts, none of which is in charge, all of which are necessary.

The moss grows. The moss is harvested. The cold is made. The damp is exhaled. The fans turn. The pipes drip. The people come and go. The springtails feed. The pollen falls. The place continues.

You stand in it.

You are not what lives here.

You are a visitor.
