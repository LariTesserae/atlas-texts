What lives in the Hall is not, properly speaking, a community. It is closer to a kind of memory — a layered archive of creatures whose lives have, over an unimaginably long time, been laid down in the stone of the place like the leaves of a pressed flower. The Hall is full, but the fullness is not the fullness of crowd. It is the fullness of strata. Every horizontal surface is occupied. Every channel is in use. Every ledge has, on it, something that was not there a hundred years ago, and beneath it, something that was not there a thousand years ago, and beneath that, something that was not there ten thousand years ago, and the eye can sometimes read the layers like the rings of a tree, except that the rings are not years but lives, and the lives are not all the same kind of life.

Begin with the ceiling, because that is where the place begins, in a certain sense, even though no creature lives there in the way the floor is lived in.

The translucent fish do not live on the ceiling so much as they *are* the ceiling, or a part of it. There are thousands of them. They hang in the upper part of the Hall in almost stationary columns, and to call them a school would be wrong, because a school moves and these do not move. They are arranged. They are held in place, in some sense, by the slow current, but they are also held in place by each other, by the faint bioluminescent signal of one body announcing itself to the next, so that the columns have a kind of organization that is older than any individual's decision. The columns are the shape of a stable equilibrium between the upward pull of the current and the downward pull of appetite, and the fish have, over a very long time, found the exact density that lets them eat what drifts past without being carried off by the water that brings the food. Each fish is roughly the length of a forearm. Each fish is almost transparent. The bellies carry a faint bluish glow — not a strong glow, not the theatrical glow of deep-sea anglerfish, but a kind of smolder, a slow phosphorescence that is only visible when the eye has fully adjusted. The eyes are wide. The eyes are wider than the eyes of any fish that lives where light is plentiful, and this is not because the fish need to see, exactly, but because the fish that did not have wide eyes did not survive. The wide-eyed fish survived. The narrow-eyed fish drifted past, into darker water, and were eaten by things that lived in darker water still. The wide-eyed fish stayed.

They eat what drifts past. They breathe, slowly. They wait. Their lives are not, in any meaningful sense, lives of action. They are lives of position. To be a translucent fish in the Hall is to maintain a body in a specific place, with a specific orientation, against a current that is always trying to drift you one way or another, and to do this for the whole of a very long life, and to do it without, in any human sense, intending to do it. The fish do not know they are part of a column. The fish do not know they are part of the ceiling. They are simply there, breathing, glowing, eating, and the column persists because each individual persists, and the column is the sum of an enormous number of small patient acts of continuation.

Below the columns, in the dim transitional zone, the ledges begin to widen, and the creatures to thicken.

These are not the same creatures as the translucent fish. These are the creatures of the wall — the ones that have, over the evolutionary time of the Hall, come to fit themselves to the carved stone so thoroughly that the boundary between creature and architecture has become difficult to discern. The first thing to understand about the wall creatures is that they have not colonized the Hall. They have *become* the Hall. Their bodies are part of the structure now, in a way that the translucent fish in the ceiling are not. The translucent fish can leave, in principle. The wall creatures, in many cases, cannot. They have grown into the stone. They have grown over the stone. They have, in some cases, become indistinguishable from the stone.

The snails are the most patient of these.

A snail in the Hall is not a snail the way a snail on a tidal rock is a snail. The tidal snail moves. The tidal snail grazes. The tidal snail's shell is a portable thing, a thing the snail carries, a thing the snail could, in principle, abandon. The snail in the Hall does not move. The snail in the Hall does not graze, exactly. The snail in the Hall has, over the course of its very long life, secreted calcium in a slow continuous spiral around the carved edge of a ledge, and the spiral has grown, and the snail has grown with it, and the shell has thickened, and the shell has become, at its base, indistinguishable from the carved stone it was once attached to. The snail's shell has, in many cases, fused with the stone. The snail is no longer a creature *on* a ledge. The snail is a feature of the ledge. The snail is part of the wall.

This is not parasitism. This is not symbiosis. This is, perhaps, the closest thing in the Hall to a kind of slow voluntary architecture — a creature that has, over its life, become a building. The snail does not know this. The snail eats. The snail secretes. The snail persists. And the wall grows, by a fraction of a millimeter per year, in the place where the snail has been.

The armored isopods are larger. They are the size of serving plates. They grip the stone with too many legs — fourteen, in the species that have been counted, though there may be others with different counts. They are not fast. They are not slow. They are simply present, in a way that has nothing to do with action. They hold themselves against the carved surfaces with a grip that is partly mechanical, partly adhesive, and they have been seen in the same position for the duration of dives that lasted, in human time, two hours. Two hours is not, of course, a meaningful duration for an isopod. The isopod's grip is measured in days, or weeks, or in the time between molts. The isopod grips because the place where it grips is the place where it eats, and the place where it eats is the place where the current brings small things, and the current brings small things because the current has always brought small things to this place, and the small things are eaten because they are there. The isopod is not making a decision. The isopod is occupying a niche. The niche has been occupied, by isopods, for longer than the isopod can remember.

The eel-shaped things coil in the wider grooves. They are thicker than eels. They are smoother. They have, in some species, a faint lateral stripe of luminescence along their length, and they use it, the biologists think, to signal to each other across the width of the Hall. The signals are not, in any obvious sense, communication. They are pulses. They are regularities. They are the kind of thing that, in a faster medium, would be a language. In the warm dense water of the Hall, the pulses are slow. They take seconds to cross the distance between two eels. They are answered, sometimes, by pulses that take more seconds to return. The exchanges are not, in human time, conversations. They are events. They happen across the Hall, in the dim, in the quiet, and the rest of the fauna do not respond to them, because the rest of the fauna have other things to do, which is mostly to wait.

At the floor, in the broader aisles that the carved ledges leave open, the larger fauna move. Or rather, the larger fauna *are* in motion, in the slow ceremonial sense in which motion is possible in this place. The water here is warm and dense and almost still. To swim fast would be to push against a medium that resists being pushed. To swim fast would cost more energy than the place can afford to spend. The floor fauna do not swim fast. They swim, when they swim at all, with a kind of deliberate patience that the divers who have watched them have a hard time not calling reverence.

The shapes with long lateral fins drift from one wall to the other. They drift. They do not swim. The difference matters. A swimming creature is pushing against the water. A drifting creature is being carried by the water, with only the gentlest corrections, only the smallest adjustments to the fins, only the suggestion of direction. The lateral-fin shapes move across the floor of the Hall the way dust moves across a still room — slowly, with a kind of inevitable grace, as if the water itself has decided, for a moment, to go this way rather than that. They are not large. They are not small. They are, in their proportions, almost elegant, and the divers who have watched them longest have written, in their reports, that watching them is like watching the water think.

The shapes with broad heads and almost no eyes feel their way along the floor. They are bottom-feeders. They eat what has settled into the channels, what has accumulated in the slight depressions the carved stone was never quite free of. They cannot see. They do not need to see. They have, instead, a kind of lateral-line sensitivity that lets them feel the pressure changes of small things moving near them, and they move toward the small things, and they eat them, and the small things are mostly the molted shells of other floor fauna, or the remains of translucent fish that drifted down from the ceiling and lost, somewhere along the way, the ability to maintain their position. The broad-headed shapes are the recyclers of the Hall. They are the ones that turn death back into the channels. They are patient with the remains. They are not, in any obvious sense, sentimental about the remains.

And there is the long flat ridged creature.

It is as long as a tall man. It is flat. Its back is ridged like a young mountain range, and the ridges are not decoration but structure — they are the supports, perhaps, of a body that has to maintain a particular shape in the dense water, a shape that lets it move without displacing too much of the medium. It moves through the aisles with the ceremonial slowness of a thing that has been doing the same thing for a very long time. The divers who have seen it have kept very still afterward. They have written that it does not seem to be moving *to* a place. It seems to be moving *through* a place. The aisles of the Hall are its paths. It knows them. It has known them, perhaps, for as long as the Hall has had aisles. Its ridges cast faint shadows in the bioluminescent light, and the shadows move across the floor in a slow ripple that is not quite rhythm and not quite random.

The Hall is lit, in patches, by the fauna themselves.

The bioluminescent fish in the upper columns glow with their smoldering blue. The luminous organs on the undersides of the larger floor fauna pulse, faintly, when the creatures are disturbed, or when they are feeding, or, perhaps, when they are simply alive. The pale phosphorescence of certain worms in the deeper channels gives off a kind of cold green light that has no obvious function except, perhaps, to mark the channels as occupied. The patches of relative brightness shift over the course of an hour as the fauna move, or as the current shifts, or as a larger creature passes and its bulk briefly occludes the glow of the upper columns. The patches of relative darkness are not absolute. The dark is not the dark of a cave. The dark is the dark of water in which almost no photon has arrived, and in which the eye has, after a few minutes, begun to invent its own dim registration of the world. To move through the Hall is to move in and out of these regions of light and dark, and the brain accommodates, after a while, to the dark as a presence rather than an absence — a kind of weather, a kind of medium, a thing that has texture, that has direction, that has, almost, weight.

The sound in the Hall is almost nothing.

The fauna make their small sounds. The soft pop of a snapping shrimp, deep in a channel. The distant creak of a grouper's swim bladder, somewhere on the seamount slope beyond the Hall. The occasional long hiss of a creature exhaling through gills. These sounds do not propagate, in the dense warm water, the way they would in air or in colder water. They are absorbed. They are muffled. They are present, but they are present the way the dark is present — as a kind of texture, a kind of weather, rather than as discrete events. To speak aloud in the Hall would be a kind of vandalism, not because anything would be harmed by it but because the place is tuned, in some way that the imagination supplies, to a frequency below speech. The Hall is tuned to the frequency of slow processes. The slow creak of the basalt. The slow grinding of the seamount into the abyssal plain. The slow accumulation of calcium on a ledge. The slow erosion of the basin's rim. To add a human voice to this would be to add a high-frequency event to a low-frequency place, and the dissonance would not be aesthetic, exactly, but it would be felt.

The fauna persist.

Nothing grows here. Nothing really decays. Things persist, in their layered strata, almost indefinitely. A ledge fills with calcium over decades. A channel silts up, and is re-excavated, perhaps, by a larger creature's habitual passage, and is silted up again. A creature dies, and its shell remains, and is colonized by smaller creatures, and is incorporated into the architecture of fauna. A new generation replaces the old, with no visible transition. The Hall is not changing. The Hall is, in some sense, finished. The Hall has reached the end of its becoming and has entered the long persistence of its being.

At the center of the floor, there is the basin.

The basin is round. It is four meters across, perhaps, and a meter deep. Its walls are smoother than the walls of the Hall. It is older than the visible fauna. It is, perhaps, the first thing cut into the tableland, the first intervention of the builders, before the walls went up around it. The divers who have seen it have a hard time describing what it is. They use words like "well" and "platter" and "offering-bowl," but none of these is quite right.

It contains water. The same warm water as everywhere else. The water in it is sometimes stiller, clearer, as if the currents of the Hall part around it. There is something at the bottom. There is always something at the bottom, though the eye cannot quite resolve what it is. Sediment, perhaps. Or an arrangement of small objects. Or nothing at all — the eye, expecting something, sees the play of faint light on the smooth stone and invents.

Around the rim of the basin, sometimes, a creature works.

It is small, by the standards of the floor fauna. It is the size of a child, perhaps, or smaller. It is hunched. Its body is adapted to the dense warm water in ways the human eye can read and the human imagination cannot fully resolve — a body that is at once familiar and not, that has the shape of a body meant to move through water but also, somehow, the shape of a body meant to do something with its hands.

The forelimbs almost look like hands. They are long. They are jointed. They are capable of gripping. They grip, now, with what looks like intention, a small object — a stone, a wedge, a length of worked bone. The tool is held the way a tool is held when the holder has held tools for a very long time. The grip is not tentative. The grip is the grip of a long habit.

The creature works at the rim of the basin.

It works slowly. It works with what can only be called patience, though whether the creature experiences patience as a feeling, or whether patience is simply the operational mode of its body, the observers cannot say. It works in a kind of circle, around the rim, attending to one section, then moving on, then returning. It does not look up when light strikes it. It does not stop when sound reaches it. It continues.

The divers who have observed it longest have written that it does not seem to be making anything. It seems to be maintaining something. The rim of the basin, perhaps, has a feature — a bevel, a glyph, a slight lip — that someone, long ago, wanted to preserve. The rim is eroding, slowly, the way all stone erodes in warm water. The creature is working against the erosion. The creature is removing the accumulation of sediment that would, over centuries, smooth the rim into shapelessness. The creature is keeping, against the patience of the water, the patience of the stone, a feature that someone wanted to keep.

The creature does not know what it is keeping. The creature has, perhaps, no memory of what the basin was for. The creature has, perhaps, no concept of the basin as a basin, or of the rim as a rim, or of the work it is doing as work. The creature is doing what its body does. The creature is continuing what its lineage has continued. The lineage is old. The lineage is the only suggestion, in the entire Hall, of a builder.

The creature is not alone.

It has, perhaps, been seen with another. The two of them, sometimes, working at the basin — one at the rim, one slightly farther out, holding something that might be a basket, or a tray, or a kind of net, catching the small particles the first one dislodges. The two of them move in a kind of loose coordination. They are not speaking. They are not signaling. They are simply working in the same place, at the same time, in a way that is not quite synchronized but is not quite independent. The divers who have seen them together have written that it looks, for lack of a better word, like a household. A small household, at the basin, in the warm dense water, in the dim, in the quiet.

The household does not look up. The household does not stop. The household continues.

Above them, in the upper columns, the translucent fish breathe and wait. Around them, in the aisles, the long flat ridged creature moves through its ceremonial slowness. Below them, in the deeper channels, the worms phosphoresce in their cold green. The Hall persists. The Hall is lit, in patches, by its own fauna. The Hall is quiet, in a way that is not quite silence but is the closest thing to silence the water has ever held.

The creature at the basin continues.

It does not know what it is keeping. It does not know what the Hall is for. It does not know that a civilization built this place, with bronze-green fishhooks and woven fiber that does not rot and patience enough to spend a hundred years on a single carved line. It does not know that the civilization is gone, or that it is not gone, or that the distinction has, in the slow time of the Hall, become meaningless. It knows the basin. It knows the rim. It knows the tool in its hands. It knows the slow erosion of the stone and the slow accumulation of sediment and the slow disappearance, into time, of a feature that someone wanted to keep.

It keeps it.

That is what lives in the Hall. A layered community of creatures that have, over an unimaginably long time, become the place they live in. A reef built on the armature of an older construction. A living architecture in which the boundary between fauna and stone has, in many places, dissolved. And at the center, at the basin, a small hunched figure with hands, with a tool, with the patient grip of a long habit, working against the slow disappearance of a thing it cannot name, in the warm dense water, in the dim, in the quiet, for as long as the work requires.
