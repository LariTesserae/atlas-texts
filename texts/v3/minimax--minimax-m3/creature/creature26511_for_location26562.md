# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here thinks of itself as living *in* this place. The place is not a container. It is a medium. The water things do not live in the water; the water is what they are made of, the way you are made of blood. The canopy things do not live in the canopy; the canopy is their weather, and they are how it happens to feel this morning.

So if you want to know who lives here, you have to begin with the smallest things, because the smallest things are doing the most, and because the small things, here, are not as small as you think.

---

In the warm tea-colored water that gathers in the seams of the basalt, in the bowls between root-tops, in the slow channels where the floor of the forest has failed to drain, there drifts a population of pale shapes that nobody has settled on a name for. They are the size of your smallest finger joint. They move in columns, all heading the same direction, at a speed so slow it looks like a trick of the light, like the water itself is sliding under them. They are following a smell. The smell is some chemical exhalation from the forest floor — from a particular bacterium, from a particular rot, from a particular breakdown of leaf and ant and frog that is happening somewhere downstream of them, always downstream, and they are drawn to it the way iron is drawn past a magnet, slowly and without apparent effort. They do not hunt. They are not hunted. They are being *oriented*. Their little lateral hairs tick against the gradient of the smell the way a compass needle ticks, and that is the entirety of their will.

They are not fish. They are not tadpoles. They are not shrimp, though a taxonomist who needed to file them would file them as shrimp and go home feeling uneasy. They are what the local mud is making this season, a draft of an animal that the forest has not yet committed to. They breathe through their legs. Their eyes, if they have eyes, do not work. They eat by filtering the brown water, and the brown water is full of the chewed-up bodies of their grandparents, and so they eat their way through their own ancestry, slowly, day by day, in the warm dim of the basalt shelves.

When the water in a particular bowl gets too crowded, or too cool, or too full of the wrong pollen, they do not swarm. They migrate. They climb out of the bowl by way of a wet root and walk, in their slow way, across the spongy rot of the forest floor, looking like something you have not been introduced to, and find another bowl, and resume. If you sit very still beside a path they have chosen, you will see them come past in a column three or four bodies wide, each one independent, each one following its own compass, and together they will read like a sentence written in a language that uses no vowels.

They are the baseline. Everything else in the water is built on top of them.

---

On top of them, in the same brown water, there are things that have decided what to be. There is a kind of small crayfish, almost transparent, with red points at the joints of its claws, and it eats the migrating pale shapes with the same unhurried attention that you might use to pick cashews from a dish. There is a flatworm, the color of a bruise, that glides over the surface film and pours itself around obstacles the way a glove is pulled off a hand. There are dragonfly nymphs, patient and explosive, that have been sitting on the same basalt face for what looks like weeks, breathing through the rectum, waiting for something meaty to drift close enough that the hinged mask under their face can shoot forward and pull it in. They are not rare. They are not charismatic. They are the actual machinery of the place.

And in the same water, around the roots of the figs and the rotten knees of fallen trees, there is a kind of small frog, no bigger than a grape, whose males begin to call in the late afternoon — not the shrill bright call of a tree frog, but a low, ventriloquial sound, like someone knocking on a wooden door with a knuckle, repeated, *tok tok tok*, and the sound does not travel cleanly through the air because the air is too wet and too full of other sounds, and so it travels through the wood, through the root, through the mud, and a female feels it in the bones of her feet and walks toward it, sometimes across the surface of the water, on her toes, without breaking the meniscus. She is not listening. She is *being conducted*.

---

Above the water, on the stones and the wet bark, there is the lizard.

The lizard is about the length of your hand. It is the color of a coal that has been held near a fire — black with a faint heat at the edges. Along its back it carries a sail of skin, supported by long spines, and the sail is the bright, almost radioactive orange of a road sign in a country where you are not supposed to drive. It sits on a basalt column in a patch of thin sun, and it watches you with one eye, the other eye on its own weather, and it does not run.

It does not run because you are not a category. To this lizard, you are a temperature. You are a vibration that arrived and is now standing there, the way a cold front arrives and stands there, and you will eventually go, and the lizard will still be sitting on its basalt watching the ants reorganize. Its bright sail is not for you. Its bright sail is for something else — a hawk, perhaps, or a larger lizard, or a snake that hunts by sight — and the fact that no such predator seems to operate in this forest anymore is not the lizard's concern. The lizard does not update its wardrobe based on local conditions. It was born bright, it will die bright, and if it gets eaten, it will get eaten for a reason that has nothing to do with its color, the way you might get hit by a falling branch whether or not you wore the right shoes.

It eats beetles. It does not chase them. It waits, and a beetle comes within range, and the lizard is suddenly not where it was, and the beetle is in its mouth, and the lizard has resumed sitting. This is its entire life. It is very good at it.

There are other lizards here, smaller, browner, that do chase, and that flick their tongues at the air to taste where you have been, and that will follow you along a fallen log for a few meters out of what can only be described as professional curiosity. They are not afraid of you. They have come to the conclusion that you are a thing that displaces a certain number of insects from a certain volume of air as it moves, and they would like to be standing where you are about to be.

---

On the forest floor, the ants are a fact.

The ants here are the size of your thumbnail, and they are not an exception; they are the rule. There are smaller ants, yes, but the dominant species, the one whose trails you actually have to step over or around, is a thumb-nailed red-black thing with a head like a drawbridge and a posture that suggests it has somewhere to be and will not be embarrassed by your presence. They cross the path in columns two meters wide and, on a good day, in unbroken formation for as far as you can see in either direction.

They are carrying things. They are always carrying things. The white soft thing they were carrying when you first noticed them was a frog, yes, but the frog had been dead for some hours already, and they were not carrying it as a kill; they were carrying it as a *find*, and they would carry it, with the slow coordination of a rope-team hauling a cable, back to a place in the ground where other ants were waiting to receive it. The place in the ground is a thing you would not recognize as an anthill if you saw it, because it is mostly under the spongy rot, and partly under a stone, and partly under the root of a tree that has not yet decided to fall, and its only surface sign is a low mound of pale fibrous pulp that looks, from a step away, like the forest's attempt to cough.

The ants in this mound are farmers. They do not farm leaves; they farm a kind of fungus, which they feed on the bodies of dead things, and the fungus feeds on the bodies in a particular way, and the ants eat the fungus, and the fungus eats the dead things, and the dead things were, until recently, alive, and were, until very recently, eating things that were, until the day before yesterday, also alive. The chain is continuous. The ants are the visible inch of it.

They are not aggressive to you. They are not docile. They are indifferent. If you stand in the middle of their column, they will climb over your boot with the unhurried air of pedestrians who have decided that this is the shortest route and that you are part of the route. If you brush them off, they will resume. If you crush a few, the others will step over the bodies and continue, because the column has a momentum that is larger than any individual in it, and the dead are simply a delay, and the dead will themselves be carried when the work allows.

---

In the air between the trunks, in the gaps where the light comes down green and heavy, there is the spider.

The spider sits in the middle of a web that has been built between two trunks three meters apart, and the web is not the architecture you are used to. It is not a flat circle with a single thread of signal running out from the center. It is a three-dimensional tangle, a thrown handful of silk that has caught on everything within reach and pulled itself into a shape that resembles nothing so much as a region of air that has been told to behave like a curtain. It is strong. It is, as you have been told, stronger than the basalt, and you do not believe it until you see a bird, a small dull-colored bird, fly into it at speed, and the web holds, and the bird struggles, and the spider, who has been sitting on the underside of a leaf a meter away, watches, and waits.

The spider is patient. The spider has nothing else. It waits the way the basalt waits — with the patience of a thing that is made of the same mineral as a coastline. Its legs span a saucer. Its body is small, almost an afterthought, a knob at the center of a system of long jointed levers. Its eyes are eight small obsidian beads that reflect nothing. It does not see you. It feels you, as a change in the air, as a shadow across the silk, and it does not move, because you are not in the web, and the web is its entire world.

It eats what the web catches. It eats mostly moths. It eats beetles that blunder in at dusk. It eats, occasionally, the small green frog that has misjudged a jump between two trunks and is now hanging by one foot from a thread of silk, slowly winding itself into a husk. The spider wraps the catch in fresh silk, and the silk is the color of a raw egg, and the wrapped parcel is hung from a side branch and left there, and the spider returns to its waiting, and the parcel is eaten later, in private, the way you might eat a sandwich you brought from home.

---

The moths the spider catches are themselves a population. There is the pale moth, the size of your spread hand, that clings to the underside of a leaf and breathes so slowly you can watch the air move in and out of it. There is a smaller moth, brown, that rests on bark and is invisible against the bark, and that visits flowers at night and has a tongue longer than its body, which it uncoils like a fishing line and dips, with the delicacy of a hand threading a needle, into the throat of a flower that has bent over to drink from a stream. There is a moth that does not eat at all. It has no mouth. It lives for the few days its body can sustain itself on the fat it ate as a caterpillar, and its only job, in those few days, is to find a female, and it finds her by following a smell so faint that the human nose, even a trained one, cannot detect it, and so the moth is navigating in a world we cannot enter, a world made entirely of gradients we cannot smell.

The moths are eaten by the spider. They are also eaten by bats. The bats live higher up, in the canopy stratum, and they hunt by sound, by chirping and listening to the echoes, and the moths, some of them, have evolved to hear those chirps and fold their wings and drop, and they fall into the leaf litter and become invisible, and the bat passes, and the moth climbs back up the trunk to its height and resumes. This is an arms race that has been going on for as long as the basalt has been cooling, and neither side has won, and neither side will win, and the moth that drops and the bat that misses and the spider that catches and the lizard that waits are all parts of a single ongoing negotiation that has no end.

---

In the canopy stratum — the layer that lives at a height of forty meters or sometimes less, where the figs have grown sideways and decided that sideways was good enough — there are things that have never seen the ground.

There is a beetle there, bright green, the color of a new leaf, that lives its entire life in a single bromeliad. The bromeliad is a cup of water formed by the overlapping leaves of an epiphytic plant, and the cup holds rainwater and the bodies of dead insects and the eggs of frogs, and the beetle eats the eggs, and the beetle drinks the rainwater, and the beetle mates, and the beetle lays its eggs in the same cup, and the larvae eat the same eggs, and so on, in a closed economy the size of a teacup. The beetle does not know that there is a ground. The ground, for it, is a rumor. It has been told, by whatever analog of rumor a beetle has, that below the bromeliads there is something called *trunk*, and below the trunks something called *floor*, and below the floor something called *water*, but these are stories the way the horizon is a story to a sailor. The beetle has never tested them.

There is a frog in the same stratum, smaller, the color of a ripe avocado, that calls from inside a bromeliad, and the call comes out distorted by the cup of leaves into something that is not quite a frog-call and not quite a mechanical sound and not quite the wet finger around the rim of a glass. It is its own sound. The frog does not know that the sound it is making is unlike any sound on the ground. It does not know that the ground has sounds. It knows only that when it makes this sound, another frog, somewhere else in the canopy, makes a similar sound back, and that this is how the world is supposed to work, and that the work is being done.

There are beetles and frogs at knee height that have never climbed. They live in the leaf litter, in the small dark weather that exists between the dead leaves and the soil, and they eat the dead leaves, and they breed in the dead leaves, and their whole world is a few centimeters of damp and rot, and they have no reason to believe that there is anything above them except the occasional falling leaf, which lands on them, and which they eat.

The two strata — canopy and floor — are not separated by distance. They are separated by a kind of mutual ignorance so complete that it functions as a wall. An animal in the canopy, lowered to the ground, would not know where it was. An animal on the ground, lifted to the canopy, would not know where it was either. The forest is, in this sense, a series of forests, stacked.

---

There are birds here, and the birds are not the birds you are used to.

There is the bird with the phrase of three notes. The phrase is a kind of small, sad question, *tew tew tew*, descending, and it is asked by a male sitting on a bare branch in the canopy, and it is answered by another male sitting on a bare branch half a kilometer away, and then by a third, further still, and the phrase travels across the lowlands like a rumor in a long hallway. The birds are not talking to each other. Each one is asking the same question to the same sky, and the sky happens to answer back in the form of another bird asking the same question, and this is, for them, communication.

There is a smaller bird, the color of damp moss, that hunts by following columns of army ants across the floor. The ants flush small insects out of the leaf litter as they march, and the small bird eats the flushed insects, and the bird follows the ant column the way a gull follows a fishing boat, and it does this all day, every day, and it does not fear the ants, because the ants are not hunting it, and it does not fear you, because you are not hunting it, and if you sit very still beside an ant column, the bird will come within arm's length of you and not even look.

There is a night bird, larger, with a face like a satellite dish, that sits on a branch over a pool of still water and waits for the frogs to begin their chorus, and when they do, it folds its wings and drops, soundlessly, into the pool, and it comes up with a frog in its mouth, and it flies back to the branch, and the frog does not scream, because the throat has already been crushed, and the night resumes.

---

And there are the things that are not animals.

There is the lichen on the basalt. It grows in slow orange circles, and the circles are concentric because the lichen is old, and each ring is a year, and a lichen that is the size of a dinner plate is older than some of the trees it is growing on. It eats the basalt. It does this by excreting, very slowly, a weak acid, and the acid eats the rock, and the lichen absorbs the minerals, and the basalt retreats by a fraction of a millimeter a year, and the lichen advances by a fraction of a millimeter a year, and in a million years the basalt will be gone and the lichen will still be here, in a slightly different color.

There is the moss on the bark. It holds more water than its own weight, and it makes the bark slippery, and the bark does not mind, because the moss is also breaking it down, and the breakdown is what feeds the trees, and the trees are what the moss is growing on, and this is, again, the continuous chain. The moss is not a parasite. The moss is a slow conversation between a plant and a surface.

There is the fungus, which is the actual nervous system of the place. The fungus runs in threads through the soil and through the wood and through the dead leaves, and it connects the roots of the trees to the roots of other trees, and it passes between them sugars and signals and warnings, and when a tree is attacked by an insect, the fungus carries the alarm to the other trees, and the other trees begin, within hours, to produce defensive chemicals in their leaves. The forest is not a collection of trees. The forest is a single organism threaded through with mycelium, and the trees are its organs, and the fungus is its blood, and the ants that farm the fungus are a kind of white cell, and the beetles and the frogs and the birds and the bats are the way the organism happens to feel, today, at this latitude, in this weather.

---

And there are the leaves that grow upside down.

This is the strangeness, and the strangeness is a fact of life here, and the life here has decided to include it.

The leaves, when they fall, do not always fall in the direction of the wind. Sometimes they rise. A leaf detaches from a branch and begins to descend, and halfway down it pauses, and then it begins, very gently, to climb back. It climbs until it finds a branch and sticks to the underside of it, where it is not supposed to be, and it stays there, and slowly it begins to root, and it becomes a thing growing upside down in the dark.

Nobody knows why. The locals, if there were locals, would not call it strange. They would call it the way the leaves do.

The leaves that grow this way are not rare. They are common. If you part the hanging roots of a fig, you find them — leaves pointed away from the sky, growing away from the light, in a direction no leaf is supposed to grow, and they are green, and they are alive, and they are doing something no textbook has described. They are not parasites. They are not sick. They are not a mutation in the usual sense. They are a different solution to the problem of being a leaf in a place where the light comes from the wrong direction and the canopy is the wrong way up. They are, in some sense, the most adapted thing in the forest. They have stopped pretending that down is down.

The creatures of the forest do not remark on them. The beetles walk over them. The frogs call from underneath them. The spider builds webs that include them. The lizard has been seen, once or twice, basking on a branch with its orange sail toward the trunk, in the gap between an upside-down leaf and the wood, and the basking is the same basking, and the leaf is the same leaf, and the strangeness is the same strangeness, and the place holds all of it without comment, the way the basalt holds the water, with great seriousness, and without spilling.
