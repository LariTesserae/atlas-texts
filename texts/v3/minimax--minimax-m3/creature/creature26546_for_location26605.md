# The Inheritors of the Still Engines

They are not what you would call *people*. The word implies too much consensus, too much agreement on what a body is. Here, the body is more of a proposal.

---

**The Patient** is the one you will see first, if you see any of them. She — or it — or the terrace's way of pretending there is a difference — sits on the second tier from the top, on a step that is always slightly warmer than the other steps. She has been sitting there, as far as anyone can tell, for as long as the place has been occupied. Her legs are crossed. Her hands rest on her knees. Her eyes are open.

She is made, mostly, of the poured substance. Her skin is the color of the terrace. The darker seams that run through her body are not cosmetic; they are functional, carrying the small glow that the buttresses carry, the banked-coal warmth that suggests something is being slowly conducted through her. She does not breathe in any way you can detect. She does not blink. The condensation gathers on her shoulders the way it gathers on a flange.

If you speak to her, she will not answer for a long time. Then she will say something that does not quite address what you asked, and the thing she says will be true, and you will not know what to do with it. Once, when a visitor was frightened by the inverted terrace above, she said: *The moss knows.* The visitor waited. She did not say anything else. The visitor went away feeling less frightened, though she could not say why.

The Patient is not waiting for anything. She has finished waiting. She is in a posture that, in other places, would indicate waiting, but here indicates a kind of completion. She is the terrace's way of demonstrating that a body can be still without being empty.

---

**The Waterman** is harder to find. He moves along the troughs.

He is tall, or seems tall, because he is always standing and he is always in the shallow water of the long glazed channels. The water comes to his ankles. It is the color of weak tea. He walks it slowly, back and forth, with a pole that has a small brass cup on the end, and he scoops, and he pours the cup back into the trough a little further along, and he does this all day. He is not moving the water anywhere. The water returns to where it was, or it does not, and he does not mind.

He is not wet. The water does not cling to him. He walks through it the way a finger walks through a projection of water, displacing without absorbing. His feet make the small clear bell-sound of a drop falling from a flange, with every step, and the animals pause to listen, and then continue.

He wears a long coat that is the color of the metal beneath the poured skin. The coat moves in a way that is not quite fabric. He has a face, or a thing that occupies the place of a face, but it is blurred in the way the air is blurred near the troughs in summer — not heat-shimmer exactly, but a softness, as if the geometry of him were not quite committed.

He does not speak. He has, it is said, spoken once, to a child who had gotten lost on the upper tiers, and he said *down* and pointed, and the child went down, and that is the only word anyone has heard him say. The child grew up here and is now one of the moss-tenders. She does not remember the word, only the direction.

---

**The Moss-Tenders** are the closest thing this place has to a population.

There are perhaps forty of them. They live in the sheltered corners, near the cylinders of imported soil, where the woody stick-trees have been planted. They tend the moss. They tend the lichen. They pull the small pale animals away from the imported soil before they eat too much of it. They are the reason the green exists at all; without them, the moss would be eaten down to the cracks and the place would be only bone and brown.

They are not all the same. Some are tall and thin and look almost human from a distance, though up close their proportions are slightly off — the arms a little too long, the joints bending in directions you did not know joints could bend. Some are short and wide and move on all fours. Some have the dull-bone color of the terrace. Some have the warm brown of the metal beneath. One of them, the oldest, is the green of the moss itself, with gray-orange lichen fruiting on her shoulders in a way that may be deliberate.

They speak to each other in a low murmur that does not carry. They have names for themselves, but the names are not used often. They refer to each other by what they do. *The one who tends the eastern bed.* *The one whose soil-plot the beetles like.* *The one who was a child and was told to go down.*

They eat the moss. They drink the trough-water. They sleep on the imported soil, curled in the cylinders like roots. They are warm in a way the rest of the terrace is not warm, because their bodies are doing the slow work of digestion, and the digestion gives off a small heat, and the heat rises into the cold air and is visible, sometimes, as a faint shimmer above them in the mornings.

They do not age in any way you can perceive. The oldest of them looks no different from the youngest, except that her lichen has fruited. It is not clear whether they die. Their droppings are not found. When one of them is no longer seen, it is assumed that she has gone deeper — into the seams, into the dark spaces between the poured skin and the wire armature — to join the larger fauna, perhaps, or to become something else, or to be absorbed back into the slow rearrangements of the terrace itself.

No one here mourns. The place does not have the texture for mourning. When a moss-tender is gone, the others simply tend her bed a little more carefully, and the moss grows in a way that is slightly different, and the difference is noted, and the work continues.

---

**The Animals** are not tended. They are endured.

The small ones — the mouse-sized, the beetle-sized, the hand-sized with tucked fingers — are everywhere. They live in the channels. They live on the surfaces. They live in the cracks between the poured panels, in the delaminated seams, in the soft places where the condensation gathers and pools. They eat the moss when they can. They eat the imported soil. They eat, sometimes, the poured substance itself, leaving the small bright toothmarks that appear overnight. They eat the metal, sometimes, and pass it in their pale dry droppings.

They are the color of the terrace. They are the color of the metal. They are the color of the trough-water and the color of the condensation and, occasionally, the gray-orange of the fruiting lichen. They are camouflage as a way of life. You do not see them until they move, and when they move you wish you had not, because the movement is wrong — too slow or too fast or in a direction the body should not be able to go.

They are quiet. They have no voices that anyone has heard. They communicate, if they communicate, by the slow rearrangement of their bodies on a surface — a leg extended a little further, a seam shifted a centimeter — and the rearrangements are read by others of their kind in patterns you cannot follow. The terrace hums with this silent traffic, the small constant adjustments of the small constant fauna, and the hum is below your hearing, and it is not for you.

The larger ones — the ones whose droppings contain fragments of metal, the ones that live in the seams and in the dark — are almost never seen. You know them by their signs. A slow scrabbling, far away, when you put your ear to a riser. A panel that is warm for no reason. A flange that breathes. The Patient, it is said, knows them. The Patient has been seen, once, to put her hand flat against a buttress and hold it there for a long time, and the buttress went quiet, and the larger fauna were not heard for many days afterward. No one knows what she said to them. No one knows if she said anything.

---

**The Terrace Itself** is the only permanent resident.

It has been here longer than any of the others. It was here before the Patient. It was here before the moss-tenders arrived with their cylinders of imported soil and their stick-trees. It was here before the animals moved into its seams and began, slowly, to become part of its operations.

It hums in its wires. It sweats from its poured skin. It cools and warms and cools again on its slow schedule. It shifts, by millimeters, toward a configuration that no one can predict. It runs.

It does not care about you. It does not care about the moss-tenders or the Patient or the Waterman or the animals. It does what it does. The fact that it has inhabitants is incidental — a side effect of the seams being wide enough to nest in, the soil being imported, the light being warm enough to support a slow photosynthesis, the condensation being drinkable. The terrace would continue to do what it does if all of them left tomorrow. It would continue to do what it does if all of them were absorbed into its seams and became part of its operations. The slow becoming does not require witnesses.

But the witnesses are here anyway. The Patient on her warm step. The Waterman in his trough. The moss-tenders in their corners, tending the green that is the only soft color in the place. The animals in their channels, eating, rearranging, being quiet.

They are not happy here. The word *happy* does not apply. They are not unhappy. The word *unhappy* also does not apply. They are *in* the place. They have made their accommodations. They have found their edges, their designated places, their tolerable relationships with the poured substance and the metal and the slow hum of the wires.

---

You sit on a step. The step is cold.

A beetle-shaped thing, the color of dull bone, crosses the riser in front of you at a pace that might be a centimeter an hour. It pauses. It is somewhere else. You do not hear it move.

Above you, the Patient sits on her warm step. She has been sitting there for as long as anyone can remember. She is not waiting. She is complete.

To your left, a moss-tender is pulling a small pale animal away from the imported soil. She does it gently. She has done it a thousand times. She will do it a thousand more.

In the trough below, the Waterman scoops and pours. The water does not move. The water does not need to move.

The terrace hums.

The damp gathers on your sleeve.
