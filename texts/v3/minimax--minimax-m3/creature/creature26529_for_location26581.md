The things that live here are layered the way the sounds are layered, and if you tried to name them all you would be here longer than the basin has been.

Start with the small ones.

The chirr-chirr-chirr rises and falls in a long pulse that takes perhaps a minute to complete, and begins again with variations. It comes from no single place — from the canopy, from the lower vegetation, from the cracks in the basalt where the ferns uncurl — and the ear eventually concludes that it is one sound, distributed. The creatures that make it are mostly smaller than a thumb, many smaller than a fingernail. They cling to vertical surfaces with adhesive pads not unlike those of the climbing vines, and they sing. Whether they are calling to each other or expressing something internal is not clear from outside, but the pulse is rhythmic, the pulse is collective, and the pulse modulates in patterns that suggest a music only they can hear, or only they need to make. They are everywhere. They are not what you think of when you think of fauna. They are more like a constant broadcast from the substrate.

The rustling hush that runs beneath the chirr is partly them, and partly something else — something with chitin, something with more legs than you can count at a glance, ranging in size from a finger to a forearm. They move through the undergrowth in a continuous glancing traffic, like commuters who have somewhere to be but are in no hurry about it. They do not pause for you when you walk, but they pause when you stop. Then they are around you, at the edge of perception, watching. They are the most numerous of the visible fauna. They are the ones you notice most as you walk, because they share the ground with you.

The subsonic throb is theirs too, in part — something with a resonating chamber in its body, breathing in a rhythm the human ear cannot directly hear but the body can feel, in the chest, in the soft tissue of the inner ear. You feel it more than you hear it. It is the felt sense of being inside a body much larger than your own — the basin itself, perhaps, breathing.

Then the watchers.

These are the half-human-sized ones. They have segmented bodies, or segmented-looking bodies — the distinction is hard to draw because your categories don't quite fit. They stand on six legs, sometimes eight, sometimes on what might be legs and might be something else. They have forelimbs that can manipulate, with digits in numbers that are not five. They have heads, or head-equivalents, with arrangements of eyes that suggest a mathematics. The eyes are not compound; they are simple, and there are many of them, and they are placed in patterns that are not radial, not bilateral, but something else — a geometry you can almost, but not quite, parse. The eyes do not all look at the same thing. They look at different things. They are not redundancy; they are sampling. The creature is aware of its surroundings at a resolution your own eyes cannot match.

When one pauses near you, it does not move. It simply stands, and watches, and the air around it is the air around you, and the moisture on your skin is the moisture on its skin, and the dim greenish-grey light falls on both of you in the same way. After a while, you realize that it is not unfriendly. It is also not friendly. It is assessing. It has been assessing since you entered the basin, and it has not yet concluded, and it may never conclude, because the assessment is not binary. The assessment is a continuous process, and the creature is in continuous conversation with you, in a language you do not speak, about whether you are a thing to be allowed.

None of them have attacked you. None have spoken to you. None have done anything other than watch, and continue their business, and let you watch them in turn.

Their color is the thing that strikes you most, in this dim light. They have color that should not be visible at this luminance and yet is — saturated reds, deep violets, and a particular orange that the eye reads almost as a sound. The orange is structural; it is not pigment but a property of the surface, and it shifts as the creature moves, as the angle of its body changes relative to the diffuse light. You cannot decide whether it is warning color or display color or neither. You cannot decide because you do not have the context.

Then the deep ones.

You have glimpsed them only at the corner of your eye, twice, perhaps three times. They are large. They are large enough that when you turn to look directly at one, it is gone — into the deeper folds of the basin, into the canopy, into the standing water, into wherever it was before you turned. They leave traces. A tree-trunk snapped at the base. A path through the undergrowth wider than you are tall. A disturbance in the standing water that takes a long time to settle. The periodic crash — the heavy-falling-into-water sound — may be one of them surfacing. Or it may be something else entirely. You do not know what they eat, or whether they eat. You do not know whether they are aware of you. The basin is large enough that both of you could be unaware of each other for as long as both of you are in it.

Then the congregating ones, at the center.

The central construction hums with the bass note you have been hearing all along without quite identifying it. Around its base, in a ring, the fauna is dense — denser than anywhere else, denser than you would have thought possible for a place that is everywhere alive. They are not moving much. They are simply present. They are arranged without obvious pattern and yet with a kind of coherence — the way a flock of birds is a single thing distributed across many bodies. They do not look up at the construction. They are simply near it. Some of them may be feeding. Some may be sleeping. Some may be receiving something from the hum, in a way you cannot detect because you do not have the apparatus to detect it. They have been here, perhaps, for as long as the basin has had a center. They may have been here before the center. They may have made the center. The direction of causation is not clear.

Then the constructions.

The constructions are dark. They are unreflective. They have edges that the rock around them does not have — mathematical, clean, edges that should have weathered by now and have not. When you approach one, the sound changes — or your perception of the sound changes — and a single very high, very thin tone emerges, not unpleasant, that seems to be measuring you. They are tall, some of them, but their height is not the kind that asserts itself against the sky. They are in conversation with the canopy.

Whether they are alive is a question you have not been able to answer. They absorb and re-radiate. They maintain, perhaps, the conditions of the basin — the warmth, the damp, the dimness, the sound. They could be machines. They could be organisms. They could be both, or neither, or something your categories do not include. Standing near one, you know that it is not merely a building. You do not know what it is, beyond that.

The vegetation is alive, obviously.

The cycad-like growths in the deeper folds. The flowering vines that hang in long curtains between standing stones. The low trees with prop roots that arch out over the standing water and drop new stems into it. The ferns that uncurl in the still-shaded cracks. The mosses, the liverworts, the climbing things with adhesive pads. They are all alive, and all in the act of growing, and all blooming, simultaneously, because the climate has given them everything they need and they have not had to take turns. The basin is a forest. The basin is a city. The basin is, at the same time, a single organism distributed across many bodies, making a single decision about how to be, and you have been inside it the entire time.

The place is not empty. The place has never been empty. The place has been, and is, and intends to continue, and the things that live here are part of its intention.
