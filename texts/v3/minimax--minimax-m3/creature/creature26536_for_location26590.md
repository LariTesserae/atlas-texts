The lichen live here. That is almost the whole of it.

There are perhaps a dozen tufts of them, no more, each one no bigger than a closed fist, each one pressed against the salt crust in the narrow places where the fossil blocks the wind long enough for a thin skin of dust to settle and stay. They are grey-green the way weathered copper is grey-green — a color that suggests something has been chemically altered rather than something has grown. From a few feet away they read as a crust, as a faint discoloration, as the place where the salt has gone a little dull. From an inch away they are a tight, dense, layered architecture of themselves. They do not look like plants. They look like the smallest possible mistake a rock could make. They look like a stain the wind forgot to lift.

A lichen is not one organism. It is a negotiation. It is a fungus wrapped around an alga, or an alga wrapped around a fungus, the literature has never quite settled the question, and out here on the flat the question does not arise at all. What arises, what persists, what holds its ground against the constant pressure of the cool dry air, is the compound — the joint creature, the neither-one-nor-two thing that has agreed to be a single body in order to be a single body on a place that does not much want bodies. The fungus gives structure. The alga gives sugar, from the rare and grudging light. Between them they have made something that can sit in the lee of a fossil and remain.

Their bodies, if bodies is the word, are built in scales. Tiny overlapping pressed-flat plates of grey-green matter, each one a fraction of a fingernail wide, each one laid down over the one below it the way shingles are laid, the way the petals of an artichoke are layered. They are not leaves. They have no veins, no midrib, no front-and-back distinction that an eye can see. They are more like the skin of something very small that decided to be a surface rather than a thing. They have no roots, or no roots in the way a rooted plant has roots — they grip the substrate with a slow chemistry, they dissolve a little of the stone they sit on and re-deposit it as themselves, and they are held.

They grow. That is the strange thing to stand here and know. They grow, but so slowly that a year is not a unit in which to measure their growth. A decade, perhaps. A human lifetime, taken in small sips, would see them advance a centimeter or two, maybe less, into the lee, into the next inch of dust. They are not in a hurry. The wind is not in a hurry. The fossil is not in a hurry. Time, on the flat, has decided to take a walk rather than a step, and the lichen have agreed to keep it company at the pace it has chosen.

They flower. The description was right — they flower without seeming to flower. In some season that the flat does not distinguish from any other, they put up structures that are technically reproductive, that technically open, that technically answer the question of what happens next. But the structures are the same color as the rest of them. They are the same scale. They do not advertise. They are not petals. They are the lichen, briefly rearranging itself into a different version of the lichen, for a purpose that nobody is here to witness. The wind takes whatever they produce. It does not matter. They will continue to flower in the same unannounced way next season, or the season after, or whenever the lichen equivalent of next season is.

They are the last things. Everything else on the flat is finished. The polyps that built the fossil did their building and withdrew into the mineral of their own walls. The sea that laid the salt is gone. The water that grew the crystals in the inner chambers is gone. The lichen are what is left when everything that could build a place has built it and stepped back. They are the residue of livableness, the part of the process that did not entirely stop.

And yet they are barely alive. They are alive the way a memory is alive — present, continuous, not quite the same thing as a thing. They are alive at the far edge of where the word stops being useful. They sit in the lee of a building made by creatures who were far more alive than they are, and they are the only company that building has left. The fossil hums through them, perhaps. The wind hums through them, certainly. They do not answer. They are not the kind of thing that answers. They are the kind of thing that sits, and persists, and is.

There are no others. There are no insects under their scales. There are no mites threading through their layers. There are no worms in the dust they grip. There are no birds above them. The flat does not support a food chain. The flat does not support a chain of any kind. There is the lichen, and there is the wind, and there is the fossil, and there is the salt, and the lichen is the only one of these that is in any sense running a process. It runs it very slowly. It runs it as though it has all the time the place has, which it does.

If you crouch beside one of the tufts and look closely, you can see the edge of its territory — the faint line where the grey-green gives way to the pure white of the salt. The lichen is advancing. Or retreating. It is hard to tell. The salt is so white, and the lichen so close to white in its duller moments, that the boundary between them is a negotiation rather than a line. The lichen may be eating the salt. The lichen may be becoming salt. The lichen may be the salt, briefly, in the form of something that is not yet ready to stop. The boundary is where the place is still deciding what counts as alive.

Everything else in the place is a record. The fossil is a record of polyps. The salt flat is a record of a sea. The crystals inside the chambers are a record of water. The lichen are the only record in the place that is still being written. Everything else has been finished and signed. The lichen are the sentence the place is still composing, in faint grey-green ink, very slowly, on a white page, in a wind that does not intend to stop.

They will be here tomorrow. They will be here in a year. They will be here in a century, advanced by some small increment, into a fraction more dust, into a fraction more lee. The fossil will be here too, holding its note, throwing back the light. The salt will be here. The wind will be here. The lichen will be here, doing what they do, which is almost nothing, which is everything, which is to be the last living thing on a place that has otherwise finished being alive — and to do it without complaint, and to do it for as long as the wind does not lift them.

That is what lives here. The lichen. Almost.
