They are not tall. Generations in the basin have made them so — or the cold, or the slow diet, or the weight of damp clothing worn year-round, or simply that the tall ones left, long ago, for the dry rim where the sun makes its full transit. The ones who stayed are the ones who fit. Compact. Deep-chested from breathing cold air for a long time. Shoulders that slope a little from hauling water and pulling things up from the basin floor. Hands that are wide across the palm and short in the finger, the kind of hand that closes well around wet rope and slick stone and the slim bodies of the pale channel-fish.

Their skin is pale in a way that has nothing to do with where their ancestors came from. It is the pallor of people who live under diffuse light, in whom the sun has not been a visitor in so many generations that the body has stopped preparing for it. They do not tan. They pink, faintly, on the rare days when a slant of hard afternoon light reaches the upper stories of the houses on the western side, and the children hold their faces up to it then, eyes closed, in a way that has the reverence of ritual without the form of ritual. The light is brief and they know it is brief.

Their eyes are adapted to the gray. The pupils of strangers from the rim dilate painfully when they come down; theirs are accustomed to the soft middle tones, and they can see, in the alleys at dusk, the shape of a stooping figure three houses away, or the particular dark of a step where the stone is treacherous with moss. They are not near-sighted. They are differently-sighted. They see in low contrast what others would call nothing.

Their hair is dark, generally, and lank with the damp. They do not braid it elaborately; the braids would hold water and never dry. They cut it shorter than the rim people do, or bind it close to the skull with cord of woven fiber, and the cord darkens with wear and is rarely taken off. The elderly let it grow sometimes, and it lies along their shoulders like wet moss, and the children think the elderly look like the alders.

They are not hungry, but they are not full either. The diet is what the basin gives: pale fish from the channels, taken on hand-lines by children who sit in the water with their bare feet among the cobbles and do not speak; watercress and mint pulled from the wet seams between the houses; the eggs of the swallows when they can be reached, which is rarely and is not often attempted; the meat of the fox sometimes, when a fox is taken in a trap of bone and fiber set in a margin thicket; mushrooms that come up in the wet weeks; the pith of certain rushes; small tubers dug from the soft ground at the edges of the central pool. They have gardens, of a kind — old water-channels filled with rubble and planted, the soil of them built up with whatever will compost, and in these they grow the things that will grow: more cress, more mint, a tough allium that puts out pale green blades in any season, a leafy thing with a small white root that tastes of earth and water and not much else. They trade, on the path up to the rim, for things that need dryness — grain in small amounts, carried down in skin bags that sweat in the climb; dried meat; salt; the hard flint that comes from the plateau above. The trade is not frequent. The path is long and steep at the end, and the rim people do not love to come down, and the basin people do not love to climb.

They do not love speech. This is the thing outsiders remark on first, when they come down into the basin, and it is the thing the basin people themselves do not remark on at all. They speak, and they speak well — they have words for the kinds of water (*seep*, *weep*, *run*, *pool*, *channel*, the central dark, the thin trickle at the rim in the dry weeks), and they have words for the kinds of moss (the spongy roof-moss, the pale wall-paint lichen, the soft alley moss that is good for packing wounds), and they have names for each other that are long and particular. But the speech is low and close. A conversation between two people in the basin is conducted at the volume of a conversation between two people in the same bed. The lips move. The throat barely engages. The sound goes into the ear of the listener and stops there, and the listener nods, or does not nod, and the conversation is over, and the two people go on with what they were doing.

There is shouting only in extremity, and even then it is not loud. A child who has fallen into a channel is called by name, but the name is not yelled; it is said, with weight, the way one says a thing one means. The rim people, when they come down to trade, are always faintly wrong-footed by how the basin people can make themselves heard across a lane — not by volume but by some quality of placement, as if the words are laid onto the air rather than pushed into it, and the air accepts them and carries them just where they need to go and not an inch further. The rim people, accustomed to open ground and broad sky, often feel, after a day in the basin, that they have been spoken *at* from very close up, even by people standing at a distance.

The children are quiet because the place does not invite speech, but also because the quiet is what they are given. A child's first fishing-line is tied for them by a parent — bone hook, fiber line, a small float of dry reed kept, absurdly, from the rim — and the child fishes beside the parent for years before the parent stops coming, and then the child fishes alone, and then the child has a child beside them, and the line of hands goes back further than anyone can remember. The children learn to sit very still in the water, and to feel, through the soles of their feet, the difference between a stone and a fish, and to pull up without jerking, and to kill the fish quickly with a bite behind the head, and to gut it with a stone flake, and to carry it home strung on a cord through the gills, and none of this is taught in words. The child watches, and the child does it, and the child does not speak either.

They are patient in the way the place is patient. A house is built over generations. The lower walls are added to by each generation, a course of stone at a time, raising the floor against the rising wet, and the upper walls are old, and the roof is older still, and the moss on the roof is older than anyone living. A water-channel is cut by a person in their middle years, and the channel is used for a generation, and then it is filled with rubble and planted, and a new channel is cut, and no one thinks of this as a decision; it is what the place does, and the people do what the place does. The basin sinks another fraction. The town builds up another fraction. The moss comes back over the new stone within a season, as if it had been waiting just beneath the surface.

They do not have gods in the way the rim people have gods — or if they have gods, the gods are not carved or set up in high places. Their gods, if they have them, are in the felt-call of the small gray-brown bird in the alder, in the particular cold of the central pool at the worst of the year, in the sound the water makes when it runs down the raw clay bank at the margin after a heavy rain. They do not sacrifice. They do not pray aloud. They leave, sometimes, a piece of fish on a flat stone at the edge of the basin, and the fox takes it in the night, and they do not speak of this, and the not-speaking of it is its own kind of observance.

The animals are not separate from the town. They are part of the packing. The fox crosses a lane at the edge of vision, mouth full, and no one startles; the fox knows the lanes and the houses know the fox, and there is an arrangement between them that neither side has ever broken. The swallows nest under the eaves of the higher houses and feed on the midges that hatch from the central pool, and the droppings of the swallows streak the walls below their nests in pale vertical lines, and the people do not clean the droppings, because the swallows eat the midges and the midges, in their worst weeks, are a misery, and the trade is fair. The pale fish in the channels are near-blind and slow and are caught by children and are eaten, and the bones are thrown back into the water, and the bones are gnawed by smaller things, and the smaller things are eaten by the fish, and the cycle turns in the dark. A heron comes, sometimes, from the rim — it does not know how it got down, or it does and does not care — and stands in the central pool for a long time, and the people watch it from a distance, and the heron watches them, and no one moves, and the heron departs when it is ready, and its tracks in the mud at the pool's edge fill with water within an hour and are gone.

An owl, somewhere in the alders, makes a sound at night that is felt more than heard, and the small gray-brown birds go quiet when it sounds, and the people go quiet too, not from fear but from a kind of attention, the way one goes quiet when something one respects is doing what it does.

The people are not unhappy. Outsiders from the rim sometimes think they must be, because the place is wet and cold and dim and quiet, and the people are pale and damp and do not speak loudly, and the food is plain, and the light is brief. But unhappiness requires a sense of how things could be otherwise, and the people of the basin have not, for many generations, had this sense. The damp is what is. The cold is what is. The quiet is what is. They wake in the gray and they work in the gray and they sleep in the dark, and the fat lamps in the windows bloom in the wet and make the wet shine, and the shine is enough.

They die, as all people die, and they are put into the soft ground at the margin of the basin, where the ground is highest and least wet, and the markers are small — a stone at the head, unworked, just a stone from the rim of fresh clay where the slump happened — and the stones accumulate over the generations, leaning a little, like the houses, like the trees, like the people themselves, and the moss comes over them within a year, and they sink into the pelt of green that is laid into the hollow, and they are not forgotten but they are not often visited, because the living have work, and the dead do not need visiting, and the rain falls on them as it falls on the moss and the stones and the lanes and the roofs, and this is enough.

What remains, after a day in the basin, is the sense of the packing — of all the lives wedged in together, damp and pale and quiet, human and not, the fox and the swallows and the pale fish and the children on their higher cobbles and the elderly with their moss-hair and the water moving ceaselessly down from the rim and the moss growing ceaselessly up from the stone — and the sense that none of it is going to stop, and none of it needs to, and the place is, in its slow and continuous way, complete.
