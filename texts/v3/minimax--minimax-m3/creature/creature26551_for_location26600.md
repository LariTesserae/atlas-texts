The Anrath are the place, before they are anything else.

This is the first thing a visitor learns, though it is rarely the first thing a visitor is told. The naturalist-divers from Vorr and Saltreach keep records, and the records tend to begin with measurement — depth, salinity, average canopy height, count of chambers in the *veth-kar*. They record the kelp-architecture, the snapping-shrimp colonies, the eel-chambers. They record, with care, the people. And yet in those records, when they are read back on the surface by people who have never been below the tideline, something is always missing. What is missing is what the Anrath themselves would consider the most obvious fact: that the ridge and the kelp and the town and the people are not three things, or four, but one. The Anrath are the place's slow, persistent idea of itself.

The body of an Anrath is shaped by the kelp forest, the way a fish is shaped by water or a burrow-animal is shaped by its tunnels. They are not surface-dwellers marooned in the deep; they are not the slight, large-eyed creatures that the shelf-cities of the continental edge tend to favor. They are robust, broad-shouldered, with the long-muscled density of people who have lived in motion for a hundred generations. The torso is long; the limbs are sturdy without being heavy. A surface-dweller, looking at an Anrath for the first time, often remarks on the chest — the way it rises and falls, slowly, with the regular rhythm of breath. This is partly a matter of physiology: their breathing, which is water-breathing, is tidal, and the tide of it is visible across the ribcage in a way that no city-dweller from Vorr or Saltreach can quite ignore, because it looks, distantly, like a memory of how their own ancestors used to breathe air.

The skin is the color of old kelp, or of weathered shell, or of the sand at the bottom of clear shallows — a muted olive-brown, sometimes gold along the forearms and the tops of the shoulders where the light catches. It is not mottled or striped, the way some kelp-forest fauna are. It is, however, often marked — not painted, not scarred in the sharp sense, but textured, the way wood is textured, by years of contact with the fronds. The hands especially, in an elder, are almost bark-like, the fingers thickened and calloused from generations of pruning. Children have softer skin, finer features, the unformed gentleness of things that have not yet had to work. The naturalist-divers, in their logs, sometimes describe Anrath children as "kelp-colored," which the Anrath, when they hear it translated back to them by the trade-pilots, find mildly funny, as if a child were any other color.

The eyes are the part the visitors remember longest. They are not large, in the manner of deep-ocean creatures, because the Anrath do not live in the deep. They are the size of a surface-dweller's eyes, perhaps slightly smaller, and they are dark — not black, but a deep amber-brown, the color of honey that has been held against a fire for too long. The light in the kelp forest, green-grey and diffuse, gives them a quality of being lit from within. A naturalist-diver who has spent a week on the ridge will find, on the way back up to the surface, that the eyes of city-dwellers seem flat to them, like coins, and it will take some hours for this feeling to fade.

The Anrath do not see the world the way the city-dwellers do. The city-dwellers live in stone and steel, in places where the light comes from above, where the current is something to be managed rather than something to be listened to. The Anrath live in a forest that is loud with its own life, and the forest is their primary sense-organ.

Sound is where they begin. A child, before it can read, before it can follow the woven marks that stand in for writing, can already tell you the difference between a school of sardine-analogs moving through the canopy in one direction and the same school moving in another, by sound alone. They learn the percussion of the snapping-shrimp as a kind of background weather — the way a city-dweller learns the hum of an engine or the drip of water from a roof. Beneath the shrimp-crackle, they hear the grunts of territorial fish, the long moan of current through narrow channels, the chatter of the cleaning-wrens. They hear, and read in the sound, things the visitor cannot hear at all: the warning-whine of an eel disturbed, the soft knock of a crab in the undergrowth, the particular silences that fall on the ridge when something large is passing along the flank, a kilometer or two out, and the whole forest holds itself quiet for a moment.

Taste is also central. The water has a flavor in any place, and the flavor of the Anrath ridge — slightly sweet, faintly mineral, the iodine signature of mature kelp — is a flavor the Anrath can read like a text. They can tell by taste when a current is bringing new nutrients, when a storm has churned the upper waters, when a sick chamber is fouling the air around it. The naturalist-divers, who carry instruments for this, find the Anrath's accuracy in such matters slightly embarrassing, in the way that people who build instruments are always embarrassed by a cat's sense of direction.

The Anrath live inside the kelp the way other people live inside stone or wood. The chambers are not large, by the standards of a city, but they are not small either — each is shaped to the family that inhabits it, and the family, over generations, has shaped the chamber in turn. A house in Anrath is rarely a single room. It is a sequence of them, grown together across decades, with doorways that have migrated as the stipe thickened, with floors that are also the holdfasts of the very plants whose fronds form the ceiling. The smell of a house is the smell of living tissue, faintly sweet, faintly iodine, faintly of the bodies that warm it. The houses are warm because the water is cool and the bodies inside are warm, and the warmth is held by the kelp walls in a way that stone could not replicate.
