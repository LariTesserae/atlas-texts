The accretors. The self-assemblers. The working. They do not have a single name because they are not a single thing — they are what the Forge-Seamount *does*, the way a reef is what a coral does, except the reef here is mineral and the medium is hot chemistry and the timescale is geological and the sound is the sound of a mill. They are dense. They are everywhere on the peak and in the water around it, in a dozen forms that are all variations on the same basic act: take in the vent-water, take in the trace metals, take in the light that falls in long green shafts from above, and accrete. Become more of yourself. Lay down another layer. Continue.

The smallest of them are the *stamps*. They are the ones that dart. They are about a finger's length, jointed in a dozen places, with small gripping plates at the ends of their limbs, and they move through the water in short bursts — a thrown file, a struck rasp, a brittle percussive clatter that carries further than you would expect because water carries sound the way iron carries heat. Stamps are thin-shelled. They are the youngest of the fauna, in the sense that their bodies have not yet had time to thicken; in the green light they are almost translucent, and you can see the chemistry moving through them in pale threads, the slow traffic of mineral being sorted and laid down. They eat the water. They are voracious converters, and the water immediately around any one of them is briefly spent — depleted — so they must keep moving to stay inside their own food. Their motion produces the small clatter. From a distance, a swarm of stamps sounds like gravel poured down a sheet of metal. From within, it sounds like a workshop full of very small, very busy things.

The mid-sized are the *plates*. Plates are flat and broad and they cling to the glassy faces of the peak in sheets, layered like roofing slates, adhered with a grip that is not glue and not suction but something more like consensus — thousands of small clamps engaged at once, a community decision to remain. They click when currents shift. Each click is the sound of one plate's edge finding or leaving another's, and the chorus of clicks across a face is a kind of slow irregular typing, full of false starts, the punctuation of a process that is otherwise silent. Plates do not hunt. They do not move much at all, once they have settled into a face. They sit in the current and let the current bring them the chemistry, and they grow outward, into the flow, away from the rock, in overlapping curtains that thicken across the generations. After enough generations — a few hundred years, a few thousand — the sheet of plates has become a wall. The wall is the *ledger*.

The ledger is the layered architecture that fills the cracks and the leeward sides of the spines, the porous mineral humming of accumulated bodies. The plates at the surface of the ledger are alive, in the sense that they are accreting; the plates beneath them are dead, in the sense that they have stopped, but they are not gone. They are the substrate. They are the floor on which the next generation of plates is being laid. A plate is alive for as long as it thickens. When it stops, it does not fall away. It is incorporated. The boundary between the individual and the structure is, in this sense, a matter of time, and the ledger has no particular opinion about which side of that boundary any given fragment is on. It is a single porous object, growing outward at the rate that new plates accrete onto its surface, internally stratified by the slow burial of the dead. It is, after enough centuries, the size of a house. It is, in some sense, what the place *is*.

Above the peak, in the long green shafts, the *columns* turn. Columns are not individuals either, exactly — they are wheeling formations of mid-sized fauna that the currents have organized into something between a flock and a screw. They rotate around the upper spines in long helixes, individuals spaced at intervals that the water seems to dictate, the whole assembly migrating up and down the spine through the day as the slight variations in chemistry shift with the tide of light. Each individual in a column is a thing of perhaps a forearm's length, segmented, plated, with a row of small oar-like limbs that it does not use for swimming so much as for *staying in formation* — the same grip-as-consensus, distributed through the water instead of against the rock. The columns are what produce the steady grinding hum that the seamount wears like a hum a mill wears when it is full of grain. From a distance a column sounds like a single sustained note. From within, it is a workshop again, but a different workshop — slower, more tonal, with the regular small percussion of oar-limbs finding their stroke and the continuous faint ringing from the interior of each body where the chemistry is still settling, still clicking into place. The ringing is almost tonal. It is the sound of mineral being mineral.

The largest of the fauna are the *presses*. Presses are the vast slow things anchored to the rock, the ones whose rhythm you feel in your chest cavity before you hear it. A press is a flattened dome of layered mineral, perhaps two of you across, adhered to the glass at a scale where adhesion has become geological. Presses do not move. They do not, so far as anyone has been able to determine, reproduce in the way the smaller fauna do. They *thicken*. They accrete from below and within, growing denser year by year, their interiors slowly differentiating into chambers and channels through which the chemistry is routed in patterns that no one has ever fully mapped. The pulse is the chemistry moving through those channels. It is slow — a rise and fall that takes a full minute, sometimes longer — and it is loud in a way that is not loudness of volume but of depth, the way a large bell is loud when you put your hand on it and feel the body of it move. The press is, in some sense, the oldest form the fauna take. A column, given enough time, will thicken, will slow, will settle onto a face and begin to differentiate, and what it will eventually be is a press. The press is what the rest of the working is on its way to becoming. The press is the long habit of the place made into a single object.

Their reproduction is simple and continuous. A mature individual — a stamp, a plate, a column-animal — extrudes a small plate of itself: a chip from the casing, a fragment of a joint, a single shed segment. The fragment is carried off by the current. It is mineral-rich, structured, viable. In the right water it nucleates new accretion. It grows. In a season, in a year, it is a new individual. This is how the working spreads outward into the brighter blue above the peak, where the columns thin but do not stop, and downward onto the rubble fields of the seamount's apron, where the fauna become sparser and slower and the presses give way to solitary things anchored to single tumbled blocks of glass. The fragment that settles onto bare rock founds a new body. The fragment that settles onto an already-occupied substrate joins the structure. The boundary, again, is time.

Nothing here dies the way soft things die. There is no decay, no falling-away, no return to a medium. There is only the slow thickening into the substrate. The old bodies become the floor on which the new bodies are laid. The seamount, taken across its full extent, is in this sense a single growing thing, and the fauna are its metabolism. The peak is a factory floor. The factory floor is alive. The factory has not been built by anyone, and it is not building anything in particular, and it will not stop.

It is not aggressive. It is not gentle. It is indifferent in the way a smelter is indifferent — it does what it does to whatever is fed into it, and it does not know it is doing it, and it does not pause. If you sit on a ledge of the glass, it will not notice you. If you break off a piece of a spine, some of the plates nearest the break will click, and the click will travel outward through the surrounding sheet as a small wave, and the wave will fade, and nothing else will happen. The currents will continue. The columns will continue to turn. The presses will continue to pulse, slow, slow, slow. The chord will be held. The chord will not change. The place will not notice you, and the light will keep falling in long green shafts across the humming iron-colored crowd, and the crowd will go on with its work, and the work will outlast the worker, and the seamount will grow another fraction of an inch toward the air it will never reach.
