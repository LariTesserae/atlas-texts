# The Lives of Low Cresting

They do not say they are from Low Cresting, not the people who were born here. They say *here*, or *the Cresting*, or nothing at all. The town has a way of taking the locational question and folding it back into the ground. Where else would one be?

A child born in the upper stories of a house on Bellweather Lane — third floor, the one whose window now opens onto a roof that was a road when the child's grandmother was young — will learn the layout of her town by the slant of the floors and the angle of the doors, the way a child in flatter country learns by the shape of the streets. The house is the map. The house is moving. The house will probably outlast the child. She will not be conscious of the fact that she is, in any geological sense, climbing. She will think of herself as living on a hill. She will be wrong about the kind of hill.

## The Old Families of the Foot

At the eastern end of town, where the ridge first broke through, there are still timber walls visible behind newer brick — the bones of the first shelters, when the men who came to see what the land was doing simply began building on top of the new ground because they could not believe it would stop. These earliest families have a particular kind of bearing. They are quieter than the rest, and they tend to be shorter, and they say *the rising* the way a coastal person says *the storm*: as something known, something weathered through. They keep gardens in any horizontal surface they can find, including the tops of abandoned cisterns. They are suspicious of mineralogists. They are not unkind; they simply do not see what the mineralogists are for.

The oldest of them, a woman named Essa who keeps bees on a roof that used to be a field, will tell you, if you ask, that the bees are fine. The bees, she says, know which vents to work. She does not elaborate. The bees are enormous and dark and do something unusual to the honey, which is black and faintly bitter and which Essa sells to the forge men in small tins, who eat it on bread and say it is medicinal. No one has tested it. The mineralogists have asked. Essa has declined.

## The Forge Men

There are two shifts. The day shift does the iron — the work that is, in its way, ordinary, the work that any foundry in any city does, and that gets shipped downriver to places that are content to know nothing about its origin. The night shift works the dark alloy.

The night shift is smaller. They are not chosen so much as they remain. A man arrives at the foundry at seventeen or nineteen, as forge men do, and he is assigned first to the stamping sheds, then to the rolling mills, and eventually, if he is suited, to the night work. No one has ever explained the criterion. The foremen shrug. *He took to it.* The men who have not taken to it have simply gone to other work; no one is forced to the night, and no one is turned away from it, but the night work seems to recognize its own.

They come out of the smelting hall at dawn looking like men who have been somewhere. Their clothes are scorched at the cuffs. Their eyes are reddened. They smell of ozone and something else, something that is not quite metallic and not quite organic, which clings to the hair and the wool and will not wash fully out for a day. They walk home through the mist, and the mist closes behind them, and they do not speak to one another on the way, but they walk together, which is its own kind of speech. Their wives know the smell. Their children know to be quiet on the mornings after a long pour. The foreman knows that the alloy behaves, on certain nights, in ways that the metallurgists from the city will not be told about, because the men themselves are not sure what they have seen, and the foreman is a man who understands the value of not being sure.

The objects they make are crated and loaded onto flatboats and sent downriver. What they are for, the forge men cannot say. They are sometimes rough. They are sometimes precise in ways that the maker did not intend, as if the alloy had decided, in the cooling, what shape it preferred. The crates are heavy. The boatmen do not ask.

## The Crows

The crows are not a population so much as a tenure. They have been here longer than the town, which is to say: they were here when the land pushed up, and they are here still, and they have not multiplied so much as they have *settled*, the way a family settles into a house it intends to stay in. They sit on the foundry chimneys in long black rows, and they watch the yard, and they know the rhythm of the shift changes better than any foreman. They will rise — all of them, together, as if on a single cue — fifteen seconds before the noon whistle, and circle once, and resettle. No one has ever been able to explain how.

They eat what they can. They take slag, they take bread, they take, in the early mornings, small bright things left on windowsills. They are not afraid of the people, but they keep their distance in a way that is not quite fear and not quite contempt. A child who grows up here learns the individual crows by the shape of the white patch on the breast, or the broken feather, or the missing toe, and will point them out by name — *that one's Molten, that one's Slowwing* — to an adult who already knows.

There is a particular crow, very old, that lives on the rhododendron above the smelting hall. The forge men leave it offerings. They are not sentimental about this. The crow has, on three separate occasions, given a call that sent the men out of the building shortly before a vent opened in the floor. The crow is credited, in the ledgers of the foreman's quiet mind, with seventeen lives. It has a name. No one will tell a stranger what it is.

## The Children of the Slag

The slag heaps at the town's edge are the children's country. They are technically forbidden, and the foremen technically enforce this, and the children go anyway, because the slag is a country worth being forbidden from: warm underfoot, crumbly, full of tunnels, full of strange small formations, full of crystals that are not quite crystals.

The cairns the children build are not play. They are not architecture either, not exactly, though they have rules. A cairn must have, at minimum, a base of the darkest slag, a midsection of the lighter porous stuff, and a crown of a single small object — a piece of glass, a button, a bird bone, a button-sized piece of the alloy if you are very lucky and the night-shift men like you. The cairns are not kept. The children build them and leave them. In the morning, some of them are gone. The ones that remain have, sometimes, the small crystals on them that the forge men pocket.

The children know about the crystals. They are not told about the crystals. They bring them home, in pockets and fists, and leave them on windowsills, and the mothers throw most of them out, and a few are kept, and no one speaks of this. The mineralogists, when they come, ask the children what they have found, and the children say *nothing* with the seamless faces of those who have been raised in a place that has taught them, early, what is not said.

## The Mapmakers

Twice a year, a man comes from the city with a surveying tripod and a notebook and the polite, desperate smile of a professional whose instruments are telling him things he cannot publish. He has been coming for eleven years. He brings gifts for the schoolroom. He asks the same questions he has asked before. *Where is the eastern boundary? Where is the river relative to the church? Has the road on the north side been rerouted, or has it only — has it only —* He does not finish the question. He redraws the map. He submits it. The cartographer's office in the city is, by now, a small archive of his revisions, and a senior cartographer has been assigned the file in the manner of a man assigned a haunting.

The mapmaker is given a room above the public house, and the public house serves him a dinner, and the older residents sit with him and tell him, with great patience, where the streets are, and he writes it all down, and in the morning he goes out with his tripod, and the ground has shifted, and the streets are not where they were. He begins again. He has begun again, by his count, two hundred and thirty-one times.

He does not seem unhappy. The work has a quality, for him, that other cartographic work does not. *Nowhere else*, he says to the foreman once, over a pint, *has the courtesy to be still moving under me.* The foreman does not know what to say to this. He nods. They drink.

## The Watcher of Vents

There is a man, older now, who keeps a private record of the vents. Not the foundry's record, which is technical, and not the mineralogists' record, which is theoretic. His is a record of *behavior*. Which vent opened when. Which fumarole is sulfurous in spring. Which well brings up the not-quite-water, and on what cycle, and what color the man who drank it last turned before he went home and did not come back.

He is a foundry man, retired. He has a small room above a cooper's shop, and the room is full of notebooks, and the notebooks are full of small careful drawings of the town, overlaid one upon another, dated, marked. He shows them to no one, except occasionally to a child who has been brought to him by a parent who senses, in the child, the same attention. He teaches the child to draw what is seen. He does not teach the child to interpret. The interpretation, he says, is for the town to do.

He is, in his way, the most patient man in the Cresting. He has been waiting thirty years for the ground to finish doing whatever it is doing, and he has begun, quietly, to suspect that waiting is the work, and that the finish is not the point.

## The Departed

Not everyone stays. The young man who goes downriver to the city, to the university, to a flat in a place where the ground does not move — he goes, and he is not unhappy, and he sends letters, and the letters are ordinary, and he comes back for the burial of an aunt, and he stands on the platform of the rail station and the platform is not where he left it, and he steps off and the earth is different under his shoes, and he does not weep, exactly, but something in his face rearranges, and the cousin who has come to meet him sees it and says nothing, and they walk together up the long crooked street, and the fog closes behind them, and he is home for as long as he stays.

Some of the departed do not write. Some come back unannounced, in the middle of a winter, and walk the alleys as if they are checking the town against a memory, and the town is not what they remember, and they go to the public house and they sit, and they do not say, in so many words, that they have come back because they could not make the ground behave elsewhere. The ground in the city is too still. The ground in the city does not answer.

## The Quiet That Is the Place

In the long hours of winter, when the steam is thick and the lamps are low and the foundry has gone to its smallest watch, the town is a held thing. The hiss is everywhere. The cold is in the lungs. The windows weep. Somewhere underfoot, a long low note rises and falls over the course of an hour, and is gone.

A woman sits in a kitchen on the second floor of a house that has been her family's for three generations, mending a coat. The floor of the kitchen is no longer level. The table has a folded cloth under one leg. A child sleeps in the next room, on a mattress that has been re-stuffed twice because the springs have settled. The kettle on the stove is not heated by the stove; it sits on a small copper coil that comes up through the floor, and the coil is warm, and the kettle is always warm, and the woman has never in her life heated a kettle any other way. She does not think of it as strange. She thinks of it as her kitchen.

Outside, the fog is in. The crows are roosting. A dog with no collar moves through an alley with the unhurried purpose of a thing that belongs here. A vent somewhere in the older part of town opens, and a thin line of warm air comes up through the cobbles, and the moss on the nearest wall takes a breath.

The woman does not hear any of this. She is mending. The needle goes in. The needle comes out. The fog moves at the window like something that has always been there. Somewhere far down, the earth is still pressing itself into shape, and the town is rising with it, and the woman is rising with it, and the coat will be mended, and tomorrow she will go down the stairs that used to be the front walk, and the foundry will be running, and the crows will rise fifteen seconds before the whistle, and the ground will have moved another inch, and she will say *ah*, and she will go to work, and the town will be a town, still, packed and quiet and strange, in the act of becoming itself.
