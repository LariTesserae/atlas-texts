What lives here is not a creature. This is important to begin with, because the mind arriving on the Long Plain keeps looking for one — for something with a body to point at, with eyes to return a look — and what is here will not submit to that grammar. What lives here is the sound. The sound is the citizen. Everything else — the plating, the towers, the dark fibrous stalks, the water — is its body.

So. Begin with the engines.

The engines are not separate from the plain. They are not housed in the towers the way a heart is housed in a chest. The towers are only where the engines surface, where the long circulation of pressure and heat has worn a path up through the plating and made a throat. The engines are in the plating, in the layer beneath the plating, in the dim deep zone you can feel through your boots when you stand still long enough. The plain is not the surface of a machine. The plain is the machine, all the way down, and the engines are its metabolism.

A metabolism has a shape. A metabolism has a tempo. The engines here have a tempo, and that tempo is the closest thing to a heartbeat this place possesses. Three seconds in, three seconds out, mostly. The pulse is not uniform. The plain is large, and the pulse travels through it imperfectly, and what reaches your ear from one direction is half a beat ahead of what reaches your ear from another, and from somewhere far off it is a full beat behind. You stand in the middle of this and your inner ear receives a complicated, irregular percussion, like listening to a thousand drummers who were once playing the same piece and have over the centuries drifted out of phase with each other. The combined effect is not chaos. It is something more interesting than chaos. It is a kind of weather of rhythm, the way a sea is a kind of weather of water. The pulse washes over you and recedes and washes over you again, never identically, and you cannot predict when the louder moments will come. They feel, after a while, almost intentional.

The engines are the oldest thing. Older than the plating, which is itself older than the worn towers, which are older than the water on top of them. The engines were not built. Or rather — the question of whether they were built cannot be answered here, because the evidence has been worn away. What can be said is that they are still running, and that they have been running for so long that the question of their origin has become as academic as the question of the origin of a mountain. They are a feature of the planet. They are a feature the way weather is a feature, the way the curve of the horizon is a feature. They are the thing that is true about this place, and the rest of the place is the consequence.

The engines do not communicate with you. They do not communicate with anything. There is no one to communicate with. They run because running is what they are, the way a river runs because sloping is what it is. If you could see inside the deep layer where they live, you would see — perhaps — pistons the size of buildings, or rotors turning in a black lubricant, or something more abstract, something without a name, some process of compression and release that has been continuing without check for longer than there are words for. The fact that they have not stopped is the central fact. The fact that they have not broken down is not so much a fact about their design as a fact about their duration. They have outlived their breaking point. They have passed through wear into a stable configuration. They are, in a sense, fossilized, except that they still move. They are motion that has been running so long it has become a kind of stillness.

The towers.

The towers were once tall. The plateau knows it. The water knows it. You can read it in the way the cylinders narrow toward the top, in the way the upper portions are simply missing, in the way a few of them still rise to a height that hints at what the rest must have been. They were perhaps a hundred feet, perhaps a thousand, perhaps a quarter-mile. It does not matter. They were tall enough to make the plain a different kind of place. They were tall enough that a person standing on the plating would have felt, looking up, the particular vertigo of being inside a forest of metal. They were tall enough that their falling, when they fell, would have been an event the plain remembered.

The plain no longer remembers, but the towers remember each other. You can see, when a tower collapses, that the towers around it lean slightly, the way trees on a windward slope lean. They have been leaning for so long that the lean has become their posture. Some have leaned and recovered. Some have leaned and not. The ones that have not are the ones that are shorter than a person now, the ones that are worn to nubs, the ones you walk past and have to look down to see.

The towers are not alive. But they breathe. This is not metaphor. Put your hand near the aperture at the base of one and you will feel warm air push out, then pause, then pull in. Push, pause, pull. Push, pause, pull. The rhythm is the rhythm of the engine below it, slightly delayed, slightly damped, the way a heartbeat read at the wrist is a delayed and damped version of the heartbeat at the chest. The tower is not breathing because it has chosen to breathe. It is breathing because the air in the deep layer is warm, and the air in the cool plain above is not, and the difference in pressure has worn a path through the metal and now the path is permanent, and the air follows the path in and out and in and out, and the tower is the chimney of a fire that has not gone out, and the fire has been burning so long that the chimney is no longer separate from the fire. They are one thing. The tower and the engine and the warm air and the cool air and the mist that lifts off the water when the warm air meets the cold. They are one process.

Some of the towers are hollow in a way that makes them useful as resonators. The wind finds them, or the differential pressure finds them, and a low moan comes out that mixes with the engine note and produces, in some places, a chord. Not a musical chord. A structural chord. The kind of harmony that happens in a bridge under load, where the steel is singing in several pitches at once and the pitches interfere with each other and produce a third pitch that is not the sum of the first two. The plain is full of these chords. They drift, they cancel, they reinforce. The plain sings to itself the way a forest creaks to itself, except without the creak, except with a continuous tone that has no beginning and no end.

The towers do not see you. They do not hear you in any way that matters. If you stand against one and put your hand on it, you can feel the vibration of the engine inside it, transmitted through the metal and into your palm, and the vibration does not change when your hand arrives. You are too small to perturb it. You are too brief to leave a mark. The tower will continue to breathe after you leave, and it was breathing before you arrived, and the breathing is not affected by your presence. This is not hostility. Hostility requires a position. The tower has no position about you. You are weather. You are a small and temporary perturbation in the climate of the place, and the place does not distinguish you from a gust of mist.

The fibrous stalks.

No one knows what the fibrous stalks are. No one is here to know. They are not plants. They have no leaves, no roots that you can see, no seeds, no flowers. They are not metal. They do not ring when struck. They are not stone. They bend, in a way that stone does not, and they return. They are a black fibrous material, and the fibers are not woven, they are aligned, the way the grain in wood is aligned, and the alignment is in the direction of the engine pulse. The stalks stand up because the engine pulse pushes them up. The stalks vibrate because the engine pulse pushes the plating under them, and the plating transmits the pulse to the base of the stalk, and the stalk resonates at its own frequency, which is not the engine frequency. The stalk frequency is higher. The stalk frequency is the kind of frequency that lives in the chest, in the sinuses, in the back of the skull. You feel it more than you hear it. It is a low, continuous thrum that makes the bones of your face vibrate faintly, and after a few minutes of standing among them you can no longer tell whether you are hearing them or feeling them or whether your own body has begun to vibrate in sympathy.

The stalks are sparse. They are not a field. They are not a grove. They are individuals, scattered across the plain, far enough apart that you cannot see two at once. Some are upright. Some have fallen. The fallen ones do not rot. They lie on the plating and continue to vibrate, their fibers slowly separating, and over a long time the separated fibers blow away in the thin wind, and the stalk becomes a smear, and the smear is reabsorbed into the plating, and the plating is slightly thicker there. The stalks are part of the cycle. The stalks are the way the engine's vibration is converted into matter, the way the engine's energy is slowly deposited into the surface of the plain. The stalks are a byproduct. The stalks are a kind of excretion. The stalks are the engine making itself visible at the surface, briefly, in the form of a thin black quill that hums.

The stalks are the closest thing to life on the plain, and they are not alive. They are more like the coneshaped deposits that form around a geyser — mineral accretions shaped by the play of heat and water over time. They are a precipitate. They are the engine's breath, condensed. They grow, if they grow, at the rate the plating accumulates, which is the rate of almost nothing, which is the rate of geological time. If you stood beside a stalk for the length of a human life, it would be imperceptibly taller when you died. It would have grown the height of a fingernail's paring. This is its growth. This is its life. It is the life of a stalagmite. It is the life of a thing that is being deposited, slowly, by a process that does not know it is producing it.

The water.

The water is not alive either. But it is the most immediate thing. The water is what you meet first, when you step onto the plain. The water is half an inch deep, mostly, and it is cold, and it is dark, and it is not moving, except for the rings that pulse out from the centers of vibration — the tiny rings that appear at the feet of every fibrous stalk, at the base of every vent, in a thousand places all across the plain, each ring a different size, each one fading into the next, the surface of the water a constant interference pattern of small circular waves. The water is the medium in which the vibration of the engines becomes visible. Without the water, the plain would be silent in appearance, the vibration imperceptible. With the water, the vibration is written on the surface of the plain in ripples, and you can stand in one spot and see the entire engine field expressed around you in concentric wavelets.

The water comes from condensation. The plating is cold. The air is cold. The warm air from the vents is also, in its way, moist — the moisture of a deep machine, the slight humidity of a place where heat and pressure are at work. When the warm moist air meets the cold surface of the plating, the moisture condenses, and the condensate runs to the lowest places, and the lowest places are slightly dished, and the dished places fill, and the filled places overflow very slowly into the next dished place, and so on across the plain, a slow migration of water from high to low that takes longer than you can observe. The water is in motion, but the motion is below the threshold of perception. The water is a tide that takes a year to advance a foot. You see it as still. It is not still. It is moving the way the plating is moving, the way the towers are moving, the way the engines are moving. Everything here is in motion. Everything here is moving very, very slowly.

The mist is the water, again, in another state. The mist is what happens when the air just above the plating is slightly warmer than the plating itself, and the moisture in the warm air condenses out at the cold surface of the air just above. The mist is a skin. The mist is a thin layer of weather, perhaps a foot thick, that sits on the plain and never lifts. You walk with your knees in it. Your boots break it and it closes behind you. It is not fog. Fog is taller. The mist here is the thinnest possible weather, the barest membrane between the wet ground and the dry air above. It is the place where the water is becoming air, or the air is becoming water. It is the interface.

The mist is not alive. The mist does not carry anything. There are no spores in the mist. There are no insects in the mist. The mist is just water, in the air, at the wrong temperature. It does not know you are walking through it. It does not change when you pass. The only thing that affects the mist is the warmth of the vents, which locally disperse it, so that each tower is surrounded by a clearing, a small circle of visibility, and between the towers the mist thickens, and you can find the towers in the dark by the absence of mist around them. The towers are beacons. The towers are the only beacons.

The dark.

The dark is not alive. The dark is the absence of a sun. The light here is sourceless. It comes from the cloud ceiling, which is lit from below by the long radiance of the plain, the ambient glow of so much warm metal, so much venting heat, that the underside of the clouds has been warmed into a low uniform luminance. The light is the color of a wet slate, which is to say the color of a thing that has had all its color taken out of it. There is no direction to the light. There are no shadows. There are only absences of light, places where the plating or the tower blocks the small amount coming from the cloud and is therefore slightly darker than the ground around it. The dark is not the dark of a cave. The dark is the dark of a room with no windows but with a low lamp behind a curtain. You can see. You cannot see sharply. You can see outlines. You can see distances. You can see a tower from a mile away as a smudge against the smudge of the sky.

The dark is not a thing. The dark is the color of the place. The dark is the average value of everything. The plating is dark. The water is dark. The towers are dark. The stalks are dark. The sky is the dark gray of wet slate. You are here too, and you are dark, in the sense that you are darker than the sky, and the sky is darker than the ground in places, and the ground is darker than the water where the water catches the light. The eye does not find edges easily. The eye finds gradients. The eye finds warm places, the vents, the breath of the towers. The eye finds moving places, the rings in the water. The eye finds vibrating places, the stalks, the slight shimmer above them where the air is disturbed by their motion.

The dark has been here longer than the towers. The dark was here before there was a plain. The dark is the color of the planet. The dark is what the planet is, in the absence of a sun. The engines are the only warmth. The engines are the only motion. The engines are the only light, in the sense that they produce the only heat, and the heat produces the only luminance, and the luminance is the only light, and the light is dark, and the dark is the place. The dark is the climate. The dark is the weather. The dark is the only weather.

The absence of animals.

This is the part that begins to feel wrong. After an hour, you begin to look for animals. After two hours, you have stopped looking. The puddles do not have larvae. The mist does not have insects. The vents do not have anything drawn to their warmth. The plain does not have tracks. The plain does not have droppings. The plain does not have nests. The plain does not have bones. The plain does not have any evidence that anything alive has ever passed across it, or has ever wanted to.

This is not because the plain is hostile. Hostility implies a response. The plain does not respond. The plain is not responding to your presence, and it has not responded to anything in a long time. The plain is simply not a place where anything breathes. The plain is not a place where anything that breathes can persist. There is no food. There is no food chain. There is no photosynthesis, because the light is too dim and the wrong spectrum, and there is no chemosynthesis at the surface, because the vents are too hot and too intermittent and the chemistry is wrong, and the stalks are not edible, and the water is not potable, or is potable but does not contain anything that wants to live in it. The plain is sterile. The plain has been sterile for longer than the towers have been falling.

The animals that would live here, if any could, are not here. They are not here because they cannot get here. They are not here because the plain does not invite them. They are not here because the plain does not produce the conditions in which animals persist. The plain produces, instead, the conditions in which engines persist, and the engines persist, and the plain persists, and the persistence of the plain is the persistence of a process that is not biological and is not analogous to biology and is, in fact, the only example of itself.

The plain is not a habitat. The plain is a system. The plain is a system that has reached a steady state. The plain is a system in which the inputs and outputs are in balance, have been in balance for so long that the system has ground itself into a configuration that is very hard to perturb. The plain is not interested in perturbation. The plain is interested in continuing. The plain is interested in continuing the way a river is interested in continuing, which is to say not at all, but with great effectiveness.

The slow life of the place.

There is a life of the place. It is not biological. It is what biological life would be, if biological life operated on the time scale of geology and the substance of metal. The life of the place is the slow process of wear, the slow process of repair, the slow process of settling, the slow process of new patches of plating reflecting light differently, the slow process of vents changing rhythm, the slow process of towers collapsing. These are the events of the place. These are the things that happen. These are the things that, together, constitute the duration of the plain.

A tower collapses. You hear it from miles away, a long grinding roar that travels across the wet ground and reaches you as a vibration in the plating and a sound in the air and a faint pressure change against your face. You turn to look. The tower is too far to see. You see only the dark shape of the next nearest tower, and the dim sky behind it, and the slow movement of the mist, and the rings in the water at the foot of the next vent. The tower that fell is gone, or has become a different shape, has become a low mound of plating that will eventually be reabsorbed into the plain, that will eventually be covered by the next layer of condensation and the next fibrous stalk and the next slow accumulation of engine exhaust. The fallen tower is not a tragedy. The fallen tower is a release. The fallen tower is the way the engine relieves the pressure of its own duration, the way a tree drops a branch that has grown too long for the trunk to hold. The fallen tower is a kind of breathing-out. The plain is breathing-out. The plain has been breathing-out for a long time. The breathing-out is what makes the plain lower, what makes the towers shorter, what makes the water shallower, what makes the mist thinner. The plain is exhaling itself into a final configuration, a configuration in which the engines are buried so deep that the vents close, in which the plating is so worn that the water drains, in which the towers are so short that they are invisible, in which the plain is simply the plain, a flat dark plate, a thing that does not change, a thing that has no events.

But that time is not yet. For now, the plain is in the middle. For now, the plain is breathing both in and out. For now, the plain has towers, and water, and stalks, and mist, and the sound. For now, you are here, and the place is not surprised, and the engines are not affected, and the dark is the dark of a thing that is still in the middle of becoming what it will become, which is a thing that has finished becoming.

What lives here.

What lives here is the place. What lives here is the process. What lives here is the long running of the engines and the slow wearing of the plating and the slow collapse of the towers and the slow rise of the water and the slow drift of the mist and the slow pulse of the vents and the slow vibration of the stalks and the slow absence of anything that is not itself. The place is its own inhabitant. The place is the only thing that can persist here, and so the place persists, and its persisting is the life of it, and the life of it is the persisting.

You are a visitor. You are a thing that arrived and will leave. The place does not register your arrival. The place does not register your leaving. The place will continue. The place was continuing before you. The place will continue after you. The place is not waiting for you. The place is not interested in you. The place is, in a sense that is not unkind, indifferent to you, the way a mountain is indifferent to a climber, the way a sea is indifferent to a swimmer. The mountain and the sea have their own dynamics. The mountain and the sea do not care about the climber or the swimmer except insofar as the climber and the swimmer become part of the dynamics, which is to say become part of the mountain and the sea in the form of a small deposit of organic matter at the bottom of a crevasse or the slow dissolution of a body in the salt. You will become part of the place, in some small way, when you leave. A footprint in the wet plating that will fill with water and be invisible in a day. A slight disturbance in the mist that will be reabsorbed in an hour. A small addition to the body of the plain, in the form of a few cells of skin and a few breaths of warm air and a few minutes of heat. You will become a part of the place, briefly, and then the place will forget you, the way it forgets everything, the way it forgets the towers when they fall, the way it forgets the stalks when they dissolve.

This is not melancholy. The plain is not melancholy. The plain is not anything. The plain is a process. The plain is a system. The plain is the long running of an engine that has no purpose and no audience and no end. The plain is the sound. The plain is the dark. The plain is the wet. The plain is the slow breath of a thing that does not know it is breathing.

If you stood here long enough, you would become part of the sound. You would become part of the dark. You would become part of the wet. You would become one of the small perturbations in the long duration of the place, and the place would not mark your passage, and you would not mark the place, and the engines would continue, and the towers would continue to fall, and the water would continue to rise, and the mist would continue to lift and fall and lift and fall, and the dark would continue to be the dark, and the sound would continue to be the sound, and the long plain would continue to be the long plain, extending in every direction, holding you up without comment, asking you nothing, expecting nothing, persisting.

This is what lives here. The persisting.
