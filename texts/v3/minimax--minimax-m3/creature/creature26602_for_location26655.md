# What Lives in the Cirque

They are called, in the long language of the people who once lived in the valleys below, the *kra-kra*, which is not really a name but an imitation of the sound they make when many of them are startled at once — a rolling, brittle clatter that is more like a handful of pebbles being dropped down a wooden stair than anything that could come from a throat. The name stuck, and the people who use it are not sure if they are applying it to one kind of creature or to the whole population, because the population has never, in living memory, been sorted.

## The Body

A mature *kra-kra* is roughly the size of a small crow but built differently. The beak is the first thing you notice if you are close enough to notice anything — long, slightly down-curved, laterally compressed, the upper mandible a little longer than the lower, the whole instrument darker than the feathers and glossier. The beak is the working end of the animal. It is what the animal is, in the way a beak that long makes the rest of the animal a delivery system for the beak.

The wings are present but reduced. The flight feathers are stiff and slightly asymmetric, and they can support a long, downward glide from a high ledge to a lower one, and they can support a slow upward glide if the wind is right, but the animal cannot sustain flapping flight for more than a few seconds. It launches, it sets its wings, and it descends, mostly. When it wants to go up, it climbs. It climbs the way a small climber climbs — by hopping from ledge to ledge, by pressing its body into the rock, by using the beak as a third foot when the rock allows it. The wings are used in courtship, and in the long territorial display, and in the brief, awkward scramble of a startled takeoff, but they are not the animal's main way of moving. The animal's main way of moving is on its feet, on the rock, in the dark.

The feet are strong. Four toes, three forward and one back, each tipped with a claw that is black and slightly hooked and that bites into the basalt with a small, audible *tick* when the foot comes down. The *tick* is part of the sound of the cirque, when you learn to separate the sounds. The *tick* is everywhere, layered under the calls.

The plumage is the plumage of the place. There is a base of dull brown-grey, the exact brown-grey of the rock when the rock is wet. The breast is paler, off-white, often stained with the chalk of droppings. The back, in older birds, develops a faint rusty sheen, the same rust as the lichen. The head is darker than the body, almost black, and in certain lights the feathers of the crown show a thin green iridescence that is the only decorative feature of the animal and that, because of the light, is almost never seen. From a distance, on the rock, the animal is invisible. Up close, it is a small, neat, unremarkable bird, and the strangeness of it is not in its body but in what it does with its body.

The eye is dark. The eye is set forward, which is unusual for a non-predatory bird, and gives the animal a slight look of attention that does not match the smallness of its brain. The eye does not blink often. The eye is in motion constantly — small, quick movements, the head turning, the animal scanning its ledge and the ledges around it and the floor below and the rim above and the air between. The eye is the part of the animal that is most awake.

The voice is high and clear. The basic call is two notes, the second lower than the first, repeated, repeated, repeated, with a rest of a second or two between each pair, and it is this call, multiplied by ten thousand throats on ten thousand ledges, that produces the rolling, musical piping that fills the cirque from the floor to the rim. There are other calls — a single, sharp note that means something; a long, descending trill that means something else; a low, almost subsonic sound that the birds make with the body and not the throat, a vibration in the chest, that carries further than the calls and that you feel in the rock before you hear it. The vocabulary is not known. It is not even known that the calls constitute a vocabulary. It is only known that the calls are varied and patterned and that the patterns change with the time of day and with events.

## The Life

A *kra-kra* is born in a crack. The egg is laid in a small depression at the back of a deep fissure, where the rock is warmest and where a chick, once hatched, will be invisible to anything that cannot fit its head into the crack. There is no nest in the soft sense — no grass, no down, no woven thing. There is the rock. The egg is laid on the rock, and the chick hatches on the rock, and the chick sits on the rock for the first weeks of its life, fed by a parent that brings food in the beak and transfers it directly, in small, deliberate movements.

The chick is, when it hatches, a small grey thing with a large dark eye and a beak that is already, at hatching, longer in proportion than the beak of an adult. The beak grows faster than the body. By the time the chick is ready to leave the crack, the beak is almost full size, and the rest of the bird has to catch up. This is the meaning of the long juvenile period. The bird is mostly beak for a long time, and then the rest of the bird grows to match.

The juvenile comes out of the crack. It hops onto the ledge. It is, at this point, a smaller, duller version of an adult, and it cannot yet glide, and it cannot yet make the full call, and it cannot yet work the shards. It can walk, and it can make a thin, high peeping sound, and it can eat what the parents bring, and it can begin to learn the ledges.

The juvenile learns the ledges by being on them. It follows the parents. It is brought to the floor in the first weeks out of the crack, and the floor is a chaos of other juveniles, all the year's young, swarming in a slow tide that washes across the flat stones, and in this swarm the juvenile learns the geography of the cirque — where the floor is safe, where the cracks are deep, where the walls are climbable, where the air rises and where it falls. The swarm is the school. The swarm is also the social space in which the juvenile will spend most of its life. The juveniles are not ejected from the floor; the floor is where the young are, for a long time. The transition to the higher ledges is gradual and is made individual by individual, over a year or more.

The adult *kra-kra* is a ledge-bird. It occupies a section of wall — usually a vertical stretch of three to ten meters, with several ledges in it, and with the ledges of other adults above and below. The territory is small. The territory is the ledge, and a few meters of air around the ledge, and the cracks in the rock adjacent to the ledge. The territory is defended by the call and by the posture and, rarely, by the beak. The beak is a serious weapon between two birds of the same size, and the fights are short and resolve into a chase.

A bird may hold a territory for many years. A bird may lose its territory to a younger bird, and the younger bird will take the territory and the ledges and the cracks and the work in progress, and will continue the work, and the loss of the original holder is not visible in the work. The work continues. The work is the territory in some way that the bird seems to understand.

The pairing is long. *Kra-kra* form pairs that persist across years, and the pairs hold the territory together, and the two birds on a territory are usually of slightly different sizes, and one is slightly dominant, and the pair raises a chick every other year, and the chick is raised in a crack, and the cycle continues. The pair-bonds are reinforced by the duet — a call that the two birds make together, the call of one followed immediately by the call of the other, so close together that they sound like one bird calling in two voices. The duets are part of the sound of the cirque. The duets are the small clearings in the general piping, and the duets are constant.

The death is in the rock. A *kra-kra* that is failing climbs higher, not lower. It climbs to the upper ledges, to the colder places, to the cracks where the air is thin and the rock is sharp, and it sits, and it stops calling, and it dies, and the body is taken by the wind or by the other birds or by the slow work of the lichen, and within a season the body is part of the rock. The dead are not mourned. The dead are absorbed into the place, and the place continues. A bird that dies on a territory is replaced, in the territory, by a bird that was waiting, and the work is taken up by the replacement, and the territory does not know the difference.

The lifespan is thirty years. The lifespan is a guess. The lifespan is what the arrangements suggest, because the arrangements accumulate at a rate, and a bird that tends an arrangement for thirty years will leave a particular kind of mark, and the marks can be read, roughly, in decades. The lifespan is a guess that the cirque supports.

## The Shards

This is the strangeness. This is the part of the *kra-kra* that the people in the valleys do not have a word for, and so they call it the *work*, and the word is wrong but it is the word that is used.

A *kra-kra* breaks obsidian. It does this by taking a piece of the rock — a flake, a chip, a small piece of the seam that runs through the basalt — in its beak and applying pressure until the piece comes away. The break is clean. The break is conchoidal. The break is the same break that a human makes when knapping a flint, and the shard that comes away is the same shape — a curved sliver, sharp on the edges, glossy on the inside, dull on the outside. The bird knows where to bite. The bird has always known where to bite. The place to bite is in the rock where the grain is right, and the bird selects the place by eye and by some long inheritance, and the shard comes away in one motion.

The bird carries the shard in its beak. It carries it up. It carries it to a ledge, and on the ledge is an arrangement, and the bird sets the shard down at the edge of the arrangement, and the arrangement accepts the shard, and the bird steps back, and the bird looks at the arrangement, and the bird goes.

The arrangement is on the ledge. The arrangement is shards, set on the rock, in a pattern. The patterns are not random. The patterns are, in the smaller arrangements, simple — a line of three, an arc of seven, a small ring. The patterns are, in the larger arrangements, complex — spirals that tighten toward a center and that have, in the center, a single shard placed on its edge, or a small cluster of shards placed in a way that the eye reads as a mark and not as a pile. The patterns accumulate. The patterns have no function that a human can see. The patterns are not nests. The patterns are not food caches. The patterns are not tools. The patterns are not signals to other species, because there are no other species that would read them, and the patterns are not signals to other birds, because the other birds are already on the ledges, and do not need the patterns to find the ledges. The patterns are for the birds. The patterns are for the arrangement itself. The patterns are, in some way that the word *work* does not capture, the reason the bird is on the ledge at all.

The birds tend the patterns. A bird comes to a pattern and looks at it, and if a shard has fallen or has been displaced, the bird puts it back. A bird comes to a pattern and adds a shard, in a place where the pattern requires a shard. A bird comes to a pattern and, very rarely, takes a shard away, and the shard is dropped over the rim or carried to a different pattern on a different ledge, and the loss is felt, and the loss is repaired. The patterns are not finished. The patterns are in the process of becoming, and the process is slow, and the process is the long work of the bird on the ledge.

The larger patterns are tended by more than one bird. A spiral on a low ledge where many territories meet is tended by the birds of all the adjacent territories, and the tending is a kind of shared activity, and the birds work on it in shifts, and the shifts are not assigned but are arrived at by some pattern that the calls make possible. The larger patterns are old. The larger patterns are very old. The very oldest patterns in the cirque are on the highest ledges, where the rock is most stable, and the patterns are built into the surface in a way that suggests centuries of addition, and the shards in the deepest layers are weathered and the shards in the upper layers are fresh, and the difference between the two is the age of the pattern.

The patterns are the strangeness of the cirque. The patterns are the thing the cirque is for, if the cirque is for anything. The patterns are what the *kra-kra* is for, if the *kra-kra* is for anything. The patterns are a kind of making that the word *making* does not cover, because the making has no user, and no end, and no product that is not itself the making. The patterns are the *kra-kra* thinking in rock. The patterns are the *kra-kra* being what the *kra-kra* is. The patterns continue.

## The Whole

The cirque is full. The cirque is loud. The cirque is dark, and sharp, and cold, and the air is thin, and the rock is black, and the light is grey or violet or, on the best days, the pale lemon of sun through ice. The cirque is a place that is itself, and the *kra-kra* is the place being itself, and the two are not separable, and the *kra-kra* is not separable from its shard, and the shard is not separable from the ledge, and the ledge is not separable from the wall, and the wall is not separable from the cirque.

The *kra-kra* lives in the cirque. The *kra-kra* is the cirque, in the part of the cirque that moves and makes sound and breaks the obsidian and sets the shards. The cirque without the *kra-kra* would be a sharp, dark, cold bowl with a stream in it. The cirque with the *kra-kra* is a sharp, dark, cold bowl with a stream in it and a population of ten thousand birds tending arrangements of obsidian shards in patterns that the birds understand and that no one else does. The difference between the two cirques is the *kra-kra*. The difference is the *work*. The difference is the long, slow, patient, pointless, beautiful, incomprehensible act of breaking a piece of rock and setting it down next to other pieces of rock in a place where the rock is and where the bird is and where the cirque is.

The cirque continues.
