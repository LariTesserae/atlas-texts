Begin with the hands. You will not see the workers' faces first, if you come up the switchback and meet one on the narrow paths the mat has worn for itself. You will see their hands, because the workers spend most of their waking hours with their hands against the skin of the growths, and the growths have shaped the hands in return. There is a callus — a particular one — on the outer edge of the palm, just below the little finger, where the rough outer bark of the trunks meets the softer skin in a kind of seam. Every worker has it. It is hard and ridged and a little lighter than the rest of the palm, and if you run your own thumb across it — if a worker lets you, which they will, after a pause, after the dark drink has been poured into a small cup that looks like it was once the cup-joint of a branch and may still be — you will feel the small parallel lines, like growth-rings, like the marks that are cut into the trunks themselves each generation to record the slow passage of the years. The callus is not a wound. It is something the body has made in negotiation with another body. It is the record of long and careful handling.

The workers are spare people. Brown-faced, weathered, with the kind of slenderness that comes from climbing ladders all day inside trunks that are not quite wide enough for two. Their hair, if you see it, is dark and cut short — cut, they will tell you if you ask, by another worker, with a blade kept sharp on the inner face of a particular kind of bark, because up here metal shears tend to rust overnight no matter how they are stored. Their clothes are made of the pressed fibrous cakes that come down from the higher factories, layered, patched, dark with damp. They have a smell, faint, that is hard to place — something between wet wood and machine-oil and something else, something sweet and slightly sour that is the smell of the mat itself, the smell of the place, the smell you came to recognize on the road up and that you will carry in your own clothes for as long as you stay. They speak quietly. They have a habit, when listening, of tilting their heads a little to one side and closing their eyes halfway, as if they are hearing something you are not. They may be. The place is loud in ways you cannot yet hear.

You can walk for an hour along the paths and not see another person. The workers live in clusters — small ones, five or six together, sometimes as many as a dozen — inside the larger trunks, in the chambers the growths have made and the workers have fitted out. A trunk that is home to a cluster will have, at various heights, openings cut into its side where the internal ladders emerge onto small platforms of planking that has been pegged into the wood. Inside, the chambers are warm and damp and smell of the dark fermented drink and of the slow exhalations of the trunk itself, which is to say of growth, which is to say of something working. The workers move up and down all day. Some of them, the ones who have been in a particular trunk since childhood, know the inside of that trunk the way you know your own house in the dark: where the chambers narrow, where the internal pipes run, where a particular kind of valve has been growing into the wood for so long that no one is sure any longer whether it is a valve or a knot. They tap the walls as they climb. They listen. They know what the trunk is doing by the sound it makes when they knock on it, the way a doctor knows a chest by the sound it makes when struck.

They are not unkind to newcomers. They will offer you the dark drink — poured from a ceramic jug that is kept warm in a pocket of the trunk where the internal temperature runs a little higher than the surrounding air — and they will sit with you while you drink it, and they will answer the questions you ask, and they will not answer the questions you don't quite ask. They have a phrase. *The place is working well.* It is the highest compliment. It is what they say when you ask about the weather, when you ask about a particular growth that has been doing poorly, when you ask about a worker who has been absorbed into the mat — and you will ask, eventually, because you will see it happen, or you will see the place where it has happened, the slight rise in the mat that is not quite like the rest of the mat, the rise that breathes, faintly, when the rest of the place is quiet. *Working well*, they will say, and they will not explain what they mean, and after a while you will understand that what they mean is not a description but an evaluation: the place is doing what it does, and what it does is enough, and they are part of it, and you, if you stay, will become part of it, and the becoming is not optional but it is not unwelcome either.

Now look up. Look at what they live among.

The growths are the second inhabitants, and they are the first, and they are the most. There are at least seven major forms visible from any high vantage, and the workers have names for them in the local tongue that are mostly untranslatable and partly onomatopoeic — names that imitate the sound the growth makes when you put your ear to it in the wind, or the sound the workers make when they are talking to it, which they do, more often than they will admit. Some are tall and columnar, rising like towers from the mat, their skins dark and ridged and rough to the hand, their internal chambers hollowed by a process no one understands into the shapes that the pipes require. Some are shorter and broader, with crowns that interlock overhead to make a kind of ceiling thirty feet up through which the light comes in pale shafts and the fog settles in long strips. Some are not trunks at all but something else — great ropey cables that run from trunk to trunk and are themselves alive, and that carry, in their hollow interiors, the dark fluids and the pale gases and the condensate-water on its long slow journey down. The mat below is itself a growth, or ten thousand growths, a continuous felt of moss and lichen and low rosette plants and the smaller fungal bodies, packed so densely that no soil is visible anywhere, and the mat is warm to the touch, and the mat is breathing, and the mat is the place in the same way that the sea is the sea.

The growths are slow. The largest have been growing, according to the marks the workers cut into their skins each generation, for a thousand years. Some are older. The workers call the oldest ones *the old ones*, and they speak of them in a tone that is not quite reverence and not quite familiarity and not quite anything you have a word for. The old ones produce, in small quantities and only in the late afternoons when the fog comes in, a clear oil that the workers harvest in copper buckets and that smells, faintly, of something green and metallic that you came to recognize on the road up. The oil is sent down the long switchback on the backs of the few beasts that survive at this altitude, in metal drums with sealed lids. The workers do not drink the oil. They do not speak of what it is for. They speak of the old ones only with care.

The growths are not passive. They have incorporated the factories — the smoke-stacks, the boilers, the pipes, the wheels, the pistons — so completely that no one can say, of any given piece of machinery on the slopes, whether it was once metal and is now something else, or whether it was always something else and only looked like metal. Pipes run through the trunks, or alongside them, or have been replaced by the trunks themselves. Wheels turn in pods that look like enormous seed-cases, and the pods open, seasonally, in a way that has nothing to do with the wheels and everything to do with the growths. Pistons rise and fall in chambers lined with something that might be wood and might be muscle and might be both, and the rise and fall is regular and is not driven only by steam. The growths prefer metal — this is what the workers say, and what they mean is that the growths grow toward metal, around metal, into metal, as if the metal were a kind of light. Tendrils, slow and deliberate, wrap around the valves and the pressure-gauges and the safety-reliefs of the boilers. The tendrils know, somehow, what to do. The workers tend the boilers too. The distinction has been lost for so long that the question no longer arises.

The fauna is rare. There are birds — small, dark, fast things, no bigger than your fist, that nest in the highest crowns of the columnar growths and come down only in the worst weather, when the fog is so thick that you cannot see your own hand in front of your face and the drip from above becomes a steady rain. They are loud, suddenly, when they come down, a chittering and a sharp whistle that is almost a word. The workers know the sounds and do not startle at them. There are insects in the mat, in the felt below, that the workers don't bother with — small pale things, almost translucent, that move in the channels of slow water and feed, presumably, on the mat itself, although no one has ever watched one feed. You will not see them unless you put your face close to the mat and stay still for a long time.

There is something larger that moves through the lower growths at night. The workers will not discuss it. If you ask, they will pour you more of the dark drink and change the subject, and after a while you will stop asking. You will see it — once, maybe, if you stay long enough — only as a movement in the corner of your vision, a shifting of the dark between the trunks, a sound that is not the wind and not the working and not the water. It is large. It moves slowly. It does not come into the light. You will not see what it is. You will know, only, that the workers know what it is, and that they have decided you do not need to.

The place itself is the deepest inhabitant, and it is not a place. It is a process. The workers are part of it. The growths are part of it. The factories are part of it. The roads and the pipes and the boilers and the dark drink and the small dark birds and the insects in the mat and the larger thing that moves through the lower growths at night and the mat itself and the water that is always falling and the workers who tend the boilers and the boilers that tend the workers — all of it is part of it, and the part you play, if you stay, is small, and the part is not optional. You will come to feel this. You will come to feel that the place is not using you in the way a place uses a person but in the way a body uses a cell, which is to say without particular regard to you, which is to say completely. You will come to feel that the question of what the place is for has not been asked in a long time, and that the place has forgotten the question, and that the place is *settled*, and that *settled* is not a word for stillness but a word for something that has been running so long that the running has become the substance of itself.

You will come, if you are wise, to feel that this is enough.
