# What Lives There

---

## The People

The first thing to say about the people is that they are quiet, and have reason to be.

The valley gives them no reason to speak loudly. Sound does not travel. The walls swallow it. The pipes catch it. The steam muffles it. After a few weeks in the valley a person stops raising their voice. After a few months they stop projecting at all. They speak in the kind of voice you'd use to talk to someone lying beside you in the dark — present, low, almost not there. They gesture more than they speak. They have developed, over generations, a small vocabulary of hand-signs for the boardwalk, for the steam, for *warm here*, for *don't touch*. The children pick it up before they pick up words.

There are perhaps thirty of them. The number wobbles, the way numbers in small places always wobble. A few more might be there in a generation. A few fewer. They die rarely — the air is clean, the water is clean, the warmth takes the edge off the winters — but they do die, the way people die anywhere, of accidents and fevers and one bad year a sickness that took three. They are buried at the northeast end of the valley, where the stream runs out, in a small unmarked plot that is always a little damp.

They are lean. They are weathered in a particular way — not by sun, which barely reaches the valley floor, but by damp, by steam, by the long patience of moving through wet air. Their skin is the color of old paper. Their clothes are heavy: oilskin, layered wool, boots that never quite dry out. They smell, if you got close enough, of iron and wet moss and the warm mineral breath of the pools. Their hands are the most distinctive thing about them. They have calluses on the heels of the palms and the sides of the fingers from years of handling the pipes, and the calluses have a faint dark sheen to them, a kind of staining that never quite washes out. They joke, sometimes, that they are turning into the iron themselves. They are not joking, exactly.

Most of them have been here all their lives. Second generation, third, fourth. The first ones — there are stories, but they are thin — came from somewhere else, a long time ago, for reasons no one quite agrees on. They came because they heard about the iron. They came because they knew how to work it. They came because the world outside had something wrong with it, though no one quite remembers what. They came and they stayed and their children stayed and now no one quite remembers how to leave.

What they have, instead, is the trade.

## The Smith

There is a man in a workshop at the edge of the iron forest who works at a forge that burns nothing. You have to look for him, sometimes, through the steam. He is there most days. He has been there most days for a long time.

The forge is piped up from below — a coupling rises out of the stone floor at the back of his shop and the heat comes up through it, a steady dry heat, the same heat that grows the pipes. He has shaped the inside of the shop around this fact. There is no chimney. There is no flue. The air inside is always a few degrees warmer than the air outside, and it shimmers over the anvil the way air shimmers over a road in summer. He works in shirtsleeves all year. He is the only person in the valley who is sometimes dry.

He does not look up when you enter. He does not look up when you leave. He is working on something — he is always working on something — and the working is a kind of conversation he has been having for a long time. He knows what the iron wants to be. He knows, in his hands, when it is ready. He takes the dark dense ingots that the smelter makes and he heats them and he hammers them and he folds them and he makes things. Tools, sometimes. Components, more often. Strange small shapes for stranger machines elsewhere, the kind of machines that need a piece of iron that has been *grown* rather than *made* and that will not work with any other iron.

He is older than he looks. He has the hands of a much older man. The muscles in his forearms stand out like ropes under the skin. He has a habit, when he is thinking, of holding the iron up to the light from the forge-coupling and turning it slowly, looking at the dark blue iridescence on its surface, the way other men might look at a woman's face. He is not sentimental about it. He is checking the grain.

He will not explain what he makes. If you ask, he will nod toward the door, and the iron forest beyond it, and the steam, and say something like: *it goes out with the others*. Then he will go back to work.

## The Pruner

The pruners go up into the canopy.

This is not, in the valley, a casual phrase. The canopy of the iron forest is twenty meters above the stone floor, and to reach it a person must climb one of the trunks — a thing of dark iron, slick with condensation, warm in places and cold in others. The trunks have, over the decades, been fitted with iron staples at intervals, and the pruners go up these staples hand over hand, with a rope and a kind of curved saw made from the local iron, and they do not look down.

There are three pruners at a time, usually. The work is skilled and not many people can do it. You have to know which new branches are the *right* new branches — the ones that have grown in the right direction, the ones that will make good iron when they are cut — and which are the *wrong* ones, the ones that will clog the canopy or distort the geometry of the lattice above. You have to know, by touch, which pipes are too hot to grip and which are merely warm. You have to be willing to be twenty meters up in a metal forest in half-light with no wind and no echo and the constant tick of cooling metal all around you.

A pruner, when you meet one on the boardwalk afterward, is a recognizable kind of person. They are quieter than the others. They have a particular way of holding their hands slightly open, as if they have just let go of something. Their eyes are slightly wrong for normal light — they have spent too many hours in the dimness under the canopy and they blink in the noon sun when it comes. They have a habit of pausing on the boardwalk and listening to the forest, the way a sailor might listen to the sea. They know, by the sound, how the forest is doing that day. They can tell you, without looking, whether a new branch has pushed up overnight, or whether one of the older trunks has shifted in the heat. They cannot always say how they know.

## The Elder

There is a woman who sits, in the late mornings, on a low stool at the edge of one of the warm pools, and watches the steam.

She is the oldest person in the valley. She does not say how old she is. She does not say where she was born. She has a way of answering questions by not answering them, and after a while you stop asking.

She knows things. She knows, for instance, which of the pools is the deepest — there is one near the center of the iron forest that the others avoid, because the water in it is hot enough to redden a hand and the steam above it is too thick to see through. She knows that the deep tonks come in clusters in some years and are absent in others, and she has a theory about why, though she will not say what it is. She knows that the iron forest grows faster in the years after a hard winter somewhere outside the valley, though no one has been able to explain to her what *outside* the valley means in this context, or how she would know. She knows the names of some of the pipes — the old ones, the ones that have been there longest. The names are not the names of pipes. They are the names of people who used to tend them.

She is not afraid of the forest. She walks the boardwalk slowly, with a stick, and the steam closes behind her like water behind a boat, and the pipes hiss softly as she passes, and she speaks to them sometimes, in a voice too low to hear. She is, in some way that no one quite articulates, the closest thing the valley has to a priest.

She has, on a shelf in her small house, a single object that is not of the valley. She will not show it to anyone. It is from before. It is the only thing in the valley that is from before.

## The Child

There is a child, perhaps seven or eight, who knows the moss-creatures by their sounds.

She spends most of her time in the undergrowth, or as close to it as the boardwalk allows. She is too small to be of much use in the iron work yet, and the elders have not decided what she will be — a pruner, a smith, a smelter, a trader. For now she watches. She has learned, by long listening, to tell the difference between the tick of a cooling pipe and the soft rustle of something moving in the moss. She has learned to sit very still on the boardwalk for a long time, until the rustling comes closer, and then to look down between the slats.

She has seen the moss-creatures. They are small — about the length of her hand, she says, though her hand is small. They are pale, almost white. They have many legs, more than she can count. They move slowly, the way everything in the valley moves, and they are smooth and soft and they leave a faint wet trail on the stone. They eat the moss — she has watched them do it — and they hide when the steam thickens. She has named them, in her own language, but the name is not translatable.

She is not afraid of them. She says, of the valley, that it is *full*. She means: more is here than you can see. She means it as a comfort.

## The Iron Forest Itself

If the iron forest is alive, no one has agreed on the word for it.

It grows. This is the simplest fact. New pipes push up out of the stone floor of the valley, slowly, season by season, taller than their neighbors within a year, branching into the canopy above. The new pipes are dark and warm and they have, when they first emerge, a faint oily smell that no one quite likes. They cool, over months, into the dark blue-black of the older structures. They join the lattice. They become part of the forest.

It drinks. The pipes draw up the warm groundwater through their roots — if they have roots; the word is imperfect — and pass it up through the trunks and into the finer branches of the canopy, where it emerges as steam. The system is closed. The water that goes in comes back out, clean and hot, at the northeast end of the valley. The forest is, in this sense, a kind of engine, though no one would call it that. It does not *do* anything with the water. It simply passes it through.

It rings. After one of the deep tonks from below, the whole iron forest resonates for a few seconds — a low chord, felt in the chest more than heard, the sound of a struck bell the size of a building. The pruners say they can tell, from the way the chord fades, whether the forest is *well* or *not well*. They say this without smiling. They mean it.

It has no mind, as far as anyone can tell. It does not choose. It does not want. It does not, in any way that the valley's people have been able to verify, notice them. They are, to it, the way weather is to a tree — something that happens around it, occasionally prunes it, occasionally harvests from it. It does not hate them. It does not love them. It simply goes on, in its slow iron way, growing itself into the air, drinking the warm water, ringing when the heat below shifts.

It is the largest living thing in the valley, if it is a living thing. It is also the oldest. Some of its trunks, the people say, have been there as long as the settlement has. The first settlers found the forest already grown. They did not plant it. They do not know who did.

## The Moss-Creatures

The thing that moves in the moss is small, and pale, and very shy.

It is about the length of a hand. It is smooth. It is soft in the way a slug is soft, but it has more shape than a slug — a clear head end, a clear tail end, and many legs, dozens of them, fine as threads, that move in a slow undulating rhythm. It is the color of bone that has been in the ground for a long time. It has no eyes that anyone has been able to find. It finds its way by some other sense, perhaps by touch, perhaps by the faint warmth of the moss, perhaps by something no one has a word for.

It eats the moss. This has been established. It also, sometimes, eats the small pale mushrooms that grow under the canopy. It is not fussy. It moves through the undergrowth at a pace that makes slugs look hurried, and it is almost impossible to see unless you know what you are looking for, because its color is the color of the moss-roots and it does not move when the steam moves.

There are more of them than anyone thought. The child who knows them by sound says there are dozens. The pruners, when they come down from the canopy and sit on the boardwalk and listen, say there are more than that. The elder says there are *enough*. She does not say enough for what.

They are, in their small way, the only other large-bodied life in the valley that anyone has seen. They are what the valley has instead of birds, or insects, or anything that moves above the moss. They are quiet and pale and they have been here longer than the people, probably, and they will be here longer than the people, almost certainly.

## The Pool-Things

At the bottom of the deepest pool, in the center of the iron forest, there is a glow.

It is faint. It is the color of an ember that has been an ember for a very long time. It is not, as far as anyone knows, fire. It is something else — a kind of slow chemical light, perhaps, given off by the mats of microbes that live in the hottest water. The mats are red. They are the red of an old wound. They spread across the bottom of the pool in soft lobes, and they pulse, very slowly, with their own faint rhythm, and they are the closest thing to an eye in the valley.

No one goes in the deep pool. The water is too hot. The steam above it is too thick. The few people who have tried to look down into it have come back with reddened hands and a feeling they cannot name. The elder says the pool is *the mouth of something*. She does not say what. The smith says it is *where the iron comes from*. The pruners say nothing about it. They have a habit, when the conversation turns to the deep pool, of looking up at the canopy, as if checking that it is still there.

Below the pool, somewhere, is the heat source. It has been here longer than the pipes and longer than the forest and longer than the moss-creatures and longer, certainly, than the people. It is the reason for everything. It is the reason the pipes drink the water. It is the reason the moss grows in the steam. It is the reason the iron forest is *ongoing*, year after year, season after season, in its slow patient way. It does not know it is the reason. It is only heat. It is only the inside of the earth, doing what the inside of the earth does.

It will outlast them all.

## The Comers and Goers

Once or twice a year, in the late autumn, someone comes down the valley.

They come from the northeast end, where the stream runs out under the pipe-cluster. They come on foot, usually alone, sometimes in pairs. They are not of the valley. They wear different clothes — drier, lighter, with none of the iron-staining on the hands. They speak in a voice that is too loud for the place, at first, and they lower it, over the course of the first day, until they sound like everyone else.

They are the traders. They take the iron — the dark dense ingots, the strange small components the smith has made, the careful bundles of new growth the pruners have cut and the smelter has worked — and they carry it out of the valley, by some route the valley's people do not discuss. They bring back, in exchange, a few things that are not of the valley. Salt, usually. Tea, sometimes. A new blade for a saw, or a part for the generator. Once, a long time ago, a book.
