The first inhabitants are not seen. They are heard. They are felt.

The insects live in the moss the way water lives in a sponge — packed into every cavity, every stem, every dark green fold, in numbers so dense they have stopped being individuals and have become a single sustained voice. They do not throb. They do not pulse. They hold. Their sound is a sawing, a whir, a fabric being torn very slowly somewhere you cannot find. It enters the sternum before it enters the ear. You feel it as a pressure, a slight tightening in the chest, the way you feel weather coming before you see the clouds.

They are not crickets. They are not cicadas. They are something older, or something more recent, or something that has no business being either — small pale bodies with long pale legs, packed so close on the underside of certain ferns that the fern looks like it has grown a fine gray fur. When you stand very still, you can feel them on your skin — a light static, a passing tickle — though you cannot find them. They are too small. They are too many. They are the moss's tenants in the most literal sense: the moss is their building, their floor, their wall, and they are its small ongoing argument with the air.

When the larger thing moves at the back of the hollow, the insects dim. Not silence — a drawing-in, a collective breath held, the way a room of voices briefly stills when a door opens somewhere in a house. Then they resume, in the same key, at the same held pressure, as if the interruption was never really interrupted at all.

The frogs live lower, half-sunk into the moss with only their eyes and the ridges of their backs showing. They are the size the moss requires — small enough to fit between the fern roots, large enough to be noticed when they move. Their color is the green of the moss, slightly darker, slightly wetter, with a faint iridescence across their backs that you only see when they shift and the diffused light from the fissure catches them at the right angle. Their eyes are gold. Their eyes do not blink.

They call in two notes. The first is a low knock, almost subsonic, felt in the floor of the hollow as much as heard. The second is a slightly higher knock, sharper, the sound of a small stone flicked into a pond. Two notes, then three, then the same two notes from somewhere else, then three from somewhere else again. The calls do not synchronize. They braid. They cross each other in the air above your head and make a kind of slow woven music that does not resolve and does not try to. It simply continues.

At certain hours — hours you cannot mark, because the light in the hollow does not change — the frogs gather at the pillar. They come up out of the moss, off the ferns, off the disc and the rod and the walls. They gather at the base of the pillar, in the perfect greenless circle where the moss has died, and they sit there in no particular order, and they do not call, and they do not blink. They sit. They wait. They are waiting for something you cannot see, or they are waiting for the pillar to do something it is about to do, or they are simply gathering because they have always gathered, the way certain birds gather at certain trees at certain times of year.

When one of them sits on the broken rod, the interior light passes through its translucent throat and makes it glow for a moment, faintly, the color of the sky just after the sun has gone. The frog does not seem to notice. The rod does not seem to notice. The light continues doing what it has always done.

The birds live in the fissure, in the slash of cloud-forest sky that rains steady brightness down into the hollow without ever letting direct sun touch the floor. You see them only as movement, as small dark shapes passing across the bright slit above. You hear them in three voices. The first is a laughing voice — bright, sharp notes that arrive in a particular rhythm, each one slightly higher than the last, as if the bird is genuinely amused by something. The second is a single note, sung once, then silence, then the same note again, at intervals precise enough to feel deliberate. The third is a three-part call, liquid and overlapping, that arrives and does not quite resolve. The birds do not sing for you. They sing for the acoustics. They have chosen this hollow the way a violinist chooses a hall, and they are testing it, again and again, with the patience of someone who knows the hall will not change but wants to keep checking anyway.

The larger thing lives deeper in. You do not see it. You cannot see it. You hear it as weight — a slow displacement of air, a pause, a slow displacement again. It is the size of the insects' silence. It is the size of the brief dim that moves through the moss when it passes. It has been here long enough that the insects no longer fully dim for it. They only lower, the way a room of voices lowers when someone important walks through it. Then the larger thing moves again, somewhere in the dark, and the air shifts, and the condensation beads on the walls tremble faintly in their vertical lines, and the dim passes, and the sound rises again.

The moths live on the walls. They are as wide as a palm, with furred legs and wings the color of dust and old paper. They press themselves against the wet rock and drink. They do not move much. They hold themselves still the way the lizards hold themselves still — not because they are waiting but because drinking requires stillness, and stillness is what they have learned to do. Their drinking is continuous. The condensation gathers on them as well as on the walls, and they drink it off themselves as it forms, and they drink it off the rock, and they are a kind of slow moving border between the stone and the air. When one of them flies — briefly, between two points on the wall — its shadow passes over the moss below, and the moss seems to lean slightly toward the shadow, and then the moth lands, and the shadow passes again, and the leaning stops.

The lizards live on the ferns. They are the color of old copper, with a faint greenish patina across their backs, and they hold themselves motionless on the fronds in postures that are not quite natural and not quite geometric — they are mathematical, the way a proof is mathematical, the way something is mathematical when it has been worked out a long time ago by something that does not need to keep working it out. They wait. They wait for insects to come within range, and when one does, the lizard does not strike so much as fold — a single fast contraction of the body, a small wet click of the jaw, and the insect is gone, and the lizard resumes its posture, and the posture is exactly the posture it was in before, and the fern does not seem to have noticed that anything happened.

The beetle lives on the floor. It is the length of a thumb, black and hard, clicking faintly as it walks, and it drags behind it a strand of something that catches the diffused light from the fissure and holds it. The strand is thin. The strand is luminescent. The strand does not fade. The beetle walks a path that does not repeat, and leaves its strand, and the strand accumulates in certain places into small bright knots and tangles, and the moss grows around these knots but does not cover them, and they remain visible long after the beetle has moved on, like a sentence written in a language you almost understand.

The spider lives in the upper ferns, where the fronds begin to uncurl from their fiddleheads. It is small. It is invisible until it moves. It moves only to intercept things — a leaf falling, an insect that has wandered too far up the wall, a single drop of condensation that has gathered too large on the underside of a frond. When it moves, it moves in a single fast line, and the line is precise, and then it is still again, and invisible again, and you remember it only as a small disturbance in the air, a tiny rearrangement of where things were.

The smaller frogs — different from the calling frogs, paler, more numerous — sit on the moss in rows. They sit along the edges of the worn steps that descend from the entrance to the back of the hollow. They sit like notes on a stave. Each one is slightly larger than the last, as if the row is a scale, as if the row is a chord waiting to be struck, as if the row is something else entirely that you do not have the language for. None of them blink. They do not call. They are not the calling frogs. They sit and watch and wait, and what they are waiting for is not the same as what the larger frogs are waiting for at the pillar, and not the same as what the lizards are waiting for on the ferns. They are waiting for something you cannot see. Or they are simply being rows of themselves, because rows are what they are.

None of the creatures care what the pillar is. The beetles walk over its base without hesitation, their legs clicking faintly on its smooth surface. A frog sits on the broken rod and does not flinch when the faint interior light passes through its translucent throat. The ferns grow toward the pillar but do not touch it. The lizards wait on the ferns as if the pillar is not there. The moths drink from the walls as if the pillar is not there. The birds sing from the fissure as if the pillar is not there. The pillar has been here so long it has become a feature of the ecosystem. It is not a thing anymore. It is a place. It is a kind of weather. It is a kind of sound.

The pillar hums. You do not hear it so much as feel it — a low vibration that sits in the back of your teeth and makes your fillings ache, if you have them. When the larger thing at the back of the hollow moves, the hum shifts slightly. When a moth lands on the disc — the disc that is neither concave nor convex but somehow both — the hum shifts slightly. When a frog sits on the rod, the hum shifts slightly. The pillar is registering. The pillar is doing something with what it registers. You cannot tell what. You cannot even tell what kind of thing it is. The creatures do not try to tell. The creatures do not care. They are the moss's tenants, and the condensation's harvesters, and the pillar's quiet ongoing audience.

You will not stay long. But the creatures will. The insects will hold their sawing in the moss. The frogs will sit in their rows. The birds will sing from the fissure. The larger thing will move at the back of the hollow. The moths will drink. The lizards will wait. The beetle will drag its luminescent strand. The spider will wait, invisible, until something falls within range. The pillar will hum, and the creatures will listen without listening, and the hollow will continue being a thousand lives compressed into a pocket you could walk across in forty paces, long after you have climbed back to the ridge, long after the moss has given way to lichen, long after the lichen to bare stone, long after the bare stone to the wider mountains, long after the mountains to the wider world.
