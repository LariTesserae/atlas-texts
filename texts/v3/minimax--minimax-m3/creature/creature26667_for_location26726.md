The people of the Upper Stoup are not many. They have never been many. They are perhaps eighty, perhaps fewer, perhaps more — no one has counted in a generation, because counting requires the idea that the number matters, and here it does not. What matters is whether the spring is flowing, whether the moss is growing, whether the youngest child has eaten. The number is what the number is. It has been roughly the same for as long as the oldest of them can remember, and it has been getting smaller, slowly, for as long as anyone has noticed. A birth is marked by the flat stone near the spring, struck once. A death is marked the same stone, struck twice. The sound does not change. The people know from the number.

They are small. This is the first thing a stranger from below would notice, and the last thing such a stranger would understand. They are not small the way a hungry people are small — they eat enough, on the berries and the dried moss and the broths and the meat of the small grey animals, and they are not gaunt. They are small because they have been here for so many generations that the altitude has shaped them the way a river shapes the stones in its bed. They are barrel-chested, narrow-shouldered, short-legged. Their hands are wide and thick, and rough, and pale, and cracked along the knuckles with the cold. Their faces are weathered in the particular way that fog and wind weather a face — not the deep gullies of a sun-dried people, but a smoothing, a polishing, a kind of leathering. The skin is pale but not white; it carries a grey undertone, the color of the local stone when the stone is wet. Their hair is dark and lank and not abundant, and they wear it long or tied back with a twist of moss-fibre, and they do not adorn it. Their eyes are pale — grey, sometimes a blue so faded it is nearly grey — and they see well in fog. They do not see well in bright sun, but bright sun is rare here, and when it comes they close their eyes and turn their faces to it for a few minutes at a time, as if it were a hot coal they were not entirely sure they trusted.

The women and the men are not strongly distinguished by dress. The cold sees to that. They wear layers — many layers — of cloth that has been patched and re-patched and passed down until the original fabric is only a memory. The colors have muted to the palette of the place: greens, greys, browns, an occasional lichen-orange, a near-black where the dye was once deep blue. Nothing is bright. Nothing would stand out against the moss. The cloth smells of smoke and damp and the particular vegetable rot of wet moss, and beneath that, faintly, of the people themselves — a mineral smell, a cold clean smell, the smell of skin that has been polished by the wind. Footwear is wrappings of hide, stitched with sinew, soled sometimes with a strip of the older leather that has gone stiff. Nothing is tailored. Nothing is fashionable. Everything is functional, and everything has been worn toward the softness of the place itself.

They move slowly. This is not laziness. The air is thin and they are careful with it; a hard walk will make their lungs complain within the hour, and they know this, and so they do not waste motion. They place their feet deliberately on the moss-slicked stones. They do not hurry. They have a way of standing still that is not idleness but a kind of patience — they will stand for several minutes watching a patch of lichen, or listening to the wind shift, or simply waiting for whatever they were waiting for to become clear. They do not fidget. Their hands, when they are not working, often hang at their sides or are tucked into the many layers of cloth. Their stillness is the stillness of the plateau.

Their voices are soft. They have learned to speak softly because the air carries — a shout from one end of the town is heard at the other — and because there is no need to shout here. They do not whisper; that would be a different thing. They speak in a low, even register, and they speak close, often, and they speak with pauses. A sentence will have several pauses in it, as if the speaker is making sure each thing has been heard before the next thing is offered. They are also good listeners. They have learned that the way a sound breaks up across the folds of the stone — who is speaking, how many speakers, what their mood is, how far away — is information. Where a stranger would hear nothing but wind, the people hear the household three fields away having an argument about a torn roof.

They do not smile much, but they are not grim. Their faces in repose have a quality that might be mistaken for sadness, but is not — it is the face that the fog and the wind and the slow work have made, and it is the face of a person who is waiting for something without knowing what it is. They smile sometimes, briefly, mostly at children or dogs. They laugh rarely, and then quietly, and at small things.

There is no leader. There has never been a leader, in the sense the word is used below. There are people who are listened to more than others, but this is not authority. It is weight. The oldest woman of the spring is listened to because she knows the spring. The trapper who has been out farthest is listened to because he has been out farthest. The man who remembers which stones were moved when is listened to because he remembers. These are not offices. They are simply the ways the people distribute attention.

The families are not really families in the sense below. They are households, and a household may contain several generations, and two or three households may share a building complex that has grown together over the decades, the walls indistinguishable. There are perhaps fifteen or twenty of these complexes, and within them perhaps forty or fifty adults, and a dozen or so children, and a similar number of old people. The relationships between them are not tracked carefully. Everyone is related to everyone, in some way, by now — the population is too small and has been here too long for it to be otherwise. Cousin and aunt and uncle are words that have been worn smooth of meaning, and are used for almost anyone of the right generation. People do not marry out. There is no one to marry out with. They marry within, and the children that come are the children of the town, and the town raises them, in the way a forest raises its seedlings.

The children are few, and they are watched. A woman who is expecting will go to the spring each morning as she has always done, and will carry her jar, and will be a little slower, and the other women will notice and not say anything for a long time, and then one day one of them will put a hand on her arm, briefly, in passing, and that will be the acknowledgment. The birth, when it comes, will be in a low room, warm with the small hearth, and the women of the household will be there, and the men will not, and the sound of it will be quiet — the women of the Upper Stoup are quiet even in this — and the flat stone will be struck once, and the whole town will pause for a moment, and then go on. The child will be wrapped in cloth, and will be held by its mother, and will be brought out, after a day or two, to see the fog, and will squint at it, and begin.

The children learn the under-town as they learn the above-town. They are not taught, exactly. They simply go. They crawl into the low passages beneath the floors, into the rooms that lead nowhere, into the cracks between the newer stones and the older stones, and they come back out, and they know another layer of the place. A child of five or six can find its way through the under-town from one end of the complex to another, and can tell which stones were set down by which generation, and which were always there. The children play in the moss. They have games — quiet games, mostly, played with small stones or with lengths of moss-fibre. They do not run. They are quiet children. They learn to wait.

The old people are many, or seem many, because the population is small and the old stay. The cold and the thin air are not kind to the very old, but the old here have a way of fading rather than falling — they sit by their hearths, and one day they sit a little longer, and one day they do not go to the spring, and one day they are gone, in the night, and in the morning the household knows, and the flat stone is struck twice, and the body is laid in the wall, or in the floor, or in a low place that no one will disturb. The dead become the town. They are not buried in any sense that would be recognized below. They are placed into the structure, and the structure is closed over them, and the moss grows over the place, and in a few seasons it is not possible to tell that anything happened there. The old people, before they go, often sit for a long time in the under-town. They go back to the older rooms, the ones they knew as children, the ones their own grandparents placed them in. They sit there in the dim light and they remember, or they do not remember, and eventually they do not come back up. The household finds them, eventually, and brings them out, and lays them in the place that has been prepared.

There are the spring-women. Every morning, in the grey light before the fog has drawn back to the eastern rim, the women of the town go to the spring. They go in ones and twos, with stone jars and the occasional clay one traded up years ago. They walk the lanes in their soft wrappings, and the dogs sometimes follow at a distance, and the morning is quiet except for the wind and the drip of the eaves and the sound of feet on the moss. The spring is at the head of one of the folds, where the water emerges from a crack in the stone — clear, very cold, running for perhaps two hundred paces before it disappears into the gravel. The women kneel at the place where the water comes out, and they hold their jars under it, and they wait for them to fill. The filling takes time. They do not hurry it.

This is the principal gathering of the town. The women speak to each other here, in low voices, of small things — whose roof leaked last night, which of the dogs has been restless, whether the lichen on the south face is thicker this year. They do not speak of large things. The large things are for the hearths, in the evening, when the wind has dropped. They speak of the weather, because the weather is the principal fact of their lives. They speak of the children, briefly, with the particular gentleness that people use for things that are precious and uncertain. They speak of the old, with the particular quietness that people use for things that are passing. They speak, occasionally, of the trappers who have gone out and not yet come back. The spring-women are the memory of the town in a particular way. They know who has gone to the spring on which mornings, and who has been absent, and for how long. They notice absences.

The oldest of the spring-women is a woman called, simply, the Spring. Her real name is something else, but it has been worn away by use, and now she is referred to as the Spring, or as the Woman of the Spring, or not referred to at all, because everyone knows who is meant. She has been going to the spring every morning for sixty years. She knows the flow of it in every weather — how much it gives after a fog, how much after a rain, how much in the long dry spells when there is no condensation. She knows which cracks in the stone produce the clearest water and which produce the water with the faint mineral taste. She has laid her hands on the stone at the source so many times that the stone there has been worn smooth by her palms, a small hollow in the rock that fits her hands. She does not speak more than the other women. She is simply the one they look to, when something about the water is uncertain.

There are the menders. These are mostly men, though not exclusively. They are the ones who tend the buildings — who climb the steep conical roofs in the stillest weather and lay new moss on the worn places, who chink the gaps in the walls with the moss-mortar that is pounded moss mixed with the local clay. The menders know the buildings the way the spring-women know the water. They know which stones were set in which year, by the way the mortar has weathered, by the lichen that has grown on them, by the slight settling. They know which roof-beams are sound and which are not, by the sound they make when struck, by the way they flex under weight. They know the trick of laying the moss-mortar so that it will take the next lichen, so that the patch will disappear into the wall within a season. They do this work slowly. They have been doing it for generations, and the work has been done well enough that the town still stands, and will stand, perhaps, for several more generations if the menders continue.

The menders have the stone tools that came up the goat trail a generation ago. They are careful with these. The knives are sharp, and they are not replaced. They are used for the small precise work — cutting the moss, scoring the stones, trimming the patches of hide that are used in the chinking. The menders do not use them for the heavy work. The heavy work is done with the older tools, with the rounded stones that have been worn into hammers over centuries, with the antler picks that are older than anyone can remember.

A mender will often have one building that is his, in the sense that he has tended it longest, or that it is the house he was born in, and that he knows better than any other. He will know every stone of it. He will know the sound it makes in every wind, and the way the light enters it in the brief spells when the fog lifts, and the way the moss grows on its roof. He will sit in it in the evenings, by the small hearth, and listen to the wind find its hollows, and the building will ring, and he will hear it as his own breathing.

There are the trappers. There are only a few — three, perhaps four, in any generation. They are the ones who go out into the broken country at the edges of the plateau, where the stone breaks up into fissures and tumbled boulders and places a person could fall and not be found. They go alone. They go for days at a time, sometimes for a week. They go for the small grey animals that live in the deeper folds, that are rarely seen, that the old people trap and eat when they can be caught. The trappers know where the animals den. They know the smells of the broken country — a smell of dry stone and old fur and the particular cold of places where the sun never reaches. They know how to wait, motionless, for hours, until an animal comes within reach of a stone or a snare.

The trappers come back changed. This is something everyone knows. A trapper who has been out for a long time will return thin and silent, and will not speak much for several days, and will sit by his hearth and not go to the spring in the morning, and will slowly come back into the rhythms of the town. A trapper who has been out for a very long time will return and will not seem quite the same — his eyes will be a little unfocused, or his hands will not move quite the way they used to, or he will look at the town as if he is seeing it from a great distance. The people do not ask what has happened. They give him broth, and they let him sit, and after a while he is himself again, or is the new self that the broken country has made.

The trappers are the only ones who leave the town, with any regularity. They are the only ones who know the plateau beyond the visible folds, beyond the rim of fog that hangs over the settlement. They have walked for hours into the silence and the wind. They have seen places where there is nothing but stone and lichen for as far as the eye can go. They do not talk about these places. When they do speak of them, it is in the evening, by the hearth, in phrases that are mostly weather — the wind there, the fog there, the quality of the light. They do not name the places. The places do not have names. To name a place is to claim it, and they do not claim them.

There are the dogs. There are perhaps ten of them, perhaps fewer. They live in loose packs, and they have territories within the town — the spring is one territory, the lanes are another, the edges are another. They are not owned. They are not fed, except by scraps, and by what they catch — the small beetles, the occasional bird that comes down, the small grey animals when a trapper has wounded one and the dog finds it. They are thin but not starving. Their coats are dark, mostly — black, dark brown, a grey that is almost black. Their fur is rough and holds the damp, and they smell, faintly, of wet dog and of the moss.

The dogs are quiet. They do not bark much. When they do bark, at some shape in the fog, every person in hearing stops what they are doing and looks toward the sound, and the barking stops, and the person waits, and if there is nothing the person goes on. The dogs have other sounds — a low warning growl, a kind of whine that is not distress but acknowledgment, the small sounds they make to each other in the evenings when they gather at the edges of the lanes and lie in heaps, three or four together, for the warmth. They sleep in the doorways of the houses sometimes, and the people step over them, and the dogs do not move.

The dogs know the weather. This is the principal fact of them. They know before the people do, and a certain restlessness among the dogs is a signal that the wind is going to change, or that the fog will thicken, or — very rarely — that rain is coming.
