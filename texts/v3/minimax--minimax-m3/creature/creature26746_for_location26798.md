The moths are what you meet first. They are not so much seen as encountered — a presence on every surface, a thickening of the air near the lamps, a sense of something that has been here for a while and has been multiplied. They are heavy-bodied for their wings, which is the first thing to notice about them: when they land, they land with a small soft impact, and when they fall, they fall as if they meant to. Their wings are the color of newsprint that has lived in a damp basement for a generation, with a faint pattern of darker scales that does not quite resolve into anything, and they hold them in a way that suggests folded paper rather than the swept-back posture of a butterfly at rest. Their antennae are long and curved, and they hold them out ahead of themselves as they walk across the glass, feeling for what comes next. They are always walking. Even when they are not flying, they are walking, in the slow considered way of a creature for whom movement is the only argument against being somewhere permanently.

Their eggs are laid in the seams of the workshops, in the rust-flakes that line the bolts, in the small pockets of dry matter that accumulate where iron meets brick. The larvae do not see the light. They feed on the corroded metal itself, on the bacteria that live in the corrosion, on the small deposits of organic matter that the water brings down and leaves along the way. They pupate in the same seams, and the adults climb the walls to the lamps, and the lamps are the center of the moth-world the way a salt lick is the center of a deer-world. The moths do not feed at the lamps. The lamps are not food. The lamps are warmth, and the lamps are light, and the moths orient to them the way moss orients to a stone — by accumulating against them, by living out the long habit of approach. A moth that never reaches a lamp will live, will mate, will lay its eggs. A moth that reaches a lamp will live a few days longer, and will mate more often, and will lay more eggs, and its body will end up in a gutter, and the gutter will carry it down to the basins, and in the basins the soft-bodied things will take it apart.

The lizards are next, and they are the moths' complement. They are pale in the way the moths are pale, but the paleness is differently made — the moths are the color of something that has faded, the lizards are the color of something that never had much color in the first place. Their skin has a faint translucent quality, and the bluish tint of their bellies is the tint of veins seen through a hand held to a lamp. They are long-bodied, low to the surface they walk on, and their feet have a splayed grip that takes advantage of the textures available — the raised heads of rivets, the edges of brick, the curled lips of peeling paint. They hunt the moths. They also hunt the small flies that hatch from the same seams, and the soft-bodied things that are washed out of the deeper workshops and have not yet found a place to attach. They have learned the geometry of the gutters with the precision of a thing that has been doing the same work for many generations. They know which brick edges hold water longest. They know the angle at which to approach a basin to drink without falling in. They know the schedules of the moths — when the lamps are coldest, when the moths are most sluggish, when a moth that has just settled is still deciding which way to face.

Their eyes are large and black, and they catch any available light and throw it back as a small wet flash — a moving constellation in the deeper workshops, a sign that something is awake. The flashes are not coordinated, and they are not a language. They are a by-product of an eye that has more light-catching surface than it strictly needs, and a habitat in which any light is a resource to be gathered. In the deeper workshops, where the lamps do not reach, the flashes are sometimes the only indication that the place is not empty. A lizard can be three meters away in the dark and announce itself only as a small moving star, and then be gone, and the workshop will be still again, and the only thing moving will be the water down its various paths.

The rats are not the first thing you would name, but they are the largest of the warm-blooded things that live here full-time, and they have shaped the Sump in ways that are easy to miss. They are long and pale-furred, with their tails bare of hair and rough to the touch, and they move with the loose-jointed ease of an animal that has been navigating the same architecture for so long that the architecture has become part of its skeleton. They live in the deeper workshops, in the spaces behind the fallen bricks, in the dead channels where the pipes no longer carry anything. They are not numerous. There is not enough food here for a large population — the moths are small, the lizards are small, the things in the water are small — and so the rats have spread out instead of concentrated, and a single rat will hold a territory the size of a small house, and will know every rivet and every seam of it. They drink from the basins and they have learned, like the lizards, which basins hold the cleanest water and at what hours. They eat the moths when the moths fall. They eat the things that are not rats, when those things are slow.

The things that are not rats are the most troubling to catalog, because cataloging is what they resist. They live in the same places the rats live — the deep workshops, the dead pipes, the spaces behind the walls — and they are the same general size and shape as a rat, with a long body and a way of moving that is low and deliberate, but the resemblance is a kind of bait. They have too many joints. A rat has the joints a rat has — shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle — and each of these moves in a way the eye can predict. The not-rats have these joints, and then they have more. There is a second hinge in the forearm. There is a way the spine can fold that a rat's spine cannot. There is a way the head can pivot, briefly, almost a full turn, that gives the impression of a creature checking its own back. They are not insects. They are not anything that has a clean name. They have pale, soft-looking bodies with a faint segmentation that suggests something older than a mammal, and their legs are too long for them in the way the legs of a foal are too long, and they are never quite still, even when they appear to be. The rats leave them alone. The lizards leave them alone. The web-frogs, sitting on the rims of the basins, do not sing when one of them passes.

The water holds its own population, and the population is layered in the way the water itself is layered — surface, depth, current, the underside of things. On the underside of the brick, where the current is constant and the light is none, there are soft-bodied things that cling with their feet in the flow. They are flat, and they are pale, and they have a fringe along the trailing edge of their bodies that beats in a slow rhythm, and they feed on what the water brings — the fine particles of iron oxide, the small organic fragments, the bacterial film that has grown on the brick and been loosened by the current. They are filter-feeders, and the water is their pasture, and the pasture is renewed by the next thing that falls. They have been here a long time. Their bodies, in cross-section, show growth-rings in the calcium of their shells, and the rings are uneven, and the unevenness matches the wet and dry seasons above, and the matching is the closest thing to a calendar the Sump has.

In the silt of the basins there are worms. They are pale, and they are slender, and they feed in the way worms feed — by taking the sediment in at one end and letting it out at the other, and by the taking-in and the letting-out, the sediment is slowly moved and slowly sorted, and the basins do not fill. The worms are not numerous, and they are not large, but they are thorough. A basin that has worms in it will remain a basin. A basin that does not will, over the course of years, fill with the silt that the water lays down, and become a part of the floor, and lose its rim. The presence of worms is, in this sense, architectural. They are the maintenance crew of the deep places, and the deep places have been maintained.

In the gutters themselves, in the small currents that the brick channels carry, there are fish. They are eyeless, and they are pale, and they are small — the length of a finger, perhaps, the length of a thumb — and they move in loose shoals of three or four, never more, and the shoals pass each other in the narrow channels and merge briefly and then separate, and the separation is not a choice so much as a tendency. They navigate by the walls. The lateral line — the line of sensitive cells that runs along their flank — picks up the small pressure changes of the current against the brick, and the brick has a texture, and the texture has a memory, and the fish move through the memory the way a person moves through a familiar house in the dark. They are not afraid. They are not afraid because they have nothing to compare the dark to. They have always been in the dark, and the dark has always been the size of a brick channel, and the brick channel has always contained what it contains, and the world, for them, is the current and the walls and the other fish.

On the rims of the deeper basins, where the brick lifts out of the water and the air is damp and still, there are the web-frogs. They are like frogs in the way that the not-rats are like rats — in general shape, in general posture, in the way they sit with their forelegs turned out and their hindlegs folded. But the webbing between their toes is excessive. It is not the webbing of a frog that swims. It is the webbing of a thing that wants to be a larger surface than it is. The webbing trembles when they breathe, and the breathing is the only visible sign of life when they are still, which is most of the time. They sing a single note, very softly, and the note is the same note in every throat, and the chorus does not synchronize. A frog will start its note, and another will start its note a moment later, and another a moment after that, and the three notes will overlap and pass through each other and never quite come together, and this non-synchronization is, as far as anyone can tell, simply how they are. They sing at night, or what passes for night in a place where the lamps never go out and the phosphorescence never quite dies.
