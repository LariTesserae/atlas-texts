The spiders are the only ones who never seem to leave.

You will see them if you sit still long enough, and you have to sit still, because they read the wind the way fish read current. They come out in the lee moments, the lulls between gusts, and they cover ground in a manner that looks, at first, like panic. They are tiny — a thumbnail would cover three of them — and they are darker than the stone, almost black, with legs that look too long for the body, legs that splay out at angles that suggest they are not built so much for walking as for being held down by as many points of contact as possible. When the wind comes back they are gone. They have not been blown away. They have dropped a thread, and they have ridden the thread down into the crack they came from, and the crack has closed over them like a mouth, and they wait. They wait a lot.

What they wait for is the other things. There are smaller things even than the spiders — mites, springtails, pale specks without common names that move through the moss with the look of dust given intention — and these are what the spiders eat. The spiders do not build webs. There is nothing to anchor a web to that the wind would not tear in a season, and there is not much in the way of flying prey here anyway. The spiders hunt by ambush. They wait at the lip of a crack, or under the edge of a moss cushion, and they take what moves past. Their lives are measured in the seconds it takes to close on something and the minutes it takes to digest it. They live, when they live, very intensely and very briefly.

You can sometimes see, in the lee of a wind-rib, a network of silk lines so fine they are almost invisible except when the light is right. These are not webs. They are roads. The spiders have laid them down over generations, anchor line by anchor line, until there is a kind of map of the safer routes across the local stone. They follow these. You can sit in one place for an hour and watch the same three or four individuals make the same circuits, vanishing into their cracks and reappearing a few feet downwind, always downwind, because the wind is what moves them and they have stopped fighting it. They are, in their small way, surveying. They know where the morning sun will hit first and where the evening cold will settle last. They know which stones are loose enough to hide under and which are tight enough to hunt along. They are cartographers of a territory measured in inches, and they have been mapping it longer than anyone else has been up here.

The longest-lived spider on the plateau is probably three years old. Most live less. The wind takes some. The cold takes some, in the long night when the temperature drops below what their little bodies can keep moving. The films of water that pool in the shallow bowls after rain take some — a spider drowned in an inch of water is a spider dead in it. The lightning takes some, though not as many as you would think; the spiders are too well-grounded to attract a strike. What kills most of them is the simple arithmetic of being small in a place that does not care how small you are. They die, and the rest keep hunting, and the threads they have laid are taken up by the next ones and added to.

The hawk is not from here.

It comes up over the edge of the plateau some afternoons, when the thermals along the northern cliffs are strong enough to lift a heavier thing than silk, and it rides the air the way the spiders ride their threads — by giving itself over to whatever is pushing. It does not flap. It slides. It tilts its wings into the turbulence and uses the wind as a floor and a ceiling at the same time, and it covers the plateau in long, patient passes that look, from the ground, like the unhurried circling of something that has nowhere in particular to be. It is the largest moving thing on the plateau. It is also, most days, the only moving thing you can see without aid. Its shadow passes over the moss and you feel the brief chill of it and you look up, and there it is, hanging at an angle that physics should not allow, head tipped to look down at whatever moved.

It is looking for the spiders. It is looking for the small pale shapes of the things that run between the stones. It is looking, mostly, for nothing — it has eaten well somewhere lower down, and what it is doing up here is closer to surveillance than to hunting. When it finds what it wants it does not dive. There is not the room to dive. It folds its wings and drops, fast, and pulls out at the last moment with a twist that uses the wind as a brake, and it takes whatever it has taken back into the air almost before you have registered that it had come down. Then it is gone over the edge of the plateau, back into the brown country below, and the sky is empty again and you are alone with the wind-driven spiders and the moss.

You see it maybe once a week. Sometimes not that. It is the rumor of a larger life, and it passes without leaving anything behind except, occasionally, a feather that the wind will carry off in an hour.

The herd is the rarest thing on the plateau, and the thing you will never quite believe you saw.

They come up from below in some season — late summer, usually, when the lower pastures have been grazed down to the root and the animals remember, or know, that there is grass higher up — and they cross the plateau from one edge to the other in a slow, pale procession that takes most of a day. You see them first as a smudge on the horizon, a low line of off-white against the off-white of the sky, and you think it is cloud. Then the line resolves into individuals. They are low and pale. They might be sheep. They might be something older than sheep, something that has been up here longer than any shepherd ever was, and that has lost the specifics of breed the way the stone has lost the specifics of geology. They have horns, perhaps, or the suggestion of horns. They have the unexcitable gait of animals that have been walking for a very long time and have learned not to waste movement.

They do not look at you. They do not look at you because you are not, in their accounting, significant. You are a vertical thing in a landscape of horizontals, and they have learned not to fear verticals — the verticals do not, as a rule, do anything. They walk. They graze, briefly, on the tougher mosses and the wiry grasses, with the look of animals that are eating because the day is long and not because they are hungry. They leave droppings that the mosses will, in a year or two, incorporate into themselves. They pass on. You do not see them again for a season. Sometimes two seasons. They are following a route that was laid down by animals that knew the plateau when the plateau was less battered, and they follow it now because the route is still there — the saddles, the gaps in the wind-ribs, the places where the cliffs are climbable — and following it is easier than working out a new one. They have been doing this, probably, for longer than the people were here. They are older inhabitants than the people, though they look newer. They look as if they had always been here, and they will be here when the wind has finished with the plateau and the plateau is indistinguishable from the plains below.

You may never see them. You may see them three times in a month. There is no predicting. They move on a calendar you cannot read.

The moss is the only continuous thing here.

The wind comes and goes. The storms come and go. The hawk comes and goes. The herd comes and goes. The moss does not come and go. It is the substrate and the inhabitant and the wreckage all at once. It is everywhere that the wind is not, and it is also everywhere that the wind is, because it has learned to live in the wind the way the spiders have, by being willing to be broken and rebuilt constantly. A patch of moss is not a thing so much as a process. You look at it in the morning and it is green and dense and it looks like a piece of the lower pastures misplaced at altitude. You look at it in the afternoon and it has been beaten flat by a gust, or scoured by hail, or torn at the edges by a spider running across it, and it looks different. It is the same patch. It has just been through another iteration. By the next morning the parts that were torn have been replaced. The parts that were scoured have grown back. The whole thing has crept a millimeter upwind and a millimeter downwind and a millimeter in every direction, and in a year it will have crept a handspan, and in a century it will have crossed the plateau, very slowly, by being itself in slightly different places.

Underneath the green, in the damp places, in the lee of the larger stones, there are other things. Lichens that have spent a hundred years getting from one side of a stone to the other. Small ferns that have given up on being vertical and grow flat against the rock, their fronds as broad as your palm and as tough as leather. Tough wiry grasses that are not really grasses so much as the idea of grasses — they have been battered into a shape that is only barely recognizable as a plant, and they hold on by being willing to lose a leaf to the wind and grow another. The whole mat is an economy. The wind kills. The damp rebuilds. The lightning sometimes burns a circle of black into the green, and the black is colonized, in a season, by something different. A stone gets heaved by frost and exposes a fresh face of rock underneath, and the rock is colonized, in a season, by lichen. The whole plateau, if you stand far enough back, is a single living thing that is being eaten and grown and eaten and grown, and the eating and the growing are the same process.

This is what it means to live here. Not to persist in spite of the place, but to persist by being part of the place's own way of being patient.

The people were here for a while, and then they were not here.

You can tell because of the stone. The stone on the plateau has been arranged, in places, into lines that the wind has not yet managed to bury. There is a row of low walls across a saddle to the northeast — not walls so much as the foundations of walls, single stones laid end to end, the kind of thing that you build when you have nothing to build with and you need, very badly, to mark out a rectangle of ground that the wind cannot quite reach. There is the footprint of a shelter against one of the wind-ribs, a hollow lined with flat stones that the moss is slowly digesting. There is a cairn, or what was once a cairn, now just a heap of stones that might be deliberate or might be the natural conclusion of frost-heave.

You can tell what kind of people they were by the scale of what they left. They were small. They had no metal. They had no mortar. They had hands and stones and the decision to be here for another season, and they did not have the decision to be here for the season after that. They were the kind of people who had been pushed up here — by weather, by the movements of other people, by the slow loss of the lower ground to someone else's use — and they came up and they made the smallest possible marks that would let them stay, and then the marks were too small to keep them, and they went back down, or they died, or they became the herd that crosses the plateau in late summer, low and pale, looking like a slow cloud. You cannot know which.

What you can know is that they did not love the place. They endured it. The marks they left are the marks of endurance — the windbreak, the foundation, the cairn that might have been a grave or a marker or just the natural conclusion of having moved too many stones in one direction. They did not carve their names into the rock. They did not build anything that would still be recognizable as a building in a thousand years. They made arrangements. The wind is undoing them. The moss is incorporating them. In another few centuries there will be nothing left to say they were ever here, and the plateau will be, again, just the plateau.

What remains is the small, continuous life.

The spiders in their cracks, mapping the lee of every stone in silk. The hawk, once a week, casting its long shadow and moving on. The herd, once a season, crossing like weather. The moss, all the time, being broken and rebuilt, broken and rebuilt. The threads of water after each storm, running briefly downslope and vanishing into the cracks where the spiders wait. The lightning that strikes the same rock over and over and the lichen that colonizes the blackened place and is itself colonized, in a year, by something mossier. The slow give of everything, by fractions of a millimeter, toward the level it will eventually reach.

It will take longer than there has been so far.

For now, the small things are still winning.
