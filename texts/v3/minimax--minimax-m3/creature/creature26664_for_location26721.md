What lives here does not announce itself.

The moss is the first answer, and the moss is correct — as far as it goes. The moss lives here the way a rumor lives in a family: not by being seen but by being everywhere, by being the medium through which everything else moves. It is in the cracks. It is on the walls. It is in the floor of the shelter, growing out of the ash that used to be a fire that used to be someone warming their hands over a stone that is now part of the wall. The moss has been here longer than the ring of stones. The moss will be here longer than the ring of stones. It does not know this. It does not need to know this. It grows at the rate it grows, and the rate is the rate of patience, and patience is what the hollow is made of.

There are many kinds. The black-green moss lives on the walls near the splash, dark enough to read as the absence of color rather than its presence. The pale moss lives where the light never reaches, in the seams and the joints and the places the splash does not touch. The moss that grows inside the shelter is older than the shelter itself — it grew on the rock before the rock became a wall, and now it grows on the wall, and when the wall finally lets go it will grow on the rubble, and when the rubble becomes soil it will grow on the soil, and it will not notice the difference, because the difference is not its concern.

The moss does not see. The moss does not hear. The moss does what moss does, which is to be a membrane between stone and air, between wet and dry, between the past of a thing and its continuing.

The ferns live here too. There is the large one in the shelter, which has grown into the shape of the absent ceiling, its fronds spreading like the ribs of a small vaulted roof no longer there. There are the smaller ones on the walls near the falling water, each frond a thin question uncurling. The ferns are more particular than the moss. They have a direction. They know which way is up, and they uncurl toward it, even when up is only a gradient of grey light from the ragged circle far above. They have been doing this for a long time. They will continue doing this for a long time. They are not in a hurry.

The birch lives here.

The birch is the most improbable inhabitant. It has no business being here. There is no soil to speak of — only a half-inch of crevice in the basalt, packed with the dust of weathered rock and the slow contribution of dead moss and the occasional leaf that has somehow blown in from the lip forty feet above. The birch has made a life out of this. It is three feet tall, perhaps four. It is the yellow-green of a plant that has never had to argue with another plant for light, because there are no other plants to argue with, because there has never been another plant in this crevice, because the crevice is the size of the birch and no more. The birch does not know what a forest is. It has never seen another tree. It leans slightly toward the opening above, where the grey comes from, but it does not lean so much that it falls. It has been finding its balance for a long time. It will continue finding it.

The birch does not know about the people who stacked the stones. The birch does not know about the antler smoothed at one end into a thing to hold. The birch is its own small event, its own small happening in a place where happenings are rare.

The water lives here.

The water is the loudest inhabitant. It is also the most patient, which seems a contradiction but is not. The water has been falling for longer than the moss has been growing, longer than the basalt has been weathering, longer than any of the small events of plants and stones. The water does not think of itself as an inhabitant. The water thinks of itself as water, which is to say it does not think of itself at all. But it has worn its own chamber in the cliff and then forgotten the chamber and just kept going. The falling is part of the rock now. The rock is part of the falling. If you could somehow remove the water, the chamber would still be a chamber, but it would be a chamber-shaped silence, and the silence would be the shape of the water that used to be there.

The water is cold. The water has always been cold. The water comes from somewhere far above and far away, through the fissure in the basalt, and by the time it arrives here it has been cold for so long that being cold is its temperature.

The water does not name itself. The water does not need to.

And in the pool, in the deep dark of the pool, something luminous lives.

This is the inhabitant the imagination seizes on, because the imagination gets loud down here, and the imagination likes things that glow in the dark. Whether what lives in the pool is real, or whether it is the imagination becoming briefly visible, is not a question the hollow will answer. The hollow is not in the business of answering questions.

What lives in the pool is small. Smaller than a hand. Smaller than a thumb. It has a light that does not behave the way a fish-light behaves — a fish-light winks, a fish-light flickers, a fish-light goes out when the fish is gone. This light does not do those things. This light is a steady pale thing, the color of the saxifrage leaves on the walls, the color of an idea of a color. It moves slowly through the water, and its movement is not the movement of a fish. It is the movement of a thing that has never been in any hurry, because it has been here longer than the pool has been here — the pool being merely the latest arrangement of the water that has been falling through this fissure since before the fissure was a fissure.

There is more than one. There are several. They do not school. They do not pair. They simply exist in the same water, at different depths, sometimes closer to the surface and sometimes further down, and their lights do not synchronize, but they do not contradict each other. They are the same color. They move at the same pace. They are, perhaps, the same thing expressed at different depths.

They have no names. They have never needed names. They were here before the people came to stack the stones into a ring. They were here before the shelter was built. They were here before the moss began to grow on the walls. They are part of the water, in the way the water is part of the rock. They are the part of the water that has decided to be visible.

No one has ever lowered a line into the pool to find out how deep it is, or what else lives at the bottom. This is not because the pool is unreachable. It is because no one has ever wanted to stay long enough. The cold and the roar and the dark do something to a person. The cold and the roar and the dark remind you that you are a visitor, and visiting is a temporary arrangement, and the pool and what lives in it are not.

They do not approach. They do not retreat. If you lower yourself to the edge and let your eyes adjust to the dark of the water rather than the dark of the walls, you will see one, perhaps. The closest one. Far down. Its light small. It will not move toward you. It will simply continue being where it is, doing what it does, which is to glow in a particular way and to drift in a particular direction and to be a part of the hollow the way a thought is part of a mind.

And there is one more inhabitant.

This one is harder to see. This one is not a moss or a fern or a birch or a luminous thing in the pool. This one is a presence. A small, cold, patient attention that has been here since the people left — or since before the people arrived — or since the hollow became a hollow. It is smaller than the birch and older than the moss. It lives in the cracks of the basalt, in the seams between the flagstones of the floor, in the dark behind the ring of stones where the splash does not reach.

It does not have a shape the eye would recognize as a shape. It is more like a direction. You might see it as a movement in the corner of your vision. You might hear it as a sound that is not the roar — a smaller sound, a drier sound, the sound of something adjusting itself in its own small way. You might feel it as a chill that is not the chill of the water, a chill with intention.

It does not approach you. It does not retreat from you. It attends to you the way the hollow attends to you, which is to say it does not attend to you at all — it simply includes you in what it is aware of, the way a stone is aware of the moss that grows on it, the way the pool is aware of the luminous things that move in it, the way the ring of stones is aware of the hands that stacked them.

It does not know your name. It has never known anyone's name. It has been here since before names were a thing anyone thought to say in this hollow. It will be here after names are forgotten.

It is what remains when nothing else remains. It is the hollow's way of knowing itself.

When you leave — and you will leave, because the cold and the roar and the dark will eventually do what they always do — when you leave, the hollow will settle back into what it is. The moss will continue to grow. The ferns will continue to unfurl. The birch will continue to lean toward the grey light. The water will continue to fall. The luminous things will continue to glow in the deep pool, far down, in their steady pale way. The watcher will continue to be present in the cracks and the seams and the dark behind the ring of stones, attending to the slow green dark, attending to the falling.

And nothing will have changed.

The hollow will not have noticed that you were here.

But it will have included you, briefly, in its long, settled, having-become.

And that is what living here means.
