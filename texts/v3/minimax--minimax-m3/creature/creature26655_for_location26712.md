# What Lives Here

The bats first. Always the bats, because they are the medium, and you do not describe a medium by counting its parts.

A single bat weighs about as much as a coin. That is the body of the thing — a folded envelope of membrane, a heart that is a small furious pump, a face that is mostly ear and nose and the wet black bead of an eye that almost certainly cannot see you. Its bones are so thin that they would bend in your fingers if you were foolish enough to try. Its wings are not wings in the way a bird's wings are wings; they are hands, very long hands, with skin stretched between the fingers and down to the ankles, and the skin is veined and warm and surprisingly tough, the way a wet paper bag is surprisingly tough. A bat is, in the end, a small hot animal that has decided to live by flying. The deciding is the only remarkable thing about any one of them.

But there is no one of them here. That is the first mistake the eye makes. The eye wants one of them — a sentinel, a portrait, a single bat to study. The chamber does not contain that. The chamber contains *bats*, the way an ocean contains water, the way a forest contains trees, the way a library contains books. The plural is not a convenience. The plural is the only accurate noun.

They number in the millions. No one has counted. Counting would require the kind of disturbance the chamber does not permit — a flash, a flush, a census, anything that would make the air move wrong for an hour. So the number is described instead: a density, a coverage, a body-mass. They pack the walls in a fur that is, in places, several bats deep. A hand laid flat against a wall does not touch stone. It touches fur, and under the fur, heat — the immense, almost embarrassing heat of so many small metabolisms banked together, the way a compost heap is warm, the way a server room is warm, the way a cathedral full of candle flames would be warm. They press against each other. They squabble in the way of small social things, with bickering chirps and the boxing of folded wings. They shift. They resettle. The wall, when you look at it for long enough, breathes. That is not a metaphor. The wall moves, in slow tidal undulations, because the bats are moving, because that is what they do, even in sleep.

Their sleep is not your sleep. They sleep folded, head down, wings wrapped, eyes shut — but the brain does not turn off. Parts of it go quiet; other parts blaze. Their ears, enormous relative to the face, remain cocked even in rest, swiveled faintly with each small sound, including yours, including your heartbeat, including the slow crackling of the ice in the rock above. They are listening the way you are breathing. They do not stop. They have not stopped. The chamber has not known a moment of true silence in living memory, and probably not for very much longer than that.

The air they make is a weather. Ultrasonic calls pour from a million open mouths continuously — short chirps, longer trills, the feedback-clicks of an animal navigating by sound so fine that the eardrum is a coarse instrument to receive it. Most of this is above you. It presses on the bones of your skull and the fillings in your teeth and the small hairs inside your ear, and you do not hear it, but you feel it as a kind of pressure, a thickness, a sense of being in a room that is more than a room. The lower harmonics — the ones that spill down into the audible — form the keening, the thin endless note that you can hear. It is not loud. It is pervasive. It gets into your clothing. After an hour in the chamber you will be humming it to yourself without realizing.

Then they fly. They do not all fly at once. A section of the wall darkens and lifts and becomes a stream, and the stream threads between the turbines with a precision that no machine could achieve — between the spinning blades, under the catwalks, around the struts, through gaps that look too small to pass. They hunt. They eat. They drink, by skimming the wet stone where the condensation runs thickest. They mate, in midair, briefly, unglamorously, and the act is so brief that it is easy to miss if you do not know to look. They give birth downward, in the roost, in summer, and the pups are pink and hairless and enormous relative to the mother, and they hang in their own crèches in the warmest pockets of the chamber, and they squeak in a register that is, for a bat, almost comically high.

The pups are the reason this is a maternity roost. The chamber is warm, by the standards of mountain places, and the walls are textured, and the turbines add a steady low vibration that may help the pups orient, and the moths — the moths are the second thing, and the moths are the reason the mothers can stay.

The moths come for the sodium light. The orange lamps attract them the way any flame attracts a moth, in the ancient and possibly fatal logic that has governed moths for as long as there have been lamps. They cluster on the fixtures. They flutter around the bulbs. They fall, stunned, to the catwalks and the floor, where the bats scoop them from the steel in a low pass, mouth open, tail membrane curled forward like a net. The moths are small, drab, easily overlooked. They are also the entire reason the bats can afford to be here in such numbers. Without the moths there would be no fuel. Without the fuel there would be no colony. Without the colony there would be no roost. The moths are the agricultural base of the chamber's economy, and the lamps are the field, and the lamps are lit for the turbines, and so the turbines, indirectly, farm the bats, and the bats farm the moths, and the lamps do not know any of this and do not care.

There is a third thing. Smaller. A mouse, perhaps, or a vole — something that has come in from the intake, through a gap in the grate that the engineers have not yet noticed or have not yet cared to seal. It lives on what falls. Guano, mostly, and the leavings of the moths, and the occasional dropped pup. It is, in the cold arithmetic of mountain animals, doing well. It is also, in the cold arithmetic of the chamber, a curiosity — a single visitor, a tourist that stayed, a thing that has decided to live in a place that was not built for it, in a tradition that the bats, on a much larger scale, have already established. It runs along the catwalks at night. It does not look up.

The lichen is the only thing here that does not move on its own. It is pale, the color of old bone, and it grows in the cracks the bats avoid — too exposed, too smooth, too recently wet for a bat to grip. It is not properly a plant. It is a negotiation between a fungus and an alga, and it persists the way lichens persist: slowly, indifferently, on the strength of nothing more than patience and the ability to wait out everything else. In a chamber where everything else is in motion — the bats, the moths, the water, the turbines, the mouse, the condensation running down the walls — the lichen is the still point. It is the part of the chamber that is not breathing. It is also, in its way, the oldest living thing here. The bats are newcomers. The turbines are newcomers. The lichen was probably here before the blasting, growing in some crack in the original rock face, and when the chamber was carved out around it, it simply continued. It does not notice the machines. It will not notice if the machines stop.

And the machines.

It is a stretch, but not a large one, to call the turbines alive. They have a continuous metabolism — water in, electricity out, heat as a byproduct, the same equation as a muscle, with the same inefficiencies and the same steady hum. They have a preferred state, which is their operating speed, and they defend it, in the sense that any deviation costs them. They have a kind of awareness, in that they can be heard to change tone when the load on the grid changes, when more or fewer stations elsewhere draw on the system. They do not sleep. They do not feed (in the bat sense) but they do require their water, and when the water is taken from them they will spin down and stay down, and this is the closest the chamber comes to a death. They are not alive. But they are not not-alive in the way a rock is not-alive. They are in the middle category — the place where engineered things and growing things share a border, where something made can have a metabolism without having a soul, where the question *does it live* becomes less interesting than the question *what is it doing in this room*.

The chamber, taken as a whole, is the only place where the question of what lives here has a satisfying answer. Everything does. The bats live here. The moths live here. The mouse lives here. The lichen lives here in a different tense. The turbines run here. The water cycles here. The lamps burn here, in their long sodium vigil, lighting a place for the moths so the moths can feed the bats so the bats can roost in the machines that drew the chamber into the mountain in the first place, in a closed loop that has no waste and no exit and no commentary, and that has been running for longer than anyone has thought to ask whether it should.

A drop falls from the ceiling and lands on the rail beside your hand. It is the size of a coin and the temperature of the stone. The bats shift overhead. A moth spirals down past the lamp. The mouse, somewhere in the dark, runs. The lichen holds its ground. The turbine turns.

The chamber breathes.
