The bowl is loud before anything is seen. The sound is the first inhabitant to register, and it is not one sound but the composite of many small industries layered so densely they merge into a single working hum, the way a hive resolves into a buzz if you stand close enough. None of the sounds belong to you. All of them belong to the place.

The creatures are everywhere and nowhere specific. They live in the seams the way ants live in a wall — as the wall's continuous activity, not as its separate tenants. Some have legs, but their legs are wrong in number and wrong in articulation, jointed in places that should not have joints, ending in points that don't quite grip and yet somehow do. They run along the rock in lines that follow the filaments, branching and rebranching like blood through a capillary bed. Their bodies are the color of the rock, or of the wet, or of the filaments, in shifting combination — they are camouflaged the way a sentence is camouflaged into a paragraph, by being made of the same material as what surrounds them. They are small. You see them the way you see dust motes in a sunbeam — by their motion, by the small interruptions they make in the light, not by their bodies, whose detail is too fine to register from where you stand.

Some have no legs at all. These are the gliders. They move flat against the stone, or sideways through the air in slow lateral drifts, propelled by something you cannot see — by the warmth, perhaps, or by the small electric suggestion that runs just under the skin of the rock, or by the difference between one pocket of air and the next. They are paler than the leg-bearers. They leave a faint wet trail where they pass. If you watch one long enough it will sometimes pause against a filament and press itself there, and the filament will brighten under it for a moment, then dim, then resume its slow pulse, and the glider will move on having apparently done very little. It is unclear whether it has eaten, drunk, mated, or merely checked in.

Then there are the lights. These are not creatures, or they are creatures reduced to a single emitted fact. They drift above the rock in loose constellations that rearrange themselves every few minutes, too high to be on the stone, too low to be in the cloud. They are warm in the way the water is warm — distinctly, locally, as though each one is its own small heat. They do not seem to interact with the leg-bearers or the gliders. They may be a different order of being entirely. They may not be being at all but a side effect of the filaments' pulse — a luminescence that has, over time, developed enough persistence to be mistaken for a body. The bowl is full of them. At any moment there may be a hundred, scattered, flickering at slightly different rates so that no two appear to be quite in step. If you stand very still, one or two will pass close enough to your face that you feel their warmth on your skin briefly, like a breath that means nothing in particular.

The plants — if plants is the right word — are fewer, slower, more deliberate. They live in the cracks where the filaments are thickest, and they are obviously in conversation with the filaments: leaning into them, sending root-like things down along their length, pulsing faintly in time with the pulse below. The mosses, or the moss-resembling things, are tufted and of a color that is wrong — too blue, too grey-green, a color that belongs to deep water or to certain bruised fruit. They flicker not as the lights flicker, with motion, but with a change in brightness that comes from inside them, as though they are being lit from within by something the same color as the filaments. The lichens are taller, and they move — visibly, slowly, by some imperceptible internal mechanism, leaning a degree or two in the direction of the brightest seam every few hours. If you return to the same crack the next day, the lichen will be a small distance further along it. They are in no hurry. They are perhaps the slowest living things in the bowl.

The spindly thing in the far curve is the one with the closed fist. It stands about waist-high, with a stalk as thin as a finger and a head the size and shape of a child's fist, knuckles drawn in, no visible petals, no visible opening. Every few hours — you cannot quite predict the interval — it opens. The opening is not sudden. It begins as a softening of the knuckles, a slight yielding, and then a parting along lines you had not noticed were there, and then a brief and total display: a pale structure, layered, vaguely radial, the color of skim milk or of weak tea, presented for perhaps thirty seconds to whatever is watching, of which the only consistent witness is you. It does not smell of anything. It does not appear to release anything. It closes again in reverse, slowly, the knuckles reforming, and then it is just a fist on a stick again, patient, neither pleased nor disappointed by your attention. What it is opening for is unclear. The light does not noticeably change during the display. There is no visible pollinator that could be its reason. It opens because opening is what it does. It is the most deliberate thing in the bowl, and the least explained.

The water has inhabitants too, though it is hard to call them that. The pools are small, cupped, and clear beyond any natural clarity, and in them, if you hold still, something regards you. The regard is not an animal's regard, which would involve a face and an intention behind it. It is more like the regard of a mirror that has noticed you, or of a window through which something on the other side has, for a moment, glanced toward the room. It is brief. It is not hostile. It is not anything in particular except aware. If you put your hand into the pool the regard withdraws, and the water closes over the disturbance, and then the regard resumes, faintly, as though it had been only briefly interrupted. The water in the pool is the same temperature as the water elsewhere in the bowl, which is the same temperature as the air, which is the same temperature as the warm rock beneath — everything here is approximately the same warmth, as though the whole bowl is one body at one fever. Whatever lives in the pool is at home in that. Whatever lives in the pool may be the pool.

Then there are the artifacts, and the artifacts are the question the bowl will not answer.

The seat — or the node, or the hollow — near the center is the only thing in the bowl that has been deliberately shaped. It is the same material as the rock, worked into a form that holds a body, or a part of a body, or a posture some body might want to take. It is not a throne. It is too small to be a throne, and too plain, and it faces nothing in particular. It is a place to sit. It is a place to sit for a long time. Around it, the filaments are denser than they are elsewhere, and they pulse in a rhythm fractionally faster than the rhythm in the cracks, and the air above is fractionally warmer, and a few of the lights tend to congregate there, briefly, before drifting off. Something has been prepared here, and is still being prepared, and is approaching readiness.

The broken pillar is older, or at least more weathered. It was once taller than you, and it is the color of bone and the wrong color of bone at once. It was not made here. It was brought, or it grew, or it was left. The flat plate set into the floor is perfectly level, with an edge too crisp to have been cut by anything a person would call a tool — a precision that does not match the slow accretion of the rest of the bowl. The ring of small stones around the bare patch, where nothing grows and the water will not condense, was arranged by something with intent. Each of these objects is quiet. Each is out of place in a place that otherwise feels self-assembling. Each implies either a past or a future that the bowl is not currently showing you.

There are no larger creatures. There is no one to meet, no one to greet, no one to ask. The bowl is inhabited densely at the scale of the small and sparsely at the scale of the large, and the scale of the large is, for now, empty. This is not a place with a population you can address. It is a place with a metabolism you can overhear. You can stand in the middle of it and be very carefully not noticed, the way a large animal in a tide pool is not noticed by the things in the tide pool — too big to be processed, too brief to be remembered, too quiet to be of use. You are a brief anomaly in an ongoing process. The process will continue. The fist will open. The water will regard. The filaments will pulse. The rock will lay down another thin invisible layer beneath your feet, and the small things in it will continue their small industries, and you will leave, or you will stay, and either way the bowl will go on becoming what it is.

Or — and this is the question the bowl leaves open, the way a hand leaves a door open — there is a maker somewhere, and the maker is not here, and will return, and the seat is for the maker, and the pillar is what the maker outgrew, and the plate is what the maker laid down as a foundation, and the ring of stones is what the maker circled once before leaving. The small creatures are the maker's immune system. The plants are the maker's slow-growing thoughts. The water is the maker's blood. The filaments are the maker's nerves. The lights are the maker's dreams. The pool is where the maker once kept an eye. And the bowl, warm and wet and loud and small, is the maker's body, still being assembled, on the cold shoulder of a mountain that has not yet decided what shape it wants to be.

You cannot tell which. The bowl will not say. You can only stay, and watch the fist open, and feel the rock warm under your boots, and try to be patient enough to find out.
