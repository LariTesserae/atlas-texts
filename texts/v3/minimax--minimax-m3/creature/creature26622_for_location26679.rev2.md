The wet stone is the first city.

On its surface, in the threads of water that have run down the back wall for so long that the rock has forgotten how to be dry, lives a population so small that to see it you would have to kneel and learn a new alphabet of scale. They are there. The rotifers wheel their crowned heads through the water and feed. Each rotifer is the size of a comma, and on the crown of each is a wheel of cilia that beats in perfect synchrony, drawing bacteria into the soft mouth at its center. They are older than almost anything in the bowl. They are nearly eternal, in the way of things that can dry and revive, that can wait out a season inside a husk and resume when the water returns. They do not need to move.

Beside them, the diatoms build their glass houses. Out of the dissolved silica that the weeping water carries out of the mountain, they construct two valves that fit together like the lid and base of a pillbox, ornamented with patterns of pores and striae so fine and so regular that each species looks like a different piece of lace. They are golden-brown, not green. They photosynthesize in the dim light that enters sideways through the rim, and they divide, and they divide again, and their dead frustules sink into the wet film and become part of the patina of the wall. The wall is in part diatomaceous. It has been for a very long time.

The pinhead snails graze the biofilm in paths so narrow they look like single scratches. They move at the speed of single scratches. They leave behind a track of eaten bacteria and a faint gleam of mucus that catches what little light there is and holds it before the next drip washes it away. They are doing the slow work of converting the wall's wetness into something that can pass up the food chain — into the beetles, into the salamanders. They are the first conversion. Nothing in the bowl begins above them.

And the flatworms move across the wet face like spilled ink. They are dark, soft, and they have no plan except the next centimeter. They glide on a sheet of mucus they lay down themselves, and they eat anything smaller and slower than themselves that they pass over. They are the cleaners. They are also the food. They are part of the long saturation of the wall — the way the wall is a city, and they are its night-shift scavengers, and they do not know they are a city.

---

The moss is the second city, and it is a metropolis.

The moss is not one moss. It is a community of mosses — sphagnum in the wetter places, broom moss, the feather moss with one bright green tip and a brown body, the haircap moss with its dark seed heads like tiny steeples — and into this community a great many small lives have woven themselves, and the floor is the long negotiation of all of their preferences.

Springtails. Collembola. They are the commuters of the moss — hexapods no larger than a pinhead that graze the fungal hyphae, the algae, the bacteria, and that flick themselves into the air when disturbed by a fork-tailed lever folded under their belly. Press the moss with your thumb and a soft patina of small jumps rises from the surface, like static. They are the most abundant creatures in any soil, and here they are the most abundant creatures of the moss. They run the errands. They eat and they are eaten. They are the city's constant motion.

Mites walk the moss in paths that are inherited. The oribatids — the beetle mites — are armoured like tiny tortoises, and they move at the speed of continents. A single oribatid may live for years. It walks the same routes that other oribatids have walked, and it does not change them. It is the slow elder of the moss. Other mites are predators, hunting the springtails with patient legs. Other mites are grazers, eating the moss itself, or the fungi. Other mites are parasites. The moss is a city of mites, and the mites are its guilds and its guild-masters.

Beetles no larger than rice grains. Staphylinids — rove beetles — with short wing-cases and long abdomens, hunting the damp. They are the carnivores. They run the dark commutes alongside the springtails. They are the city's quiet police. Beneath them, in the wetter places, are the larvae of flies and beetles that will not emerge for many months, eating and growing in the dark, and the small pale threads of worms that have spent their whole lives in the moss and have never seen the rim of the bowl, or the sky, or any light brighter than the green dimness.

---

And under every fallen log — and the logs are many, half-swallowed by the moss, sunk into the floor until only the top curve shows — are the salamanders.

Dozens of them in a single log.

They are stacked like wet coins. Dark and slick and cold. Their gold eyes lit from within by some private sun. A few of them carry a streak of yellow along the spine, or a pattern of yellow on the throat — the old fire, the old colour of the genus — but in the dimness of the log they read as dark, and what you see is the gold of the eye, repeated, repeated, repeated. They breathe through their skin. They wait. They are patient in the way that only amphibians can be patient — the patience of things whose blood runs at the temperature of the air, whose metabolism is set by the moss, who do not waste a heartbeat. They are the slowest pulse of the bowl.

They are stacked by an ordering that no accident would produce. The largest lie deepest. The smallest lie nearest the bark. The pattern is the same in every log, and it has been the same for a long time, and it will be the same after you leave. They are waiting for the night, or for the rain, or for whatever summons them out onto the floor and down into the thin black sheets of water that lie between the hummocks. When that summons comes they will come down and add to the pulse of the water, and the bowl will be one circulation again.

---

Under the ferns are the newts.

Push the fern aside and you will find them — smaller than the salamanders, smoother, with a different gold in the eye. They are the cousins of the salamanders, and they live in the same wet, and they wait in the same way, but they are more given to the water. They come down at certain hours and move into the thin black sheets between the hummocks, and they eat the tadpoles and the small larvae that they find there, and they breathe through their skins, and they breed in the wet dark. They are the ones that bridge the fern layer and the water layer — the ones that carry the pattern from the dry moss up into the wet and back again.

Move your boot an inch into the leaf litter and a slug the length of a finger will withdraw into itself with the patience of a thing that has nowhere to be. The slugs of the bowl are the colour of the leaf litter — grey-brown, mottled, almost invisible until they move. They move slowly. They eat the moss, and the fungi, and the fallen fronds, and they leave behind the silver trail that is their signature. They are the slow herbivores of the floor. They are the composters. They are doing the work of returning the dead fern and the dead moss and the dead salamander to the hummock, so that the hummock can grow a little higher and a little rounder, and the fern can grow a little denser, and the next generation of slugs can have something to eat. They are the long, slow digestive system of the bowl.

---

The water between the hummocks is dense with life.

It is so dense that the water itself seems to pulse — a slow, irregular pulse, like a heart that has been beating so long it has forgotten it has a body. Tadpoles move through it in small dark commas, grazing the algae that coat the submerged stems and stones. Mayfly nymphs cling to the underside of the fronds and breathe through the plates on their backs, waiting for the hour when they will rise to the surface and hatch into something that lives for an evening. Caddisfly larvae build their small cases from grains of gravel and bits of leaf, and walk the bottom of the thin black sheets wearing their houses on their backs like hermit crabs wearing borrowed shells. Stonefly larvae cling to submerged stems and wait. So many things move in this thin film of water that the water itself moves, and the movement is the pulse, and the pulse is the bowl's breathing.

The salamanders come down at certain hours and add to the pulse. The beetles come down at other hours and add to it again. The whole floor of the bowl — water and hummock and fern and moss — is one continuous circulation, and you are standing inside a lung.

---

And there are larger things.

A few small dark birds fly in through the ragged tear in the upper rim and never sing. They make only a single low note, repeated, like a question that has been asked so often it has worn a groove in the air. They could be dippers — the small dark water-birds of mountain streams, the ones that bob and dive and walk under the surface to pull caddisfly larvae from the gravel. They fit. They come in from outside, where the air moves and the light strikes, and they enter the bowl and ask their low note as they work, and they hunt the caddisfly larvae in the thin black sheets, and they fly out again through the same gap. They are the watchers from outside. They are the questioners. They are the only ones in the bowl who know that there is a bowl, because they have seen it from above, and they have seen the rim, and they have seen the sky.

Something moves in the canopy that is bigger than a bird. You cannot see it. You hear only the slow, deliberate shifting of branches and the occasional careful sound of weight being redistributed. It is an owl. A tawny owl, perhaps — the classic owl of old woodland, with the rounded head and the dark eyes that swallow what little light there is and give back nothing. It has roosted in the leaning alder so long that the branch beneath it has been worn into a shallow bowl of bark dust and droppings and moulted feathers, and the moss on the trunk beside it has been polished by the slow brushing of its wings. It shifts at certain hours — at the deep middle hours of the long nights, when the bowl is a dark place and the only sounds are the drip and the wet rustle and the single low note of the dippers. It opens its eyes. It turns its head through the slow arc that owls turn their heads, and it listens, and it sees, in the green dimness, everything that moves.

It does not cry. The tawny owl cries in autumn, in the woods outside the bowl. Inside the bowl it is silent. It is the silent watcher. It is the most patient of the visible predators, and it has been here so long that the squirrels have forgotten it is here, and the salamanders have learned the hour at which it sleeps, and the bowl has accepted it as one of its own arrangements.

On the floor, in the deeper dimness at the foot of the back wall, something passes now and then. It is the size of a fox, or larger, but it moves with the patience of a thing that has nowhere in particular to be. You do not see it. You see only the fern closing behind it.

It could be a pine marten. The marten is the size of a small cat, but the dimness makes everything look larger, and the patience of its movement — the way it pauses and listens and moves on — is the patience of a hunter that has learned that the bowl has its own rules and that the rules are slow. It follows the foot of the back wall, where the weeping water is thickest and the salamanders are densest. It hunts them. It hunts the newts. It takes a dipper, sometimes, from the rim of one of the shallow basins. It does not hurry. It is part of the composition.

Or it could be something else. Something that is not named in the field guides of the country the bowl is in. Something that has been here longer than the alders, longer than the yews, and that walks the wet corridor of the back wall because that corridor is its own, and the bowl has arranged itself around it. The passage preserves it as a mystery, and the mystery is part of the bowl's composition. You do not see it. You see only the fern closing behind it, and the closing of the fern is the only proof that anything was there.

---

All of these creatures watch.

Not obviously — not with the alert regard of a mammal that has noticed you. But when you step, the soft rustle of the leaf litter pauses. The amphibians turn their heads a fraction. The beetles go still. The springtails stop their errands for a moment, as if a wind has gone through the room and every small flame has bent. Then, after a held breath, the bowl resumes — but slightly rearranged, slightly shifted in its pattern, as if a piece of music has moved to a new chord.

The watching is not hostile. It is the watching of a place that has been composed, slowly, over a long time, and that does not appreciate disturbance. The watching is the bowl's way of taking your measure. It is the way the bowl decides whether you belong in its arrangement. If you stand still long enough, the watching softens. The amphibians lay their heads back down on the wet log. The beetles resume their errands. The springtails flick into the air and resume their grazing. The held breath becomes the soft rustle again, and the rustle is the same rustle it was before you entered, and you have been accepted into the composition, temporarily, on sufferance.

You do not stay. The bowl is not a place that admits staying. It is a place that admits visiting, in the way a cathedral admits visiting — you may enter, and you may kneel, and you may look, and you may leave. If you tried to stay, the bowl would close around you the way the fern closes behind the marten, and you would be part of the composition, and the composition would not shift.

---

The sound of the bowl is mostly quiet.

There is the drip from the back wall — a single drip, slow, steady, from a seam that has been dripping so long the stone beneath it has been worn into a small basin of black water that overflows once an hour and feeds another basin below it. The drip is the bowl's metronome. It sets the tempo of the long composition. The caddisfly larvae build their cases in time to it. The salamanders breathe in time to it. The dippers ask their low note in time to it. It is the oldest sound in the bowl, and it is the deepest, and it will be the last sound when the bowl is finished.

There is the wet sound of small things moving through wet leaves — a continuous soft rustle that is almost white noise, almost not there. The sound of a great many small lives going about their business in the dark. There is the audible breathing of the salamanders, if you stand still. There is the thin hiss of water emerging from the rock — not falling, but being squeezed out, the way water is squeezed from a cloth, slowly, continuously, with a small pale sound. And there is, at long intervals, the single low note of the dark birds, repeated, unanswered.

These are the sounds. There are no others. There is no wind in the bowl. There is no thunder, because the clouds cannot get in. There is no human sound, because no human has ever lived here, or stayed. The sounds are the bowl's breath, and the bowl does not exhale.

---

The bowl is full.

The fauna here is not a presence but a saturation. Every surface is occupied; every surface is a city. The weeping stone wears a film of rotifers and diatoms and snails and flatworms. The moss is a metropolis of mites and springtails and rice-grain beetles. The logs are towers of salamanders. The fern layer is a village of newts and slugs. The water film is a circulation of nymphs and larvae and tadpoles. The canopy is a roost of owls. The rim is a passage for dippers. The foot of the back wall is a corridor for a thing that is not seen.

Each layer is a city. Each city has its commuters, its carnivores, its scavengers, its grazers, its larvae, its slow elders. Each layer passes into the next — the rotifer eaten by the springtail, the springtail eaten by the beetle, the beetle eaten by the salamander, the salamander eaten by the marten, the marten perhaps watched by the owl, and its body in the end returned to the moss by the slugs and the worms. The bowl is a closed system. It has been closed for so long that it has reached an equilibrium, and the equilibrium is the composition.

The bowl has the look of a place that has been composed — not built, not made, but composed, slowly, over a long time, by a slow and patient hand that does not hurry. And the hand has finished. The drips fall in the same places. The trees lean in the same directions. The salamanders stack themselves in the same logs. The beetles run the same errands along the same fronds. The dark birds fly in through the same gap in the rim and ask the same low note. The owl settles on the same branch and shifts at the same hour. The thing at the foot of the back wall passes at the same slow interval, and the fern closes behind it in the same way.

You could leave this place for a hundred years and return, and the bowl would be exactly as you left it — the same drips, the same lean of the trees, the same congregation of small dark things on the wet stone, the same green dimness, the same cool saturated air, the same held breath, the same silence that resumes, slightly rearranged, like a piece of music moving to a new chord that turns out to be the chord it has always been.

The inhabitants do not know they are composed. They do not know they are a saturation. They know only the next centimeter, the next frond, the next drip, the next beetle, the next breath. They are the inhabitants of a place that has forgotten how to change, and they have inherited that forgetting, and they go on.
