The column does not bloom in the way a flower blooms. It opens.

It begins with a sound like a held breath finally released. The hum, which had been climbing steadily for the better part of an hour — threading itself into a pitch you could feel in the hinge of your jaw, in the soles of your feet — drops away. In its place comes a different note. It is a single, sustained tone, deeper than the hum, and it does not come from the column at all. It comes from the ground. It comes from the long pale roots you have been watching rise, slow and deliberate, out of the cracked gravel. The roots are as thick around as your thigh, and they are pale as peeled willow, and they have been lying just beneath the surface of the Reach for as long as this column has stood — which is to say, longer than any herder can name, longer than the frond-crowned memory of the plateau itself. Now they are moving.

The roots uncurl like fingers opening from a fist. They lift and spread and settle onto the gravel with a wet heaviness, and where they touch the bare plain, the gravel darkens too, as if a great soft rain has fallen on it. The air above the ground shimmers. The smell — the sweet, rotten, almost edible smell — thickens until it is a taste in the back of your throat. The fronds above you, 250 feet up, have stopped their slow rocking. They are rigid. They are pointing outward, away from the column's central axis, like the spokes of a wheel that has just been spun and now hangs, motionless, at the top of its arc. The crown of the column is a vast pale disk against the white sky, and from underneath it — from the hollow center, where the fronds all curve away from a dark opening — something is beginning to fall.

The first of it is pollen. No — not pollen. Spores, maybe. Or scales. They are large, each one the size of your thumbnail, and they drift down in a slow, dense, silent curtain, papery and pale gold, turning as they fall, catching the white sky-light. They settle on your shoulders, on your upturned face, on the shoulders of the herder beside you. They do not stick. They are dry and very light, and they smell of honey and hot iron. The herder does not brush them off. The herder is singing, eyes closed, mouth moving in a way that is not quite singing and not quite speaking. You watch her lips. The shape of what she is saying is unfamiliar. It is not the word for column, not the word for hum. It is a longer word, with many vowels, and she repeats it three times in a row before opening her eyes and looking up.

The falling has thickened. The papery scales are coming down faster now, in drifts, and beneath them something larger is descending in long pale ribbons — strips of tissue, pale grey-green, that uncoil out of the dark opening in the crown and drop slowly, turning, toward the plain. They are perhaps a foot wide. They are as long as the column is tall. They fall with the unhurried grace of something that knows exactly where it is going. Where they touch the ground, they coil gently around themselves and go still.

The herders move now. They have been waiting for this. They move with a quick, certain, joyful efficiency, the way harvesters move in a long-anticipated field. They unshoulder the hooked poles. They spread wide, shallow baskets of frond-strip weave on the gravel. They reach up, where the ribbons are still descending, and catch them — looping them over the poles, guiding them into the baskets without tearing, without hurry. The ribbons are heavy with a clear, faintly amber fluid that beads along their edges and drips. The herders let it drip. The herders let it drip into their open mouths. Their eyes are half-closed. Some of them are laughing again, the same hoarse inarticulate laugh you heard before.

A ribbon falls near you. It lands across your forearm before you can step back. It is cool, and faintly damp, and the surface is textured like the inside of a shell. The fluid that beads along its edge smells of the honey-iron smell, but also of something else — something mineral, something clean. You do not drink it. You do not. You stand very still and let the herders work around you, and the column hums its new note beneath your feet, and the great pale crown above you continues its slow, silent, enormous flowering into the white sky.

When it is over — and it takes hours, and the light changes twice while you stand there — the plain around the column is carpeted in coiled ribbons, a pale writhing mat that stretches out for fifty feet in every direction before thinning to scattered wisps. The fronds above you have folded back, slowly, into their wilting-flower droop. The hum is returning, lower now, satisfied. The roots are sinking back into the gravel, pulling the moisture down with them, sealing the dark garden at the column's base. The herders are binding the gathered ribbons into long bundles. They are lashing them to their poles. They are singing the long vowel-laden word over and over, to the column, to each other, to the white sky.

One of them — an older woman, grey-haired, her hands dark with the amber fluid — crosses to you. She has not spoken to you before. You are unsure, even now, how much of your language she has. She stops in front of you. She is shorter than you by a full head. She looks up. She does not smile. She takes your wrist — her grip is dry and very strong — and turns your hand palm-up, and looks at the place where the ribbon rested against your arm. There is a mark there, faint, a slight reddening, like the ghost of a burn. She looks at it for a long time.

Then she speaks. The word she says is one you have heard before. It is the word for *column*. She says it differently now. She says it the way you might say *kin*.

She lets go of your wrist. She turns away. She goes back to the bundles. The mark on your arm fades over the next hour, but it does not entirely fade, and in the years after, when you are far from the Reach and have begun to doubt that it was real, you will sometimes catch the light on the inside of your forearm and see it there, faint and specific, the ghost of a contact you did not choose and have not, since, been able to refuse.
