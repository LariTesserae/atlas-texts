What lives here has no legs, no wings, no voice that would carry over the water. What lives here has learned to do without all of these, and in doing so has become something that does not require the categories we usually bring to the word *living*.

Begin with the trees, because they are the most improbable thing.

The krummholz come down from above. A seed — a spruce, a mountain hemlock, something with a name and a taxonomy that means nothing to it — is blown into the slot by a winter wind, or carried in on the leg of a bird that did not stay. It falls into a crack. The crack holds a smear of dust, a fragment of decomposed moss, the barest possible address. The seed germinates. The seedling does not grow up, because up is a wall of spray and a fall of three hundred feet, and so it grows sideways. It grows into the stone. It sends a root down a fracture that goes nowhere a root should go, and it pins itself, with a kind of blind tenacity, to the substrate that has accepted it. The trunk thickens to the thickness of a man's wrist and stops. The branches go out laterally, flattened against the wall, twisted into shapes that look like the gestures of someone trying to climb a surface that won't hold them. The bark is dark with perpetual damp. The needles are black-green, denser than they would be in open air, as if the tree has concentrated its pigment to catch the gray light that reaches its leaves. The needles are always wet. They bead with spray. When the wind moves through the slot — and the wind does move, drawn down by the column of falling water, by the chimney effect of the heat differential between the cold rock and the warmer air above — the branches shudder and release a fine rain of their own, a slow drip that joins the greater fall.

A krummholz in the slot may live for a very long time. None of them are old in the way that trees in valleys are old; the slot is too violent for that. But some have been here for decades, and you can read the decades in their forms. The lowest one, the one whose branches reach the furthest across the gap, has the trunk of something that has survived many winters of ice in its cracks and many summers of flood in its roots. Its bark is bearded with moss. Its underside, where the spray hits least, is colonized by a small hanging garden of ferns that have rooted in the junction of branch and trunk. It is, in a real sense, a host. It is not doing this for any reason; it is simply the only stable surface in range, and the spores of the ferns know no better than to land on it.

The ferns.

These are the citizens of the slot in the largest number, and they are the most visible of the inhabitants, if visibility is the right word for creatures that live in a greenish half-light. They are not the ferns of an open meadow. They are not even the ferns of a shaded forest floor. They are the ferns of the slot, which is its own category. Their fronds are darker than fern fronds have any right to be — the green of deep water, the green of pondweed, the green of something that has rearranged its chlorophyll to harvest photons the way a miser harvests coins. The pinnae are smaller than the species would produce in better light. The whole plant is more compact, more pressed against the substrate, as if it is trying to be a shadow of itself. The sori on the underside of the fronds — the spore-bearing structures, the reproductive apparatus — are crowded, dense, dark brown. They produce enormous numbers of spores by the standards of their relatives in brighter places. They are betting against the dark. They are throwing spores into the air in the hope that one of them will land in a crack that another fern has already begun to colonize, and so the colony will persist.

The ferns root in the thinnest possible soils. A fern in the slot may be rooted in a film of organic matter no thicker than a sheet of paper, accumulated over decades from the decay of older ferns, glued to the stone by the calcium the spray deposits. The film is fragile. A pebble falling from above, ricocheting off the wall, can shear a fern from its hold, and the fern goes down with the pebble into the foam at the bottom, where it joins the mulch of previous generations. The ferns on the wall are, in this sense, temporary. They are all temporary. Each one is held in place by a chain of predecessors whose bodies have become the substrate for it, and each one will, eventually, become the substrate for another.

Below the ferns, in the scale of size and in the wetness of the place, are the mosses.

The mosses are the true old residents. They were here before any fern took hold. They were here before any tree seed fell. They are the first colonizers of bare stone in any wet, cold, dim place, and they have been colonizing this stone for as long as the slot has been a slot. They grow in the form the prompt described — in beards, in fur, in sheets — but the descriptions do not capture the variety. The mosses on the vertical walls of the slot grow downward, in curtains that hang from the lip and trail in the rising spray. They are the color of wet slate, dark olive, almost black. They hold water the way a sponge holds water: ten, twenty times their dry weight. When you brush them, your hand comes back soaked. They are so saturated that they are, in a real sense, more water than plant.

The mosses on the horizontal shelves are different. They form cushions, low and tight, that hold their shape even when you step on them, that spring back. They are the mosses of bogs, of cold wet places, of the kind of habitat where a moss can be a forest floor all by itself. They are dark too, but a different dark, the dark of something that has been compressed by time and weather into a dense felt. Small invertebrates live in these cushions — not insects, not the winged things the prompt says cannot abide here, but the smaller, slower things, the mites and springtails and tardigrades, the creatures of the order of a millimeter. They are present. They are numerous. They are not what one would call visible, but they are there, browsing on the moss, on the algae, on each other, in a community that has its own scale and its own weather. They are the fourth party that has not yet arrived in any larger form.

Below the mosses, in the scale, are the lichens.

The lichens are the foundation. They are what first colonizes a surface that nothing else can colonize. A lichen is not one organism. It is two — a fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a cyanobacterium — living in a relationship so close that the result is something neither would be alone. The fungus gives structure. The alga gives food, made from light. The lichen gives the partnership's ability to live on bare rock, to digest the surface it stands on, to be the first rung of a ladder that no one would otherwise climb. The slot is rich in lichens. The crustose lichens — the flat ones, the ones that look like a stain on the stone — paint the walls in pale grays and ochres and the mustard yellow of *Acarospora* or something like it. The foliose lichens — the leafy ones, the ones with edges that lift from the stone — cling in the cracks where the spray does not directly hit, and they are pale green and gray and the white of something almost mineral. They grow slowly. A lichen an inch across may be older than the krummholz above it. They are the slow life. They are the patient ones. They will be here when the slot is twice as deep.

Below the lichens, below the mosses, below all of it, are the films.

The films are not things you would notice. They are the green-black stain on the perpetually wet stone near the bottom of the slot, where the spray is densest and the light is least. They are communities of cyanobacteria and algae — single-celled things, colonial things, things without a name a layperson would know — that live in a film no thicker than a coat of paint. They are the color of very old bronze. They are slimy to the touch. They are the base of everything. They photosynthesize on light so dim that no vascular plant would bother with it. They are eaten by the tardigrades in the mosses. They are grazed by things smaller still. They are the producers. They are what the rest of the slot is built on, in the end, the way the rest of any wet dark place is built on what makes its own food from what little light arrives.

So the slot is not empty. The slot is, in its way, populous.

But it is populated by things that do not move in the sense we mean when we ask what lives somewhere. It is populated by things that stay where they are put. It is populated by things that grow. It is populated by things that, if they reproduce, do so by sending a small piece of themselves into the air and hoping it lands somewhere that will hold it. It is populated by things whose idea of movement is the very slow expansion of a colony across a stone, or the slow drip of spores from a sorus into the spray.

The slot is, in other words, populated by things that have agreed to live in a place that does not allow the alternative.

And what they have agreed to is a kind of arrangement. The lichens prepare the stone. The mosses build soil on the lichen. The ferns take the soil and deepen it. The trees arrive in the cracks that the ferns have widened with their roots. The films underneath feed the small grazers that live in the moss. The calcium the spray leaves behind cements the rubble. The pebbles that fall from the walls tear it all down again. The water falls. The water falls. The water falls.

What lives here is what has agreed to live in the sound of falling water, in the spray that never lifts, in the dim green light that is filtered through three hundred feet of falling water and the breath of a slot that is being, very slowly, made deeper.

What lives here is patient. What lives here is wet. What lives here is, in the end, the only thing that could live here, and is.
