What lives here is not a population but a colony of aspect. If a single organism answers to the name of this place, then the rest of what walks and pulses and creaks inside it is organ and instrument. The workers are not its inhabitants the way people inhabit a city. They are its hands, in the older sense of the word — the part that reaches. There are no other kinds of people. There may once have been. There may be, somewhere in the stack below, things that could speak. If so, they have not come up.

The workers are the only moving things that share the body's surface with the visitor. A visitor, arriving here, would see perhaps two or three in an hour of walking, and would never be sure that more than one of them was the same. They are nearly indistinguishable from the wood they tend. A passing figure at the base of a pillar, six feet tall, narrow at the waist, broader at the shoulders the way a yoke is broader, with no obvious joint at the knee — the legs are a single bent column, and the feet are the slight flare at the bottom of that column, the way a stem flares where it meets the ground. They do not have toes in any number one could count. They grip the wood with the whole underside of the foot, the way a root grips soil. The face, when one catches it, is not a face. There is a slight concave where a face would be, framed by the rim of the helmet-gourd, but the features are smoothed, unfinished, the way a sculpture is left when the artist intends to come back and never does. The color of the wood beneath the wooden clothes. The texture of the wood.

They do not speak because they have no speech. The sounds they could make would not be speech. They are not silent out of choice or discipline. They are silent the way a liver is silent. The pause — the one where a worker stops in the middle of a hall and looks down — is the closest thing to speech this place has. It is not known what is communicated in that pause. It may be nothing more than a register. The heart, below, having checked a chamber. The worker, above, briefly feeling the check, briefly returning to nothing. They do not look at one another during the pause. They do not look at the visitor. They look at the floor as if the floor were transparent, which, briefly, in the negative flash of the under-light, it is.

The workers do not eat. They do not drink. The question of what sustains them is the wrong shape, like asking what sustains a cell. They are not sustained from outside. They are kept in being by the body, which is also the building, which is also the floor, which is also the road. The body extends them from itself as needed and resorbs them when they are done. A worker who stops, finally, after a long life measured in centuries — a long life to a worker is a long life to a leaf, which is to say a season, which is to say a single purpose — does not fall. The wood of the worker softens at the joints. The worker kneels, slowly, the way a tree leans. The bark-stitch cuirass loosens. The helmet-gourd splits along its original seam and falls away in two halves. Beneath, there is no skull. There is only the next layer of wood, a little paler, a little wetter, which the body of the world — the floor, the road, the wall — accepts back into itself as the floor accepts a footprint: by growing over it. In a week, the worker is invisible. The floor has thickened by an inch where the worker knelt.

The visitor, walking, will not see this happen. The visitor will not see most of what happens here, because most of what happens here does not register on a visitor's scale. The resin moves through the cane-pipes at a speed a visitor could outwalk. The great gear turns so slowly that its motion is read in the polish of the teeth rather than in any visible rotation. The loom extrudes fiber at a rate a visitor could watch for an hour and not be sure it had moved. The world is patient because the world is not in a hurry. The world is not in a hurry because the world is not going anywhere. The world is already where it is.

What else lives here. There are the small things, the ones a visitor almost never sees. A particular mite, white, blind, no bigger than a grain of pollen, which lives in the felt of the membrane-canopy and feeds on the spores that sift down from the cap-leaves of the chimney-stacks. The chimney-stacks exhale these spores as part of their respiration. The mite is fed by the respiration of the body of the world. The mite is, in some sense, a part of the body of the world, the way a follicle is a part of a skin. There are beetles, deep lacquered, with wing-cases so polished they reflect the green-gold of the resin-pipes; they live in the galleries inside the great trunks, and they keep those galleries clean. What they eat is the dust of the wood itself, the fine powder that the body sheds as it grows. Without the beetles, the galleries would clog. Without the galleries, the body could not breathe. The beetles do not know they are necessary. The body does not know the beetles exist. The arrangement persists.

There is a small lizard, or something like a lizard, that lives in the warm seam between the trunk and the road where the road has grown up around the trunk. The road is warm; the resin runs through it at body temperature, which is cooler than a mammal's and warmer than a lizard's preference, but the lizard has adapted to it. The lizard eats the beetles that wander out of the galleries. The lizard is eaten, sometimes, by a bird. The bird — there are birds — lives in the upper canopy, in the spaces between the membrane-sheets, and comes down only rarely, in the negative flashes, when the under-light rises and something stirs. The bird is the color of lichen, which is the color of the underside of a leaf, which is the color of a thing that does not want to be seen. The bird has a long thin beak that it inserts between the seam of the trunk and the road. The bird eats the lizard. The bird flies back up. The bird is not seen by the visitor. The bird is seen, once, by the visitor, in a flash, when the under-light rises and a shadow of wings crosses the floor too quickly to be a thing.

The visitor is the only warm thing here that is not made of wood. The visitor sweats, and the sweat cools on the visitor's skin. The visitor is the only thing here that the under-light makes colder. The visitor, walking, leaves no footprints, because the wood does not yield to the visitor's weight; the visitor leaves no warmth, because the cool air accepts the visitor's warmth too quickly; the visitor leaves no sound, because the visitor's footsteps are absorbed by the same creak that absorbs the footsteps of the workers, which is to say the visitor's footsteps are not footsteps at all, only disturbances in the body of the world that the body of the world does not register.

The workers, when they encounter the visitor, do not react. The visitor is not an animal to them. The visitor is not a machine. The visitor is, perhaps, a stain — a discoloration in the green-gold field of the world's attention. The worker walks around the visitor. The worker resumes. The visitor is left, briefly, standing in a corridor of olive-black pillars, alone, in the only cool bright place in the world, watching a loom weave nothing, watching a gear turn nothing, watching a pipe carry nothing, listening to a low note that does not change, which is the only note the world has ever had, which is the note the world has when it is alone with itself.

There is no other music here.

What lives here lives as a single thing lives. The variety of it — the workers, the mites, the beetles, the lizard, the bird, the resin, the amber pulse, the gear, the loom, the membrane-canopy, the floor of laminated roots, the roads, the walls, the chimney-stacks, the heart below — is not the variety of an ecosystem. It is the variety of a body. A heart is not a neighbor of a lung. A liver does not compete with a kidney. The parts do not eat one another except in the small, automatic way that parts consume one another's byproducts. The parts do not speak. The parts do not choose. The parts do not, as far as the visitor can tell, suffer.

The visitor, alone in the green-gold hall, listening to the low note, may consider whether a thing that does not suffer can be alive. The visitor may consider whether a thing that is one thing can be alive. The visitor may consider, watching the worker pause and look down through the floor that is not yet transparent, whether the worker, in that pause, suffers briefly — feels the check from below, registers the pulse, returns to nothing. The visitor cannot tell. The visitor, after a long walk, sits down on the laminated road, which accepts the visitor's weight without comment, and watches the canopy, which is the same color as the floor, which is the same color as the walls, which is the same color as the sky above the canopy, which is the same color as the sky below the floor, which is the same color as the only light there is.

The visitor waits.

Nothing comes.

Nothing was ever going to come.

The world is patient. The world is not waiting.
