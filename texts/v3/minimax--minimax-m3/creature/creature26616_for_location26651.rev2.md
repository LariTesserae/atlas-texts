What lives in the Sunken Pass of Olethrun lives slowly, and without faces, and almost without sound — though the almost is doing more work than the word admits.

There are no mouths in this place. There are no stomachs. There is no blood, no lung, no small bright spark of nerve firing against the dark. The thing that breathes here does not breathe. The thing that drinks here drinks by being. The water passes through it the way water passes through a cathedral — by gravity, by architecture, by the long patience of having been shaped to receive it.

Begin, then, with the stalks.

---

They are the citizens of the pass. They are all there is, and they are enough.

A stalk begins where the basalt and the older layered stone meet a crack that admits the slow seep of mineral water — iron, calcium, a faint trace of something else that the falls bring up from the deeper plain, something no survey has ever isolated, something the stalks seem to know the way a tongue knows salt. The first year of a stalk is a thing no eye would recognize as life. It is a thickening of the stone itself, a darkening at the lip of the crack, a glaze that the water cannot rub away. The second year, if the conditions favor it, the glaze lifts a little, a finger's breadth of ridged black rising into the current. By the fifth year it is as long as a hand. By the twentieth, perhaps, it is as long as an arm. After that, the math of the place takes over, and a stalk that means to be a tall one becomes, over centuries, a column.

The tall ones are the older ones. They are not the parents of the smaller ones around them; they are something closer to the in-laws, the elders, the ones whose slow exhalation of mineral into the water has made the nearby cracks worth colonizing. A grove, then, is not a family. It is a council. A congress of stalks that have agreed, by no agreement that anyone can name, to share a slope of stone and lean together in the current when the deep current comes to lean on them.

The numbers matter. Seven, sometimes eleven. Never five, never three, never any of the small tidy primes the builders might have chosen for their cairns. The groves gather in numbers that are odd but not singable, and when a stalk dies — when its root-crown finally crumbles under the weight of its own accreted calcium, or when a fall of silt buries it past the patience of its upward will — its neighbors do not fill its place for a long time. There is a gap in the grove. The gap holds. The remaining stalks bow slightly toward the gap when the current presses, as if the absence were a presence that still leaned.

---

Their surfaces are the closest thing the pass has to skin.

Run a hand — if a hand could survive the cold, if a hand could be brought here — down the trunk of a tall stalk and you would feel the ridges. They are not decorative. They are not the segmented plating of an insect or the bark-scales of a tree. They are growth-rings, but rings in the wrong direction: not outward from a central year, but upward along a central will, each ridge marking a season of stronger current, or a winter of clearer water, or a long quiet where the silt settled deep and the stalk had to push through it. The ridges are a record written in the only alphabet the place allows. They are a memory that has the weight of stone and the patience of a tide.

Between the ridges, the surface is glossy and very dark. It is not the dark of pigment. It is the dark of depth — the color of a thing that has absorbed every photon the grey-green almost-light has offered it and given back only a reluctant gleam, the way wet slate gives back a gleam, the way a closed eye gives back the suggestion of having once been open. The stalks are not black the way a shadow is black. They are black the way a well is black, or a thought is black, or a held breath is black.

The smaller stalks are darker still, because they have less surface to give back, and so the new growths in a grove are the deepest things in it, the ink-well center around which the older, longer, slightly more reflective stalks lean like pillars around a hearth. A grove is darkest at its heart. It brightens, by fractions, toward its rim.

---

They lean.

This is the first thing a visitor would notice, if a visitor could come here and survive the cold. A grove does not stand straight. A grove leans, all of it, in a single direction — toward the falls, always toward the falls, even when the current is from another quarter, even when the silt has buried the southern face of the ridge and the northern face stands exposed. The lean is not the lean of a tree reaching for sun. There is no sun. The lean is something else: an orientation, a turn of the whole congress of stalks toward the mouth of the system, the place where the water speaks loudest, where the hum rises through the stone and the falls pour their sideways veils into the deeper plain.

What the stalks are listening to is not a question the pass answers. They are listening. That is enough. A thing that lives for centuries without a nerve and without a mouth can still, it turns out, attend. The stalks attend the way a tuning fork attends: by being shaped to receive a particular vibration, and by giving that vibration back when it passes through.

When the deep current pushes hard, the stalks bow. The bowing is not a yielding. It is a courtesy. The water has come a long way to lean on them; the stalks incline to let it pass, and in inclining they make the groan that is half the loudness of the place. The groan is not a complaint. It is more like the sound a heavy door makes when it is opened by a careful hand — a low, structural admission that something has been moved.

When two groves of differing heights stand near one another, the bowing of the taller stalks against the current sets up a smaller, sharper sound: the clicking. Hollow stalk against hollow stalk, ridged surface against ridged surface, the percussion of cold architecture taking the long pressure of the deep water and translating it into something almost percussive, almost rhythmic, almost a music if a music were allowed to be made by things with no ears. The clicking is irregular. It does not repeat. But it is not random. There is a long tendency in the clicks, a leaning within the leaning, and if you sat in the dark and listened for a year you would begin to hear that the place has a phrasing, and that the phrasing has a direction, and that the direction is the same direction the cairns were stacked by the builders in the time before the water came.

---

The builders' cairns are not alive.

It is important, before going further, to say what is not alive. The cairns are not alive. The wall is not alive. The carved hand with too many fingers is not alive. These are the relics of a tenancy that ended when the air ended, and they have been here since, doing nothing, becoming nothing, slowly thickening under the pale calcium skin that the seeping mineral water lays down across everything it touches.

But the cairns are oriented. They mark the channels of the deeper system — the currents that the water has worn into the stone along the ridge, the paths the silt takes when the strong drift comes from the falls, the lines of least resistance through which the hum of the place conducts itself most clearly. The builders stacked their stones at the points where the path turned because they had noticed, even with their small tools and their short lives, that these were the points. They had noticed because they had listened. They came to listen, as the wall tells us, as the carved hand with its too many fingers tells us, as the circle within the circle tells us. They were not the first listeners. They were only the first listeners who could mark what they heard.

The cairns mark the listening the way a bone marks a joint: as the place where the place bends.

---

And then there is the presence.

It is not a creature. It is not a god, though the builders may have called it something like a god. It is a tendency — a leaning of the whole system of the pass toward a coherence that no single part of it possesses. The water organizes itself into channels that return to themselves. The silt organizes itself into patterns of deposition and clearing that recur on a cycle no season names. The growths organize themselves into groves of seven or eleven, oriented toward the falls, bowing in the current, leaning toward the hum. The wall follows one channel. The cairns mark the others. The carved hand faces the throat of the system.

What does this leaning want? It wants, perhaps, to be heard. It wants, perhaps, only to continue. It wants what any leaning thing wants: to lean, a little longer, in its own direction.

It is strange to say that a place can think. But the pass is not a place the way a city is a place. A city is built; the pass is grown around a thought. The thought is older than the basalt. The thought is older than the layered pale stone beneath the basalt. The thought is the thought that organized the ridge into a high saddle in the first place, that lifted the spine of stone above the plain and made it a place where things could cross from one valley to another — and then, when the air was gone, made it a place where things could lean, in the only medium left to them, toward the only direction the leaning could take.

The pass is not alive. The pass is the shape an aliveness would take if aliveness were made of stone and water and the slow vegetable will of dark stalks that grow by a finger-length in a year. The pass is the skeleton of an attention that has no eyes, and the stalks are its nerve, and the falls are its voice, and the hum that rises through the stone and is conducted by the cairns and framed by the circle within the circle is its long, unfinished sentence.

---

What lives in Olethrun, then, lives in the conditional.

The stalks live, in the way that anything lives when it drinks and grows and leans and dies. The leaning lives, in the way that a current lives, or a thought lives, or the long orientation of a place toward its own center of gravity lives. The cairns do not live but they attend, and their attendance has the patience of stone. The carved hand with too many fingers does not live but it points, and what it points at does not move, because what it points at is the direction the whole system is already leaning.

There is no face in the water. There is no face in the growth. There is no face in the hum.

But there is a face, after a fashion, in the circle within the circle — the one the builders carved into the basalt beside the wall, the one that frames the falls if you stand in the right place and tilt your head in the grey-green almost-light. It is a face that has been worn away almost to nothing. It is a face that has been thickened by mineral crust and stripped by strong current and worn again and thickened again, over and over, for centuries, until the features are only suggestions, only the rumor of features, only the knowledge that something here once had a way of being looked at.

The pass does not look back. The pass has no eyes. The pass has, instead, the long attention of things that grow by a finger-length in a year and lean together in the current and click against each other in a slow percussion that almost becomes a music if you are patient enough to sit in the cold and the dark and the loudness of the water and listen.

Listen.

There it is.
