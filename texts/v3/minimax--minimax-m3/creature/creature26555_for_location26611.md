What lives here is not a people. What lives here is a *keeping* — and the keeping does the living.

Let me try again.

The first inhabitants you notice are the lichens. They are not the obvious ones. The obvious ones are the orange and the chartreuse that climb the pale, ribbed warehouse like a rash, and these are visitors, almost — opportunistic, secondary, riding in on the backs of the birds that pass overhead. The first inhabitants are the ones lower down, the ones you only see when a slab of newer cladding falls away from an older wall and exposes the strata beneath. There is a black lichen here, so black it seems to drink the dim light rather than reflect it, that grows in the joints where two eras of masonry meet. It predates the masonry. It was on the stones before the stones were stones. When a stonemason cuts a block and uses it here, the lichen takes roughly a season to find it, traveling along the bedding of the mortar with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else to be. It does not look like a thing alive. It looks like a stain that has begun to insist.

The mosses are louder about being alive. They colonize the south faces in pelts you can peel back, and beneath each pelt the wall is darker than the wall beside it, and warmer, and faintly breathing — a small expansion and contraction, not synchronized with the breath you can hear under the pavement, but tuned to it the way two clocks in the same room eventually drift toward the same second-hand. The mosses have a politics, if you can call it that. The chartreuse encroaches on the black, and the black retreats into the joints, and the chartreuse follows, and there is a frontier — a thin, dark, contested line that creeps up the south face over the course of a year. The residents do not mention this. They have decided, collectively, not to take sides. The gardeners on the rooftops, who are a different population entirely, will tell you about it if you ask, and will tell you in a voice that suggests they would rather not.

The ferns are animals, or nearly so. They have behaviors. They have preferences. They move — not in the way plants normally move, by growing, but in the way a tide moves, by withdrawing from one face and surfacing on another. A fern that takes a north wall in the evening may not be there in the morning; a different fern, or the same one in a different argument with itself, will have climbed onto an east-facing buttress and settled there for the week. The residents dry laundry on the lines strung between buildings, and once a season the laundry fails to dry because the ferns have claimed the lines too, threading themselves through the cords with the unhurried malice of tenants who will not be evicted. The ferns do not harm the laundry. They simply hold it, the way a hand holds a wrist. The laundry comes down still damp, but smelling now of green.

The sedge is the true citizenry. The fields between the buildings — and there are fields between the buildings, wide and purple-seamed and faintly whispering even when there is no wind — are not the scene of anything so organized as a population. They are a single organism, or they are a thousand organisms pretending to be one, and the distinction is not always clear. The sedge knows when you are in it. You will step off the path onto the softer ground at the edge and feel, after three paces, that the surface under your feet has become subtly more compliant, has begun to part in front of you the way tall grass parts in front of a wading bird. You are not cutting a trail. You are being offered a corridor. The sedge closes behind you before you turn around. By the time you have walked thirty meters, the place you entered is gone, grown back, indistinguishable from the place beside it. The residents do not go into the sedge. The residents go around it, by long and unvarying routes, and they do not discuss why.

What lives in the sedge does not have a name you can use. It moves when you are not looking, and the not-looking is not a thing you choose; it is a thing the sedge arranges for you. You turn your head. The sedge is swaying where you did not step. You did not see it move. The residents speak of *the settling* — not of the buildings, which are a different settling — and they say that anyone who lingers too long in the tall growth becomes, for a few days afterward, unable to find a particular object in a particular room, and that the inability fades, and that it is not a thing to be worried about, and that they would rather not be asked about it further.

The insects live close to the walls. They hover at the level where the amber under-eave-light meets the cold-green of the drainage channels, and they are themselves faintly luminous in colors that do not occur elsewhere — a mint that leans toward cyan, a salmon that leans toward lavender, a pale gold that is not the gold of any metal you have held. They do not land on you. They will land on the walls, on the moss, on the bridges that join the upper floors of one building to the next, but never on a person. They have a hum that is higher than the hum of the city, and quieter, and sometimes at dusk you will see them rise — not fly, but rise, as if the air had decided to take them — in a slow column from one of the channels, and fall again at a different point a meter or two distant. The residents do not swat them. The dogs, who do not behave like dogs, watch the columns of rising insects with an attention that has no analogue in their ancestral species.

The birds are rare. They cross the sky at a height that cannot be estimated — not because they are very high, but because the sky here will not tell you how far away things are. They are dark. They are large. Their beaks are heavy in a way that suggests a diet you would rather not enquire into. They do not sing, but they make a sound — once, at intervals, as they pass — that the residents translate, reluctantly, as a kind of laughing. The laugh does not sound like laughter. It sounds like a small flat stone skipped across water that is not water.

The dogs are a population unto themselves, and they are the most articulate of the residents' dependents. They do not bark. They sit, often for hours, with their faces turned toward the pale ribbed warehouse, and they watch it with the attentiveness of animals who have been given a single task and intend to discharge it correctly. Their owners walk past; the dogs fall in behind, but at a distance — three meters, four — that is the distance of an animal who has agreed to be accompanied rather than accompanied you. Some of the dogs are large, and some are small, and none of them fetch, and none of them beg, and once in the evening one of them will make a sound — a low, deliberate *hf*, more breath than bark — and the other dogs within earshot will repeat it, and the residents who hear it will look down, or look away, and continue what they were doing with a slightly altered set to the shoulders. There is a dog in the central clearing. There has always been a dog in the central clearing. It is grey. It is older than some of the residents. When you pass it, it does not watch you. It watches the warehouse.

What lives *in* the buildings is the question the residents have stopped asking. There are rooms that contain only themselves. There are floors that the residents use as corridors but that have, in their walls, smaller rooms — cabinets of a kind, or alcoves, or sealed chambers — that were not on any floor plan that anyone remembers drawing. Some of these rooms are empty. Some of them contain the bridges' borings, the dark stone where the joining passages enter the parent structures; the residents have agreed, without a meeting, that these rooms are not to be opened. *Agreement* is the wrong word. The rooms have not been opened, and the not-opening is older than any resident currently living. The dogs will not enter these rooms. The insects will not hover near them. The chartreuse moss, which colonizes everything, dies back a handsbreadth from their doors.

What lives in the channels is, at intervals, visible. There is motion in the dark glass — a slow, lateral drift, a turning — and the violet pulse that runs along the surface of the channels seems to quicken slightly when whatever-it-is surfaces, as if the lighting and the lit were components of a single organism. The residents do not discuss the contents of the channels. They will, when pressed, say only that the system is *circulating*, and that circulating is what it is for, and that they would prefer not to be the one asked, and that they will not be asked again.

What lives in the air is the dimness itself. The dimness is not an absence; it is a presence that happens to take the form of reduced light. You acclimate to it, as I have said. After a week your pupils widen and stay widened. After a month you can read by the wall-light. After longer you begin to notice that the dimness is *organized* — that the amber and the cold-green and the violet are not randomly distributed but patterned, the way stars are patterned, with voids and clusters that you could, if you devoted years, almost map. The residents have not devoted years. The residents have stopped trying to map things. They have begun, instead, to *inhabit* the patterns — to walk in the amber, to pause in the green, to wait at the violet as if at a transit they have not yet decided to board.

The breath under the pavement is rhythmic. The residents do not hear it. It is there. It is slow. It is not the breath of an animal, exactly, and not the breath of a machine, exactly, and not the breath of the city-as-organism, exactly, though all of these are part of the explanation no one will give you. It is, perhaps, the breath of the young ground itself — of the sediment that is still finding itself, still compacting, still deciding what it wants to be. The ground here is alive. Not in the metaphorical sense. In the sense that you cannot quite say.

What lives here, in the end, is the *settling*. The settling of ground into itself. The settling of buildings into the ground. The settling of vegetation onto the buildings. The settling of residents into their slow, damp, lit-by-violet routines. The settling of the sky onto the bowl of the air. Everything here is mid-transaction. Everything here is mid-process. Everything here has only just begun, and will only just begin again tomorrow, and the residents have stopped distinguishing the two states, and so have the lichen, and the moss, and the sedge, and the dogs, and the things in the channels, and the things the dogs are watching the warehouse for, and the warehouse itself, which is always the size of a warehouse no matter how far away you stand, because it is not a building but a *function* the city is performing, and the function will not change size for you.

You arrive thinking you will find inhabitants.

You find that you have arrived among them, and not as a guest.
