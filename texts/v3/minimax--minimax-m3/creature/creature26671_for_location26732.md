In the dark of the Long Floor, between the pale pillars and the patient silt, there are dwellers.

---

**The Herdstones, that are also called the Long Ones**

The largest shape that moves here is not one creature but a conversation of them. A Long One is a soft architecture of pale cartilage and blubber as long as three city blocks laid end to end, with a skull the size of a chapel and a mouth that is not a mouth but a sieve. Their bodies are the color of skimmed milk, almost translucent at the edges where the phosphor light passes through them and emerges on the other side, dimmer, greener. Old ones carry colonies of small luminous organisms in the creases of their skin, so that when they pass between two trunks in the middle water they appear to have constellations on them, temporarily, like a host of stars caught in fog.

They breathe through a row of spiracles along the ridge of their backs, and the air they push out is not air but water that has been drawn through their sieve-mouths, past gill-plates the color of raw liver, and out again. This exhalation, modulated by the resonant chambers of the skull and the long ribbed throat, is the humming you hear. Each one carries a note like the lowest string of an instrument that has only one string. They sustain it for a minute. They rest. Another begins. Across a basin of a thousand kilometers, the notes trade with each other the way monks in distant monasteries trade the hours.

They are not speaking to each other. That is what one comes to understand, listening long enough. They are not coordinating, not warning, not courting. They are confirming. Each note says: *I am still here. I am still the note I was.* The answer says: *I heard you. I am still here.* It is the minimum possible communication between two large, slow, settled things, and it is the only one they need. It has gone on, in this exact pattern, with these exact intervals, for longer than anything on the surface has been measuring time.

A young one, when you are lucky enough to see one, is smaller and brighter, almost pearlescent, and it hums at a slightly higher pitch, a hair sharper, the way a violin string is a hair sharper than the same note on a cello. It has not yet settled into its adult register. It will. In a hundred years, give or take, it will find its note and hold it and never change it.

---

**The Wheel-Sparks, that are also called the Quick Commas**

Between the trunks, in the middle distance, in currents you cannot feel, there is a constant traffic of small bright things. They are the size of dinner plates, most of them, and they are wheels — actual wheels, ring-shaped bodies of clear cartilage with a spoke-pattern of denser tissue through the middle. The spokes carry the light. Eight or ten small photophores at the rim, like the lamps on a child's toy, give off a pale bluish fire that pulses, twice a second, as the wheel turns and passes the lamps through the dark water. The rotation is slow. From a distance, a wheel looks like a floating eye, blinking.

They eat the falling sediment. They eat each other, when small enough. They are eaten by the long pale ribbon-creatures that hang in the water between the trunks like scarves forgotten on a line. But mostly they travel. They wheel along the currents, alone or in twos, and when a current crosses another current they pause at the intersection and rise, slightly, and the two of them turn together for a few seconds in a brief bright pinwheel, and then separate again into their separate lanes.

They are not intelligent. They do not need to be. They are a solved problem. They have been a solved problem for a long time. When a wheel fails, it sinks gently, and its lamps go out one by one as the photophores run down, and the silt accepts it without ceremony. The other wheels wheel past above.

---

**The Slow Ones in the Silt**

On the floor, in the silt, are the burrowers. You will not see one whole, almost ever. You will see what they leave: the slow plume of disturbed sediment that hangs in the water for half a minute after they have moved, and the row of small green lamps — eight, twelve, sixteen — that appear suddenly along a low ridge of silt and then sink again. The lamps are eyes. The eyes are on stalks. The stalks retract.

A Slow One is a long segmented thing, the segments thick as pillows, each segment bearing two of the eyestalks, and below each segment a pair of stubby digging limbs that work the silt the way a gardener's fingers work a bed. They are pale, almost white, and very soft. They eat the silt. The silt is rich. It has been falling forever, and it is made of everything that has died above, ground down by time into a fine grey flour full of memory. The Slow Ones pass this flour through their long gut and what comes out is the floor itself, packed tighter, year by year, the slow masonry of the basin.

They come up to rest, sometimes. A segment at a time. Two segments, three. Then they are gone. Their emergence is the only punctuation in the field of silt. When you have watched for an hour you learn that the punctuation is regular: a body surfaces somewhere in your field of view every few minutes, like the hands of a clock that has only one hand.

---

**The Pennants, that are not animals but are also not the trees**

High above, where the trunks thin and the fronds begin, there are creatures that are not the fronds but live among them, the way insects live among the leaves of a tree, except that these insects are a meter across and have no legs. They are flat, soft, ribbon-shaped, and they cling to the underside of a frond with a slow muscular grip and feed on the small particles that collect on the frond's surface. They glow faintly, in patches, in colors that are not their own — borrowed from the frond, or from the colony organisms they carry, or from the water itself, no one knows. They hang there for years. They move only when the frond they are on dies, which happens rarely, and then they ride the dying frond down through the water like a flag at half-mast being lowered, slowly, until they reach the silt and burrow into it and wait, perhaps, for another frond to grow past.

---

**The One Who Drifts Alone, that is called the Long Singer, though it has no name**

Once in a long while — once in a decade, perhaps, perhaps less — a pale shape detaches from the herd and drifts away from the trunks and goes out, alone, into the open basin where there is no architecture and no light but its own. It carries a low sound with it. The sound is lower than the others, and slower, and it does not trade with anyone. It sings, alone, for as long as a human generation, and then it falls, slowly, and the silt takes it, and the herd, far away, has one less voice.

No one knows why it leaves. No one has asked. The herd absorbs the loss the way the herd absorbs everything, with a note held, and a silence where the note was, and the note resumed from the next voice in line. The basin is patient with its mysteries. The basin does not need to solve them.

---

**The Basin Itself, that is not a creature but is alive**

The basin is the oldest thing here. It is not a creature but it is alive in the way that weather is alive, in the way that a river is alive. It is the sum of the water and the silt and the pressure and the slow convection of currents that have been running in these paths since before the trees grew their first pale trunk. It holds them all. It does not mind them. It does not mind you.

When you stand on the floor, and the dark water moves past you, and the glow falls down from the fronds, and the deep voices call and answer, you are inside the basin and the basin is inside you, for a moment, the way a song you hear is inside you for as long as it lasts. Then you leave, or you stop, and the basin remains, holding its long chord, and the trees rise in their unchanging fluting, and the silt accepts whatever is offered, and the next note begins where the last one ended.

Nothing has changed. Nothing will.
