What lives here lives in layers, and the layers are not strata but weather.

The columns are the first inhabitants. Everything else lives in them, on them, or because of them. They are not constructed. They are not grown in any sense you would recognize. They are extruded, the way a stalagmite is extruded, but they are also warm and they are also breathing and the air that comes out of their pleats when the morning sun strikes them is humid and faintly sweet, the way the breath of a sleeper is sweet. They are alive the way a tide is alive — present, rhythmic, indifferent to scale. They are the size of buildings. They are the size of weather. The tallest of them, in the inner Reach, are said to be nine hundred feet, and no one has measured because no one has climbed that high and come back to confirm, but the herders who live in their shadow do not argue about height; they argue about *tone*, because the columns hum at different frequencies and the herders have names for them and they prefer one tone to another the way you might prefer one river to another.

The columns are not the same color twice. They are bone-pale in dry wind. They are wine-dark when the rains come in off the eastern plateau and the pleats fill with shadow. They are the color of a bruise when the bloom is near — a deep, bruised purple, the color of an overripe plum — that begins at the crown and walks down the column in a slow stain over the course of a week. The herders watch for that color. They time their lives by it. The columns are scored, too — with hundreds of small marks carved into the cork, climbing the pleats in slow spirals, that no living herder made and no living herder can read.

Inside the columns is the fauna.

To call it fauna is to use a word that has come loose from its meaning. What lives in and around the columns is a community that does not parse into species. It parses into *occupations*. There are those that climb, those that fly, those that fall, those that drink, those that hunt, those that eat the hunters, those that decompose the eaten. It is an economy of bodies, and the currency is the column itself, which sweats and feeds and shelters in turn.

The largest are the gliders. They live in the crowns, in the wind that does not reach the ground. Up there, three hundred feet up, the air is in constant motion — thermal on thermal, in long slow rollers that run along the length of a frond and tip it gently back and forth. The gliders hang in these rollers the way moths hang in a lit window: motionless, then suddenly correcting, then motionless again. They have four wings — not two pairs but four distinct membranes arranged fore and aft on a long central body, each wing a different shape, each beating at a different cadence. They are pale. They are the color of the sky they ride. Their mouths are downturned and they open them when they bank, and the whistling they make as they descend — the long descending whistles that are part of the Reach's permanent soundtrack — is air being forced across the membranes as the wing-surfaces twist. They eat what they catch. They catch what falls. They will stoop from three hundred feet and strike a small dark thing in the ferns at the base of a column, and the strike is not graceful; it is a tumble of pale wings and dark soil and a muffled sound that you can hear from fifty paces away. After the strike they are still for a moment, and then they rise, slowly, awkwardly, back into the updraft, carrying something you cannot see.

The worm-things live in the pleats. They are as thick as your forearm and as long as a man is tall, and they are pale, almost translucent, with the pinkish-cream color of a peeled grape. They move by peristalsis — a slow muscular wave that runs the length of them and makes them look as if they are being squeezed forward by an invisible hand. They have no visible eyes. They have a mouth at the front end — a soft lipless opening that tastes the air as it goes, the way a snake tastes the air, except the tongue here is the whole head and the head is the whole front of the body. They live on the moisture that seeps out of the pleats. They eat the mosses that grow in the wet grooves. They are the prey of the herders, who skewer them on hooked poles and roast them on resin fires and eat them with the absent-minded satisfaction of people eating something that has always been there. The worms taste, very faintly, of iodine. The herders will tell you this. They will also tell you that some worms are better than others, and they have names for the better ones, but they will not tell you the names.

The beetle-things are the largest of the ground fauna. They are as big as a midsized dog. Their shells are a deep blue-black, the color of a crow's wing, and they ring against the pleats when they click down the columns head-first — a fast descending percussion, the sound of someone running a stick down a wooden fence. They have six legs and a low flat body and a head that is mostly jaw. They are detritivores. They eat the falling fronds. They eat the dead worms. They eat the husks of the seed-funnels after they have pegged into the gravel and split open. They are everywhere. At any moment there are dozens of them clicking down a single column, and the combined sound is a low woodblock clatter that is itself part of the Reach's permanent hum. The herders do not eat them. The herders do not appear to notice them.

The small dark things in the ferns — the things with too many eyes — are the most numerous. They are the size of your thumb and they are the color of wet bark and they live in the leaf-litter at the foot of every column, where the air is humid and the light is dim and the mosses glow faintly at night. They are hunters. They eat the beetle larvae. They eat each other. They eat the things the gliders drop. They have many eyes — six, sometimes eight, set into the flat front of a wedge-shaped head — and they watch you when you stand still, and they watch you when you move, and they are not afraid of you, which is unsettling until you understand that they are not afraid because you are not in their economy; you are too large to be prey and too slow to be predator and they have no category for you. They weave. They build small low structures in the leaf-litter out of fallen frond-strips and beetle husks, and they sit in these structures and watch. They do not sing.

The seed-funnels are the strangest. They are not animals. They are not plants. They are the reproductive bodies of the columns themselves, or of something that lives symbiotically in the crowns, and they are five-foot cones of pale translucent tissue that tumble end over end through the air on the rising thermals and then, when the thermal fails them, they fall — straight down, a long slow spin, a soft *thuk* as the pointed end pegs itself into the gravel. There are dozens of them in the air at any moment. There are hundreds of them in the gravel at any moment, each one a small pale spike sticking out of the pale ground, and inside each spike, if you cut it open, is a knot of dark tissue and a few small bright things that are either larvae or seeds or eggs and that the herders gather and roast and eat. The *thock* you hear every fifteen or twenty minutes is not a seed-funnel. It is something else, something larger, that hits the gravel hard and goes silent. No one you have asked will tell you what it is. The herders will not look at you when you ask.

And then there are the herders.

The herders are the small thing that lives in the wet ring at the foot of the column. They are dark-skinned and they are short and they move slowly and they do not look at you very often. They are few. Across the Reach there are perhaps three thousand of them, scattered in column-dwellings of two or three families, eighteen people to a column, sometimes fewer, sometimes — in the years of the bloom — many more, because the bloom brings them in from the outer Reach and they gather in twos and threes around the columns that are about to flower and they sing and they climb and they wait.

They live in low curving huts of woven frond-strips. The huts are plastered with a resin that smells, in the heat, of pine and pepper and something metallic — copper, perhaps, or blood. The huts are the color of the column they are built against, which is to say the color of bone in the dry season and the color of wine in the wet, and from fifty paces you cannot see them. This is intentional. The herders do not want to be seen from far away. They do not want to be seen, period, if they can help it; they are a people who have made a long accommodation with a place that is much larger than they are, and the accommodation is, in part, to be quiet.

Their clothes are woven from the same frond-strips. Soft grey and pale green. The cloth is light and it is cool and it does not seem to wear out. They wear it loose. They do not seem to mind the heat. Their hair is black and they wear it short or braided close to the head and they have small dark eyes and broad flat faces and the calluses on their hands are thick as leather.

They do not have a word for *city*. They have a word for *column* — *reth*, it sounds like, a low syllable with a hum in it that is itself a kind of miniature of the hum of the columns. They have a word for *the hum* — *kal* — and a word for *the changing* — *enu*, said with the falling tone of a long wait. They have words for the colors of the columns in the different seasons. They have words for the worms that are good to eat and the worms that are not. They have a word for *you*, which is *voth*, and it is not unkind but it is wary, and they use it sparingly, and they look at you when they say it, and you feel that you have been weighed.

Their tools are simple and they are enough. They have the hooked poles of black frond-wood, light as a fishing rod and strong as iron, with which they reach up into the pleats and pull down the worms. They have the woven baskets, set into the pleats at the base, that fill with the sweat of the column overnight. They have the stone-flake knives, lashed with sinew to a wooden handle, sharp enough to part a hair. They have the resin and the embers. They have nothing else. They do not seem to want anything else. When you offer them something from your pack — a metal knife, a piece of cloth, a small mirror — they take it politely and they turn it over in their hands and they hand it back. They do not refuse. They simply do not see the use of it. The mirror is the one that hurts you.
