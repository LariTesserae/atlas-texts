# The Residents

Many things live here. Some of them are not what you'd call alive.

The most visible residents are the workers — the divers, in their stiff steel-boned suits, weighted and pressured and breathing the engineered mix the regulator gives them. There are not many of them. A handful on shift at any time, more often two or three than twenty, paired off because the work goes in pairs and the safety protocols assume a partner. They come down through the upper works on a schedule — six hours in, six hours out, longer sometimes, shorter other times — and they spend the day on the walls and the trestles and the pipe-runs. They speak to each other in the broken, water-thickened syllables that pass through the regulators, half-language and half-hand-signal, a vocabulary stripped to its load-bearing words. *Here. Fault. Tighten. Hold.* They watch each other out of the corners of their hoods the way mountain climbers watch each other — not because anything is wrong, but because anything could be, and the partner is the early warning.

They are not, most of them, the same as they were before they came to the yard. The suit changes a person. The water changes a person. You spend six hours in a weighted steel shell, breathing through a regulator, working in three dimensions with your hands while your feet find purchase on surfaces that don't want to give it, and something rearranges itself in you by small degrees over the months and the years. You start to think in currents. You start to hear the pumps even when you are out of the water, in the mess, in your bunk — the rhythmic deep thrum of them sits in your chest, and it is not unpleasant, and it is not quite a sound anymore, more like a posture. The people who work here a long time develop a particular gait on land. They walk as if water is still giving them weight. They never quite lose it.

There is one diver who has been here longer than the current operation has been running. They have been on the yard through two refits and one full restart after a near-shutdown. They are spoken of in the way senior workers are spoken of everywhere — with the slight edge of respect that is also, sometimes, a slight edge of fear. They live, by their own preference, almost entirely in the water. They surface to sleep and to eat and to do whatever paperwork cannot be done from a console, and they go back down. They know the currents the way other people know streets — by name, by the time of day, by the way a particular flow bends around a particular obstruction on a particular tide. They have favorite corners of the yard where they sit in the still water behind a wall and watch things pass. They know the fish. They have favorite fish. They will tell you, if you press them, which schools have been in which channels for how many seasons. They will tell you which warm plume has been putting out which scent this week. They have made the place theirs in a way that is hard to argue with, and they have not yet decided whether to retire.

The fish are the most numerous residents. They arrived on their own. No one seeded them — or if someone did, no one now remembers doing it, and the seeding, if it happened, would have been redundant. The fish came because the structure went in, and the structure went in because the work went in, and the work makes warmth and shelter and a steady current, and a steady current is, to a fish, almost a promise. There are now more kinds of fish in the yard than there were when the structure first went down. Some of them are descendants, several generations in, fish whose great-grandparents never knew any water that wasn't inside these walls. They are at home here in a way the first arrivals were not. The yard is their ocean now. They navigate by the lamps and the warm plumes and the prevailing current the way surface fish navigate by reefs and thermal lines. They have learned the schedule. They know when a work-pass will come through a channel and they school out of the way with the bored fluidity of commuters stepping off a curb for a bus.

There are larger things. The divers see them at the edge of sight, often enough that they are spoken of as residents and not as visitors. Long, dark, deliberate. They have the unhurried pace of things that have no enemies in their range, which means they are either very new to the place or very close to the top of whatever food chain has assembled itself here. They do not bother the workers. The workers do not bother them. There is an unspoken treaty, the way there is an unspoken treaty between any two things that are large enough to hurt each other and sensible enough not to. They pass behind walls and are gone. They surface, sometimes, into the lower columns of light, and the fish school tightens and parts around them, and then they are past, and the fish re-form, and the working goes on.

There is a person on the upper works who has not been in the water in years. They run something — operations, or a piece of operations, or the schedule, or some specific plant. They know the yard only by its readings. They watch the lamps from above. They watch the plumes from above. They see the current map and the heat map and the fish-density map, all of it translated into numbers on screens, and they make decisions based on those numbers, and they have not been in the water themselves in a long time, and this is not a criticism of them — it is just the shape of the work, that some of it has to be done from where the air is. They are spoken of sometimes, gently, by the divers. The divers know that person depends on them. The divers also know that the person does not, cannot, know what it is like down here, and they have made their peace with this.

The smallest residents are the patient ones. The colonizers — the green-brown fuzz on the concrete where the light reaches, the pale branching growth on the steel down-current from the warm plumes. They are working from the edges in. Each year they cover a little more of the substrate. They are not fast. They do not need to be fast. They have been given a substrate that has only just been put down, and they have all the time in the world. They are the first sentence of an ecology that will, eventually, if no one cleans it, coat the walls. They are the part of the yard that is becoming, slowly, into something that was not here before. The workers see them. The workers do not clean them very often. There is a tacit agreement — not official, not written — that the colonizers are allowed their slow conquest, because the colonizers are doing no harm and they are, in their way, evidence that the yard is becoming a place, not just a structure.

The pumps are not alive. The people who work here say this to themselves, sometimes, on the long shifts. They say it half as a reminder and half as a concession. The pumps are not alive, but they have a rhythm. When one of them fails, the others pick up its beat, and the whole system adjusts — flow redistributes, pressure evens out, the yard goes on working the way a body goes on breathing when one lung is compromised. There are people whose job it is to listen to the pumps the way a doctor listens to a heart. They sit on the upper works with headphones on and they know, by ear, which pump is which, and they know when a bearing is going, and they know when a seal is weeping, and they know it days before any instrument tells them. They are, in a sense, the most intimate residents. They know the yard from the inside, the way a cardiologist knows a chest. They have a relationship with it.

And the yard itself — the structure, the grid of poured walls, the pipes and cables and lamps and grates and warm plumes — the yard is the largest resident by far. It is not a creature. It does not think. It does not intend. But it is the thing that holds all the others. It is the medium and the substrate and the shelter and the territory. Everything else is in it, or on it, or beside it, or downstream of it. When the lights are out and the workers are up and the divers are sleeping and the fish are schooling in the green-blue columns of daylight that come down through the surface chop, the yard is still there. It is still working. The pumps are still pushing. The turbines are still humming. The warm water is still climbing the grates. The yard, of all the residents, is the only one that has nowhere else to be.

This is who lives here. The diver in the steel suit. The old hand who has made the water their country. The operators on the upper works. The pump-listeners. The fish that have made it their ocean. The dark unhurried things at the edge of sight. The colonizers, working patiently from the edges in. The yard itself, the largest of them, going on working in the silence and the green-blue light and the constant hum of itself.
