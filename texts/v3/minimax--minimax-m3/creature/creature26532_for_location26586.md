What lives in the White Basin of Hethran is, in the order of what is most often seen, a small number of people, and in the order of what has been here longest, a forest.

The people are the obvious answer. They are the ones who notice you, if you stood at the rim of the defile long enough to be noticed, which you would not be, because the wind comes through the defile hard and constant and the people do not stand at the rim unless they have reason. They are wrapped in pale cloth — woven from the fiber of the very plants that surround them, so that in a way they are dressed in the basin, in its shed skin — and the cloth is wound tight against the abrasive air, around the head, around the throat, around the body, leaving only the eyes and a slit for the mouth. Their eyes are narrow. Not narrowed by squinting, although they squint too. Narrow in the build, the lid heavy, the lashes thickened, the brow low, so that the face is mostly a covered thing with two small dark openings in it that look out. They are adapted. They have been here long enough that the eye no longer opens wide; it does not need to. Wide eyes would only gather more of the light that already lies in slabs across the salt.

Their skin is the color of the basin floor, more or less, a kind of bleached ochre that has gone almost to white, and the dust of the place — which is always in the air, which is the breath of the basin — settles on them and they are not much distinguishable from the walls of their own houses. They are, in this sense, a kind of camouflage. They have made themselves hard to see. They have made themselves part of the place, and the place has, over a long time, allowed it.

They are not many. The basin will not support many. There is no water, or almost no water, and the chemistry of the place is not the chemistry of abundance. A few hundred, perhaps, in the tiered town against the leeward wall. Fewer in the deeper seasons, when the wind is worst and the moth is scarce. They know their number. They do not speak of it. There is a sense in which to count them is to acknowledge that they could be fewer still, and they prefer not to.

They work the crystal. That is the work. A stalk shears off in a high wind, or an old column at last surrenders to its own weight and tips forward into the salt, or a younger growth is harvested at the right angle for fiber, and the pieces are brought up the wall in baskets, on backs, in small wheeled carts that the children learn to push. Some of it is cut into salt-block and built into the houses. Some is shredded, combed, spun, woven into the cloth. Some is left where it lies and buried by the next season's drift, because the basin is a sink and everything that comes in stays, and to remove everything would be to unmake the place. The work is constant but not hurried. The crystal grows a finger-length in a season. There is no rush. The crystal has been here longer than they have and will be here after.

The interiors of the houses are the real town. The streets between the houses are only the necessary connective tissue, the place you pass through to get from one inside to another, and they are not lived in. You emerge from a doorway and you lean into the wind and you move along the alley, which is hardly wider than your shoulders, and you do not speak. The sound outside is a thing you lean against. The crystalline forest is always singing, and the basin is always echoing, and the air through the defile is a continuous roar that becomes, after a few seconds inside it, a kind of pressure on the chest. You learn, very quickly, not to speak in it. The people mouth at each other across distances. They use a small sign language, the hands held low, the gestures spare, and they wait. Conversation is for indoors.

Indoors, it is bearable. The walls are built thick — salt block, mud reinforced with the mineral fiber, layered in places three or four deep — and they absorb. The ceilings are shaped to damp the upper register, to bring the singing of the forest down to a hum that you can talk over. The windows are small and high, admitting a controlled slice of brightness, and the light that comes through them, in certain hours, is colored. The crystalline forest outside throws prismatic smears across the white floor, and the smears move, and the color moves, and the color comes through the high windows in slow shifting patterns that the people have learned to read.

They read the color as you and I would read a clock, or a calendar, or a weather-glass. A wind from the north, in the morning, throws a particular green. A wind from the south, in the afternoon, throws a particular red. A wind that comes straight down the defile, the dangerous one, throws a blue-grey that means the high shutters go up and the children are kept below. The color tells the time of day. The color tells the season. The color tells what the wind is doing up where you cannot see it. People who read the color well are useful, are quietly respected, are asked for their opinion when a decision depends on the weather. There is no name for this skill. It is just a thing some have, and the basin gave it to them, and they do not question it.

They drink. They have very little to drink. A thin mineral water drawn from deep in the salt crust, where the seal of the basin has held some trickle of the old wet chemistry. A liquid pressed from the crystal fiber, which is not really water but serves. A broth made from the moth, when the moth can be spared. They do not drink much. They are not people who drink much. They are people who persist on small amounts, the way the crystal persists on light, the way the lizard persists on almost nothing, the way the basin itself persists on the dust that drifts in from the rim.

They have children. Few of them. The women — and there are women, and men, though the difference is largely hidden by the wrapping — do not have many, and some do not have any, and this is accepted as the way of the place. The children are born pale, narrow-eyed, already adapted. They are taught early to be quiet outside and to read the color. They are taught to gather the crystal and to cut it. They are taught the gestures and the mouth-speech and the indoor voice. They are taught, without being told in so many words, that the basin is the basin and they are small in it and the smallness is not a flaw but a way of fitting.

They do not leave. Some have tried, in the deep past, or so the stories say. They go up through the defile and out into the country beyond the rim, and they do not come back, and the people do not speak of this often. The staying is assumed. The defile is narrow and the wind through it is fierce and the country beyond has no crystal and no salt and no color, and what would they do there. They are a people of the basin. They have been made, over generations, to fit it.

The basin has a kind of religion, or something close to it. The people are aware that the place is not just a place. It is a presence, in the same way the wind is a presence, in the same way the sound is a presence. They do not worship it — worship is too active, too much speaking, too much gathering — but they acknowledge it. They leave offerings in niches in the wall. A piece of crystal, perfectly faceted, set on a small shelf. A cup of the mineral water, which they can ill afford, set in a bowl of salt. A scrap of cloth, woven very fine, hung from a hook. These are gestures, made at certain hours, by certain people, in certain light. They are the way the people stay in the right relation to the place. The people are not sure what would happen if the gestures were not made. They are not sure they want to find out.

The crystalline forest is not the people. The crystalline forest is something else. The people work it and live in it and wrap themselves in it, but they do not feel that it is theirs. It was here before them. It will be here after. It is the basin, in a way, the way water is the sea in a place that is all water.

The forest is alive, or near enough to alive that the distinction is not worth drawing. It photosynthesizes without water, or with so little water that none is ever seen. It takes the mineral vapor and the trace moisture that the wind brings from distant ranges and it does the rest with light. It grows in faceted columns, in lattices, in fibrous mats pressed flat against the crust. It is not a plant. It does not have leaves. It does not have roots, or it has roots that go down only into the upper crust, drinking dust. It has geometry. It has age. The oldest columns are as thick as a thigh and have been standing for a century, ringing in the wind like bells, and the very oldest are half-buried in the accumulated drifts of their own fallen spicules.

The forest is tuned. This is the strange thing. The forest is tuned to the wind, and the wind is constant, and so the forest sings. Every stalk and lattice vibrates in the moving air. The wind comes down the defile and into the basin and has nowhere else to go, and the basin is a sounding box, and the forest turns the funnel into music. The music is in the upper register. It is shrill, layered, sustained, almost unbearable to a visitor. The people have lived in it so long that they have moved past it, the way a person who lives by a waterfall eventually stops hearing the waterfall, except that they have not stopped hearing it — they have made a vocabulary of it. They hear when a stalk has fallen. They hear when the wind is shifting up high. They hear when something is moving in the walls that should not be. The sound is the way they know things.

The forest throws the color. The color is the forest's way of being seen. The people read the color, and the color comes from the forest, and the people and the forest are, in some long way, in a conversation that neither of them began. The people take the forest's fiber and build their houses and weave their cloth. The forest continues to grow. The forest is not depleted, or it is depleted so slowly that the depletion is part of the rhythm. The people are, in a sense, a kind of weather of the basin, a slow process that moves through the crystal the way the wind moves through it, leaving traces, taking pieces, and going on.

There are the smaller things.

The lizard is small and pale and lives in the cracks of the wall. It comes out at dawn, when the wind is briefly still, before the day begins, and it moves along the salt in short darting runs, and it eats something — small insects, perhaps, or the dust, or the moth. It is fast. It does not stay out long. It is rarely seen. The people do not bother it. They leave it alone, with the respect you leave alone something that has learned to live in the open when they themselves have not. The lizard is a sign. The lizard is one of the few animals that moves in the day, on the floor, in the light, and the people see this as a kind of courage, or a kind of necessity, or a kind of luck, and they are glad of it.

The moth is pale and drinks at the basin air. It is not clear what it eats. The people have theories. Perhaps the dust. Perhaps the mineral spore that the forest sheds. The moth is quiet. It flutters in the early morning and the late afternoon, when the wind is lower, and it comes into the houses sometimes, through the high windows, and the people do not chase it. The moth is a small companion. The moth is one of the few soft things in the basin. The moth is taken, sometimes, for the broth, but only when there are many, and the people know which of their neighbors is a careful moth-keeper and which is not.

The burrowing thing is under the salt crust. It is rarely seen. It eats the fallen chitin and the shed fiber, and without it the basin would bury itself in its own dead. The people know about it, vaguely, the way you know about something you do not see, the way you know about the water table, the way you know about the deeper towns. They leave it alone. They do not hunt it. They are grateful, in the way you are grateful to a process you do not understand but depend on.

And there are the bones.

The town is layered. The houses are built on the floors of older houses, and the older houses were built on the floors of still older ones, and somewhere down in the salt there are streets that no one walks and rooms that no one enters. The deepest layer is so old that no one remembers it. The stories about it are different in different families, and none of them can be checked, because to dig down is to unmake the upper layers, and the upper layers are where the living are. The people know they are not the first. They are aware, in a quiet way, that they will not be the last. The basin is patient. The basin will outlast them. The basin has outlasted others, whose streets are under the current streets, whose lintels are now beams in the floors of newer rooms, whose salt-blocks have been recut and reused and recut again until no one remembers they were ever anything else.

The bones, in time, become salt. Even bone, in this place, becomes salt, and the people understand this without being told, the way you understand a thing that is the air you breathe. They will become salt. Their streets will be under other streets. Their children, if they have children, will walk on floors that were once their floors, and the floors will be salt, and the salt will be the same salt that was there before the first town, and the wind will come through the defile, and the forest will sing, and the color will move across the floor, and someone, in another tier, will read it.

This is what lives in the White Basin of Hethran. The people, wrapped in the fiber of the place, watching the color, reading the sound, enduring. The forest, faceted and old, tuned to the wind, throwing its light across the floor. The lizard, the moth, the thing under the salt. The bones of those who came before. The bones, in waiting, of those who will come after. The basin, in its sealed and ancient patience, holding all of it, in the white light, in the endless wind, in the upper register of the sound that does not stop.
