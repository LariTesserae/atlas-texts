# What Lives There

What lives there is not a census. It is a settling.

The first thing to understand is that the Verdant Deep does not have inhabitants the way a forest has inhabitants — as separate creatures who happen to share a region. The Deep has *occupants*, in the older sense: things that have been taken into the body of a larger thing, the way a forest floor is taken into a forest, the way your gut is taken into you. The dwellers are not guests in the cavern. They are also not hosts. They are something the cavern has digested, partially, and the digestion has not finished.

The place is the first dweller. Whatever the roots are — plant, animal, colony, single organism disguised as a community — they were there before anything with a name walked the floor. They are the walls, the floor in many places, the ceiling, the shelves the shelters are lashed to, the air. They breathe. You can stand for an hour and watch the trunk nearest you swell and soften and swell again, and the motion is too regular to be wind and too organic to be tide, and the warmth that comes off it is not heat so much as the slow leak of some internal fire that does not burn. The roots do not think, or they do not think in any way that produces a thought you could point to. They extend. They thicken. They drink. They remember the shape of every foot that has pressed them, in the way a mattress remembers a body, and they push back, gently, against the next foot that comes. They are the substrate and they are also the weather. The deep is not a place where the roots live. The deep is what the roots have made, and continue to make, by being.

The pillars are the roots' slow fingers. They have grown down through the ceiling for centuries, groping, branching, thickening, and the dwellers cut them back and cut them back and they grow back and are cut back again, and the rung-sap that wells up warm from a fresh cut is not a wound so much as a kind of speech. The deep is talking to the dwellers in the only language that lasts down here, which is the language of slow replacement. You climb the pillars and they become the ladder. You lick the sap and the ladder becomes you. This is not metaphor. It is how the place is structured.

Above the roots and through them and in them, the blooms. They are the only things in the deep that are unambiguously beautiful in the way a surface-dweller means the word, and they are not unambiguously beautiful — they are too large, too bright, too patient, too much the color of things that should not be eaten. A bloom opens over days. It unfurls in the slow way a hand opens, in the slow way a mouth opens, and when it is fully open it is the size of a small house and it is warm to approach and the light it gives is a light you feel in your sinuses, in the back of your skull. Some blooms are the color of a sky that has decided to be ill. Some are the deep red of an exposed organ, and they pulse, and you do not want to stand beneath them but you do, because the warmth is the warmth of being held, and the dwellers are pragmatic about the difference between being held and being swallowed, and they have decided, for now, that the difference is on their side. The bloom-light is not a byproduct. The bloom-light is the way the growth eats. It draws the insects in. It draws the flyers down from the upper layers. It draws you in. When the festival ends and the bloom folds shut, what is left inside is mostly mineral, mostly a thin gray ash that the dwellers gather and mix with their star-soil glaze, and the bloom does not open again on that spot. The place has eaten and the place has moved on.

The insects are next, and they are the foundation, and you must not think of them as the insects of any field. They are the size of your hand. They are the size of your arm. They move in towers that you could fit a house inside, spiraling columns of chitin and hook and the soft pale bodies of the things that are not quite chitin and not quite soft, that are something in between. They eat the root-mat and they eat each other and the things that eat them are also insects, or the cousins of insects, or the descendants of insects who have given up the project of being one thing and have become many things at once. A column passes over you like weather. You feel the air change. The smaller ones, the climbers, the freckles that settle on your skin — you do not feel them at all until you do, and then you feel them as a kind of attention, a moving warmth, and if you brush them off they will take a piece of you, the way a current takes a piece of a bank. You learn to let them pass. You learn to be terrain. The dwellers are terrain in a way you will not be, and you can see it in the way an insect column will split around a dweller and close behind them as though they were a stone in a stream, and the dweller does not look up, because this is what it is to be a stone in a stream.

Above the insects, the flyers. There is no clean word. They are bat and bird and neither, layered by altitude, each species holding its own height the way a school of fish holds its own depth. The lowest layer is all membrane and quick wings and the wet eyes of things that hunt by echo in a place that is already full of echo. Above them the next layer, larger, slower, with the vestigial wings of creatures who almost left the deep and decided not to, who use the fans of their flightless wings to push the warm columns of air upward and to court each other in displays that look, from a distance, like fans being opened and closed in a green-lit room. Above them the next layer, and the next. They feed on each other in a tidy stack. Their droppings are the rain, and the rain is gathered, and the gathered rain is star-soil, and the star-soil is the deep's only harvest, and the dwellers shape it on small potter's wheels and glaze their thick-walled jars with it and fire the jars in pits that they dig into the mat and which the mat closes behind them, slowly, the way everything in the deep closes behind you.

On the ground, in the spaces between the great trunks, the larger things move. The text used a word I will not repeat here, but I will say they are the closest thing the deep has to livestock, and they are not livestock, and the dwellers do not pretend they are. They are six-legged and eight-legged and they have an uncountable number of legs. They browse the mat in patient circuits, and the circuits become grooves in the floor, and the grooves deepen over years until they are the paths the dwellers use, and the dwellers and the grazers share the paths without ever quite meeting, because each knows the other's hour. Some of the grazers are armored, plate over plate, the color of old bone, and they move like furniture, like something that was placed and has decided to stay. Some are soft and pale and they glow from within, faintly, the way a paper lantern glows when there is a small flame behind it, and the light is the light of their digestion, and if you watch one long enough you can see the food pass through it in a slow luminous pulse, and it is not unpleasant to watch, and it is not comfortable to watch, and you do it anyway because you are in the deep and the deep is where the watching happens. The strange ones are the ones that are both. Rooted to a spot for a season, drinking from the mat, blooming in their own small way, and then one morning they are not rooted, and they have legs, and they walk away. The dwellers do not approach these. The dwellers do not approach these because the dwellers know that the walking is a kind of answer to a question they have not been asked, and they have decided to let the question go.

The strange things. I have kept them for here because they belong at the end, or they belong to a part of the deep that is not really a part at all. They live in the dim zones, in the spaces between the blooms where the light does not reach and the hum of the place is a hum and not a roar. Shapes that assemble themselves from smaller shapes — a dozen insects drawn together into a slow-moving silhouette, then releasing, then reassembling a yard further on, the way a school of fish might, except that the fish is also the flock, and the flock is also the herd, and you cannot tell whether you are seeing one thing or fifty. Lights that move against the current of the insect columns, going up while everything goes down, and the dwellers do not speak of them, or they speak of them in a tone that is the tone of speaking of weather. The sound from beneath. The low patient sound you feel in your teeth. The dwellers call it the dreaming and they are mostly right, in the way that people who live in a place are mostly right: not because they have measured it, but because they have made their peace with it, and the peace is the only instrument that takes a reading.

The dwellers. Thirty, forty, scattered through the upper cavern in clusters the size of small families, lashed to the trunks, woven into the mat. They are small and brown and they do not look like a people who have made a choice. They look like a people who have been *allowed*, in the way the insects are allowed, in the way the flyers are allowed. Their faces are the faces of people who have not had to be loud for many generations. They speak a language that is half words and half imitations, and the imitations are not always imitations of animals — sometimes they are imitations of trunks, of mats, of the slow sound from below, and the deep answers sometimes, in the same voice, and the dwellers nod, and this is a kind of conversation, and you are not part of it, and you will not be part of it, and the dwellers are not unkind about this. They are simply not unkind at all. They are the kind of people who have stopped performing kindness because they no longer need to.

They have tools. Knives of bone, of flint, of insect-shell. Axes with stone heads, used to trim the pillars. Needles of thorn. Pottery small and thick. They do not smelt. They do not need to. The deep gives, in its way, and what the deep does not give they trade for, in small bundles sent up the stairwell on the backs of children who do not yet know to be afraid of the climb. The dead are carried up. The deep will not let a body rest, and the dwellers have stopped arguing with the deep about this, the way you stop arguing with the weather.

And then the thing that is not in the text but is in the text, the way the floor is in the text, the way the air is in the text. The deep is unfinished. The deep is younger than the morning. New roots probe the ceiling. New blooms open in places that were dim. The floor is higher than it was. The map is wrong before it is drawn. The dwellers live in a place that is being *made*, by something that is not them, around them, through them, and they have made their arrangements with this, and the arrangements are not defeat and are not victory and are not anything you have a word for in your language, which was not built for this.

You go up. Most people go up the same day. The surface is bright and dry and has no roar. You stand in it and you remember how to hear yourself, and what you hear is small, and the smallness is a kind of relief, and the relief is a kind of loss, and you do not go back down, mostly, but you think about it, and the thinking is the only thing you carry out of the deep that is not the taste of the sap.
