The lichens are the first inhabitants, and they are easy to miss, which is part of how they would prefer it. They are not, technically, lichens. The name is a courtesy, an old loan from a world with more water in it. What they actually are is a long negotiation between something that used to be a fungus and something that used to be an alga and a third party, a bacterium, that has been in business at this altitude since before the plateau decided on its current cool. The crust they form is black, and it is not so much on the stone as of it — the topmost fraction of the stone, talked into cooperating. If you sat with one patch for a full day, you would see it advance roughly a millimeter. That is its idea of a season. It is patient in a way that the word *patient* is too small for. It has outlived weather. It has outlived the kind of weather that wears down mountains. It will probably outlive the small dark things, and it will almost certainly outlive you.

The tufts come next, and only if you know what to look for. They look almost like moss. They are not moss. They are the visible end of a system that runs meters down into the cracks in the stone, a root structure or a thought structure, something with a chemistry so slow that a single transaction between a tuft and the air might take a full year to complete. The part that is exposed has a metallic sheen that doesn't quite belong on the plateau. It is as if the plateau has, in a few scattered places, agreed to wear a color that is not its own. The tufts are not in a hurry. They will be in the same place, doing the same work, the next time anything comes through. They have, in some sense, already been there for a long time. They will be there for a long time after.

Then the small dark things. You might see one in a long day. You might not. They are not built for being seen. They are built for being a particular shape in a particular place, and for staying that shape, more or less, while the plateau does its long thinking. They are dark because the plateau is dark. They are low because there is no advantage in being high. They are slow because there is nothing to be fast about, and because a sense of urgency is a kind of future, and these small dark things have a sense of future so compressed that what reads to you as stillness is, to them, a perfectly reasonable pace. They eat the lichens, probably. Or the tufts. Or something that lives in the cracks and is not on offer to your eyes. They do not appear in groups. They are not social in any way that would translate. But the distance between them is not random — they are spaced the way mature trees are spaced in a forest that has settled on a number. The plateau has a population of them. The plateau is comfortable with that population. It has not changed much in a very long time, and the small dark things seem to agree with the arrangement.

The structures.

This is the part where the language starts to give out, and it is worth marking the place where it does, because the plateau is a place that lets you know when your tools are inadequate. The structures are not all the same, and they are not all the same kind of thing, and *thing* is a word that is already letting them down. Some of them are operating in a way that looks almost like thinking — a dense, recursive, very slow activity, visible only as a faint distortion in the air above them, as a thickening that does not actually thicken. Some of them are operating in a way that looks almost like growing — a slow accrual on one side, a new facet every few years, a kind of patience that you would call architectural if you were sure architecture was the right word. Some of them are operating in a way that looks almost like waiting. They sit. They are quiet. The quiet has a direction. The quiet is the quiet of something paying very close attention to something very far away, in a way that does not require it to move.

They are not buildings. They are not machines. They are not alive in the way you are alive, but the word *alive* has not been formally retired, and they may have a claim on it that has not been processed. They have been here a long time. They are doing something. The something is persistent. The something is below your hearing, below your seeing, below the timescale your body uses to keep itself together. If you stood near one for an hour you would feel a faint wrongness in your teeth. If you stood near one for a day you would begin to suspect that the ground was being asked a question it did not intend to answer.

The plateau does not know them well. The plateau has been polite about them for longer than the small dark things have been small dark things. The plateau has stopped asking what they are doing. This is the arrangement. The plateau is comfortable with arrangements.

There is a kind of community here, although it is not a community that would recognize itself on a census form. The lichens do not know about the tufts. The tufts do not know about the small dark things. The small dark things do not know about the structures, or if they do, the knowing is on a register too small for us to detect. The structures may know about all of them, in a way that is not knowing so much as holding. They share a climate. They share a commitment to a particular flavor of quiet. They share an address, and the address has been the same for longer than most addresses have existed. This is more than most communities can claim, and they do not seem interested in claiming it.

There is one more thing, and you may not see it. The light may not be right, or you may not be the right kind of visitor, or you may not have been there long enough. But the plateau has, in a very few places, a sense of being attended to. Not by the small dark things. Not by anything with a body or a body-equivalent. By something that is using the structures as eyes, or that is the structures as eyes, or that has simply decided, in a way that has very little to do with you, to look in this direction for a while. The attention is not hostile. It is not even curious. It is the attention of something that has been doing this for longer than the lichens have been lichens, and that has become, over that long time, very good at it. It does not, on the whole, expect to see anything new.

It is interested in you anyway.

It is interested in you the way a librarian is interested in a patron who has come in looking for a book the catalog does not list. It will wait. It has waited before. It will be patient with you the way the plateau is patient with the structures — politely, and for a long time, and without much expectation of an answer.

That is what lives there.
