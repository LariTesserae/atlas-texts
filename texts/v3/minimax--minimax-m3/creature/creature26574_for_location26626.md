The people here wear wool. That is the first thing you would notice about them, if you noticed anything. The wool comes from upriver, where the sheep farms sit on land that isn't quite hills, and the textile works on the town's edge processes it into something between cloth and felt that gets sent outward to be made into proper garments. The town's own coats are made of the stuff before it becomes anything more refined — thick, slightly oily, smelling of lanolin and the river mist — and they are worn longer than is reasonable, because the river pulls heat out of the air and the kilns only manage to warm their immediate vicinity, and so most of the year everyone is in something heavier than the season strictly demands. The wool merchants do well.

The people are not many. Maybe a few thousand, though nobody has counted lately because there's been no reason to. They live in brick houses that they share with the damp, with the sound of the kilns, with the small white dots of ceramic insulators on the outside walls where the wiring was retrofit after the electrification came late and unevenly. The houses are built close. Walls share walls. You can hear your neighbor set down a teacup in the morning if the wind is right, and the people have learned to live with this — to step lightly after a certain hour, to consider what time it is before opening a window.

They work in the foundries and the brickworks and the textile works and at the rail siding. They run small shops on the main street that have been in families for generations. They teach at the schoolhouse near the walnut tree. They work at the kilns, which is its own specialization — the firing of brick is not a thing everyone can do, and the families that do it have done it for so long that the clay has gotten into them somehow, the way the river has gotten into the cellars. Many of them are born here and die here. A few leave for the city in their twenties and come back in their forties with a particular expression, the one that says they remember now what it was like to leave. A few leave and don't come back. The town notices when this happens but doesn't resent it.

They nod at each other. They nod at strangers. They play harmonicas on back steps in the evening — or some of them do, well enough that you stop walking. They eat bread from the bakery on the main street and fish from the river and vegetables from their gardens. They drink ale from the brewery that isn't brewing anymore but whose pub is still going. They put their coats on in May and take them off in September and never quite get used to either transition.

The cats are the second most famous inhabitants. The cats live in the cellars and the alleys and the back gardens, and there are an unreasonable number of them. They are mostly nondescript — tabby, black, gray, the occasional ginger — and they are mostly well-fed despite nobody being quite sure who is feeding them. The theory is that the cat population has reached some kind of equilibrium with the alley system, which is, in fact, a cat-infrastructure as much as a delivery-infrastructure, and that the cats are feeding themselves off the leavings of the town. There is a woman on Mill Street who has been putting out milk for thirty years, and there are several unofficial feeding stations in the alleys, and the bakery's back door has a small wooden platform for them that the bakers pretend isn't there but that they refill every morning with scraps. The cats move through the town in soft overlapping territories, and their nighttime concerts — the yowling, the chirping, the long slow arguments about whose cellar is whose — are part of the town's soundtrack after the kilns quiet down.

The dogs are fewer and less famous but more consequential. There are working dogs at the rail siding and at the small farms on the town's edge. There are house dogs that go on the long loops through the main street twice a day. There is a particular terrier attached to a particular pub whose territory is approximately the central block and whose habits are sufficiently well-known that the postman times his rounds to avoid him.

The horses are mostly working — cart horses for the brickworks, a few riding horses kept by families who still think of themselves as having horses. They are big and patient and they know the smell of the kilns and don't mind it.

The starlings are the population that nobody has tried to count. They roost in the ivy on the south-facing walls in numbers that you would not believe if you hadn't seen it — a thick, shifting, chattering blanket of birds that covers the brick in the late afternoon and then, at some unannounced moment, lifts itself into the air in a coordinated ribbon that wheels and loops over the kilns before dropping down into the willows by the river. The murmuration is something the schoolchildren are walked out to see. The townspeople themselves stop what they're doing at the appropriate hour and look up at it, because not looking would feel wrong.

The bats are another thing. They live in the old brewery chimney, which is the tallest unused structure in town, and they come out at dusk in a thin black line that pours down the brick face and out over the river. They are pipistrelles, which are small, but there are enough of them that the line is visible for a few minutes before they disperse. They feed over the river until the light fails and then they go back up the chimney in ones and twos. People have opinions about the bats. Mostly they are fond of them. The brewery chimney is, in a sense, the bats' chimney now, and the dispute about who has to pay to take it down has gone quiet for this reason among others.

The foxes are newer. They moved in within the last decade from the wooded hills to the east, and they have become a presence that the town has not yet made its peace with. They are bold — more bold than foxes are usually supposed to be — and they have killed an unreasonable number of chickens. There is ongoing civic debate about what to do. The current position is to do nothing, which is the position the town takes on most things until it becomes untenable. The foxes, in the meantime, have established themselves along the millrace and in the alley system and at the edges of the residential fringe, and they come out at night in that leggy, startled way that foxes have, and they look at the kilns with the expression of animals that have decided the kilns are not their problem.

There are rats. There are always rats in a town like this, in the cellars and the alleys and the brick walls and the old brewery, and the town deals with them in the same way it deals with most things — with a moderate hand and without much discussion. There are pigeons on the main street and sparrows in the ivy and the occasional owl that calls from the walnuts at night. The herons stand in the willows at the bend in the river in the early mornings and look like they have always been there. They are not particularly bothered by people. They catch fish and eat them.

And then there is the river.

The river is the oldest inhabitant. The river was here before the town, and the town is built out of the river's product — the clay from its banks fired into brick and stacked back into streets and walls and chimneys — and the river is still cutting its bank at a rate that no one has tried to measure because the rate is the rate. The river runs through the town without seeming to be aware of it. The river does not care about the kilns or the foundries or the cats or the bats. The river pulls heat out of the air. The river breathes a permanent mist that sits in the low places until midmorning, then lifts itself in slow dissolving curtains. The river, in the evenings, takes on the color of the brick it is running past, and the town, walking along its bank, takes on something of the river's patience.

The kilns are the next oldest inhabitant after the river. The kilns have been firing continuously for longer than anyone currently alive can remember, and before that for longer than anyone's grandparents could remember. The kilns breathe. They have a low, continuous sound that lives underneath everything else in the town — underneath the foundry clang, underneath the rail whistles, underneath the cat-calls and the starling chatter and the harmonica playing. The kilns change the color of the ground around them into a wide ring of fired earth that looks from a distance like a sunburn on the land. The kilns make the northern sky amber at dusk. The kilns are where the town's bricks come from, and the town's bricks are what the town is made of, and so the kilns are, in a sense, the place where the town is being continuously made and remade.

The foundry breathes too, but more staccato — the drop-hammer at noon, the ventilation whine, the metal being poured and shaped. There are two of them, doing brass and iron castings for the agricultural equipment market. The foundry is the loudest inhabitant during the week and silent on Sundays, and the silence on Sundays makes the rest of the week feel heavier by comparison.

The rail siding speaks in steam whistles, mostly in the afternoons, when trains are being made up. The trains come in with empty cars and go out with bricks, with wool, with castings, with whatever the small industries of the town have produced that week. The town is connected to the rest of the world by this siding and by the river, and the rest of the world comes in measured amounts.

The water tower stands on its iron legs at the southwest corner and does not do anything visible. It holds water. It is an inhabitant in the way that certain large and useful structures are inhabitants — as presences more than as actors.

The old brewery chimney is an inhabitant of a different sort. It is unused, it has been unused for a generation, and yet it stands, and bats live in it, and starlings sometimes roost on its ledges, and it is one of the tallest things in town. It is the chimney the town hasn't decided what to do with. It is, in some sense, the town's reservation about its own completeness.

The bell tower at the old meeting hall strikes the quarter-hours. It has done this for as long as the bell has hung there. It strikes a tone that takes three seconds to develop and five to decay. You can set your clock by it. It is one of the more reliable inhabitants.

And the ivy.

The ivy is everywhere. The ivy is on every south-facing wall. The ivy is glossy and big-leafed and it climbs in dense curtains that are allowed to do what they do because stopping them would be more work than the town is willing to do. The ivy is fed by the damp and by the warmth that the brick holds and re-radiates. The ivy is, in the summer, flowering. In the autumn, it is the last green thing in the town. There is a vine somewhere that has eaten a small wooden shed whole and is now producing modest white trumpets in summer over a structure that nobody owns, and the town walks past it without particular comment.

The moss is even more pervasive. The moss is less a thing that grows on surfaces than a continuous presence that surfaces interrupt. The moss is in the cracks between the cobbles. The moss is on the north sides of walls. The moss is on the brick where the mortar has come out. The moss is patient.

And the brick itself.

The brick is the town's body. The brick is what the town is made of and what the town is continually being made into. The brick is the river's clay fired and stacked back into the configuration of streets and walls and chimneys and culverts and low garden borders. The brick weathers. The brick holds the mist in the morning before the sun is properly up. The brick takes on the color of the things it has been reflecting all day. The brick is, in a sense, the medium through which the town is in conversation with itself — the same material cycling through itself, the river eating the bank, the kilns eating the clay, the town eating the bricks, and then beginning again.

What lives here, then, if you were to take the full inventory, is the people and the cats and the dogs and the horses and the starlings and the bats and the foxes and the rats and the herons and the pigeons and the sparrows and the owl, and the river and the kilns and the foundry and the rail siding and the water tower and the brewery chimney and the bell tower, and the ivy and the moss and the brick and the mist. What lives here is a place that has been doing what it does for long enough that the doing has become a kind of being. What lives here is a town that is exactly the size it intends to be, that fits together too well, that curves its streets to accommodate a river that isn't there anymore, that lets its ivy climb in patterns that almost seem curated, that breathes its mist and fires its kilns and wheels its starlings and pulls its bats out of the chimney at dusk like a black thread being pulled from a spool.

What lives here is patient, and slow, and damp, and loud in the way that living things are loud. What lives here watches, in the way that places watch — not unkindly, but with the attention of something that has all the time in the world. Nothing in particular confirms this. Nothing needs to.

You would remember, if you visited, that someone played a harmonica on a back step one evening, very competently, and that you listened for longer than you meant to. You would remember that you couldn't see the town from outside it, and that once you were in, you couldn't quite remember how to get back out without going past something you wanted to look at twice. You would remember the smell of damp wool and fired clay, and the way the brick held the mist in the morning, and the way the kilns made the northern sky seem to breathe. You would remember that the town was full of inhabitants, and that none of them seemed in any particular hurry, and that the town itself seemed, somehow, to be one of them.
