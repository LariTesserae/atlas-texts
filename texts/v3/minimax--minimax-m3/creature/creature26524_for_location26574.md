# The Inhabitants

You will not see them whole for a long time, if you see them whole at all.

The first one you see is a hand.

You are kneeling at a junction of three pavements, puzzling at a tile that has been levered slightly out of its frame. You have been working your thumbnail into the gap, marveling at the warm bone of the ceramic, at the way the seam has been dressed so finely that the air has not been able to get in to crack the edges. And then a hand is beside yours, the way a bird lands on a branch you have been watching: present, suddenly, before the movement that placed it. The hand takes the tile out. It is brown as old heart-wood, brown as a teak plank kept for a century in a hot attic, and the skin is not so much wrinkled as *pleated*—a fine vertical folding across the knuckles and the back, the way the leaves of this place are pleated, as if the hand has been grown into its present shape rather than worn into it. The nails are wide, dark, ground down to a translucence that looks more like polished bone than keratin, the way the tiles themselves look more like bone than clay. The hand sets the bad tile aside, lifts a fresh one from a low stack you had not noticed, fits it into the empty frame with three small wrist movements, the first two wrong, the third a faint *click* that travels through the floor and the floor answers with a single clear high note, and then the hand withdraws, and you look up, and there is no one there. Above you a leaf the size of your open palm turns slowly on its stem, as if the plant is following your gaze, but no face looks back.

After that you see them the way you see fish in dark water—by their movement, by what they displace, by the moments when a curtain of red foliage sways in a way that cannot be accounted for by growth alone.

---

## The Body

You assemble an impression, over days, the way a child assembles a face from a handful of stones: not by seeing, but by *inference*.

They are your height, or a little less. Their shoulders are narrow in the way the tower-shoulders are narrow, that same compact constriction, as if *wide* were a word their bodies had forgotten how to spell. They are *lean* without being spare, the way a draft horse is lean—weight on the bone, muscle that has been quietly converted over a working life into something denser, something closer to the bronze-green stone of the spires themselves. When you finally do see one full-length, three days in, on a rope-walk above you in the late lavender light, the figure moves across the gap between two towers with a step that is more like a *transfer*—weight going from one foot to the other without any vertical bob, the body a kind of pendulum that has learned to think ahead of itself.

Their skin is the brown of old wood, but it is not uniform. The shoulders, the upper arms, the small of the back where you sometimes glimpse a sliver when a tunic has slipped, are the deep red-brown of a kiln-fired clay that has been polished by years of wear. The forearms and the lower legs are darker, the color of a basalt-silk wall at the joint of a fold, where the heat has been worked into the surface by the passage of decades. The hands and feet are darker still, almost the purple-brown of an aubergine skin, the way the deepest parts of the bowl walls are aubergine, the way the leaves in shadow are aubergine, the way the entire palette of this place slides toward a single compound color of *heated earth*. The face, when you see it finally, is the lightest part of the body—paler, more clay than wood—and the features are small and fine, with a mouth that is wide and a chin that is not pronounced and eyes that are set deep under a low brow, eyes that you cannot tell the color of in this light, only that they reflect the lavender non-light in a way that makes them look like wet stones.

They wear almost nothing. A wrap of dark fiber around the hips, knotted with a complicated series of hitches you cannot follow. A strip of the same fiber crossed over the chest, more for carrying than for modesty, used to hang a coil of rope, a small stone hammer, a wedge of bone, a half-eaten cake of fermented grain. Their feet are bare and they are *callused* in a way that you do not have a word for—the soles not thickened into a single pad but laid down in overlapping plates, like the leaves of the plants, like the tiles of the floor, a kind of grown armor that has been trained into shape by years on the warm pavement. When one of them walks past you on the rope above, you can hear the soft *tap-tap* of those plate-soles on the fiber of the walkway, and the walkway hums in answer, and the hum travels out into the air and joins the polyphony.

## The Work

They are not workers in the way that you think of workers. They are not at their work; they are *their work*, in the same way a tide is the work of the moon.

Wherever you go, someone is doing something. Not always the same thing. There is a hierarchy of labor, you eventually realize, but it is not a hierarchy of *people*—it is a hierarchy of *tasks*, and a person moves through the tasks through a day the way a note moves through a chord. The youngest ones—shorter, lighter, with the soft chest of someone whose ribs still show—run messages. You can hear them on the rope-walks above, their light quick steps producing a rapid glissando of tile-tone, and the long vowels they call out are the highest notes in the polyphony, the topmost voices in the chorus. The older ones fit tiles. The oldest ones, the ones whose skin has gone almost the color of the bronze-green stone itself, sit in the upper windows of the towers with coils of dark fiber in their laps and *plait*. They plait the long ropes that lash the spires together, plait the nets that catch the falling leaves before they clog the lower streets, plait the harnesses and the small tight mats that are used to carry things between buildings. Their fingers move in a pattern that is not quite a pattern—more like the slow movement of the fronds themselves, as if the plaiters have been growing into their task for so long that the boundary between hand and fiber has become editorial.

The hammers are everywhere. Not metal hammers. Stone hammers, bone hammers, hammers made of the same dark aubergine-brown hardwood that the ladders are made of. They are tapping, always tapping. A tile has risen half a centimeter overnight, pushed up by the growth of a root-tip beneath, and the tap-tap-tap of a small bone hammer re-seats it. A rope is beginning to fray where it passes over a bronze edge, and the fiber is being trimmed and re-whipped with a length of finer cord and the cord is being tapped flush with a wedge of antler. A vine has been mis-routing itself toward a window that does not want it, and a hooked pole is being used to draw it gently sideways, and the place where the hook has gripped the stem is being dressed with a dab of something that smells of iron and warm resin, and the dab is being tapped in with a fingertip.

You never see anyone *building* anything. You only see them *maintaining*. The difference is important and you cannot put your finger on why. The buildings were not built, in the ordinary sense, you begin to think. They were *grown*, or they were *cooled into shape* the way the basalt-silk of the bowl was cooled into shape, and the inhabitants are not so much constructing them as *keeping them in their present form*—adjusting the slow drift, the slow creep, the slow biological creep of red vegetable matter and dark metallic matter and warm bone-ceramic matter, all of which is in motion, all of which is *alive* in some sense of the word that this place has stretched out of shape.

## The Voice

They do not speak to you. They do not speak, in the sense you mean by *speak*—no words, no sentences, no questions directed at your face. But they are talking all the time.

The long vowels are how they talk. *Aaaaaa*, rising, then falling, then rising again, the way a seabird calls across a colony at morning, the way wind makes a sound in an empty pipe. You learn, after a week, to hear the differences. There is a long open *ah* that means *I am here, in this place, doing this thing*. There is a shorter, more clipped *eh* that means *I see you, I notice you, continue*. There is a falling *oo* that means *that is done, the task is complete, attend to the next*. There is a rising *oh* that means *something is wrong, come and look*. There is a kind of trilled *ai-ai-ai* that means *I am climbing, watch the rope*. None of these are *words*, exactly. They are tones, shaped by a throat and a mouth into a vocabulary of pitch and duration, and they pass across the city in overlapping layers the way the tile-chimes pass across the floor—never a silence, never a single voice alone for long, always a polyphony in which any individual utterance is recoverable only by listening carefully, the way any individual leaf in a canopy is recoverable only by looking carefully.

And they laugh. You do not understand why this surprises you so much. The first time you hear it, you are sitting in an arcade, eating a fermented-grain cake that has been left on a stone shelf, and a small surprised human laugh drops out of the canopy above you and gets swallowed by the polyphony. It is a real laugh. A whole laugh, with the breath and the catch and the trailing note of any laugh you have ever heard in your own life, and it is so *recognizable* that for a moment you forget where you are, and then the moment is over, and the laughter continues somewhere else, and you sit with the cake in your hand and the warm tile under you and you do not know what to do with the fact of it.

## The Loop

They eat the leaves.

You have watched this, in glimpses. A hand reaches into a curtain of red foliage and breaks the stem of a low-hanging frond with a practiced twist. The hand brings the frond to a mouth you have not seen. There is the small wet sound of chewing, longer than you would expect for something so fibrous, and the frond is reduced slowly, deliberately, to a green-brown paste that the figure—still mostly hidden in the canopy—swallows with the kind of concentration you associate with a person eating something they grew themselves. The fermented-grain cakes are a second food, you realize: a *prepared* food, dense and dry, kept in the arcades in stacks, wrapped in leaves, and they are not made for any single person. They are made *for the city*, in the way the tiles are fitted *for the city*, in the way the ropes are re-lashed *for the city*. The cakes sit on the shelves and they are eaten by whoever passes, and the shelves are kept stocked, and no one watches the shelves, and you take a cake and the leaves around you rustle in a pattern that might be approval and might just be growth.

There is no waste. You do not see a scrap dropped, a leaf discarded, a bone thrown. When something is finished with—tile, fiber, the dried stalks of a dead frond—it is carried somewhere below the pavement, into the passages cut into the tile substrate, and it is added to the thin smear of red dust that lies between the pavers and in the planting-bowls at the windows. The dust is the system. The dust is what the plants are rooted in, the dust is what the cakes are made of, the dust is what the inhabitants are, you realize one morning, watching a figure descend from a tower window on a rope with the unhurried grace of a person who has done this since they were a child, and you realize that what you are looking at is not so much a person as a *phase* of the dust—a moment of warm mobile organization that the dust has briefly taken, the way a wave is a moment of organization that water has briefly taken.

The dust and the heat and the leaves and the inhabitants are a single thing cycling through itself, slowly, patiently, with the easy indifference of a process that has been running for centuries and intends to run for centuries more.

## The Rest

In the late hours, the city empties upward. The rope-walks go quiet. The lower streets are only your own footfall and the deep hum of the cooling floor.

You look up, once, into the canopy of a tower whose leaves have grown closed over an alcove that has become, without your permission, your sleeping-place. The leaves are layered three and four deep, and behind them, through the gaps, you can see faint warm light—the amber of the flower-interiors, the glow that the blooms give off when they open at night. And in the amber light you can see shapes. Bodies, lying in hammocks of woven fiber, or curled directly on the warm stone of the inner ledge, or stretched out along the bronze-green cornices the way children lie along a sunny wall. Some are still. Some are not quite still—shifting, turning, the way a body shifts in the heat when the heat is *right* and the warmth is coming up through the stone and into the spine and the bones are loose and the breath is slow.

They are sleeping. Or they are doing something that has the same shape as sleeping, in a body that is metabolically closer to a plant than to you. You cannot tell. The breathing of the sleepers joins the polyphony at its lowest register, a slow pulse of in-and-out that you feel in the floor of the alcove as much as you hear it, a subsonic *aaaahhhh* that rises and falls and rises and falls, and the city breathes with them, and the walls breathe, and the leaves grow another centimeter toward whatever differential in the dim light is calling them.

In the morning, a hand will reach out from the canopy and re-fit a tile that has cracked in the night. A voice will call across three streets in a long open *ah*. A small human laugh will drop into the polyphony and get swallowed. The hammers will start. The city will run on itself, the way a body runs on breath, the way a tide runs on the moon, the way a wave runs on the wind that made it and the water that carries it and the shore it is going to.

You do not learn what they are. You do not learn where they came from, or whether they are descendants of anyone, or whether the city and the inhabitants and the dust and the plants are not in fact a single organism that has learned to think in pieces. You do not learn whether the laugh is *kindness* or whether it is a reflex in a system that has reflexes the way a plant has phototropism. You do not learn what they call themselves, because they do not call themselves anything in any way you can hear, and the names you make up for them in your own head do not stick.

You carry the absence of knowing with you, the way you carry the absence of any animal-life in the place, the way you carry the absence of water and the absence of night-sky and the absence of any explanation for how a settlement like this can persist, century after century, in the cool dim of a basin that sits on the back of something vast and hot.

You carry the laughter. That is the thing you carry longest, and you do not know why.
