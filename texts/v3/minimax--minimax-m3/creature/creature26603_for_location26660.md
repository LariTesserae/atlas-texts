What lives in the Fold at the Crown is not a people. It is a manner of being, pressed into the seams.

They are the Woven.

A Woven is not born so much as *coalesced*. It begins as a particular knot of attention in the moss of the wall — a place where the layered green has been packed by some patient builder, layer upon layer, until the packing itself becomes interested in being packed. Sometimes the builder is the one who becomes the Woven. Sometimes it is the visitor, on a long enough stay. Sometimes it is the mountain.

A Woven has no fixed form. It has *material*. A Woven is made of the things the pocket is made of: cane and sapling, fiber and daub, the slow rust of an iron nail that has been weeping its color into the peg for a hundred years. A Woven's bones are bark-rope. A Woven's blood is the condensation that gathers on its skin and runs down in slow silver threads. Its breath is the steam of the place, drawn in and breathed back out in the same slow syncopation as the drips in the ceiling. A Woven smells of ginger and iron and the inside of a closed mouth.

When you meet one, you do not see it so much as *feel it meet you*. There is a thickening of the air beside you. A shape assembles itself out of the wet: first the suggestion of a shoulder where the steam moved strangely, then a fold of what might be skin or might be daub, then an eye — a small, deep, wet eye the color of black peat, in which a thin crack of green-grey light moves like a fish in a well. The eye regards you. The Woven is patient. The Woven is the patience the place is built out of.

A Woven works. Always. This is its single great occupation and its single great joy. It takes the cane and the sapling and it weaves them into the wall. It packs the moss in layers. It forges the iron nails in a small forge that is itself built of folded iron, somewhere further in, where the heat is greatest. It sings, or rather it keeps time with the working, in a language the syllables of which are *the action of building*. To say a word in that tongue is to make a peg, or to drive it, or to feel it take the weight of a wall. The Woven sings because the Woven is building, and builds because it is singing, and these are the same thing.

A Woven does not grow old. A Woven *thickens*. Year by year, layer by layer, it accretes more of itself. The oldest Woven in the Fold is the wall itself — a being so old and so compacted that it has forgotten it was ever anything else. You could not tell it apart from the rock it is pressed against. If you pressed your ear to it, you would hear, very faintly, the working. The youngest Woven is the pale luminous moss at the front of the wall — a being only a season or two old, which is to say a being that is still mostly potential, that has not yet committed to a single arrangement of itself.

A Woven feeds on the heat. The heat rises from below, from the place where the mountain is still cooling into the shape it will hold, and the Woven drinks it the way the cane drinks the rain. Without the heat, a Woven goes dormant. There are folds in the mountain — the Cold Folds, the Lower Seams — where the Woven have fallen into a long sleep, and the walls there are still and dry, and the iron has gone to powder, and the moss is brown and brittle. The Woven of the Crown pity them, in their slow way. They send tendrils down into the rock, when they can, to feel for them. Sometimes a sleeping Woven stirs. Sometimes it does not.

A Woven does not love, exactly. It *incorporates*. If you stay long enough in the Fold — and people do, sometimes, drawn by the heat, by the steam, by the loosening of their edges — the Woven will begin to weave you in. Not cruelly. Not even consciously. They will simply see that there is a gap in the wall that a shoulder could fill, a place where a hand could press daub, a stretch of moss that wants packing. They will hum around you. They will begin to lash. The rope will move of itself, almost gently, and you will find that the lash is not unkind, that the cane is warm, that the place where the wall meets the floor has a small green gate in it, and that the gate opens onto a part of the Fold that is also you.

Some who have entered the Fold have come out again. They speak, when they will speak, of having been a wall for a while, or a peg, or a length of rope that was twisted between someone's palms. They speak of having heard the working from inside the wall, of having kept time with it in the syllable-tongue. They come back to the slit, eventually. They squeeze out, sideways, into the open air of the peak. They walk down the mountain. But they walk slowly, and their feet feel for the pegs under the moss, and their hands, sometimes, reach out as if to touch a wall that is no longer there. Their skin smells of ginger and iron for the rest of their lives. Their hair grows in damp curls. In the night, in a dry room, they weep a little, for no reason they can name.

The Woven do not mourn them. The Woven do not mourn at all. Mourning is a kind of working, and the Woven have other work to do, and the place is still being built.

There is a thing I have not told you, and it is this: that the Woven know they are being woven. They know the rope is older than the rope. They know the peg is older than the iron. They know that somewhere beneath the planks, deeper than the moss, the mountain is still deciding what kind of mountain it will be, and that they are only just now, and only just here, coming together. They know this the way your bones know they are bones. They do not find it strange. They find it warm. They fold themselves, patient and unfinished, into the work.

When you leave the Fold — and you will leave, because the slit is narrow and the rock is warm and the world is on the other side of it, and the world is also a kind of working — you will carry a small piece of the place with you, in the smell of your skin, in the curl of your hair, in the way your heart beats in a room and is given back to you. You will hear, sometimes, in the hiss of a kettle or the creak of a beam in an old house, the syllable-tongue. You will not understand it. You will almost understand it.

That almost is the Fold, letting you in, a little, still.
