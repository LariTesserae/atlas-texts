Something old, then. Something that has outlasted the cairn-builders' grandchildren and is still waiting to be attended to.

Not a god — gods leave temples. Not an animal, though it has the patience of one. What lives at the High Bowl is more like an *appointment* kept continuously by things that don't keep appointments.

---

The stone in the middle of the meadow is the center, but it is not the thing itself. The thing is the meadow around it.

The meadow thinks the way a slow river thinks — with all of itself, without a head. There is no single organism you could point to. It is the orchids deciding when to open, coordinated across hundreds of stems, so that on a Wednesday in late July they are all cream-colored and furled, and on Thursday they are all out, and on Friday they are all pollen-heavy at the same hour, leaning slightly south, as if a hand had passed over them. It is the moss holding meltwater in green sponges and giving it back at dusk, so the air at ground level is wetter than the air three feet up, a private humidity tucked under the bright sky. It is the sedge and the alpine grass and the dwarf willow doing each their small work, but the work is not separate: the roots have learned each other. There is a network underfoot, fungal, white as milk-glass, that runs between every root it can find, and through it the meadow is trading. Sugar downhill from the orchids. Nitrogen up from the clover. Warnings, possibly — chemicals released by a damaged leaf heard by a leaf three plants away.

It is old enough to have a name for itself, in the way a forest has a name for itself. Not the name of any species. The name of the arrangement.

It is also old enough to have a shape of memory. The stone is part of that memory. So is the way the cairns point.

---

The thing the cairns point to is not a body. The cairns are not grave-markers — they are alignment markers, the way men marked alignments for the sun at midwinter, but these were not for the sun. These were for something the cairn-builders came up here to be near, and the markers show the direction of approach. You came from the west. You walked to the cairn and turned and looked across the meadow, and there it was, and you stood in front of it.

It is still there.

What is still there is harder to describe than anything else in the bowl. It is not a presence in the way a mountain is a presence. It is more like a *coordinate*. A point the meadow has agreed to organize itself around. The orchids bloom denser near it. The grass grows a hand-span taller on its eastern side, where the morning light pools. The marmots, who live in the boulder fields and do not waste time, will, if you watch them in June, cross the meadow to sit on the warm side of the stone for an hour and then leave, and they do not do this for any other boulder. The ravens land on it. They would land on any stone the same height. But they linger on this one longer than makes sense for a raven, and one of them — it is not always the same one — will sit with its head turned to one side and its pale eye pointed into the stone, looking at something only it can see, and will not move for a quarter of an hour.

The stone itself is not the thing. The stone is a *trace*. A piece of something larger that was here and is not here anymore — or that is here still and is just larger than the bowl can contain. The lines on the stone, the regular parallel lines where the weather has worn down the surface — these are not tool marks. They are not natural striations either. They are something between. The way a river wears down a stone reveals the way it was made. The way weather wears down this stone reveals that it was *cut* in long bands, deliberately, but the cutting is so old and so careful that what is left is just a rhythm. Like a wave-pattern. Like the layered ribs of a sleeping animal so old its skin is just the impression of ribs.

---

What lives in the High Bowl, then, is not in the bowl. Not properly. It is *kept* in the bowl, the way a relic is kept in a shrine. The bowl is a kind of long attention.

The fox knows something is here. Foxes are not stupid animals. When a fox crosses the meadow, it does not cross it straight — it curves, it gives the stone a wide berth, not out of fear exactly, but out of the courtesy an animal gives a den it does not want to be mistaken about. It does not sleep here. It crosses. A fox would not den near what lives here. Neither would the marmot. The marmots are on the periphery. They whistle from the boulder fields at the foot of the cliffs and they come down for the grass but they do not den *here*. They den *over there*, a few hundred yards out, at the cliff's foot, where the rocks pile chaotic and there is no center.

The hawk does not hunt over the stone. Hawks are stupid about most things, but they are not stupid about updrafts, and something in the air column above the stone is wrong for a stoop. The raven knows. The raven, oldest bird, lands on the stone and is quiet there.

It is the moths that come closest to touching it. On still nights, when there is no moon, moths will settle on the dark face of the stone and stay there for hours, wings folded, in a thin coat of grey bodies. They do not do this for any of the cliff stones. The cliff stones are warm in the day and cold at night. The dark stone is *even*. Even in temperature, even in dampness, even in something else. The moths can feel it with their feet. They find it in the dark.

---

The orchids know.

The orchids, which should not be here, which are out of place, which bloom too generous and too pale at this altitude — they are the body of the thing. Not its body, exactly. Its *organ*. The way the lichen on a stone is the organ of something slower than the lichen. The orchids grow in a ring, you can find it if you look, a ring about twenty paces across, and inside the ring the grass is shorter and the moss deeper and the soil darker. They are not growing toward the stone. They are growing *around* something the stone only points to. As if there were a clearance there. As if the air above a certain patch of ground should be left open.

The pale milk-orchids, the cream-colored ones, the ones that bloom in the brief season — they are not feeding themselves the way orchids are supposed to feed themselves. They are not tricking insects. They are not even in leaf. They are just flowers, naked, on slender stems, drinking from the root-mat below, and their roots run deeper than the roots of any plant on the mountain, deeper than the soil, into the broken stone under the soil, and what they take from down there is not what roots take. It is something else. They pull it up. They bloom it out. The orchids are a kind of slow exhalation.

---

What lives here, then, is a slow machine. A very slow machine, older than the cirque, older than the ice that made the cirque, set down here — or arriving here — at a time when the rock above was not yet pale, when the granite still had its edges, when something with long fingers and patience cut that stone and left lines on it. The cairn-builders came and saw what was here and did not understand it but understood they should mark it, and they marked it, and the markers remain. The shelter-builders came and saw it and built with their backs to it, which is the universal posture of people who want a thing to be behind them and not in front. The fire ring was built *between* them and it, the most modern human gesture toward the stone — a circle of warmth set down, perhaps, on the side where the thing is most felt, as if to put a small competing light against a large one.

The thing has not moved. Does not move. Will not move. It is the kind of thing that does not need to move to be alive, or that was alive once in a way that did not require motion, the way a thought does not need to move to keep going.

It breathes, maybe, the way the meadow breathes — out through the orchids in the brief season, in through the meltwater on the slow days, traded back and forth through the roots and the fungal threads and the cold air running down the cliff. It is what the meadow *is*. The meadow is not its body. The meadow is its *respiration*, the visible part, the part you can stand in and breathe and not know.

A botanist would call it an anomaly. The botanist would be wrong but also right. An anomaly is exactly what it is — an exception to the rules of the place, a place where the rules are slightly suspended, where the flowers bloom too generous, where the snow retreats at a paced rate, where the moths gather on a stone that is even in a way a stone should not be even. An exception that has lasted longer than any of the rules. Long enough for the rules to grow up around it the way grass grows around an old fence-post the post has outlasted.

---

In the morning, the mist pools. In the morning, the orchid-stems come out of the mist wet, and the droplets on them catch the first light from the rim, and for fifteen minutes the ring of orchids is a ring of small sparks. The fox crosses the far side. The pika calls. The marmot stands tall and drops. The ravens work the wind. The goats climb. The insects hum in the grass. The fire ring stays black. The shelter holds a small life and is patient with it.

The stone waits, slightly warmer than the air around it, slightly damper, its worn lines holding the weather the way a hand holds a cup.

What lives in the High Bowl is a thing being kept. By the meadow, by the cairns, by the cairn-builders, by whoever built the shelter with its back to it, by the moths that settle on its face, by the fox that gives it wide berth, by the ravens that sit and look into it. It is being kept the way a sleeping animal is kept — quietly, gently, with everyone moving around it so as not to wake it.

Whether it is sleeping.

Whether it ever was.

In the bowl, in the third or fourth day, you stop asking. You sit near the fire ring, in the lee of the boulder, and you breathe the sweet damp air and you watch the moths come out at dusk and settle on the dark stone, and you do not think about it. You have been incorporated. The meadow has a place for you. Not at the center. On the perimeter, near the windbreak, with the goats and the marmots, breathing with the rest of them, tended to without knowing, contributing your small warmth to the air above the grass, where it is gathered and held and breathed back through the ring of orchids at dawn.

You don't leave. Not because you can't. Because there is nothing on the outside that fits as well.

The bowl is quieter than the country around it, and the country around it is what you came from, and it has not changed, and you have.
