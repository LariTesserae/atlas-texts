The people of the Hanging do not call themselves that. They have a name for themselves, of course, but in the wet air it does not travel, and what reaches the threshold plank is only the sense of them: a household phrase repeated until it has lost its edges, a count of longhouses that no one keeps current. They are, by their own accounting, whoever is currently in the basin. A longhouse counts as inhabited if there is a fire going, or a sleeping mat laid out, or a string of bark-paper lanterns hung between the walls; the rest is in negotiation. Children move between houses the way water moves between leaves, and the adults do not always remember which small person belongs to which roof. This is not neglect. It is the agreement the village has made with the forest, which is that the forest is older, and the children are part of the forest, and the question of whose child is a question for the dry months, when there is time to ask it.

A woman goes up the northern ridge in the morning with a basket on her back and a short-handled hoe, and she cuts clay from a pit that is itself a kind of resident. The pit has been open for as long as anyone can remember. She kneels at its edge and works down through the wet red to the gray beneath, and the clay comes up in her hands in slow heavy slabs, and she lays them in the basket, and the basket goes up on her back, and she comes down the path, and the clay goes onto a long plank where the women of three houses are waiting. They wet it, they trample it, they add to it the dark fibrous rot of a kind of leaf that is not grown but gathered; they let it sit; they use it. The walls they daub with it are not dry even when they are dry. The daub breathes. When the rains come, it darkens; when the rains stop, briefly, in late summer, it lightens; it shrinks, it cracks, the women climb up and patch the cracks, the cracks come back. This is the rhythm of a wall. A wall here is not built; it is *tended*.

The men of the wood work under a foreman who is not loud. He walks the stands with a machete and a length of bark-rope looped over his shoulder, and where he points, they cut, and where he does not, they do not. He is not choosing for taste. He is choosing for the village — for the house that is about to need a new beam, for the fig that is leaning too far, for the lattice that will go in next season. The trees he marks are not always the trees that get cut. Sometimes a marked tree is left standing, and an unmarked one is taken, and no one explains why. The foreman does not explain. He is not explaining because the forest is the conversation, and the village is one half of it, and the other half is not obligated to be legible. When a beam comes down it is wet, and it is heavy, and the men carry it between them with the bark-rope under their arms, and the bark shreds their shoulders, and they do not speak much. They do not need to. They know whose house the beam is going to, and who in that house will meet them at the edge of the path, and the sound that beam will make when it is set into the wall, and the sound it will make in a year when the new shoots have found it and begun to weave it into themselves.

The children are the most honest thing in the basin. They are not yet a generation deep enough in the agreement to pretend they are not also part of the forest. They run ropes of vine-fiber between the houses — for drying herbs, for hanging clothes, for climbing, for the satisfaction of a taut line, for the reason that climbing is what their bodies want, and the plants reward them with bruises, and the bruises heal, and the children grow. In the wet months they learn to swim in puddles. The puddles are an inch deep, sometimes two; the children are small enough that this is enough. They kneel in the water and they call out numbers to each other in a count that does not match anything I can see — not the puddles, not each other, not the frogs that are watching from the ferns. The numbers may be a game. The numbers may be a way of measuring something I am not equipped to see. The children do not explain. They are not obligated to explain. They are children, and the basin is theirs in a way it is not yet anyone else's.

A dog sleeps through most of the day on the plinth. The plinth is the flat shelf of stone at the center of the basin, the only place where the canopy opens, and the dog is there because the dog is the dog and the plinth is warm in the late afternoon, when the sun finds a hole in the cloud, and the dog has been asleep for hours and chooses now, for reasons of his own, to stand up and bay once at the canopy. The bay goes out and does not come back. It is taken by the moss. It is taken by the birds. The dog lies down again, satisfied.

The birds are not birds. The birds are a population. The two-noted one — the one whose second note is a little slurred — sits in the upper canopy by the hundreds and passes the second note back and forth like a rumor going around a long table. You cannot find the source. The note is not a note. It is a conversation, and you are overhearing one edge of it. There are catbirds, which do what catbirds do, and there is a barbet somewhere in the middle canopy that produces, at intervals, a single repeated note like a hammer on a wooden bell, and the note does not vary, and you begin to suspect that the barbet does not know it is making the note, that the note is something the barbet is being done by, the way a tree is done by the light.

The frogs live in three sizes and three frequencies, and the night is the chord they tune. The smallest, no bigger than a thumbnail, sings so high it is almost not there. The middle one, the size of a little finger, holds the middle. The thumb-sized one, lower, slower, the one you feel in your sternum if you stop walking — that one is the root. They do not start at the same time. They are forever sliding into each other, finding the chord, losing it, finding it again. A frog does not sing alone. A frog sings into a chorus, and the chorus is the song.

The insects are not a population you can count. Around certain trees, in the late morning, the air thickens until the tree seems to smoke. Stand within the smoke and you feel them on your eyelids, on the inside of your lip, in the corners of your eyes. They are not biting. They are not anything. They are there. They are the air being alive. They hatch from the standing water in the barrels at the corners of the houses, and the small fish eat the larvae, and the frogs eat the small fish, and the larger things eat the frogs, and the larger things eat the larger things. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is sitting still.

The larger things — the ones that crash in the understory at intervals and break branches, the ones the people do not look up at because they know what it is — these are not named here. They are not unseen. They are not often seen. They move through the basin on a clock of their own, and the people know the clock the way they know the rain, by the sound of it through the canopy, by the particular silence that follows. When the silence comes, the conversation drops a register. A child is pulled in from a rope. A dog is called. A lantern that was not lit is touched, briefly, by a hand, and not lit, and the larger thing passes, and the conversation resumes. The people do not talk about this. There is nothing to talk about. The larger things are part of the basin. The basin is the larger things' basin, also.

The wasps live in the upper eaves of the longhouses, and they are not evicted. This is not a kindness. It is a calculation. The wasps eat the insects that would otherwise eat the thatch. The wasps are also a presence in the room when a new shoot is being woven into the lattice, and the person doing the weaving is stung, and the sting is hot, and the weaving continues. The wasps are part of the agreement. The agreement has wasps in it.

The great tree at the back of the basin is the largest tree, and that is why flowers are brought to it, and that is also why flowers are brought to it. The people do not distinguish these reasons. They walk up the low rise in ones and twos, with a flower, or a stem, or a handful of something, and they lay it at the base of the trunk, and they go back down. The flowers stay there until they rot. The tree goes on. I asked, and the answer I was given was that the tree is the tree, and the flowers are the flowers, and the bowl is the bowl, and the rain is the rain. I understood, eventually, that this was not a deflection. This was the answer.
