No animal has ever lived here. What lives here is older than animals, and slower, and in some sense still.

The moss lives here. The first thing to say. The dominant tenant, the one that has done the most work, the way a tenant who has been in a place long enough becomes the place. There are several kinds, layered according to the logic of their patience. Cushion moss, dense and lensed, holds the most water — each cushion a small reservoir, a cupped palm of green-black that gives back moisture slowly to the air. Sheet moss spreads flat against the rock, papery in the way liverworts are papery, anchoring itself with rhizoids so fine they are almost theoretical. Sphagnum trails in the wettest places, pale-tipped, almost luminous in the dark. Liverworts hang from the ceiling in ribbons that look like wet paper until you notice they have been moving — not now, not in this minute, but across months, extending a millimeter here, retracting a millimeter there, feeling along the stone with the patience of a blind hand. Clubmosses lean sideways, finding the cracks where the rivulet runs. Ferns sit at the level where the humidity is most constant, their fronds held out in the cool air like hands held up to rain that will never fall.

None of these are individuals. They are a community the way a reef is a community — a thing that has become more than the sum of its parts, that has its own weather, its own dampness, its own particular stillness. Together they do something the cold alone could not do: they hold the humidity up. They breathe it out, they breathe it back in, they trade it between their tissues and the air, and the air of the pocket is a thing they have made. A body walking in would feel it as a closeness, a pressing-in, the way a greenhouse feels, except cold. The moss has made a climate for itself. It is the climate's only resident.

The water lives here. The rivulet is small — a finger's width, less in places — but it is the most articulate thing in the pocket. It has a voice, or the closest thing to a voice the place contains. It speaks a single tone, or near-single, the drip-drop-drip that has been going on for longer than the moss has been growing, or so the place seems to suggest. It carries mineral from somewhere. It carries the faint taste of stone. It smells of nothing. It is what the moss drinks, what the ice on the ceiling is melted from, what the plate on the far wall is warm against. The water is the medium. It is the broker between the technology and the plants and the cold outside, the way blood is the broker in a body.

The built things live here, if they live. The cylinder is the most present. It has been here long enough that the moss has tried to climb it and failed — refused by the surface, slid away from it. This is its only concession to time. It is unchanged in every other way: same diameter, same smoothness, same color (pale, the color of bone or old tin), same temperature, which is the temperature of the air around it, neither warmer nor colder. It is vertical. It does not lean. It does not rust. It does not corrode. If you put your hand on it — and no hand has ever been put on it, but if — you would feel a thing that is not quite a vibration, not quite a stillness. Something in between. As if the cylinder is listening at a frequency your hand cannot hear. As if it is a part of a sentence you have walked into the middle of.

The lattice lives here. It is the most diffuse of the built things — fine wire, no thicker than a spider's thread, threaded through the rock in a pattern that suggests purpose without revealing what purpose is. It hums. The hum is below hearing. You feel it as a held breath in the room, a slight tension in the eardrum, the way you feel the air before a thunderclap that never comes. It has been humming for so long that the ice has crept over part of it without damping it, without silencing it, without changing its pitch. Whatever it is doing, it is doing it continuously and without effort. It is the closest thing to a heartbeat the pocket has.

The plate lives here. It is the warmest thing, and it is warm without explanation. Set flat against the far wall, glass or ice or something between, the rivulet breaks across it and runs down in a sheet. The warmth is not heat, exactly. It is the held warmth of something that has always been warm, in a place that should not allow anything to be warm. You can feel it from a meter away, the way you feel a sleeping animal, the way you feel a hand held near your cheek. The plate does not work at it. It is simply warm, in the way a stone is warm in the sun — except this stone has never not been warm, and the sun is ten meters above, through permafrost. The plate is what keeps the pocket a pocket. Without it the cold would close in. Without it the moss would freeze. Without it the rivulet would stop. The plate holds open what the permafrost wants to swallow.

And then there is what I am not sure how to name.

The place itself lives here.

Not as a metaphor — not the way a place "lives" in a poem. As a thing. As an organism in the loose sense, an ecology that has achieved a kind of slow coherence. The pocket is a body. The moss is its skin. The water is its blood. The cylinder is its bone — its anchor, its still point. The lattice is its nerve. The plate is its hearth, or its fever, or its stubborn refusal to die. The whole thing breathes — that bass note in the air, that texture of respiration, that creak of the permafrost overhead like a rib cage. The whole thing has a metabolism. Water comes in, somewhere, from the melt along the fracture's lip or from a pipe half-embedded in the wall or from condensation on the cold ceiling. Water goes out, down through the saturated soil, into whatever is below. The moss takes what it needs. The technology takes what it needs, or gives what it gives. The pocket is a system that has been running long enough to be its own purpose. It does not need to be observed to keep going. It does not need to be understood. It only needs to be left.

What lives here, then, is a kind of patience. A patience that has made itself physical. A patience that has built itself a chamber and a climate and a small warm plate to keep itself company. It does not think quickly. It does not move quickly. It is the moss's slowness and the water's slowness and the cylinder's patience and the lattice's held note, all gathered up into a thing that has no name but that you can feel in the air when you stand inside the pocket and stop pushing and start receiving.

It is not waiting for anything. That is the strange part. It is not waiting for us, or for whatever was supposed to come. It does not appear to want. It is just here, the way a tide pool is here, the way a forest is here, the way a stone is here. It is the moss continuing. It is the water continuing. It is the warmth continuing. It is the dark continuing, but as a soft dark, a dark with something in it, a dark that is not empty.

If you had to point at one thing and say this is what lives here, you would point at the plate. Or at the cylinder. Or at the rivulet. Or at nothing in particular — at the air between them, at the damp that holds them all. You would point at the place itself, and the place would not mind.
