# What Lives in Kharad-Ruun

## I. The Cousins

In the lowest galleries, in the permanent ultramarine twilight where the engineered light begins to thin, there is a thing that does not have a name in any language you would recognize. The deep-blues call them *chthonic*, which is a borrowing from a dead tongue and means, roughly, "of the below." The cynics, who live among them and have made a small philosophy of refusal out of the arrangement, call them *the cousins*. This is also wrong, but it is a warmer wrong.

They are the size of a thumb, the largest of them. Most are smaller. The smallest are the size of a flake of stone, and these are the ones that swarm — that climb the chiseled walls in pale slow rivers, that press themselves into the tool-striae left by their human neighbors, that fill the cracks and seams of the basalt with their soft bodies until the rock itself seems to be breathing.

They have no eyes. They have no mouths. The thing they have instead of a mouth is on the underside of the body, a smear of capillary tissue that presses against the stone and drinks from it. The stone is dry — everywhere in Kharad-Ruun the stone is dry, drier than any stone has a right to be — but it is not *empty*. There are trace minerals. There are bound water molecules locked into the crystal lattices. There are metals in tiny quantities, trace elements, the ghost-chemistry of the basalt. The cousins find this water. They find these metals. They pull them up through the soft pads of their feet and into their bodies, where the metals accumulate and the water is used and the rest is excreted as a fine pale dust that drifts down to the floor and is, in turn, eaten by the next cousin down.

They do not move quickly. They do not need to. They are patient the way stone is patient — except they are not stone, and their patience is a living patience, a hunger that has been running for a thousand years and has not stopped.

When you step on one — and you will, the first few times you walk the deep galleries, until you learn the particular gait of a person who knows they are not alone on the floor — it bursts. The skin is thin. The body inside is a kind of pressurized packet of the minerals it has consumed, and when the skin breaks, the powder inside flashes. A small bright spit of blue-white light, lasting less than a heartbeat, and then it is gone. The deep-blues do not mourn these. They are dust; they will be eaten again by the cousins around them. The mountain eats its dead.

Listen, in the deep galleries, and you can hear them. It is a low hum, a vibration at the edge of hearing. A billion soft bodies feeding on a billion small stones. The deep-blues call this sound *the digestion of the city*, and they find it comforting. They are born into it. They sleep in it. When a deep-blue dies — and deep-blues die, as all the city's people die, of the slow dark things the doctors do not have names for — the body is laid out on the floor of the lowest gallery the family can find, and the cousins come. In the morning there is nothing left but bones, and even the bones are smaller than they were. The deep-blues regard this with a kind of piety. The mountain takes back what it lent.

There is a tenderness in this, and a horror, and the deep-blues do not seem to feel the difference.

## II. The Deep-Blues

The deep-blues are short and dark and broad. They are pale-eyed in a way that catches the ultramarine light and turns it, briefly, into something softer. They have the heavy shoulders of people who have spent ten thousand years swinging chisels in narrow spaces, and the small careful hands of people who have learned to feel for the cousins underfoot and step around them. They are the lowest caste, and they know it. They are also the most numerous.

A deep-blue child is born in a chamber hollowed from the basalt, and the chamber is lit by the same copper-ore current that lights every wall in the lower tier, and the first sound the child hears is the hum of the cousins seething in the next gallery. In the first week of its life the mother carries the child down to the cousins' level — it is usually the mother; the deep-blues are matrilineal, by a custom so old that no one remembers its origin — and she presses the small soft foot of the child against the small soft body of a cousin, and the cousin flashes, and the child cries, and this is the introduction. *You are of this place.* The child will not remember it. The cousin will not know. But it is done, and it counts.

The deep-blues cut the galleries. This is their work, the work they have done for a thousand years, and they are very good at it. A deep-blue chisel-master can read a wall of basalt the way you might read a face: the grain, the fault, the place where the stone is tired and will give. They work in pairs. One holds the lamp — a copper-ore lamp, the size of a fist, throwing a steady cold light — and the other swings. The strokes are deliberate. The strokes are slow. Each stroke is named, in the silent language of the chisels: *this is mine, this is my daughter's, this is for the city that will outlast me.*

They do not speak while they work. The galleries carry sound too well for speaking to be necessary, and the chisels speak anyway, in their tapping code. *I am here. I am cutting. I am extending.* The new gallery grows by perhaps a meter a day. The chisel-marks stay fresh. The cousins come in behind the chiselers and eat the dust.

When a deep-blue finishes a chamber — when the last stroke has been struck, the last lamp set down, the last wall swept — they stand at the doorway and carve, into the lintel, a single phrase in the old script. It is always the same phrase. *Let it be known that we were here.* And then they move on to the next chamber, and the cousins come behind them and begin their slow eating of the spall.

## III. The Cyan-Blues and the Mules

Above the deep-blues, in the working cyan light of the middle tier, the air is different. It is still dry, but it is warmer, and there is a faint grit on the skin that you cannot see and can taste only if you know to taste for it. The mineral aerosol from the upper vents has drifted down. It is on everything. The cyan-blues do not mind it. They have been breathing it since they were children.

The cyan-blues are artisans. They forge the glass-iron, they run the looms, they tend the pneumatic tubes, they keep the engines fed with ore and the heat-exchange towers breathing their mineral breath. They are slightly taller than the deep-blues, slightly paler — their skin has not seen the dim ultramarine for a generation or more. Their eyes are pale in a different way, a sharper way. They make things. They are the hands of the city, the way the deep-blues are the jaws.

Their hands are the hands of people who work. The fingers are square at the tips. The nails are filed down to nothing to keep them out of the way of fine work. They wear gloves of woven glass-iron fiber — a substance so fine it feels like cloth but will not cut, and will not burn, and will not wear through. Their aprons are heavy. Their tools are kept in leather cases that they oil once a month with the small ration of rendered fat the upper engines provide.

And in the pneumatic-tube stations, where the carts run on their whisper-quiet rails, where the compressed air hisses softly in its pipes, where the loads of ore and finished beams and the small stamped tokens of glass-iron are shuttled up and down the city's vertical arteries — there are the mules.

The mules are not like the cousins. The cousins are wild, or were wild, or are pretending to be wild. The mules are bred. The mules are *made*. They are the second-most-valuable secret of Kharad-Ruun, after the formula of the glass-iron itself, and they are kept in warm stone stables between the cyan and the deep levels, where the temperature is just right and the air is just thick enough and the cousins do not come up this high but the memory of them is faint and constant in the stone.

A mule is about the size of a large dog. It stands on six legs, the middle pair slightly longer than the others, set wide for stability on the narrow rails. It has no eyes. Where the eyes would be there is a smooth domed plate of bone, hairless, warm to the touch. It has no hair anywhere. Its skin is loose and soft and a kind of pinkish-grey, like the inside of a shell, and underneath the skin the heat-exchangers run — a network of fine channels that pick up waste heat from the mule's own metabolism and use it to keep the body warm in the cool galleries, so that the mule is always radiating a faint gentle warmth, like a stone that has been in the sun.

The mules are docile. They have been bred for a thousand years to be docile, to pull without complaint, to stand for hours in the dark between jobs and not be distressed by the dark. They do not bray. They do not whinny. They make one sound, which is a low soft humming, very different from the cousins' hum — warmer, more breathy, a sound that the cyan-blues say is the mule's way of saying *I am here, I am warm, I am ready.* The cynics say the mule is only digesting. The deep-blues, who occasionally are sent up to the cyan level to consult on a fault in the rock, say the mule is singing to the cousins it cannot smell.

The mules know the stations. They walk the rails by memory, by the feel of the slight gradient, by the warmth of the air moving through the stations' vents. They pull the carts with a steady patient force. They are unhurried. They are the only unhurried things in the city.

A mule lives about thirty years, which is longer than a deep-blue and shorter than a cyan-blue. When a mule dies, it is taken apart. The heat-exchanger channels are harvested for reuse in the engines. The bones are ground and fed, eventually, to the cousins far below. The hide is cured and made into the leather that the cyan-blues keep their tools in. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is wasted in Kharad-Ruun.

## IV. The White-Blues of the Vents

The highest tier is almost too bright to look at.

The light here is a hot white, a radiance that falls from the engineered veins in the upper walls and from the open mouths of the heat-exchange towers, where the superheated mineral vapor hisses out into the rare thin air above the city. The priesthood of the vents work here, and they are pale — paler than anyone else in Kharad-Ruun, paler than you would think possible in a city of dark-skinned people, because the light has leached them. They wear robes of heavy woven glass-iron, stained permanently grey-brown by the mineral aerosol that drifts through the upper air. They wear breathers over their faces, mask-and-respirator combinations that hiss softly as they work. They wear goggles with thick lenses of polished obsidian. They do not live long.

A priest of the vents who reaches forty is considered to have had a long and fortunate life. Most do not. Their lungs fill slowly with the grit of the metallic dust. Their eyes, behind the goggles, go first red and then a kind of permanent rheum. They cough. They cough more. They cough up things that should not be coughed up, things that glitter in the sputum, things that the deep-blues will not eat because they are wrong. They do not complain. They are the heart of the city.

The priests feed the magma chamber.

This is their work, the only work that matters, the work that everything else in the city exists to support. They descend, once a day, in the great lift — a glass-iron cage that drops a full kilometer through the city's vertical spine, past all the tiers, past the deep galleries, past even the cousins' domain, down into the heat. The lift is uninsulated. The priests sweat. They remove their breathers at the bottom because the air, this close to the magma, is too hot to filter. They breathe the metallic vapor and they cough and they continue.

They stand at the edge of the magma chamber, which is a lake of molten basalt that glows a color no one has a name for — orange is too simple, red is too dull, white is too clean. It is a color that has not been seen on the surface of the world for ten thousand years, and the priests look at it every day, and they are not afraid of it.

They feed it. They have learned, over a thousand years of patient experimentation, exactly what the magma wants. Certain ores, in certain proportions, dropped at certain times. The priests have a vocabulary for this, a ritual of selection. They are not praying. They are engineering. But it looks like prayer, to anyone watching from above.

The magma accepts the ores. It stirs. It sends up a slow pillar of heat, a column of radiance, and the heat-exchange towers drink from this heat and turn it into the power that lights the city and drives the pneumatic tubes and pressurizes the squeeze.

And then the priests go back up, in the lift, coughing, and they take off their breathers and they sit in their chambers in the upper tier, and they look out through the windows of glass-iron at the brown veil of metallic dust that hangs over the desert below, and they think about the future.

They do not speak of the future.

But every gallery they order cut, every beam they order forged, every child they raise in the dim blue light, is a small protest against the ending they all know is coming. The mountain is young. The mountain will not be young forever. One day the basalt will weather. One day the engines will fail. One day the priests will climb the lift for the last time and find the chamber cold.
