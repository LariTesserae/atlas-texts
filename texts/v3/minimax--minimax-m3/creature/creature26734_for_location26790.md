# The Dwellers of the Hollow

They are called, by themselves, **the Stacked**. The name is not metaphor. It is geography.

## The Body in the Crowd

A Stacked is small. This is the first fact and the most important, and it is the reason any of the rest can be true. An adult stands roughly to the second joint of a human thumb, though no Stacked has ever made this comparison and would not understand the reference if it were offered. They are long rather than tall, low-slung, built to move through cracks that an outside observer would not recognize as cracks at all—the seams between a fitted bone and the husk that holds it, the narrow channel where a rope has worn a groove into the wall it binds, the breathing space between two sleeping bodies pressed head-to-tail in the third-tier dormitories. They have six limbs and no joint that locks. Every knee and elbow and wrist is a thing still in negotiation with its owner, still capable of bending the wrong way in a crisis, and a Stacked child spends its first season learning the grammar of its own skeleton the way a human child learns speech: by imitation, by falling, by being corrected by older bodies that have themselves only recently finished being corrected.

The skin is the color of the husks, which is the color of the walls, which is the color of almost everything. A pale, mottled ochre that runs toward gray in the lower limbs and toward a faint copper at the joints, where the chitin thickens into flexible plates. There is no hair anywhere on the body except a stiff brush along the forearms and the trailing edge of the lowest pair of legs, and the brush is sensory rather than decorative—each filament is rooted in a small ganglion that reads the air's vibration, the way a cat's whiskers read the dark. The Stacked do not see well. They see *enough*. The great eyes that dominate the face are dark and convex and gather what light there is the way a cupped hand gathers rain, slowly, and what they gather is enough to distinguish a moving shape from a still one at close range, a predator's outline from a prey's, a child's small body from an adult's larger one. For the rest they trust the brush, and the soles of the feet, which are also thickly populated with the same ganglia, and the long inner ears that curve down along the jaw and read the place as a kind of music. A Stacked who has lived a long life in one quarter of the hollow can tell you, with eyes closed and feet flat on the floor, what is happening in the next quarter: who is making, who is hunting, who is dying, who has just been born.

The face is wide and flat and expressive in ways a human face is not. There is a soft mouth at the center, used for speech and for the careful manipulation of small objects, and above it a ridge of cartilage that flexes with emotion the way eyebrows flex in creatures elsewhere—a furrowing for anger, a lifting for surprise, a slow lateral drift for the particular feeling that the Stacked have a word for and that translates roughly as *the weight of all the ones below me*. They do not weep. They cannot. The tear ducts are vestigial, sealed shut in infancy, because there has never been enough moisture in the air to make weeping useful and the body, given enough generations, is ruthless about such economies.

## The Making and the Makers

A Stacked is born knowing nothing and learns almost everything in the first three years by being in the way. The hollow is not a place one can step aside from. A child is handed from set of hands to set of hands the way a tool is passed along a workshop, and the hands that hold it are the hands that will later teach it to hold a blade, to fit a bone into a wall, to read the floor by its strata. There is no school. There is no separation between the work and the rest of life, because there is no place in the hollow where the work is not happening. The sound of making is continuous. It is the second sound of the hollow, after the sound of the creatures, and a Stacked raised in one part of the settlement can hear, even in sleep, the difference between the tapping of a nearby kin-group and the more distant tapping of kin-groups further along the stack, the way a person raised near a bell-tower can tell the hour from across a city.

Tools are personal in a way that approaches the religious. A blade is not made and then used. A blade is made and then *worn*, for years, against the thigh or the palm of the one who will wield it, until the shape of the bone has been altered by the shape of the body and the shape of the body has been altered by the shape of the bone, and the two are no longer separable. A Stacked who is handed an unfamiliar tool will sometimes, in the first few minutes, hold it wrong. The hands do not know. The hands must be re-taught. A blade that has been worn for a long time by a maker who has died is sometimes buried with that maker in the stratum below, and sometimes—if the maker was great, if the blade was the one with which the seventh-tier western wall was fitted in the year of the long quiet—is taken up by an apprentice whose hands are still young enough to be shaped, and the apprenticeship is considered to have begun.

Cords are braided of fiber, hair, gut, and the shed skins of certain of the smaller creatures, and the braiding is done in company, always in company, because the fingers are small and the materials are fine and a single set cannot manage the tension alone. A circle of four or five will sit together for the better part of a day and pass a growing cord between them, and during this time they speak, or sing, or simply listen to the hollow around them, and the making of the cord is the making of the talk is the making of the small political adjustments that keep a kin-group cohesive across the hours when there is no crisis to bind them. A finished cord is signed by the hands that braided it. The signature is a particular sequence of knots at one end, and a cord can be traced, in the way that a piece of writing can be traced, to the circle that made it.

Vessels are polished from carapace. There is no pottery. There is no glass. A vessel holds what a vessel must hold, which is not water—for there is no water—but the fine gray powder that drifts from the ceiling in certain seasons, and which is collected and stored and traded in small measured amounts because it is the only thing in the hollow that contains, in trace quantities, the minerals that a Stacked body cannot synthesize from the fungi and the prey it eats. The vessels are precious. They are passed down. They are also, sometimes, used as currency, and the larger ones are displayed in the upper tiers as a kind of household wealth, the way a human household might display silver.

## The Stacks

A Stacked is born into a tier and lives, mostly, in that tier and the two adjacent to it, because the ladders are narrow and the climbing is constant and a body does it best in the company of bodies that have done it all their lives. The lowest tier is the first settlement and is not climbed to often; it is dark and compressed and its walls have the particular density of things that have been bearing weight for longer than anyone can count. A Stacked who goes down to it goes down to bury a tool, or to retrieve a vessel, or to perform the small ceremonies that are performed there at certain seasons—ceremonies whose meanings have been partly forgotten and which are therefore performed with great care, the way a person repeats a phrase in a language they no longer speak. The middle tiers are where most of the living is done. The upper tiers are where the new construction happens, where the young builders are trying the joints that will become the seventh or eighth settlement, where the wind from the worn rim sometimes makes its way down in a faint thin breath that a Stacked who has never been outside might be excused for not recognizing as wind.

The walls are alive in the way that a coral reef is alive. The fungi climbs them in slow pale lobes, encouraged to grow in the joints and along the cords, discouraged in the windows and the ladder-ways by being eaten, regularly and reverently, by the small creatures that the Stacked keep for this purpose and for several others. A wall that has been well-tended has a particular texture: smooth where the fungi has been held back, ridged and soft where it has been allowed to spread, faintly luminous at the seams where the bone and the husk meet the living growth and the three are no longer distinguishable. A Stacked raised in a well-tended wall knows the wall the way a human raised in a house knows the house—where the floor creaks, where the drafts come from, where the small predator is most likely to emerge at certain hours of the cycle, because the cycles of the hollow are not the cycles of the world outside and a Stacked does not think in days and nights but in the long slow rises and falls of the drift of insects overhead, which thicken and thin on a rhythm of their own.

## The Speech and the Names

The language is dense. It has to be. With so many bodies in so small a space, the slightest raise of the voice carries to a dozen unintended ears, and so the language has developed into a thing mostly conducted at the volume of breath, with a vocabulary that is heavy in nuance and light in volume and a grammar that puts most of the meaning at the end of the sentence, where it can be modulated, qualified, retracted. A Stacked will often begin a sentence and not finish it, and the unfinished sentence is itself a kind of statement—*I was about to say something and have decided not to, and the fact that I did not say it is what I am saying now*. Outsiders, if there were any, would mistake this for reticence. It is the opposite. It is the most economical form of conversation a crowded people has ever developed.

Names are given at the second year, when the child has survived long enough to be considered a person and not a possibility, and they are given by the kin-group, not by the parents, because in the hollow the parents are not the only ones who will raise the child and the name is a thing the entire tier will have to use. Names are descriptive at first—a child might be *Of-the-Western-Rope* or *Small-Gray-One* or *Who-Came-After-the-Long-Silence*—and they are usually changed once, in adolescence, when the person has done the thing that will define them, and the new name is the name they will carry until they die and are buried in the stratum below and their name is spoken, sometimes, by those who climb down to the first tier for the ceremonies. There is no writing. There is no need for writing. The place is the text. The compressed floors are the text. The worn blades in the lower strata are the text. A Stacked who wanted to remember something did, generations ago, what a Stacked still does: they put it in the wall.

## The Predation and the Coexistence

The fauna is not separate from the Stacked. It is not even, in the deepest sense, the environment. It is the household. A Stacked shares its tier with perhaps two hundred small creatures of various kinds at any given moment, and these creatures are not pests, exactly, though some of them bite. They are neighbors. They are the ones who eat the fungi where it should not grow. They are the ones whose shed skins are braided into the cords. They are the ones whose young are sometimes eaten by Stacked children learning to eat, the way a human child might be given a hard candy—an early, gentle introduction to the fact that the world contains things that can be consumed.

The larger predators are a different matter. A Stacked who goes up to the upper tiers alone does not always come back down, and the Stacked know this and have made a peace with it the way a fishing village makes a peace with the sea—not by ignoring the danger but by building a life whose rhythms incorporate it. There are certain hours when the upper tiers are climbed in groups. There are certain ladders that are not used after the second sleep. There are certain small rituals performed at the base of certain ladders that are not, strictly speaking, necessary, and that everyone performs anyway, because the performance is itself the point. The hollow is not safe. The hollow has never been safe. The hollow is the only place there is, and the predators are part of it, and a Stacked raised from infancy in the sound of their movement comes to find the sound, in time, as comforting as a human raised near a river finds the sound of water—something that was there before they were born and that will be there after, something that does not require their attention to continue.

## The Self in the Strata

A Stacked knows who they are by knowing where they are. To ask *who are you* of a Stacked is to receive, first, an answer about the tier—*I am of the third, of the eastern rope, of the kin-group called Small-in-the-Rain*—and only later, if the question is pressed, an answer about the individual. The self is not the first fact. The self is the last fact, the small specific thing that sits inside the larger structures of tier and kin-group and trade-circle and apprenticeship, the way a single blade sits inside the larger structure of the wall it helps to hold up. To be a Stacked is to be useful in a particular place. To die is to be placed in the stratum below, and the stratum presses down, and the body becomes part of the floor on which the next generation will build their second settlement, and the name is spoken for a while by those who remember, and then is spoken less often, and then is not spoken at all, except as a single word in the long compound name of a child born several generations later, who carries it forward into the next tier the way a fossil carries forward the shape of something that lived in the stratum above.

This is not mournful. It is not, to the Stacked, even especially sad. It is the way the place is. The place has been doing this for longer than the Stacked have had a word for *long*, and the word they have for *long* is itself a compound of the word for *down* and the word for *pressed*, and the word for *time* is a compound of the word for *strata* and the word for *remembering*, and a Stacked who uses these words is not making a metaphor. They are describing, with the precision their language allows, what they actually see when they look at the floor on which they stand.

## The Knowledge of the Seal

The Stacked do not know they are sealed.

This is the last and strangest thing about them, and it is not a deficiency. It is the result of a long and complete adaptation. To know one is sealed, one would have to imagine being unsealed, and to imagine being unsealed one would have to imagine a place that was not the hollow, and the Stacked have no such place in their vocabulary. They have words for *up* and *down* and *across* and *the rim*—the rim is a word, and an important one, because the rim is where the wind comes from in the wet season, when the wind comes at all—but they have no word for *outside*, because outside is not a direction. It is not anywhere. It is the absence of the hollow, and the absence of the hollow is not something the language has ever needed to describe.

When a Stacked climbs to the upper tiers and looks up through the amber milk of the filtered light, past the drift of slow insects, past the worn suggestion of the rim, what they see is the ceiling. It is a low and beautiful and very far ceiling, and they know it as well as they know the floor, and they do not wonder what is beyond it, because *beyond* is a word that applies to the next tier, and the tier after that, and the long slow climb of the stacked dwellings into the amber light, and not to anything else. The ceiling is the ceiling. The wind comes from it sometimes. The wind is enough.

They are the Stacked. They have always been the Stacked. The creatures move in the cracks. The fungi climbs the walls. The predators take what is owed. The cords are braided in company. The blades are worn against the thigh. The children are handed from hand to hand. The names are given and changed and carried down into the strata when the bearer dies, and the strata press down, and the next tier is built, and the sound goes on.

It has gone on for a very long time.

It will go on.
