What lives here is what has agreed to stay.

The colonists came in ships with sealed water-tanks and stayed because they came to stay, and the staying has been long enough now that the youngest of them do not remember leaving. They do not remember the water-worlds their great-grandparents spoke of in the long evenings — the wet ones, the ones where the rain fell sideways and the rivers ran downhill and you could stand in a field and let the sky fill your lungs with something free. They hear the stories the way one hears about a grandparent's childhood: politely, without true belief. They have been born into the dim.

The old ones still believe it. The nine-generation families, the ones whose bones have thickened along the lineage, whose blood runs darker and slower than the newcomers' — they remember in the body. They feel, sometimes, the pull of a wetness their great-grandparents had and they do not. They do not speak of it often. They have learned the shape of the grief and they have made a peace with it that is not so different from the peace the pillars have made with the wind, which is to say it is a peace that goes on happening.

They are tall. The gravity here is slightly less and they have grown into it across the generations, stretched a thumb's length taller than their ancestors were, longer in the limbs, narrower in the chest. The narrowness is not a vanity; it is a thermodynamics. Less mass to heat, less surface to lose water through, less lung to fill with the thin violet air. They are built for retention. Their skin is dry and pale and slightly chalky where the dim sun has never quite reached it, and slightly yellowed where it has, and the difference between the two tones is one of the small calibrations by which a colonist can be read for age and station.

Their eyes are large. The light is dim and the eyes have learned to take more of it, and the corners have grown a small fold — not quite a nictitating membrane, not yet, but a thickening of the skin that the old-timers can pull half across the eye in the long evenings when the methane rises and the air goes sharp. The newcomers do this with masks. The locals do it with their faces.

The newcomers arrive every few years, in ships that come down through the violet haze and settle on the long flat rock outside the settlement, and they stand on the plateau and they look around and they ask the first question. The locals have learned to answer it before it is asked.

The first question is always about the water.

The second question is about the wind, because the wind has met them already, on the way down from the ship, and is still meeting them, and will not stop. The third question is about the singing, because the pillars have begun to sing, and the call-birds have begun to call, and the hum of the shuttles on their cables has joined in, and the newcomer is standing in the middle of a chord they did not expect, and they ask what it is, and the locals say *that is the place talking* and the newcomer does not understand and will not for some years.

The locals live in the pillars.

The pillars were not built for this. They were — and are — their own things, their own slow lives, their own ten-thousand-year chemistries that the colonists have only begun to map. But the pillars are cooperative. This is the word the locals use, and they use it seriously. The pillars can be coaxed. Certain fields — fields that are not fields as physics has named them, certain arrangements of intention and frequency and a patience that is itself almost a field — can be applied to a growing pillar, and over decades the pillar will respond. It will thicken here. It will thin there. It will hollow itself a chamber, and seal that chamber against its porous breathing skin, and grow a window in the wall of that chamber so thin that light comes through it mottled and pale and the color of old paper. The colonists do not build their houses. They grow them the way one grows a thought into a sentence: slowly, with attention, asking.

The chambers are large. A mature pillar can be coaxed to grow a room the size of a small house inside its trunk, with the bone of the pillar as the walls and the windows as the only true openings. The bone conducts vibration beautifully. The colonists have learned to use this. A tap on the inner wall of a chamber travels down through the pillar and out through the root-network that runs beneath the rock between pillars, the slow underground braiding of pillar-root and pillar-root, and emerges in a neighboring pillar as a soft knock. Sometimes it travels further. A message sent in the right rhythm can reach across the whole settlement in an hour.

The rhythms have names.

There is the morning call, which is three short and one long, which means *I am awake.* There is the meal-knock, which is two short and two short and one long, which means *come eat if you want.* There is the long slow pulse that means *a birth in a far pillar*, which is felt rather than heard, a deep shuddering in the bone that the colonists learn to recognize across their bodies, in their teeth and the soles of their feet and the backs of their hands. There is the answering pulse, softer, that means *we know.* There are rhythms for illness and rhythms for mourning and rhythms for the small announcements that the colonists make to one another through the day, the soft traffic of a community that has learned to live inside the bones of a forest and speak through them. Visitors from off-world find it unsettling, this constant quiet knock that they cannot quite hear. The locals find it quieter than speech, and they mean it.

Between the pillars, the sky.

Between the pillars, the walkways. They are narrow — wide enough for one person, or two if they know each other well — and they are strung between pillars at the height of the chambers, swaying in the wind. A person on a walkway is exposed to the open air, and the wind passes them, and the pillar on either side sings, and they hear themselves in the chord. They wear layers, always. They wear masks in the long evenings. They step carefully, because a fall from a walkway is a long one and the rock below is hard and there is no water to soften it.

The shuttles hang from cables between pillars, or hover at chest-height on a principle no one fully explains anymore, or drift in the slightly weaker gravity by mechanisms the colonists have stopped troubleshooting. They make a hum. The hum joins the chord. The colonists do not mind. The hum is part of the place.

At night the chambers glow faintly from within, the pale yellow of the bone-light grown windows, and the pillars breathe out their slow exhalations, and the breath rises in pale columns that braid where they meet, and a person standing on a walkway at night can see the columns rising and the dim violet of the sky above and the soft luminescence of the radium-trace rocks in the basins far below. The call-birds have not quieted. They have only changed key, dropping into a lower register that carries further in the cooler air. A knock comes through the bone from a far pillar. Someone is announcing something. Someone answers.

The fauna are louder than the colonists.

The colonists have lived alongside them long enough that they have stopped being surprised. The call-birds are the loudness. They are fist-sized, with hard shells the color of the pillars, and they scuttle across the porous surfaces with a patience that looks like attention. They sing all day and most of the night. From a single pillar at midday you can hear a thousand competing songs overlapping into a constant static of melody — short phrases, repeated phrases, long descending trills, two-note calls answered by three-note calls from a neighboring pillar. The static does not resolve. The colonists have stopped waiting for it to resolve. They have learned to live inside it the way they have learned to live inside the wind.

The call-birds are not eaten. This is one of the small pieties the colonists hold. The call-birds are part of the chord. Without them the place would be quieter and the colonists do not want it quieter.

The gliders are eaten, sometimes. They are not birds. They are the size of a small dog and they have no feathers but they have long thin fingers of silica-bone stretched with thin membranes, and they leap from pillar to pillar on bladders of methane lighter than the air they displace, and they coast across the gaps in silence except for the soft hiss of their bladders venting. They are good eating, if you can catch one. Their meat is dense and slightly ammoniac and the old-timers prepare it in a dozen ways that the newcomers find hard to learn. The newcomers cannot tell a young glider from an old one. The old-timers can, by the membrane, by the length of the fingers, by the way the bladder sits in the body.

The chitter-things are not eaten. They crawl through the porous surfaces of the pillars and eat the pillar-substance, leaving trails of pale dust that the wind takes. They are small and many and they make a sound like a great many small teeth on a great many small bones. The colonists have made a peace with them the way they have made a peace with everything else — by leaving them room, by not asking too much of the pillars, by understanding that the chitter-things are part of the pillars' way and the pillars' way is older than the colonists' way.

The burrowers surface at night, leaving small craters in the pale rock between pillars. The colonists avoid the craters. The burrowers are shy and they do not like to be seen and they have a bite that the old-timers respect and the newcomers learn to respect. The craters fill in over the years.

The newcomers stay or they do not stay. The ones who stay for a year usually stay longer. The ones who stay for five years begin to thicken in the blood. The ones who stay for nine years have children, and the children are born with assumptions that the parents did not have, and the children grow tall and narrow in the dim light and learn to harvest the night's condensation with a casualness that unsettles the next wave of newcomers, who have not yet understood that the water is in them and will be in them again and that is all.

The pillars stand. The wind moves through them. The call-birds sing. The colonists knock softly through the bone and listen for the answer, and the answer comes, always comes, from somewhere in the network of root and chamber and quiet rhythm that the colony has become.

The plateau goes on.
