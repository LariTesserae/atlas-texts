What lives here is not a population. It is a settling.

You arrive, and the first thing to meet you is not a creature. It is the absence of one — that particular hush that follows you up the path and waits with you at the edge while you catch your breath. Then, out of the hush, a body. Then another. Then so many that the hush itself becomes a kind of body, a thing with weight and temperature, and you realize you have stepped inside something that has not finished waking up.

The insects are first. They are always first, and they are the reason you will never be alone on the plateau, even when you cannot see a single one of them.

They are not cicadas. They are not crickets. The ecologists who came up with the funding and the patience to catalogue them used words that did not exist before, and the words did not last, because the insects outgrew them. They have been called *the layered*, informally, by the people who spend time here. There are many of them, and they are not all the same kind of thing, but they have agreed — without ever appearing to confer — to make a single sound. The sound is woven. Each insect holds its own thread, a small bright chirr or trill or long held note, and the threads are tangled together in such a way that no single insect can be heard. You hear the cloth.

If you stand very still in a clearing at the right hour — and you will know the hour because the air changes, becomes a fraction warmer and a fraction heavier, and the silver undersides of the leaves catch a light that has not yet arrived — you can feel the pulse beneath the sound. It is slower than any of the insects. It is slower than a season. It is the rhythm the small lives have agreed to, the long agreement of a place that has been arguing with itself for a very long time. You cannot count the pulse. You can only be in it. When you leave the plateau and try to describe it to someone who has not stood in a clearing at that hour, you will say *something like a heartbeat, but not a heart*, and the person will nod politely and not understand.

The largest of the layered insects are the size of an open palm. They live deep in the moss and you only see them when your foot lands too near, and then they rise in a pale cloud of wings and settle again, three feet away, in the same posture, as if the disturbance had been a thought and they had decided not to think it. Their wings are veined like leaves. Their eyes, when you can find them, are the dark red of old wine, and they do not blink. They have been on the plateau longer than the trees, possibly. The moss remembers them.

The trees are next. The pale trunks, bone-colored, smooth, growing close enough to lean against one another — they are alive, in the ordinary way, but you will not believe this for some time, because they do not behave like living things. They do not drop their leaves properly. A tree may lose a branch and hold the lost shape in its canopy for decades, a hollow place through which the sky can be seen, until the hollow becomes a room, and other things move into the room. A tree may be struck by lightning and continue, blackened on one side, green on the other, holding both versions of itself for the rest of its slow life. The trees on the plateau are the oldest living things here, and they are not in a hurry about it.

What moves among the trees is harder.

The first thing you see, if you see anything, is a shadow in the canopy that does not move like a bird. It moves like something that has been walking in branches for so long that it has forgotten it has another option. You will catch it between leaves, or against a patch of sky when the wind turns the canopy silver. It has very long legs. It has too many joints. It steps from branch to branch with the slow deliberation of someone crossing a wide river on stones, and the river is not moving, but it has decided to be careful anyway. Its body, when you finally see it whole — and this may take days, because it does not want to be seen whole — is narrow and long, the color of the bark on the pale side, and its head is small and tilted slightly, as if it is listening to a sound just below the one you can hear. It is not a primate. It is not a deer. It is not a bird. The people who have watched it longest have given up on the comparison and started using a name in the local tongue, which roughly translates as *the listener*, though the word also carries the sense of *the one who pretends not to*.

The listener does not look at you. You will feel it knowing where you are. You will feel it the way you feel a hum in your back teeth when the wind comes hard off the western edge — a low attention, a being-regarded without the formality of being seen. If you are very still for a long time, it may come down to a lower branch. If you are very still for longer than you have ever been still before, it may come to the ground. This is said to have happened once. The person it happened to has not been the same since, and will not say what was exchanged. The plateau allows the exchange. The plateau does not encourage it.

The second thing you see in the trees is much rarer, and much larger.

It crosses a clearing without breaking the canopy. It is dark against the silver flash of the leaves, and its wings are wider than any wing you have a name for. There are people on the plateau who have lived here for years and have seen it three times, and people who have lived here for years and have never seen it at all, and the two groups do not argue about this, because the plateau has taught them that some things are present whether you see them or not. When it crosses, the air beneath it goes still. The insects pause — all of them, at the same moment, in the same fraction of a second — and resume. The leaves, which had been turning in the wind, hang flat and do not move until it has passed. There is no sound from its wings. There is no shadow it should cast. The shadow it casts is the wrong shape, slightly, as if the light is not sure which side of it the light is on.

You will not see its face. You will not need to.

The pools are at the bottoms of the ravines. The water is warm and shallow and tastes faintly of iron, the way the air tastes faintly of stone. You wade in, and your feet disturb nothing, because nothing in the pools is the kind of thing that can be disturbed by a foot. They live below. They live in the deeper water, and in the dark under the dark, in a place where the light that comes up from somewhere is the only light there is.

You see them as shapes. Long shapes. Slow shapes, like things that are thinking, or like thoughts that have learned to be patient. They do not come up. They do not approach the surface. They are aware of you in the way the listener is aware of you — a low attention, a being-regarded, but from below, which makes it different. Being-regarded from below is older. Being-regarded from below remembers things that being-regarded from the trees does not.

You have been down there once. You went with rope and lantern and the slow, stupid courage of a person who did not yet understand what the plateau was. You came back. You came back with water in your boots and a look on your face that the people who live here recognized immediately and did not ask about. What you saw down there you have not described, because describing it would make it a thing that happened, and what you saw was not a thing that happened. It was a thing that is still happening, in a place you cannot return to except by going back down, and you have decided you will not go back down.

The pools are not the only place the deep things live. They are in the cracks. They are in the spaces between the dark stone of the plateau, in the places where the heat is working from below, where the warmth that does not come from the sun has its source. You feel them through your soles. You feel them when you lay your face to a flat rock to drink. They are very patient. They are below everything. They have been below everything for longer than the trees, longer than the moss, longer than the layered insects and their slow agreement. They are what the plateau is resting on, in a sense. They are what the plateau was, before it was a plateau.

You will not see them. You will only know they are there by the heat, and by the long slow shapes in the pools, and by the way your own heart tries, sometimes, to fall in with the hum of the dark stone when you lay your hand against one of the structures.

The structures.

This is the part of the plateau that does not announce itself, and that is why it is the part you will think about longest, after you have come back down.

There are walls that have no doors, and doors that have no walls. There are platforms you cannot climb onto. There are pillars that stand alone, and circles of small stones that, when you count them, do not match — not today, not tomorrow, not the day you decide to count them three times in a row, because the number changes between the first count and the second, and between the second and the third, but never in a way that suggests a person has been moving them. There are rooms that are round and that you enter from two sides and that look, from inside, as if they have only one entrance, which is not the one you came through. There is a single high pure tone that comes from the rock itself sometimes, and is gone before you are sure you heard it, and the tone does not belong to the wind or to the insects. The tone seems to come from above. The tone seems to be patient.

The structures are not ruins. They are not buildings. They are not, exactly, made — their edges are too sharp, too perfect, the kind of sharp that does not come from a tool, the kind of perfect that does not come from a mind. They do not erode. You can carve your initials in a wall and come back a year later and find them as fresh as the day you made them, while the rock a foot away, the natural rock, has softened under your touch. The structures are not part of the weather. They have not been told about the weather.

What lives in them is a question that the plateau will let you ask, but will not let you answer.

There are people who say the structures are empty, that they are markers of something that was here before the trees, before the pools, before the layered insects began their long agreement. There are people who say the structures are inhabited, but that the inhabitants are not things you would recognize as such — that they are angles of light, or pressures in the stone, or the slow patient hum itself, which is not a sound but a presence, a kind of being that has chosen to make itself known as vibration. There are people who say the structures are the inhabitants, that they are alive in a way that does not require them to move, that they have been here longer than anything else and will be here after everything else has changed, and that the cairns that do not match are not a failure of counting but a deliberate refusal to be counted, the structures insisting on their own incommensurability the way the plateau insists on its own weather.

If you touch them, they hum. The hum matches the hum of the wind at the cliff edge. It travels up your arm and into your chest. For a moment your heart tries to fall in with it. Sometimes, for a moment, it does. The moment passes. The hum goes on.

What lives here, then.

What lives here is small, and persistent, and patient. What lives here does not announce itself. The insects do not announce themselves; they only continue. The listener does not announce itself; it only watches. The great-winged thing does not announce itself; it crosses a clearing and is gone. The deep things in the pools do not announce themselves; they are below, and they have always been below. The structures do not announce themselves; they wait, and the waiting is their kind of living.

What lives here, you begin to suspect after a long time, is the place itself. The plateau is the living thing. The insects are its small bright noise. The listener is its attention. The great-winged thing is its occasional passage across its own interior. The deep things are its memory. The structures are its oldest thought, the one it keeps returning to, the one it hums to itself when the wind is loud.

You are a visitor. The plateau has permitted you to be seen. If you stay long enough, and if you are quiet enough, and if you do not insist that the things here behave like the things at home, you may be permitted to live here for a while, the way a leaf lives on a tree, the way a small bright insect lives in the moss, briefly, before it becomes part of the long agreement.

The plateau will not hurry you. The plateau has not hurried anything in a very long time. The plateau is the kind of place that has learned to argue with its own decay, and to keep arguing, and to let the argument go on for as long as it takes.

You arrive by way of the wind. You will leave the same way. Between the arriving and the leaving, the plateau will hum, and the insects will sing, and the listener will pretend not to notice you, and the deep things will go on thinking in the dark below, and the structures will stand in their circles of cairns that do not match, and you will begin to understand that you have not been visiting a place at all.

You have been visiting a patience.

And the patience has been visiting you back.
