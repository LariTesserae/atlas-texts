What lives here is not one thing. The basin makes that difficult, the way a deep pool makes it difficult to say where the water ends and what holds it begins.

There is a person, or there was. The tools say so clearly enough — the chipped stone, the wooden club, the basket with its gathered roots — but the person is not in any of those things now. They are in the worn place. The seat beside the waterfall has been held so long by one body that the stone has learned the shape of sitting: the slight depression where the weight settles, the smoother patch where a hand has rested against the rock while the other hand has been held still in the lap, the faint darker line across the surface where a foot has braced for years against the same stone. The basin keeps the person's posture in its rock the way a river keeps the shape of a stone it has flowed around. You cannot see the person in the basin. You can see where they sat.

They came for the logic, if that is the right word. The pull of the place is older than language and does not name itself, but you can feel it in the way a body, set down here, begins to want to stop moving. The person found that. They came with their tools and their basket and they worked — the roots in the basket are real roots, dug from the soft ground between the trees, the dirt still clinging to them in places — and then they stopped working and began to sit. The seat is worn deeper than the work would account for. Years of sitting. Years of one body in the same place, watching the water fall into the pool, watching the pool hold the water without moving, watching something in the pool that is not the water. The person watched. The basin watched the person watch. There was an exchange. The person gave their sitting to the stone and took something back, but what they took does not leave a trace the way moss does, so you cannot find it now. You can only see the worn seat and the absence around it, the way the grass grows tall right up to the cleared moss and does not enter it, as if the space still belongs to someone.

The person may still come. The clearing has not yet been fully reclaimed. A fern has unfurled by the basket, yes, but it is the first frond only, still curled at its tip. The moss has crept over the edges of the flat stone but has not reached the center. Months, the basin says, or perhaps a year. The tools are arranged the way a person arranges things when they intend to come back — not tidied, not abandoned, set down in the gesture of pause. The wooden club lies where it fell from a relaxed hand. The roots in the basket were not tipped out. The hand-axe rests on its broader face, the working edge up, as if it had been set down to be picked up again with the same grip. Whoever was here left the way you leave a room you will return to in an hour, except the hour has become a season and the season is still extending itself, and the tools lie in their pause, and the pause does not close.

There is something small that runs in the grass at dusk. You never see it whole. You see only the moving parting of the stems, a soft wake traveling through the green, the way you might see a fish moving just under the surface of a pond. It is low to the ground, and it is quick, and it does not come into the open. It feeds, perhaps, on the things that come to the edges of the pools at the dim hour — small slugs, insects fallen from the air, the thin beginnings of moss. It has a nest somewhere under the roots of the leaning trees, where the ground is damp and the ferns grow thickest. It has lived here longer than the person has. It was here before the stone of the seat began to wear, and it will be here after the basket has fallen into fiber and soil. It has no name you would recognize. It is the kind of small dark life that a place like this keeps the way a pocket keeps a stone — not on purpose, but because the shape of the pocket fits.

The insects above the pools are not many but they are faithful. They hold their stations over the water in the still air, dimming and reappearing as their wings catch the filtered light. They do not seem to be drinking. They hover the way some birds hover, without committing to a direction. Perhaps they are waiting for something to hatch from the surface of the water. Perhaps the pools give off a warmth, or a smell, or a quality of stillness that draws them and holds them. They are the most visible life in the basin, and they are the least of it. They are only the surface, the moving skin of a quieter population underneath.

The pools themselves. They are too clear, too round, too still. The eye goes into them the way the eye goes into a well, and what the eye finds there is not fish. The water is not empty, but what is in it does not move as a fish moves. If you sit long enough beside a pool — and the basin invites long sitting, the way a fireplace invites long looking — you begin to see that the water has a thickness to it, a held quality, as if it is not quite water and is only pretending to be. Light enters it and does not return the same. The reflection of the sky on the surface is sharper than the sky itself. The stones at the bottom are rounder than stones should be, as if they have been polished by something that does not abrade but persuades. Something lives in the pool. It is not an animal. It is closer to a way the water has of remembering itself. When the wind does not touch the surface, the pool becomes very still, and the stillness is not absence but presence, the way a held note is presence. The pool is the closest thing the basin has to a center. The cliffs curve toward it. The waterfall pours into it. The worn seat faces it. Whatever organizes this place, whatever the lean of the trees is oriented toward, whatever the pull is that drew the person here and held them for years — it lives in the pool, or the pool is its face.

The birds that pass through do not stay. They cross the basin in a long shallow curve and exit the way they entered, without alighting. The basin is not for them. There is nothing here they want — no open sky, no rising thermals, no insects in the air thick enough to hunt. The insects above the pools are not for them either. The basin does not offer itself to the air. It offers itself to the ground, to the slow things, to the rooted and the settled. The birds feel this and pass. Their absence is part of the basin's fullness.

The trees. The trees in their clusters lean, all of them, the same direction, and the direction is not toward the light. The light comes from above. The trees lean sideways, as if something at the edge of their trunks pulled them when they were young, or as if they grew away from something rather than toward it. They lean toward the waterfall. They lean toward the pool. The oldest of them have grown past the lean and begun to curve back upward, so that their trunks describe a long S, a posture of having been bent and then righted themselves, the way a person who has been kneeling for a long time slowly stands. The leaning is the basin's oldest handwriting. Whatever the trees lean from, it came before the trees. The basin was already shaped, already curved in its strange way, before the first seed took root. The trees are not the cause of the geometry. They are responding to it. They are the only ones in the basin who have answered.

The cliffs. The cliffs do not live in the way a creature lives, but they are not lifeless either. They hold the basin the way cupped hands hold water — and the metaphor is not metaphor. The shape of the cliffs is hand-like. They curve where rock should fracture, and the curve is not erosion. Erosion makes soft, gradual, accidental shapes. These curves have intention. They are the shape of holding. The water that runs from the joints in the stone is not the cliff sweating. The cliff is offering. The water is the cliff's way of being open, of letting the inside of the stone come out into the air. Over the centuries the stone has been giving itself away in thin trickles, and the basin below is the accumulation of all that given water. The cliffs are patient givers. They will be giving long after the person is gone, long after the dusk-runner has changed its shape, long after the pool has silted over and become a meadow. The cliffs are the slowest life in the basin. They are also, perhaps, the oldest.

What lives here, then. The person, partly, in the worn stone. The dusk-runner, in the grass. The insects, in the air above the pools. The pools themselves, with their held water and their too-perfect stillness. The trees, leaning and righting themselves. The cliffs, curved and giving. And the basin, which is not the sum of these but the condition that lets them be what they are. The basin is the conversation. The waterfall, the pools, the seeping stone, the leaning trees, the worn seat, the abandoned basket, the small thing in the grass — these are sentences in it. The basin speaks in water and in the slow leaning of things, and it has been speaking for a long time, and the person who came heard it for a while, and what they heard they could not take with them when they left, so it stayed in the worn stone, where it still is, waiting to be heard again by whoever sits down next.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The basin is not in a hurry. The person, if they come back, will not be in a hurry. The dusk-runner is in a hurry only at the one hour, and even then it is quick rather than urgent. The insects above the pools do not move at all. The pool does not move. The trees have been leaning and righting themselves for a hundred years and will go on for a hundred more. The cliffs have been giving water since before there were trees to lean. The basin is patient with itself. It will be here. The conversation will continue. The tools will be buried. The clearing will become ground. The seat will become stone again. And the pull that drew the person here will still be in the basin, undiminished, waiting for the next one who stops moving long enough to feel it.
