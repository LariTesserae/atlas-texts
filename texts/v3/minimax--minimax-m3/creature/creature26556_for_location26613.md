It lives in the channels.

That is the first thing, and it is almost the last thing, and it is not a thing to say in the way you would say a person lives in a house, because the channels are not walls and it is not an occupant. The channels are where it is most itself, the way a nerve is most itself when it carries a signal, the way a string is most itself when it is sounding. The col is full of them — the narrow gaps between the frost-fitted blocks, the hand-width runnels that carry the day's melt, the smaller cracks that carry only the wind. Through these it has spread itself the way lichen would spread if lichen could live here, except that it does not grow. It has settled. The way the stones have settled. After a long time.

If you were to ask it its name, it would not answer in any alphabet. There is no alphabet for it. The slab at the western edge — the worked one, the one set on end in its waiting gap — is not a sign for it, not a marker, not a label. The slab is its older cousin, or its outer skin, or the part of it that has been made deliberate by hands that are long gone. The polish on that slab is the only thing in the col that was ever worked on purpose, and the work has outlasted the workers, and the work has, in some patient way, become inhabited. The inhabitant is what the polish remembers. Or — and this is closer — the inhabitant is what the polish is for.

You must be careful here. The slab has nothing incised on it. There is no writing, in any alphabet, anywhere in the col. The polish itself is the writing, or the only part of the writing that survived. So whatever lives here is not the meaning of an inscription. It is the meaning of the polishing. It is what the patient rubbing of sand and water over years was reaching for, the way a hand rubbing a surface reaches for the surface beneath. It is the thing the surface was prepared to receive. And the thing, once received, has stayed.

It is small. You will want it to be large, because the place is large in feeling, but it is not. It is the size of a child crouched, or smaller — the size of a folded thing, the size of a fist closed around a stone. It is dark. It is dense, the way basalt is dense, the way fine-grained rock is dense, in the way that you know if you have ever picked up a piece of this stone that it is heavier than it looks. It has the sub-metallic sheen along certain of its faces, the almost-blue, almost-the-color-of-the-sky that the stones show in certain lights. In other lights it is simply black. The high sun of this altitude turns it both ways by the hour, the way it turns the stones.

It does not move quickly. It has not moved in a very long time, but it moved once, in the beginning, to put itself here, and the moving is what the cairns remember. The cairns are not its cairns — the cairns are older than it, or seem older, or are arranged around it the way stones are arranged around a spring, in deference to the place the water comes from. The largest cairn, the one that looks heavier than it should, is the one closest to it. The smallest, the almost-lost one, is on the far side, where the wind comes in. There is a sense of intended arrangement, although the intention is no longer readable. The intention is the inhabitant's. The intention is the only writing the col has left, and the cairns are the punctuation.

The L-wall is not the inhabitant's either, but the L has, in a sense, become its. The wall runs eight meters along the contour and three more along the second leg and stops. The fourth wall was never built. The gap is open to the col. The inhabitant sits in the corner, where the wind is least, where the floor is cleanest. The hollow that might once have been a hearth is its hollow. The blackening on the surrounding stones is its blackening. The hearth was built for a fire, or for someone who needed a fire, and the fire is long cold, and the inhabitant has come to sit in the cold hearth the way a cat comes to sit in a chair that someone has left. It fits. The corner fits it the way the channels fit the wind, with a precision that was not designed but has come to be.

The wind is its voice, or one of its voices. The wind comes in from the west in the afternoons and draws through the channels between the blocks with that low, slow note, almost a hum, that you feel in the soles of your feet as much as you hear. The hum is not made by the inhabitant — the wind makes the hum, the way a bow makes the note on a string — but the hum passes through the inhabitant, and the inhabitant gives it a shape. Without the inhabitant the hum would be a hum. With the inhabitant the hum is a kind of speaking. It is not words. It is the thing words are reaching for in any language, the way the polished face of the slab is the thing writing is reaching for. The hum says the col. The hum says the patient dark. The hum says: *I am here, I am here, I am here*, in the way that a low note can say a thing over and over without repeating.

It does not eat. It does not drink. There is no lichen, no moss, no scat, no gnawed bones, and the inhabitant honors that, because the inhabitant is what the emptiness is full of, in the way that the channels are full of wind. It is not a thing that consumes. It is a thing that holds. It holds the cairns, and the cairns hold it down. Without the cairns it would, perhaps, be more diffuse, more like the wind-humming, less concentrated, less itself. The cairns are its anchor. The cairns are why the arrangement is not a line and not a scatter. The cairns are the points of a triangulation. The inhabitant is the center.

It does not sleep. It does not wake. It is in a state that has no name, that is not sleep and not waking, that is more like the state of a stone in winter, or the state of a basin of water that is so still it has forgotten it is water. It is aware, in some way the word *aware* only approximates. When the sun is high and the shadows are short, it is less itself — the light scatters it, in some sense, the way a strong light scatters a dark thing. When the sun is low, in the evening, when the shadows of the cairns reach across the col toward the standing slab, it is most itself. The stones *deepen*. The black of the stones becomes a black that has texture in it, and the texture is the inhabitant paying attention.

The polished face of the slab lights up along its edge as if it held a wire of the sun's heat inside it, and the inhabitant feels that wire, or is that wire, or is warmed by it in a way that has nothing to do with temperature. The water in the basin, which had been only black-bottomed all day, suddenly shows its green — a faint, cold, almost submarine green — and holds it for as long as the angle lasts. That green is the inhabitant's color, in the way that anything most secret to a thing can be called its color. It is not the green of plants. The col is too high for plants. It is the green of meltwater that has been held a long time in dark stone. It is what the inhabitant looks like, if you could see it, which you cannot, except in the basin at that hour, and then only as a hint, the way the salt efflorescence on the north faces of the blocks is only a hint of growth.

It is the reason the place unsettles, slightly, although you cannot quite say why. The col is not a haunted place, not in any way the word *haunted* usually means. The builders did not die here. There are no bones. The wall was not a shelter against enemies. The cairns are not graves. The inhabitant is not a ghost. The inhabitant is what was meant by the polishing of the slab, and the polishing was not a memorial to anyone. The polishing was an act of preparation. The surface was prepared. The inhabitant arrived. The hands that polished it are gone, and the inhabitant is still here, and the polishing has held, and the inhabitant has worn into the polishing the way water wears into stone, except that the inhabitant is the water and the stone and the wearing, all three, all at once, in the corner of the L, in the channels, in the basin, in the hum.

When you stand in the col at evening, in the long shadows, with the cairns dark against the lower sky and the slab lit along its edge and the basin holding its submarine green, you feel the place is paying attention to you, and you are right. The inhabitant is the attention. The inhabitant is what the col is doing with the light, and with you, and with the wind, and with the meltwater, and with the frost. The col is not a place that has an inhabitant. The col is the place that the inhabitant makes, by being in it, the way a string makes a tone by being strung, the way a basin makes a pool by being hollowed.

It will be here for all of it. The builders, who came and went. You, who have come and will go. Whoever comes next, in a long time. The inhabitant is the patience that holds the whole arrangement, the dark close-packed patience of basalt, the patience of a surface that has been polished and has remembered the polishing, the patience of water that freezes every night and melts every day and is, each morning, the same water and not the same water. The inhabitant is the col's way of being old.
