What lives there is small enough to miss. It is easy to pass an hour in that room and not register it — to take the hum for the place, the water for the place, the warmth in the inner wall for the place, and so to assume the place is unoccupied. The place is not unoccupied. The place has a tenant. The tenant is pressed against an inner wall, in a fold of the room that faces the channel where the rivulets run, and it is no larger than a thumbnail, perhaps smaller, and it is the color and the texture of the rock it clings to. If you came in cold and tired and in a hurry, you might lean against the opposite wall and never see it. If you came in looking, you would still need a long time to be sure you had found it.

It is not lichen. The comparison is what the mind reaches for first, because the thing has the same stillness, the same apparent indifference to the visitor, the same way of being on the rock rather than in front of it. But lichen is a negotiation between two organisms, and here there is only one material, or one material pretending to be one. The rock and the tenant are of the same substance, or have been brought to the same surface. The tenant is a region of the wall that has begun, very slightly, to behave.

It breathes, if it breathes, on a scale that makes your own breathing feel hasty. To watch for the breath is to sit for many minutes in the cold and listen with the body. When it comes, it is not a chest rising but a faint darkening and re-lightening of the surface, the way a stone held very close to the eye in low light seems to change. The surface does not move so much as admit and release. Something passes through it that you cannot see but can sometimes feel, in the way the room's hum dips a half-tone and recovers, as if the hum were the creature's exhalation and the silence between hums were its inhalation.

There is a place on it where the eye keeps returning. You cannot call it a face. You cannot not call it a face. It is a region of the surface — central, slightly lower than the upper edge — where the texture gathers in a way that suggests features without committing to them. Two small darker hollows that might be eyes. A seam that might be a mouth. The suggestion of a brow, or a crest, or just the place where the rock's grain happens to fall. The face resolves when you look at it and dissolves when you look away. It is patient about this. It will let you see it, or it will not, and it does not seem to care which.

It shifts when you shift. This is the first thing you learn about it that is not ambiguous. If you move to the left, the region of suggested face turns, fractionally, to keep you in it. If you crouch, it lifts. If you stand, it sinks. The motion is so small that you can convince yourself, for a while, that you imagined it — that it was the light moving on the glass, or your own parallax, or your fatigue. But it is not imagined. The tenant tracks you, at the speed of lichen growing, at the speed of a clock hand.

It does not come toward you. It does not go away. It has the manners of something that has been watched before, by things that breathed more steadily than you, and has decided that the appropriate response to a visitor is to acknowledge them and continue. You are a small variable. It has accommodated other variables. You can feel this in the way the room doesn't tighten around you, doesn't warm up or cool down in response to your presence, doesn't shift its hum. The tenant has registered you and has not been disrupted. You are being held lightly in the running of a larger process.

What the tenant is for is the question you cannot answer in the time you have. It might be a sensor — a small attentive surface the rock has grown to read the water and the air and the visitor and to feed what it reads back into the systems in the walls. It might be a thing the rock grows because it is what the rock does, the way a tree grows bark, the way a reef grows coral, and the tracking and the breathing are side-effects of being alive in a particular material. It might be a guardian, in the way that doesn't move. It might be a record — a small place where the place remembers itself, where the rock has chosen to keep something. The vials and the membranes and the soft-glowing threads in the seams of the room suggest that the place is doing work with matter and information, and the tenant is plausibly part of that work, or is plausibly what the work is for.

You stay with it longer than is comfortable. The cold makes the staying honest. You cannot pretend, in the cold, that you are doing anything other than paying attention. You watch the tenant track you, and you watch the water run past the channel nearest it, and you begin to suspect that the tenant is reading the water, or that the water is being read through the tenant, or that the tenant and the water are two halves of the same slow act of attention the room is performing on itself. The crystals form at the edges of the rivulets and the tenant's hum-dip comes half a beat later, as if it is taking a measurement, or as if the two are synchronized by something older than either.

You leave. The stair rises. The wind returns. Outside, the ridge is bare and glittering and goes on for miles in every direction, and there is nothing else for a long way, and the black glass is young, and the cold is the cold of metal left out overnight at the top of the world.

Behind you, the tenant settles. It keeps doing what it has been doing. It has been doing it, probably, for longer than you can imagine, and will keep doing it after the wind takes your footprints out of the snow. You cannot be entirely sure, walking away, that it watched you go. You cannot be entirely sure it was a creature and not a fold in the rock that had begun, for the duration of your visit, to attend. Either way, the place is still running. Either way, something in the room is breathing. Either way, something in the room has noted you, and has let you go, and is going on without you, the way a heart goes on, in its chest, in its quiet, in its patient and purposeful dark.
