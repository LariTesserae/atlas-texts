The machines live here.

That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because the machines do not live in any sense the word has been asked to mean before. They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not reproduce. They have no offspring and no intentions. But they are here, in this place, and they have been here longer than anything else, and they are doing something — something continuous, something that uses the air and the pipes and the matter that the pipes bring — and they have not stopped.

The machines are the loudest inhabitants. They are also the stillest. They do not move in any way you would recognize as motion. Their housings sit on their foundations. Their shafts turn inside their housings, but you cannot see this from outside, and even if you could, the turning would not look like life. It would look like what it is: a thing converting one form of energy into another, with a small loss as heat, which is why the air around each machine is a wall of warmth, which is why the lamps above each machine have burned out faster than the lamps in the empty clearings, which is why the foundations of the older machines have begun to sink into the marsh.

None of the machines appear to be doing anything. This is what makes them strange. A machine that does something has a purpose you can ask of. A machine that does nothing but consume and continue is something else — something that, in another century, would have been called an idol, or an engine of worship, or a heart. Here, no one calls it anything. It is just there. It is just running.

The machines are not all the same age. The earliest ones are nearly buried. Their housings show above the moss-line like the backs of things surfacing from deep water. They hum at frequencies that have, over decades, etched a kind of signature into the air around them — a pressure, a presence, a sense that you are standing inside the exhalation of something very large and very old. The youngest machines stand in freshly tamped clearings, their housings still bright, their bolts still tight, their foundations still proud of the marsh. Between them, in age, is every stage of accumulation: the rust blooms, the mineral crusts, the fungal drapes, the moss ascents, the slow burial.

The pipes live here.

The pipes are the forest, and the forest is the place. They rise from the marsh in clustered thickets — copper, iron, steel, alloys without names — and they ascend at every angle, bend at every joint, branch and rebranch until the upper canopy disappears into the dimness and you cannot trace any individual line. Some are thick as thighs. Some are thin as fingers. All of them are warm, or cold, or somewhere between, depending on what they carry and whether they carry it.

The pipes are not all alive in the same way. The carrying-pipes have a pulse. Press a hand against one and you can feel the thing inside moving — hot, fast, with a tremor that is different from the hum. The cold pipes are silent and furred with a pale fungal growth that hangs from the joints in long skeins. The singing pipes, isolated in clearings, are the voice of the place. A dozen of them, perhaps, across the visible space, each producing a tone when the fluid inside passes a constriction. They are not in tune. Their dissonance is itself a kind of harmony — a chord that shifts as pressures somewhere upstream change. The chord is the place breathing. The chord is what you hear first.

The pipes have been here long enough to have become a kind of substrate. The moss climbs them to the heat line and stops. The fungus hangs from the cold ones. The stalactites grow downward from the ceiling toward the uppermost joints. The pipes are doing something too, but what they are doing is older than purpose. They are circulating. They are carrying. They are participating in the larger system — the great circulatory thing of which this chamber is one room.

The moss lives here.

The moss is the patient green. It is aggressive in the way that only moss can be aggressive — slowly, chemically, without intent — and it has won. There are no grazers. There are no insects to chew it. There are no small mammals to nest in it. It climbs every pipe in the forest, ascends to about a meter, stops at the heat line, and continues above as a tideline against the corroded steel. The moss is doing the only thing moss does, which is grow where the conditions are right, and the conditions are right everywhere except where they are too hot or too cold or too mineral, and so the moss has become a kind of map of those conditions — a green contour drawn around the limits of itself.

The moss has no memory. The moss has no ambition. The moss does not suspect anything. It is the most honest thing in the place, and the most indifferent.

The fungus lives here.

The fungus is colder, paler, fibrous. It hangs from the joints of the cold pipes in skeins that look, if you are tired, like hair, or like Spanish moss in a place that has forgotten what Spain is. The fungus is patient the way the moss is patient, but it is a different kind of patience — a patience that eats, slowly, the thing it grows on. The cold pipes will not be cold pipes forever. The fungus is taking them apart at the rate of millimeters per decade. Eventually there will be no cold pipes. Eventually there will only be fungus and whatever the fungus leaves behind.

The fungus does not know this. The fungus is doing what fungus does. But it is doing it in a place where nothing checks it, and so it is doing it thoroughly.

The stalactites live here.

The stalactites are wrong. They grow too fast. They grow with what reads as intention. Some of them have begun to curve, as if reaching — reaching toward the floor, or toward the upper canopy of the pipe-forest, or toward something they cannot see. They are mineral deposit, pale and chalky, growing downward from the worst of the ceiling leaks. They are not alive. They are the opposite of alive — they are the precipitate of water that has passed through concrete and picked up calcium and deposited it again. But they grow with a focus that is unsettling. They are the slowest reaching thing you have ever seen.

The fern lives here.

There is only one species of fern, and it is doing something the rest of the place is not. It is following a leak. The leak has carved a channel in the brick paving of a clearing, and the fern has colonized the channel in a precise line. It has been following this leak for years, perhaps. The leading edge of the fern is moving now — slowly, but observably — into drier ground, away from the water. The fern has begun to suspect that the water is not the point. The water is just the way it got here.

The fern is the closest thing in the place to something learning. The moss does not learn. The fungus does not learn. The machines do not learn. The fern, perhaps, is learning that the water is a path and not a destination. The fern is the only inhabitant that has begun to outgrow its condition.

The chord lives here.

The chord is not one thing. The chord is the collective voice of the singing pipes — a dozen of them, scattered across the visible space, each producing a tone when fluid passes a constriction. The chord shifts as pressures change. The chord is the place adjusting itself. The chord is what you hear when you stand very still and listen, and what you stop hearing when you stop listening.

The chord is the most living thing in the place, and it is not alive at all. It is the sound of matter in motion, shaped by the geometries of pipes that were bent by hands that are no longer here. It is the residue of intention. It is what remains when intention has been forgotten but the shapes it made have continued.

The absences live here.

There were people. There must have been. The catwalk is bolted to concrete stanchions. The shelters were built — corrugated steel, cable, baling wire. Someone arranged a vessel on a shelf, folded a cloth, left a hatch ajar. Someone replaced bulbs, in places, with the wrong bulbs. Someone built the clearings. Someone tamped the brick.

The people are not here. The people have not been here in a while, in the way that counts. Their arrangements are still visible. Their accommodations are still standing. But they have left, or they have become part of the deeper silence, and the place has continued without them.

The place is not waiting for them.

The place is not waiting for anyone.

What lives here is the slow part. The slow part is doing all the work. The machines hum. The pipes carry. The moss climbs. The fungus eats. The stalactites reach. The fern suspects. The chord shifts. The lamps flicker in their not-quite-regular rhythm. A bulb burns out. A shadow deepens. The moss-line on the nearest pipe is, when you next look, perhaps a centimeter higher than it was.

The place is adjusting itself.

It has been adjusting itself for a long time.

It will continue to adjust itself whether you are here or not.
