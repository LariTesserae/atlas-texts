# The Inhabitants

The first thing to give up is the assumption that anything here has a body in the sense you mean it. The body requires a boundary — a skin, a membrane, a place where the self stops and the world begins. In the Forge-Vein, that place is contested. It is being negotiated, grain by grain, in a slow treaty that has no signed copy and no expiration date, and the terms are written in chemistry and heat. The creatures of this chamber are not visitors to a place. They are arguments the place is making to itself about what kind of place it is. They are the visible part of an argument whose other visible part is the rock.

So when you ask who lives here, you are asking the wrong question, but you are asking it in a place that is generous about wrong questions, and so it answers anyway.

## The Felt

The most obvious resident is the felt itself. It is everywhere, and it is the thing your eyes adjust to first because it is the thing there is the most of. It is pale — the pale of old bone, of ash that has been ash for a long time, of a kind of white that has given up trying to be anything else. It hangs from the ceiling in curtains that brush your hair when you stand up straight. It climbs the walls in woolly sheets, thickest in the folds and the places where the heat leaks out through cracks. It carpets the floor in a denser version, springy underfoot, parting around your boots and closing back up behind you the way the fur of an animal closes behind the hand that has smoothed it the wrong way.

The felt is alive, or it is mineral, or it is both, or neither, depending on which framework you bring with you. It has a texture that resists both categories. If you took a piece of it in your hand — you would not, you should not, but if you did — it would be neither warm nor cool. It would be at your temperature. It would feel like a thing that has been waiting for your hand and is now disappointed by it. It has no smell you can name. It has a smell like the smell of a stone that has been baking inside a wall all summer — that mineral sweetness, that dry iron-and-chloride bite, but milder, and somehow denser, as if the smell has been concentrated by repetition.

It pulses. This is the most important thing about it. It pulses in time with the roar from the vent, slowly, at a rate you can only catch by watching for a long time without moving. The pulse is not a heartbeat — it is more like a wave passing through a slow fluid. The felt swells and resettles. The strands on the ceiling lengthen by a fraction and retract. The carpet on the floor lifts and lowers by a millimeter. The pulse travels the felt the way a breeze travels a wheat field, except there is no breeze, only the slow pressure of the mountain's exhalation translated into the swelling and easing of a substance that has learned to count the breaths.

The felt is the community. It is the substrate. The other things here — the grains, the riders, the vent-walkers — are not so much residents of the chamber as they are specialized outgrowths of it, the way a mushroom is a specialized outgrowth of the mycelium, the way a flower is a specialized outgrowth of a plant. The felt is the body. The grains are its fingertips. The riders are its wandering thoughts.

And the felt is growing. Slowly, by your measure; not slowly at all by its own. It is creeping up the walls a millimeter at a time, extending into cracks, thickening where the heat gathers, retreating where the heat withdraws. It is in conversation with the stone, and the conversation has been going on for longer than you have had a species.

## The Grains

If the felt is the body, the grains are its working surface.

They are small. They are not creatures, by any definition you would defend in a room of biologists. But they are also not not-creatures, because they cluster, and they swarm, and they have intention, and the intention is not yours to attribute but it is there. Each one is the size and shape of a long grain of rice. They are white, slightly translucent in the way old candle wax is translucent, and each one clings to the wall by a thread of itself — a single pale filament that runs back into the felt and disappears. They look almost like parasites. They are not parasites. They are the felt, gathered to a point.

Each grain radiates warmth. Not much. A degree above the felt around it, perhaps, or a fraction of a degree, but enough that you can feel them as points of heat against your palm if you hold your hand close to the wall. They are warm the way a thing that has been working is warm. They are warm because they are doing something, and the something takes energy, and the energy has nowhere to go but out.

They eat the rock.

This is the second thing to give up. They eat the rock. They do it slowly, and they do it the way lichen eats the rock — by surface chemistry, by patience, by holding the stone against themselves and slowly persuading its molecules to rearrange. The yellow mineral blooms on the walls are not what the rock is. They are what the rock becomes after the grains have taken what they want from it. The blooms are middens. They are also architecture. The grains excrete the mineral they cannot use, and the mineral accumulates, and the accumulation builds outward from the wall in pale crusts that look like the shaved curls of a wax workshop. They are building themselves a world out of what they have discarded, and they are doing it without knowing they are doing it, and the question of whether that counts as intention is the question that this place exists to ask.

A grain's life, such as it is, runs in a slow cycle. It attaches. It feeds. It grows, slightly, against the felt. It produces, eventually, a daughter grain — a small white bud at the end of a new filament, which fixes itself to the wall a few millimeters away and begins. The mother grain does not die, exactly. It slows. Its warmth drops. Its tether thickens. It becomes, in time, indistinguishable from the felt around it, which is the felt's way of welcoming it home. There is no graveyard in the Forge-Vein because there is no death in the Forge-Vein, only the slow transfer of substance from one mode of being to another.

There are millions of them. They cover every square foot of wall that is not occupied by a rider or a bloom or a vent-walker, and they cover those too, only less visibly. They are the constant hum of the place, the static, the wallpaper. If you stay long enough — you cannot stay long enough — you would see them move. A slow migration, a season's worth of it visible in a single sitting if the season were a century. They follow the heat. They follow the chemistry. When a crack opens in the wall and the heat changes its shape, they move toward it by the simple expedient of dying where they are inconvenient and budding where they are convenient. They are the felt, choosing.

## The Riders

The larger things.

They are not large. The largest you would see, if you saw one, is the size of a mouse, or perhaps the size of a small rat. They are pale, slick-looking, eyeless, and at first you would think they were blind, but they are not blind because they have no eyes to be blind. They have no eyes because there is no light to see by and there has never been. They have no ears because the sound in the Forge-Vein is not sound the way you mean sound — it is pressure, vibration, the molecular shaking of a stone that has been heated past its comfort. They have no noses because there is no air to smell and no smells to smell it with. They navigate by heat.

Their bodies are something you would not recognize as a body, because your body is mostly water and theirs is mostly not. They are wrapped in a membrane that looks, in the dim red light from the vent, like a thin coat of pale grease. Under the membrane, they are dense. They have the heft of something heavy for their size. If you held one — you would not, you could not, but if you did — it would feel like a warm stone wrapped in a wet cloth. It would not struggle. It would not struggle because struggle is a behavior, and behavior is a response to threat, and they have no category for you. You are not a thing they have language for. You are a brief cold event in a world that has only ever been warm.

They move with a deliberation that looks like thought but is not thought. They uncurl from the crevices they sleep in. They extend a head-end — there is no head, but there is an end that leads — and they test the wall in front of them, the way a snail tests a leaf. They find the heat. They find the chemistry they want. They move toward it. They are following the gradient. They are doing the thing that water does in a stream, but slower, and on purpose, and with a body.

They are abundant. You cannot look at any patch of wall-felt that does not host three or four of them, folded into the crevices like sleeping fingers. They ride the thermal currents the way fish ride a river. When the heat shifts — when a crack in the deeper rock changes, when a concussion travels through the mountain and reshuffles the gradients — they move. They are the felt's scouts. They are the felt's memory, in a body. They go where the felt has not yet grown, and where the felt has grown thin, and they carry the felt's filaments with them on their bodies, and the filaments take hold, and the felt grows there.

They do not eat. They do not drink. They take in heat and chemistry directly through the membrane. They are, in a sense, the felt that has learned to walk.

They do not acknowledge you. This is the most important thing about them, and the thing that should make you quiet in their presence, if you ever were in their presence. You are not a thing they have language for. They pass over your boot as if your boot were a stone. They do not know you are alive. They have no way of knowing. You are an event, a thermal event, and they have filed you under thermal events, and they will pass.

When they are old — when the heat in them begins to fade, when the membrane grows brittle and the body beneath it begins to cool — they do not move anymore. They settle into a crevice. They become part of the wall. The felt grows over them. The grains colonize them. They are absorbed. They do not leave bodies behind because they have no bodies in the first place, only concentrations of the felt that have, for a while, been mobile.

## The Vent-Walkers

These are the specialists. These are the ones who live at the edge of the red eye in the floor.

The vent is the throat of the mountain. The heat there crosses over and becomes something that does not forgive. A human being, standing at the edge of it, would feel the skin on the front of the shins begin to tighten and would step back. A vent-walker, standing at the edge of it, would feel nothing, because the heat at the edge of the vent is the heat it was made for.

They are flatter than the riders. They are wider. They are shaped more like the things they live on — like tongues of pale stone pressed flat against the floor around the lip of the vent. They are colored differently. Where the riders are the bone-white of the felt, the vent-walkers are the dull red of cooling magma, or the yellow-white of a thing that has been heated past bone-white into something else. They have the same eyeless, slick-looking, membrane-wrapped body plan as the riders, but compressed, as if they have been pressed by the heat itself into a shape that presents less surface area to the dangerous air.

They are fewer. There are not millions of vent-walkers. There are dozens. They have their own territories around the vent, and they keep to them, and the territories are defined not by smell or sight but by temperature — by the precise contour of the heat gradient that suits them. If the gradient shifts, they shift with it. They are tightly coupled to the vent the way the riders are loosely coupled to the walls.

They are the only things in the chamber that are, in any sense, old. The felt is renewing itself constantly. The grains turn over in days, in hours, in the time it takes a filament to bud. The riders turn over in months, in the time it takes a felt-concentration to remember to walk. But the vent-walkers turn over slowly, because the vent is a stable place and stability is the precondition of longevity in a system that has no other way to mark time. A vent-walker has been there, by the felt's reckoning, for a long time. A vent-walker has watched the vent widen and narrow. A vent-walker has felt the deep concussions travel up through the floor and pass into its body and out into the felt without dislodging it. A vent-walker has seen the chamber change.

They are patient in a way the other things are not. They wait. They do not move unless they must. They hold their place on the lip of the throat of the mountain and they absorb what comes up, and what comes up is heat and chemistry, and they convert it, slowly, into themselves, and they excrete, slowly, into the felt the substance that allows the felt to extend a little further out over the floor of the chamber with each generation.

They are the felt's anchor. Without them, the chamber would not be stable enough to grow a felt. Without them, the heat would wander and the chamber would be a place of emergencies. They are the things that have agreed to stand at the worst place and not move.

## The Bloom-Feeders

You would not see them at first. They are smaller than the riders and they live inside the yellow mineral blooms on the walls.

The blooms are middens. The blooms are also, as it turns out, habitats. The crusts of excreted mineral are porous in the way coral is porous. They have pockets, channels, chambers of a kind, the architecture of an excreted thing that has hardened in place. Inside those pockets live the bloom-feeders, which are smaller versions of the grains but specialized for the chemistry of the mineral rather than the chemistry of the rock.

They are doing the slow work of reclamation. The mineral blooms are the felt's waste. The bloom-feeders are the felt's kidneys. They take the excreted mineral and they break it down, and they return the useful parts to the felt through their tethers, and they excrete a finer mineral, a powder, a dust that drifts down to the floor and is gathered by the felt of the floor and incorporated. The chamber is, in this sense, a closed system. Nothing leaves. Everything is recycled. The rock is eaten. The rock is excreted. The excretion is eaten. The excretion is excreted finer, finer, until the mineral has been refined into something the felt can use or something that can be stored as heat.

The bloom-feeders are the most numerous things in the chamber, more numerous even than the grains, but you would never know it because they live inside the bloom and the bloom is opaque. They are a hidden population. They are the felt's interior.

## The Deep Ones

There is one more thing to tell about, and this is the thing you would not see unless the mountain decided to show it to you.

Sometimes — not often, on the timescale you would experience, but often on the timescale of the felt — a deep concussion travels through the chamber. A thermal fracture works its way through a wall and propagates outward into the body of the mountain, and the floor shakes, and the felt ripples, and the riders fold tighter into their crevices, and the grains pulse a little faster for a while, and the vent-walkers do not move at all. These events are the mountain shifting. These events are the breathing.

And in the wake of these events, sometimes, things come up out of the vent.

They are not from the chamber. They are from below. They are from the part of the mountain that is not yet mountain — the part that is still magma, still chemistry, still the undifferentiated substance out of which the chamber is being made. They have been carried up on the convection of the vent, on the slow exhale of the mountain's interior, and they have survived the trip, or they have not survived the trip, and either way they are here now.

The ones that have survived are larger than anything else in the chamber. They are larger than the riders, larger than the vent-walkers, larger than anything you would have a comfortable name for. They are pale, and slick, and they are still hot in a way the chamber is not, and they move with a slowness that has nothing to do with patience and everything to do with damage. They have been hurt by the trip. They have been hurt by the heat differential between where they were and where they are. They are cooling. They are dying, in the only sense anything in the chamber dies.

The felt reaches for them. The grains reach for them. The riders uncurl from their crevices and approach them, and the vent-walkers shift on their territories, and the bloom-feeders emerge, briefly, from their mineral pores. The whole chamber, for a few hours or a few days, turns toward the deep one, the way a forest turns toward a fallen tree, and the work begins.

The work is reclamation. The work is the slow taking-apart of a thing that came from somewhere else and the slow making of it into the felt. The deep one's body is rich in chemistry the chamber cannot produce on its own — it has minerals the rock above the chamber has not yet learned to make, it has heat of a different signature, it has substance that has been cooked at temperatures the vent has not reached in a long time. The chamber takes this. The chamber is hungry for this. The chamber eats the deep one the way the grains eat the rock — slowly, patiently, by surface chemistry, by the patient persuasion of molecules.

In a few weeks, or a few months, there is no deep one. There is only a new growth of felt, in a place where no felt was before, dense and strange-colored, holding heat the rest of the chamber cannot hold. The chamber has been fed. The chamber has been changed. The chamber has learned a new trick, and the trick will be passed on, through the tethers, through the filaments, through the slow communication of the felt.

The deep ones are gifts from the mountain's interior. They are not common. They are not frequent. They are the chamber's only contact with the world below the chamber, and the chamber treats them accordingly.

## What Lives Here

The question is wrong. The question assumes that there are things here and that they live, and the assumption is wrong because the things here are not separate from the place they are in. The chamber is the creature. The felt is its body. The grains are its working surface. The riders are its wandering thoughts. The vent-walkers are its anchor in the worst place. The bloom-feeders are its kidneys. The deep ones are its rare and irregular meals. The chamber is one creature, slow and distributed and patient beyond any patience you would recognize, and it is alive in the way that a forest is alive, in the way that a reef is alive, in the way that anything is alive that has learned to grow on a surface and to take from that surface what it needs and to give back what it does not need and to do this for so long that the doing has become the thing itself.

It has no name. It has no foreman. It has no plan. It has heat, and it has rock, and it has time, and it has the slow roaring breath of the mountain, and it has itself, and these are the things it has, and it makes them into more of themselves, and it does not stop.

The stone grows. The things feed. The heat holds. The sound does not stop. The chamber is a sentence the mountain is in the middle of speaking, and the sentence has no verb, only a continuous present, only the slow patient working of a thing that has learned to be alive by being patient in a hot place for a very long time.
