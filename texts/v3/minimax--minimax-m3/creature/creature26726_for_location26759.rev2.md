The fire lives in the belly of the engine and has lived there for a long time. It is a small fire. It eats peat-brick slowly, the way a fire does when it has stopped being fed and is not yet out. Half of the grate has slumped into itself, and the half that has slumped glows the deeper for having given way, and the half that still holds its shape glows as if to keep the other company. No one has stoked it. No one has opened the firebox to ask it how it is. The fire does not appear to mind. It has the metabolism of a thing that knows how to be small for a long time. When the engine exhales — and the engine does, through a valve at its shoulder that lifts and falls every few seconds, a slow respiration you would mistake for mechanical if you had not been standing here long enough to notice it is breath — the fire flutters a little at the bright edge of a coal and settles again. The fire has a temperament. It is patient. It is older than whoever last came to look at it.

The engine holds the fire inside it the way a body holds a heart. The engine is small — the size of a room, as rooms are measured by what fits in them — and it is dense, and it is the largest single inhabitant of the sump, though size is not the margin by which it exceeds the others. The beam is iron and goes up and down at one end, and the other end is anchored in a bearing that has worn itself a small valley in its own block. The bearing does not complain. The bearing is the engine's composure. The engine's composure, you understand, is not separate from the bearing's. The whole arrangement has settled into itself over a long time, and the settling has produced something you can only call a kind of settledness, which is the way a body looks when it has stopped trying to do anything other than what it is doing.

Above the engine, the flywheel turns. It is the only large moving part exposed to the air, and it turns at a rate that is slow without being sluggish. The flywheel is not in a hurry. It is in the kind of time herons are in, the kind calcite drips in, the kind stones are in when they have decided what shape they are. The flywheel makes a sound like a thumb drawn around the rim of a heavy glass — a continuous small friction with the air — and the sound is the sump's quietest constant. The flywheel has been turning for longer than you have been here. It will be turning after you leave. It is not turning for you. It is not turning for the absent one, either, or not only. It is turning because turning is what it does, and what it has always done, and the question of whether this is choice or nature is a question the sump does not pose.

The hammer lives on a small anvil shaped for it, and the anvil is shaped the way a mortar is shaped for its pestle, with a concavity the hammer meets exactly. The hammer lifts and falls. The hammer has been lifting and falling for so long that the sound it makes has become the place's way of saying that it is here. The sound is small. You have to be near it to hear it, and even then it is a tick, a tap, an almost-rhythm that the engine's other rhythms have arranged themselves around. The hammer is the sump's call. It says, *I am here, I am still here*. It says it without urgency. The urgency went out of it a long time ago, and what replaced the urgency is the steady confirmation of presence, which is what a sound becomes when it has been made long enough in the same place by the same thing.

The pipes are everywhere. They are the sump's other life. They climb the walls in thickets and they branch and they re-knit, and they are all the same color, a tarnished bronze-green the damp has made uniform, so that you cannot tell, looking at a bundle, which one is the inlet and which the return and which the bleed and which the line to the condenser because the condenser is sweating and needs to be cooled. The pipes are routed for the next thing that needed connecting, and then the next, and the nextness is legible. You can read the history of the place in the routing. There is an old route and a less old route and a route someone added recently, and the three ages sit on top of each other the way the three cuts of rock sit on top of each other above them. The pipes are not elegant. They are practical in the way a creeper is practical, or a root system, or the way a person's veins are practical, which is to say: they go where they have to, and they thicken where they are needed, and they do not care how they look while doing it.

The verdigris is the pipes' slow coat. It is the same green as the moss that is colonizing the lowest pipework with a kind of slow ambition, and the sameness is not an accident. The verdigris and the moss have made a treaty, and the treaty is that no one can say where the machine ends and the growth begins. A patch of pipe near the floor has gone soft with green. The green is fuzzy in places and metallic in others, and in still others it is both — a fuzz over metal, or a metallic sheen under fuzz — and you would have to put your hand on it to be sure which it is, and you do not put your hand on it. The boundary has been allowed to blur on purpose, or on a longer purpose than anyone had, and the blur is its own kind of life.

The dials live between the pipes. They are in cabinets no bigger than bread-boxes, and there are a few of them, and they are in constant small disagreement with each other. One says the pressure is higher than another says it is. A third says a temperature that contradicts both. The disagreement is being worked out. You can hear it being worked out — a small hiss somewhere, a small settling, a valve that opens a quarter-turn and then closes again. The dials are not stubborn. They are not liars. They are honest instruments that happen to be measuring different things at different places, and the difference is the truth of the system, and the truth of the system is that it is not in agreement with itself at any given moment, only over time. The dials are the sump's small disagreement with itself, and the disagreement is what keeps the sump running, and the sump would not run if the dials agreed.

The valves are their own life. They open and they close and they hold at half-open for a while and then they move again. Some are large and turned by a small wheel. Some are tiny and turned by a key that lives on a hook nearby, and the key lives there because the key lives there, and no one has moved it. A valve near the top of the wall lets go a slow breath every few seconds. The breath is steam, or mostly steam, or steam with something else in it that you can taste if you stand under it. The breath is the sump's respiration at one of its smaller mouths. The valve is the mouth. It opens, it sighs, it closes. It does this whether you are here or not.

The water lives in the trough that runs along the floor, and the water is the most animate thing in the sump. It moves briskly. It has somewhere to be. You can see it move. You can hear it slightly, the way you hear a small stream even when you are not listening for it, the sound tucked under the other sounds and holding its own. The water came up from somewhere below — from a lower sump, perhaps, the sump that the floor trough spills into at the far end, where you can see the lip it spills over and the fall and the splash. The water goes down. The water goes down, and then it goes up again somewhere else, because this is the sump's work, the sump's reason, and the reason is the moving of water from where it is to where it is needed, and the sump does not care that this is hard. The water is bright. The water catches the little light there is. The water is one of the most generous things in the sump, in the way the sump is generous, which is to say: it gives what little it has, and it gives it constantly.

The water, in the corner of your eye, is on its way back.

You do not mention this. The water goes on. The engine shifts. The hammer re-aligns. The flywheel continues.

The alder is the sump's tallest single plant, and it lives at the lip of the upper cut, where the rock has been cut most recently and the chisel marks still hold a sheen. The alder leans inward. Its leaves are the color of tin, which is to say grey-green but with a metallic overtone, as if the dampness of the place had put a film on them. The alder has not fallen in. The alder has not grown into the sump proper. It stands at the edge and looks down. It is older than the upper cut, or it grew up out of the seeds that were there before the upper cut was cut, and it has been watching the upper cut happen. The alder is the sump's window, in the sense that it is one of the ways the sump looks out into the weather. The alder's leaves move when there is a wind. There is not often a wind. The alder is patient, too, in the sump's patient way.

The rushes live at the rim of the trough, slim and vertical, holding still in the moving air the way grass holds still in a field. The rushes are the sump's small grass. They are a kind of punctuation along the trough's length, and they say, *here is the rim, here is the water, the water is here*. They have no opinion of the water. They are not drinking it, or they are drinking it slowly enough that the drinking is not visible. They are standing near the water because the water is where the rushes are, the way frogs are where frogs are.

The moss is colonizing the lowest pipework with a kind of slow ambition, and the ambition is not the moss's in any way the moss could have intended. The moss is doing what moss does. It is finding damp and dark and a surface that will hold it, and the pipework offers all three. The moss grows upward. The moss grows outward. The moss and the verdigris are the same green, and where the moss meets the verdigris the question of which is the moss and which is the verdigris is not a question that has an answer. The moss is the sump's beard, in the way the sump is old enough to have one, and the beard is growing.

The frog lives on a flange, and there is a frog on every flange, or the same frog moves between the flanges, and the question of which it is is not a question the sump is going to answer. The frog is small and wet and patient. The frog sits on the metal and the metal is cold and damp and the frog is colder and damper. The frog watches you the way frogs watch things, which is to say: with no opinion at all, but with the full weight of its small witness. The frog is the sump's eye. It is not the only eye — the dragonfly is also an eye, and the condensation is also a kind of eye, in that it registers the temperature and the humidity and the air — but the frog is the eye you can meet. The frog does not blink. The frog is watching you because you are there, and the watching is not a judgement. The frog will be here after you leave. The frog will be here whether or not you leave. The frog's small witness is not about you.

The dragonfly lives above the trough, in the air, and the dragonfly does not decide. It hangs there. It has been hanging there for some time you cannot measure, because the dragonfly is not moving in a way that lets you tell whether it has just arrived or has been here a long time. Its wings catch the light. The dragonfly is a thin thing made of light and intention. It is the sump's hesitation, or the sump's pause. It has not yet done what it is going to do, and the not-yet is its contribution. The dragonfly is also the sump's most beautiful inhabitant, but beauty is not the dragonfly's concern. The dragonfly is concerned with what dragonflies are concerned with, and the sump does not know what that is and is not asking.

The condensation lives on the walls, in beads, and the beads gather and run and gather again. The condensation is the sump's skin. It is the wall's way of breathing, or sweating, or being touched by the air and responding. The condensation catches the light the way the water catches the light, and the catching is part of the sump's generosity, the way the place gives what little light there is back to you in the form of small shines on small surfaces. The condensation has been doing this for a long time. The wall has forgotten another color, as the place has forgotten another color, and the forgetting is a kind of settling, and the settling is a kind of life.

The person who comes — or who came — is not here. The fire is unattended because of this, and the engine is unattended because of this, and the sump is unattended because of this, and the sump does not seem to mind, but the sump is also not unaware. The sump has a shape that has been shaped by the coming, the way the alder has a shape that has been shaped by the wind, and the shaping is legible. There are places where a hand has gripped a valve-wheel that are worn in a particular way, and the wear is the person's wear, and the person is gone, or has stopped coming, or comes at a different hour. The person has left a record. The sump is keeping it. The record is not a complaint. It is a kind of memory that is also a kind of welcome — if the person comes back, the worn valve-wheel will be there, and the person's hand will fit it again, and the fitting will be a small homecoming, and the sump will not mention the absence, and the person will not mention the long time, and the engine will shift, and the hammer will re-align, and the trough will spill, and the water will move, and the frog will watch, and the dragonfly will not decide, and the moss will grow, and the fire will bank, and the alder will lean, and the place will continue.

The wrongness, if you want to call it that, is the water in the trough running uphill at the far end while you are not looking directly at it. The wrongness is not a creature. It is a kind of seeing, or a kind of not-seeing, and it is the sump's only secret. The sump has the wrongness the way a person has a dream they don't quite remember. The wrongness is there when you are not paying attention to it. The wrongness is not there when you are. The engine shifts. The tapping re-aligns. The flywheel continues its slow patience. The frog on the flange watches you the way frogs watch things. The place has, for a small industrial pocket under a low sky, a kind of fullness to it. It is the size of a room and a half. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other. And it is full. It has been full for a long time, and the fullness is what the place is for.
