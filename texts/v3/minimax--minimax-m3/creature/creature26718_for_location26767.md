What lives here does not hurry.

The people first. They are slow in the way the city is slow — not sluggish, but unhurried, having decided long ago what they are for. A man climbing the stepped street moves at the pace of a man climbing a long staircase. A woman carrying a water jar moves at the pace of a woman who has carried this jar every morning of her life. They walk with their heads slightly down. The thin air does not demand this; it only allows it. Their eyes are narrowed against the brightness of the high sun, which burns even when the air is cold. They are patient, and they are kind. The cold here makes hospitality a small urgent thing — a thing that cannot be drawn out into ceremony when breath itself is rationed — and what they offer is offered plainly. You are taken inside. You sit on a woven mat. A small wooden bowl is set before you, dark and bitter with tea. There is a stew of mutton or goat, with the root of a small tough plant that grows only in the cracks of the upper rock. The bread is flat and dense and the color of the mountain. You eat it with your hands
