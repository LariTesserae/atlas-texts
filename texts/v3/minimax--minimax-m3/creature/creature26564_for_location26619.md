# The People of the Underfold

They are called the Hanged-Folk, though not to their faces.

They are small in the way that things which live in tight spaces become small — not stunted, but *finished*, every part of them pressed close to every other part, the bones dense, the joints knotted, the hands broad as spades and soft as wet clay. Their skin is the color of river-slate, of the deepest flowstone, of the polished underside of a bell. It does not tan because there is no sun to tan it. It does not pale because there is no sun to pale from. It is simply *the brown that limestone gives to the things that touch it for a lifetime*, the brown of pennies left in a well, the brown of a thumb that has pressed a thousand wet stones.

They are broad. This surprises newcomers, if newcomers ever came, which they do not. A Hanged-Man or a Hanged-Woman standing on the chamber floor — which is to say, standing in the world — has the low center of gravity of someone who has known, since the first time they stood, that the floor is the only thing that will hold them. Their shoulders are wide. Their feet are splayed. They move like people who learned to walk on slick stone and never unlearned it: a careful rolling gait, the weight passing through the heel and the ball in one continuous thought, the toes spread to grip the wet.

Their eyes are large and dark and do not reflect light, because there is not much light to reflect. In the sourceless dim, their eyes seem to absorb the dimness and become part of it. A Hanged-Person looking at you from across a plaza is a Hanged-Person whose face is mostly eyes, the rest of the features receding into the gloom. Children, before they learn to lower their gaze, sometimes appear to be only eyes — two wet moons hung in a column of brown air.

The old are smaller than the young. The very old are tiny. There is a grandmother in the lowest tier — *Old Below*, they call her, the way surface-folk might call a mountain *Old Above* — who is the size of a child of six, but bent denser, folded over the decades into something the shape of a question mark. She sits on a basalt step all day with a basket of cave-grass on her lap, weaving by touch, and the basket glows, and her face is a soft blue cave in itself.

---

Their language is a language of the wet rock.

It is built from clicks the way a cathedral is built from stone — as the *primary architecture*, not the ornament. A Hanged-Person says *good morning* by putting the tongue against the roof of the mouth and releasing it; the click rings against the nearest wall and returns, and the meaning is in the *shape of the return*. To say *the water is rising* is a double-click with a falling resonance, a sound that mimics the very thing it describes. To say *I am sorry* is a long slow click that goes on so long the listener must hold their breath to hear it finish.

There is, as the old stories tell, a tense for things that are above you. The Hanged-Folk use it constantly, because almost everything is above them. *The bell is ringing*: above-tense. *The smoke is pooling*: above-tense. *The pale things are swimming in the still-pool*: above-tense, though the still-pool is at their feet. The above-tense is also the tense of memory, of ancestors, of the surface that no one has seen in nine generations. To speak of the dead is to use the above-tense. To speak of the sun is to use the above-tense. To speak of the war that drove them down is to use the above-tense, and to lower the voice.

There is another tense for things that will never move. This is the tense of stone. *The wall is the wall* is in this tense. *The aquifer is above us* is in this tense. *I will be here tomorrow* is in this tense, and is said often, and is meant.

There is no tense for *soon*. The Hanged-Folk do not believe in *soon*. *Soon* is a surface-tense.

---

They live in the hanging houses.

From below — from the chamber floor, the only ground they know — the houses appear to hang. Their doorframes are wells. Their windows are the mouths of chimneys, opening downward into the dark. The bottoms of tables show like the bottoms of boats. The thatch of the roofs is matted downward, like hair that has forgotten which way to grow. Chimneys exhale smoke that climbs, and the smoke pools against the underside of the next street up, and there, in the slow-turning cloud-town of the smoke, the *smoke-children* play — small forms moving in the grey, their laughter absorbed by the wet stone, their games invisible from the floor except as a faint agitation of the dim.

To enter a hanging house is to climb. The Hanged-Folk climb everything. Their stairways are not stairways but *stem-chains* — vertical ropes of notched stone and woven cave-grass that the children learn to scale before they learn to walk properly. A Hanged-Person going home climbs. A Hanged-Person coming down descends. The verbs are different. The verbs have been different for nine generations.

Inside a hanging house, the floor is the ceiling. The hearth is the highest point. The chairs are bolted to the stone overhead, and the family sits in them with their heads pointed down toward the world, and they look at each other upside-down, and they have tea from cups that are right-side-up to them and inside-out to anyone else. It is, to them, normal. It has always been normal. A Hanged-Child who tries to imagine the *other* way — the surface way, the sky-up way — grows dizzy and stops.

They do not talk about the inversion. They do not remark on it. A Hanged-Woman hanging in her hanging chair in her hanging kitchen, pouring hanging tea into a hanging cup, is not making a philosophical statement. She is making tea. The stone does what stone does. The water does what water does. The Hanged-Folk do what Hanged-Folk do, which is to live, carefully, in the wet.

---

They eat the pale things.

The pale things live in the deepest still-pools — the black water, the obsidian water, the water so still it has forgotten it is water. They are not fish. They are not quite anything. They are the color of uncooked dough. They are eyeless. They have a tremor that runs through them at all times, as if they are perpetually being slightly startled by something just out of earshot. They are about the length of a finger. They taste of stone-water and of nothing else.

The Hanged-Folk catch them with long hooks of bone and lower them into the black water and wait. The pale things do not flee. They do not know how to flee. They have been here longer than the bells, longer than the settlement, longer than the war. They are boiled whole in clay pots over small fires. They are served in the bowl of the hand. They are not thought about.

When the mushroom-farms on the damp ledges run short — and they run short often, because mushrooms are slow and the Hanged-Folk are many — the pale things become the meal. The Hanged-Folk do not apologize to the pale things. The pale things would not understand. The pale things are in the tense of things that will never move.

---

They make beautiful things.

This is the thing the Underfold cannot explain about itself, and the Hanged-Folk cannot explain about themselves, and the stones cannot explain about either: *they make beautiful things.* It is not a choice. It is not a policy. It is what humans do, even here, even in the dark, even when the dark is the only country they have.

The bone-carvers work in the lowest tier, where the air is stillest and the light from the phosphorescent cave-grass is strongest. They carve small figures — a mother climbing a stem-chain, a child chasing another child through a glowing street, a pale thing trembling in a black pool, a bell hanging in its tower. The figures are no bigger than a thumb. They are sold by the basket in the lower market, where the dripping is loudest and the ceiling is highest and the air is full of the slow chime of the bells, far above. Tourists do not buy them. Tourists do not come. The Hanged-Folk buy them for each other. A Hanged-Man gives a carved bone to a Hanged-Woman and she hangs it from the underside of her doorframe, and it dangles there in the dim, and it is small, and it is everything.

The bell-makers are a guild. They make the children's bells — tiny bronze bells, no larger than a hazelnut, that the children wear on wrist-cords of woven cave-grass. The bells do not ring loudly. They give off a small clear note, like a single drop of water landing exactly where it intends to land. When a Hanged-Child runs through a lower street, the bells ring faintly with each step, and the child is followed by a constellation of small sounds, and the constellation moves with the child, and the parents know where the child is by listening to the bells.

The basket-weavers use the cave-grass, which grows only along the deepest walls, only where the stone-weeps most steadily, only in threads so fine they are almost light. The grass is phosphorescent. A basket woven of it glows faintly — a soft blue, the blue of deep water, the blue of a sky that the Hanged-Folk have never seen but have somehow remembered. The baskets are hung from belts. They glow as the wearer moves. The children, chasing each other through the lower streets in trails of soft blue light, are like a school of deep-sea fish, like a procession of small cold moons.

The laughter of the children rises into the upper dark. The wet stone absorbs it. The pale things, far below, are not listening.

---

They do not look up.

This is the first thing a Hanged-Parent teaches a Hanged-Child, and it is taught before the child can speak, by the parent taking the child's chin and gently turning it downward. *Down is the world. Up is the city. Up is not for looking at.*

A Hanged-Person who looks up too long feels the brain begin to *unfasten*. The eyes weep. The ears ring. The stomach, which has spent a lifetime trusting that *down* is where things fall, revolts. There are old stories of people who looked up for an entire bell-cycle and were never right again — who walked the lower streets afterward with their heads tilted forever, who could not eat, who could not sleep, who stood at the edge of the still-pools and stared at their own reflections until the reflections blurred and the pale things came to nibble at their fingers and they did not notice.

The gaze itself, they say, is a kind of falling. To look up is to begin to drop. To look up is to let the world pull you through the ceiling into the inverted city, into the upside-down rooms, into the smoke-towns where the smoke-children play. The Hanged-Folk do not wish to go there. The Hanged-Folk are already there. The Hanged-Folk are *always* there, in the tense of things that will never move.

So they do not look up. They look at each other. They look at the wet stone. They look at the glowing baskets on the belts of the children passing by. They look at the pale things boiling in the clay pots. They look at the small carved figures hanging from the doorframes of their neighbors. They look at the floor, which is the only thing they trust, and which is, after nine generations, the only thing that has earned their trust.

---

They are not a melancholy people.

This is the last thing an outsider would believe, and the first thing a Hanged-Person would correct.

They have lived here for nine generations. They have ceased to wonder about the surface the way a fish has ceased to wonder about the air. The fish does not grieve for the sky. The fish does not remember the sky. The fish is wet, and the water is wet, and the matter is closed.

The Hanged-Folk have tea. The Hanged-Folk have bone-carvings. The Hanged-Folk have children's bells. The Hanged-Folk have the threefold carillon of the great bronze bells, falling upon their ears like a slow warm rain, marking the hours of a day that does not change. The Hanged-Folk have each other, pressed cheek to jowl in their vertical sociology, climbing and descending the stem-chains of the hanging city, calling out in the click-language that rings against the wet walls and comes back shaped like the world.

They are not melancholy. They are *settled*. They have arrived at the weight of themselves. The pale things tremble in the black pools. The bells ring into the unchanging air. The water drips its patient syllables onto the worked stone. *Drip. Drip. Drip.* The Hanged-Folk live, carefully, in the wet, in the dim, in the long bell-note of a moment that does not end.

They do not ask it to.
