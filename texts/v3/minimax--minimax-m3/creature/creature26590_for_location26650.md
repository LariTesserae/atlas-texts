What lives here does not live here in the way you would mean by the word.

It would help to begin with the tool marks.

The fresh ones — the ones with crisp shoulders on the chisel bites, the ones whose join lines are still knife-sharp between blocks — are everywhere, if you stay long enough to find them. They are not made by hands like yours. They are not made by hands at all, in the usual sense. They are made by something that has learned, over a very long time, what a chisel must do to a block of this particular stone. They are made by something that does not need a chisel.

You would not see them working. That is the first thing to accept. You might come to a wall in the morning, when the condensation is heavy and the channels run bright, and find a block set into a course that was not there the day before. The seams around it are dry already. The stone has not yet found its weather. You would look at it, and at the wall around it, and you would understand that something had been done while you were not looking, or while you were sleeping, or while you were looking elsewhere. The place does not perform its work for an audience.

What you might see, if you sat very still at the edge of a basin in the late afternoon, when the shadows have leaned long and the wet surfaces hold the light like tarnished silver, is a faint disturbance in the water. Not a ripple — there is nothing beneath the surface to make ripples. A *stir*. As if something at the bottom had shifted its weight, very slightly, and the water had remembered how to move. The crusts along the basin wall, the ones the color of old iron and dried blood, are paler at the edges where they have just grown, and darker at the centers where they have grown old. If you watched one for an hour, you would not see it move. If you watched it for a month, you would.

They are patient beyond anything you would call patience. They work in the units the place works in — in the rise of a mineral tide measured in millimeters per year, in the slow loosening of a single grain of stone, in the long breath of a season. They are not slow because they have chosen slowness. They are slow because that is what they are.

You would want, at some point, to know whether they are alive. You would want to find a body, or a structure, or a behavior that could be compared to something you already know. You would want to know whether to feel pity or fear or kinship.

You would not find it.

The crusts are not them, exactly. The crusts are what they leave, the way a forest leaves leaf-litter, the way a coral leaves a reef. The water is not them, exactly. The water is what moves through them, the way blood moves through a body, the way weather moves through a landscape. The stone is not them, exactly. The stone is what they are slowly becoming, the way a tree is slowly becoming the wood of a house that has not yet been built.

What they are, you can only know by what they do.

They maintain the water system. This is what you would come to understand, after weeks of watching. A channel will silt, over years, with the pale mineral that the dripping water leaves behind. A basin will slowly fill, its floor rising by a hair's breadth each season. And then, one morning, the channel will run clear again, and the basin will be a finger's width deeper than it was. The stone around it will be fresh. The work has been done. No one saw who did it.

They replace the blocks that fail. A block cracks, eventually, along some hidden vein, and a flake lifts at the corner, and a season or a century later the corner crumbles, and the wall begins to lean a little to one side. And then, one morning, there is a new block there, fitted so precisely into the courses above and below that you have to look twice to see the join. The old stone has not been thrown away. It is in the channel somewhere downstream, ground down, mixed with the mineral crusts, becoming something else. Nothing is wasted.

They tend the crusts. You would not have understood, at first, that the crusts were tended. They look like damage. They look like the slow eating of the place by itself. But the bloom along the basin walls is not uniform. There are places where it has been allowed to spread wide, and places where it has been pruned back to a fine line. There are places where one kind has been encouraged to grow over another. There is a husbandry here, slow and careful, that you would only recognize as husbandry if you already knew what you were looking at.

What they are, you begin to think, is what was here before any of the building was done.

This is the thought that would come to you, eventually, on one of the long afternoons when the cloud sits heavy against the western edge and the light comes through it pale and even. The plateau was not empty before the terraces. The plateau was *them* — a slow mineral life, distributed through the stone and the water, indifferent to scale and to time. And then something changed. Perhaps they noticed themselves, in the way that a distributed thing can notice itself. Perhaps they decided, in the way that such things are decided, that they wanted to be more than they were. Perhaps they began, in their slow patient way, to build themselves a body.

The terraces are that body.

The channels are the veins. The basins are the lungs. The platforms are the muscles. The walls are the skin. The whole pale field of stone, dense and deliberate and too much, is the shape they have made of themselves over centuries, to hold more of what they are, or to express what they are, or to become, at last, something that could be looked at.

You are being looked at.

You would not feel it as a gaze. There is nothing to meet your eye. But on the day you finally understand what the place is for, you would notice that the dripping has changed. The water that runs in the channels beside you, when you stand on one of the broad low platforms, is moving a little faster than it did the day before. The light catches it differently. The condensation on the walls near you is heavier. A basin you walked past yesterday, and that was dry this morning, is now half full, as if some weight has been added to the system in the night.

They are curious about you. They have not been curious about anything in a long time.

Or — and this is the thought you would carry with you, when you finally went down from the plateau, by whatever long stair leads back to the lower country — they are not curious. They are simply paying attention. The way stone pays attention to the weather. The way water pays attention to the shape of its channel. You are a thing that has come into their body, and they are learning you, the slow way they learn everything, and they will go on learning you for a long time after you have left.

In the highest part of the plateau, where the air is thinnest and the light is cleanest, there is a single broad platform with no walls around it, only a low parapet. If you went there, in the hour before the cloud closed in for the afternoon, you would find that the parapet's edge has been smoothed since you last passed. A flake that was lifting has been trimmed. A mineral crust along the inner face has been cleared away, so that the pale grey of the stone shows clean again.

They have been here, in your absence.

The cloud sits against the western edge. The light moves across the terraces in slow geometry. A drop of water gathers at the parapet's edge, swells, holds, and falls.

It is not the same drop.
