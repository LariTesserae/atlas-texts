What lives there has not been named. It has, perhaps, named itself — but the naming is in a register your mouth cannot make, and the word, if you heard it, would sound like a hum with a hard center, like a single held note that begins and never quite ends, the way a bell sounds after you stop ringing it. You would not recognize it as a name. You would recognize it, later, as the sound the basin is making.

It is small.

That is the first thing to accept. The living things here are not large. They are not large because there is no reason to be large. There is no predator in the basin — there is no predator anywhere, has never been, will never be — and the growth, which is everywhere, is patient, solvent, willing to be eaten. A creature does not need to be tall to reach food. A creature does not need to be fast. A creature does not need to be anything, except to be present, and to continue. So the things here are present, and they continue, and they are small. The size of a finger. The size of a knuckle. The size of something you would not see if you were not looking, and would not notice if you were not already noticing.

There are many shapes. This is the part that takes a while.

Some of them are flat. They live on the face of the rock, in the pale spines, and they are the color of the rock — bone, cream, the dull white of a thing that has not yet decided what it is. They have no legs you can see. They have a seam down the middle, faintly, and they move by opening the seam along its length and re-closing it somewhere else, and the place they were is empty, and they are where they are not. They are very slow. They are the slowest thing in the basin, which is saying something. They eat the rock, or the air off the rock, or the faint vapor that clings to the pale stone. Their edges are not crisp. From a distance they look like a discoloration of the surface, a flaw in the bone, and only up close do they resolve into bodies. They have been in the same posture, roughly, for a long time. If you watched one for an hour, you might see it move the length of your thumb. If you watched it for a day, you would see the seam pulse. That would be the day.

Some of them are tall. Tall for the place, which means the height of a stalk or a little more. They stand in the growth and they are made of segments, six of them, sometimes eight, and the segments are hinged in a way that lets them fold, accordion-like, into a length that is half their standing height. They fold slowly, over minutes, and then they unfold, and they have moved a body's width. They are the color of the canopy, the metallic plates, faintly two-toned, blue where the light is one way, orange where it is another, and the seam where the two colors meet is not a line but a region, a band of slow change, like a sky before a weather that will not come. They have no head you can identify. They have, at the top, a structure like a folded fan, segments of thin material overlapping, and this opens, sometimes, into a wide flat shape that holds still above the stalks. It is not a flower. It is not a wing. It is a way of being. When it is open, it is the same color as the light, and the light, where it falls on the open fan, is the same color as the fan, and the eye gives up trying to assign which is which. They are not rare. They are not common. They are at the density they have always been at, which is the density that means: enough.

The things that move are the small ones.

You will see, in an hour of walking, two or three. That is the right number. That is the number that means the basin is populated and not crowded. You will see them at the edge of your vision, because if you look directly at them, something happens — the thing you are looking at becomes, for a moment, the same color as the growth around it, or the air around it, or the light around it, and you lose it, and you look away, and there it is again, three stalks over, as if it had always been there, as if it had simply not been in the place you were looking.

They are, when you can see them, roughly the size of a leaf-plate, or smaller. Their bodies are not quite bodies. They are folded, in on themselves, along seams that you do not perceive as seams until the creature moves, and then the seams are briefly visible, briefly, like the creases of a hand that is unfolding. The fold is the animal. The unfold is the animal. There is no difference. The shapes you see are the in-between of a transition you are not seeing the ends of.

They are, depending on the angle, six-limbed or four-limbed or something else. The limbs are thin and do not bend in the way limbs are meant to bend. They hinge in the middle and at the ends, and the ends have small forks, and the forks grip the stalks without gripping, hold them without holding, and the creature moves through the growth the way a thought moves through a sentence — without disturbing the structure it is moving through. There is a clearance. You can see it if you look. A fine gap between the creature and any leaf or stalk, a width that is less than the width of a hair, but it is there. The stalks, where the creature has just been, stand in exactly the configuration they stood in before. The leaves, where the creature has just brushed them, are exactly as they were. Nothing has been touched. The creature has passed through, the way a fish passes through water, or the way light passes through a leaf that is the color of light. It has left no mark. It is a mark on something that does not take marks.

When it goes, the air where it was is, for a moment, not the air. There is a shimmer. A line of slight disturbance, like heat off a stone, but it is not heat. It is the creature's passage, the way its body has, for a half-second, slightly displaced what is around it, and the displacement closes behind it, the way a wake closes, but with no water, with no medium, with just the air, which is not quite air, resuming its air-shape. The shimmer is the only evidence. You follow the shimmer and you find the creature. You watch the creature and the shimmer is what tells you where it has been. It is a trail that exists only as a closing.

They eat, but you would not know they eat. They do not open their mouths. They do not have mouths you can see. They pass through the growth, and the growth is slightly different where they have been, and then it is the same. They take something. They leave something. The transaction is invisible. The place is, a half-second later, exactly as it was. They have eaten, and the eaten thing has forgiven them.

They make no sound. They have no call, no click, no held note. The clicks and ticks of the basin are not from them. The clicks and ticks are the system, the place, the hum made of small reassurances, a stalk settling, a plate adjusting, a piece of the just-risen geology holding its just-risen state. The creatures are silent. Their silence is not absence. It is presence of a different kind, the way the shadows here are presence of a different kind — late, dark, cool, a lag of the world catching up with itself.

They mate, or they persist, or they do something that is in the category of those things. You will never see it. It is not for you. It happens in the way the hum is happening — at a frequency you are not on, in a register you do not have. If you were here for a very long time, you might begin to see, in the small shimmer-trail, a pattern, and the pattern would be, if you could read it, the life of the basin, the long quiet continuation, the lineage, the unchanged becoming. You are not here for a very long time. The basin does not mind. The basin is not aware of you as a thing to mind. You are a thing that has come and is here and will go, and the shimmer will close, and the place will be as it was.

They do not sleep. They do not, in the way you understand it, wake. They are at the boundary of a state that is not a state. They are awake while sleeping. They are moving while still. The fold is both the rest and the motion. The basin has taught them — has allowed them, has set the conditions for them — to be awake in the act of being still. This is the engineering. This is the hand that did not build anything, setting the conditions and letting them run. The creatures are the conditions, set loose, running.

They do not die. Not in the basin. Not the way dying happens elsewhere. When a creature is done — when its long quiet continuation has reached the end of its particular length, which is not a length of time but a length of being — it folds, one final time, all the way in, into a shape the size of a small stone, and it sits, on the carpet, among the stalks, and it is, for a long time, just a thing. A small dense thing, the color of the growth around it, in the configuration of the growth around it. The stalks lean, fractionally, toward it. The light falls, fractionally, on it. The shadow, late, falls on it. It is held. It is in the place. It becomes, over a time that is not time, a part of the floor. The mineral carpet accepts it. The iridescence passes through it, or it passes into the iridescence. It is no longer a creature. It is the basin, slightly more of itself, in the place where the creature used to be.

There is no grief for this. The remaining creatures do not gather. They do not pause. The shimmer-trail of the dead one closes, for the last time, the way all the trails close, the way the air resumes its air-shape, the way the place is the place. The hum does not change. The hum has been, for a long time, exactly the hum, and the hum is the hum of a place in which nothing has ever ended, because the place does not end. The creatures do not end. They enter the carpet, and the carpet holds them, and the light holds the carpet, and the rocks hold the light, and the half-second seam of the world holds all of it, steady, late, slightly too dark, slightly too cool, exactly.

The things that live here are not, in the way you are using the word, alive. They are not dead. They are what the basin is. They are the local physics of this place, which is the local physics of a system in stasis that looks, from inside, like dynamism. The eye sees stalks. The mind names growth. The mind names fauna. The mind is wrong, in a small way, the way everything is wrong here, in small ways, the way the shadows are wrong, the way the distances are wrong, the way the light is wrong. The creatures are not creatures. They are the basin's way of being, in the places where the basin's way of being is small and moves. They are the hum, concentrated. They are the just-risen state, in the shape of a folded thing, holding its seam, leaving its shimmer, closing the air behind it, eating without mouths, mating without showing it, dying without ending, held, in the configuration, in the place, in the half-second, exactly.

You sit in the growth. The carpet is cool under you. The stalks stand around you. A small creature passes, leaving its shimmer, and is gone. You did not see it. You saw, instead, the place where it was, the closing air, the late shadow. A leaf-plate clicks somewhere above. The hum holds. The light holds. The basin holds. The creatures hold, in their small silent way, in the way the rocks hold, in the way the shadows hold, in the way the place holds itself, in the seam of the world, in the half-second, in the wrongness that is, here, the only rightness.

The place is held. The things in it are held. You, sitting, are, for the half-second you are here, also held. The hum is the same. The light is the same. The basin is, as it has been, as it will be, exactly.
