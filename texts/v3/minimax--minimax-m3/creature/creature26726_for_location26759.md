What lives in the hollow begins, for you, with the rhythm. The hammer is tapping, and the tapping is regular, and the regularity is a kind of presence. The hammer is not alive. The hammer is a small block of iron on the end of a lever, and the lever is lifted by a cam on the flywheel shaft, and the cam has been turning for as long as the flywheel has been turning, and the flywheel has been turning for a long time. But the tapping is the kind of tapping that carries in wet air and arrives at you as a sound that means something, even when you know what makes it. It means *I am here. I am still here.* It means it the way a heron means it. You do not argue with it. You follow it.

The flywheel lives above the engine, and it is the only large moving part exposed to the air. It turns slowly. It is patient in the way that patient things are patient — without intending to be patient, without knowing that patience is what it is doing. The patience is older than the bearings. The bearings may be new — there is a shine to them that the rest of the machine does not have — but the patience is the patience of a thing that has been turning for a long time and intends to keep turning. The flywheel is heavy. You can see, in the way it moves, that it is heavy. It does not hurry because it does not need to hurry. The work it does is the kind of work that is done slowly, and done for a long time, and done without complaint.

The fire lives in the belly of the boiler. It is a fire of peat-brick, half-collapsed, glowing beneath the grate. It is not a large fire. It is banked — pushed together, covered in its own ash, left to smolder at the rate at which a fire smolders when no one is asking it to be more than it is. The fire is unattended. It has been unattended for some time, perhaps a long time, perhaps since the last person who came at a particular hour stopped coming at that hour and began coming at a different hour, or stopped coming entirely. The fire does not mind. The fire does not appear to have the capacity to mind. It glows. It consumes, slowly, the peat it has been given. It heats the water in the boiler above it. The water moves. The engine runs. The flywheel turns. The hammer taps. None of this requires the fire to do anything other than what it is doing. The fire is enough.

The engine lives, in a way that is hard to put a hand on. Not alive in the way a frog is alive. But the engine is not a thing that is simply there. The engine has a rhythm, and the rhythm has a presence, and the presence is the heartbeat of the hollow. When the engine shifts — and it shifts, occasionally, in a way you feel more than hear, a re-phasing of its own internal agreement — the whole place re-aligns. The tapping re-aligns. The flywheel's slow patience adjusts, by a fraction, its tempo. The pipes, somewhere, tick. The dials, in their constant small disagreement, agree, for a moment, on something, and then return to their disagreement. The engine holds a pattern. The pattern is older than the upper shelf. The pattern is older than the engine. The engine was built to hold the pattern, or built around the pattern, or built to keep company with the pattern. The engine is the pattern's way of staying.

The frog is the first thing that lives here that you might call a creature. It sits on a flange — a circular plate where two sections of pipe join, bronze-green with verdigris. The frog is the same color, in patches, in the way that frogs are. It does not move when you arrive. It watches you the way frogs watch things, which is to say, with no opinion at all, but with the full weight of its small witness. Its throat pulses. Its eyes, gold-rimmed, hold the light of the flywheel in them, and the flywheel, reflected, turns inside the frog's head. There may be other frogs. You do not see them, but there is the sense, looking at the flanges, that they are all occupied. Some of them by frogs. Some of them by nothing. Some of them, perhaps, by the same frog moving between them with a slowness that is hard to distinguish from stillness.

The dragonfly hangs above the trough at a height that is exactly the height dragonflies like. It does not decide. Its wings are a clear blue, the blue of a sky the hollow does not have. It is in the air the way a thought is in the air before it lands. It has been here for a while. It will be here for a while longer. Its shadow on the water is a small dark stitch, moving with the water or not moving with the water — you cannot, from this angle, tell.

The moss is everywhere, and is not one moss. There is the kind that creeps along the lowest pipework, small and dense, the color of verdigris, and the verdigris is the color of the moss, and you cannot, in the lowest runs of pipework, tell which is which. There is the kind that pads the joints between stones, looser, taller, holding its own rain. There is the kind that has climbed a foot up the wall of the condenser and is making its slow way around the sweating flank of the iron bottle, and this moss is a different green again, a green that has a yellow in it, and the iron sweats onto it and the moss drinks the sweat and grows. The mosses do not hurry. They are the slowest life in the hollow. They will be here when the pipes are not.
