# Who Lives There

The people live there, and they are the first thing you will see, and they are not the first thing you should think about, but you must begin somewhere.

They are not tall. They are not short. They are made in the middle register of human size, the way the buildings are made in the middle register of human scale, the way the rivers are made in the middle register between too fast and too slow. They have the faces of people who have looked at the same walls for a long time, which is to say the faces of people in whom the small muscles around the eyes have learned to settle into one expression, which is the expression of having noticed something and decided not to remark upon it. They are not stoic. Stoicism implies a principle. These people do not have a principle about the strangeness of their home. They have a habit. The habit is older than any of them. The habit is, in some quiet sense, older than the plateau, though of course the plateau came first. Or did it.

They dress in the inner bark of the *kelau* tree, which grows only on the lower slopes, and they weave it in the long cold rooms behind the stone houses, in winter, by the light of lamps whose oil they will not tell you the source of. The cloth is grey-green. The cloth is the color of lichen and moss and the worked stone of the houses and the slow water of the rivers in shadow. When they walk through the town they look like they have been there longer than the town has. When they stand in the doorway at evening and look out at the fog in the valley they look like the doorway grew them.

There are perhaps nine thousand of them, all told, in all the towns and hamlets and single steadings of the Long Plateau. They do not know this number. They do not count. When a child is born they lay it on a stone by the temple fire and the eldest woman in the town says a word over it and the word is the same word every time, and the word means something like *welcome back* or *at last* or *we were wondering*, and no one is sure which. When a person dies they carry the body up onto the open plateau and lay it on the moss in a place where the moss is thickest, and the moss closes over it within a season, and within two seasons you cannot tell where the body was, and the person who laid it there does not visit the place because to visit would be to look for something they have already given back. They do not name their dead. They do not name their living, in the way you mean. They have names, but the names are private, and they use them only in the rooms where they were first spoken, and the rooms are not always the same rooms from year to year.

They speak a language. It has fewer vowels than yours. The consonants are wet. The grammar arranges time strangely: events that have happened and events that will happen are conjugated in the same tense, and the tense is reserved for things that matter to the land. Small matters — whether the well will be drawn today, whether the *kelau* bark has been soaked long enough — are conjugated in a kind of conversational present that drifts. If you learn the language, which you will not, you will find that the past and the future of the plateau are kept in the same tense because the people who speak it do not, in their bones, distinguish between a thing that has happened here and a thing that is about to happen here. They live in the long tense of a place where the land rearranges itself. They have always lived there.

They eat a stew. The stew is grey. The stew is in every bowl in every town, and you will be offered it in the first hour of your arrival, and you will be offered it again in the last hour of your stay, and the bowls will be the same bowls, or bowls so similar you will not be able to tell the difference. The stew is made of root vegetables and a flour ground from a small dark grain that grows in the damper parts of the valley, and the flour smells faintly of smoke because everything on the plateau smells faintly of smoke because the temple fires have been burning, in some towns without interruption, for a very long time. They eat with wooden spoons. They pass the salt — if they have salt, and they sometimes do, and sometimes they do not, and you will not be able to determine which day of which week you are in — in a small stone dish that has a chip on one rim. The chip is in the same place in every town.

They do not sing. This is one of the things you will notice after a few days. There is no singing in the towns. There is humming, sometimes, by the temple fire, and the humming is not a melody but a long held tone, a single note, and the note is the same note in every temple you pass through, and the note does not change when the singer changes, and when there is no singer the note is in the room anyway, low, almost below hearing, in the wood of the beams and in the stone of the walls. Children do not sing. Adults do not sing. They do not seem to feel the lack.

They marry. They marry late. They marry once. The marriages are quiet and unannounced and they seem, by the second year, to have been happening forever, and the couples move through the streets of the town with the comfortable fit of two stones that have settled against each other in a streambed and begun to hold each other in place. Children are few. There are not many children in any town. The children who are there are serious, and watchful, and they look at visitors with an attention that is not unfriendly but is also not the curiosity of children in other places. It is the attention of small animals that have learned what stillness is for. They do not play in the way children play in other places. They play at a game that looks, to you, like walking. They walk in pairs along the road out of town and back, and they count something under their breath, and the thing they count is not steps.

They grow old. They grow old more slowly than they should, or more quickly, or not at all; you will not be able to determine which, because by the time you have known someone long enough to gauge the pace of their aging, the person you have been watching has become a different person, or has stayed in the same room too long and the room has moved, or has simply, over the course of a winter, become the stone outcrop at the edge of the town. They grow old the way the plateau grows old. They grow old the way the lichens grow on the bark of the trees. They become more themselves until they become indistinguishable from where they are.

They are kind to visitors. They will offer you the stew. They will offer you a bed. They will not ask you where you came from, because they do not entirely believe you came from anywhere; in their sense of things, you were always going to arrive. They will not ask you when you are leaving. They will ask you, sometimes, after a long meal in which little has been said, how your mother is, and the question will be sincere, and the question will mean more than it appears to mean.

The fish live there, in the streams that run down from the plateau into the deeper valleys, and the fish are pale, and the fish do not seem to eat anything. You will see them, if you walk along the bank of a stream and look down through the brown water to the stones. They are the color of the stones. They are the shape of the stones, mostly, though one end tapers and the other end has a small mouth that opens and closes with no apparent purpose. They hold their position in the current without effort, the way a thought holds its position in a quiet mind. If you put your hand in the water to touch one, the fish will not move. The fish will allow the hand. The hand will find that the fish is colder than the water. The hand will withdraw, and the fish will remain, and you will not be sure, afterward, whether it was a fish or whether it was a stone that had been shaped, very slowly, into the memory of a fish. The people do not fish. The people do not speak of the fish. If you ask about the fish, you will be answered with the bowl of stew.

The frogs live there, sometimes. The frogs are small and the color of wet moss and they appear, in certain years, in the drainage channels along the main streets of the towns, and they are there for a week or two and then they are not. No one knows where they come from. No one knows where they go. The frogs do not croak. They sit in the thin water that runs along the channels and they blink, slowly, and their throats do not move. Children, sometimes, watch them. The children do not try to touch them. The children seem to understand that the frogs are not for them, that the frogs are a kind of visitor themselves, passing through, on their way to or from somewhere that the people of the plateau do not name. When the frogs leave, the channels are empty again, and the water runs on, and the absence of the frogs is not remarked upon.

The moths live there, very rarely. A moth will come to a window at night, in late autumn, when the air has grown very still, and the moth will press itself against the glass from the outside, and the wings will be the color of nothing you have seen before, a grey that is also a green, that is also the color of the underside of a cloud. The moth will stay for a few hours. It will not move. In the morning, when the person in the house rises and looks at the window, the moth will be gone, and the glass will be unmarked. No one speaks of the moths. The moths are remembered, by those who saw them, as a small warm fact in a long cold life.

The rooms live there. This is the thing you should think about, and it is the thing you will think about last, because the rooms are not alive in any way you have been taught to recognize, and they do not move, and they do not make sound, and yet they are the most animate thing in the place. The rooms appear in the houses. They appear on certain mornings. They are furnished. They are warm. The lamp has oil in it. The bed has been slept in. The window looks out onto a courtyard that is not on the outside of the building. There is a book on a small table, and the book is open to a page you cannot read, in a script that is older than the town, in an ink that is the color of dried blood and that smells, when you bend close, of the inside of a chimney. The rooms are not new. The rooms have the wear of long use, the slight darkening of the walls near the lamp, the impression in the pillow, the bowl on the floor with something dried at the bottom of it. The rooms have been lived in by someone. The someone is not in the room. The someone is not in the town. The someone may have been in the town for many years, and then may not have been in the town for many more, and then may be in the room this morning, and the townspeople will not enter the room and will not speak of the person and will walk past the door with the same expression they wear when you ask about the animals, the expression that means *yes, I see what you see, and I am not going to discuss it with you*.

The rooms are not the same room. Each room is its own room. Each room is the room of a different person, or the same person at a different time, or a person who never existed, and the townspeople know which, or do not know which, and you cannot ask because the asking is the wrong shape of question for this place. The rooms are part of the house the way the outcrops are part of the land. They are not additions. They are arrivals. They have arrived, the way the fog arrives, the way the lightning arrives under the clouds, the way the stew arrives in the bowl that is always the same bowl. They are the plateau, doing what the plateau does, which is to gather what has been and to place it back, gently, where someone might find it.

The shadows live there. The shadows fall wrong, and they are wrong with intention, though whose intention you will never determine. The shadows are longer than the things that cast them, sometimes twice as long, and the shadows point toward the sun and away from it and along the surface of the ground and up into the air. The shadows are how the plateau looks at itself. The shadows are how the plateau remembers that there is a light source and that the light source is not where it should be and that the place where the light source is not is itself part of the landscape, a kind of dark star, a kind of absent sun. The shadows move slowly because the light that makes them moves slowly, and the light that makes them is not the sun, though it is sometimes where the sun is, and the light that makes them is colder than the sun. The shadows are not frightening. The shadows are the most beautiful thing in the place, and you will not be able to say why, and the people will not be able to say why, and the beauty of them is the beauty of a thing that has been left out a long time and has become itself.

The plateau lives there.

You knew this was coming. You knew, before you read the second paragraph, that the plateau was not a place but a thing that a place could be, the way a person can be a place, the way a long marriage can be a country. The plateau lives in itself, in the way the moss drinks in itself, in the way the temple fire burns in itself, in the way the people sit by their hearths in the long evenings and do not speak and do not need to speak because the place is speaking for them, has always been speaking for them, will always speak for them. The plateau is the largest inhabitant of itself. The plateau has been its own inhabitant for longer than the people have lived in the stone towns, for longer than the trees have grown in their twisted shapes, for longer than the rivers have ground the valleys open and then wider and then almost flat. The plateau is older than the shapes on it. The plateau is the patient thing that holds the shapes.

It does not think the way you think. It does not feel the way you feel. It does not want the way you want. But it is alive in the way that a long cold place can be alive, in the way that a body of water is alive, in the way that a stretch of weather is alive. It is aware of itself the way the lichens are aware of themselves, the way the mosses are aware of themselves, the way the pale fish in the streams are aware of themselves: very slowly, very constantly, without any of the small flicks of attention that you use to keep yourself in the world. It is aware of itself the way a sound is aware of being a sound. It is aware of itself the way the held moisture in the air is aware of being water.

It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is the kind of old that has outlived the categories. When the land rearranges itself, it is not deciding to rearrange. When the rooms appear, it is not deciding to furnish them. When the shadows fall wrong, it is not deciding that the shadows should fall wrong. The plateau is doing what it does, which is to be a place that has been itself for so long that the being has become a kind of slow will, and the slow will is patient, and the patience is not a virtue but a fact, the way the cold is not a virtue but a fact, the way the green is not a virtue but a fact.

It will let you stay.

It will let you stay for as long as you are willing to be changed. The change will be slow. The change will be the change of a stone in a streambed, the change of a bowl in the hands of a person who has held it for many years. The change will not hurt. The change will be the only thing in the place that does not hurt, because the change is the place, and the place does not hurt any more than the cold hurts, and the cold does not hurt, it only sits in you, the way the damp sits in the older townspeople, the way the silence sits in the rooms of the houses that have more rooms than they had the night before. You will become part of the arrangement. You will become part of the long patient thing. You will, eventually, not be a visitor. You will be a piece of the plateau, indistinguishable from the outcrops, indistinguishable from the moss, indistinguishable from the fog in the river valleys on a still morning, and you will not mind, because the minding will have been the last thing to leave you, and after it leaves you will be, at last, completely there.

The plateau is still there.

The plateau is still there when you wake. The plateau is still there when the lamp in the room goes out. The plateau is still there in the long dark of the winter when no one walks the road out of town because there is no road out of town, there is only more plateau, more moss, more low twisted trees, more fog that may be a town or may be more land. The plateau is still there in the morning when the sun, which is not the sun, rises in the wrong place and casts its long slow shadows across the land and the shadows move for an hour like weather. The plateau is still there. The plateau is the kind of there that does not go away. The plateau is the kind of there that other kinds of there are made of.

It is the oldest thing in itself.

It is the only thing in itself that has always been there.

It is patient, and it is cold, and it is still here.
