# Who Lives in the Lowland That Was a Mill

Nothing lives here. That is the first thing, and the most important thing, and it is a kind of life in itself — a negative space where lives ought to be, an outline drawn in the shape of an absence. The heron that is not standing on the smokestack. The rat that is not under the turbine housing. The swallows that are not under the eaves. These absences are so specific, so pointed, so *positioned* that they begin, after a while, to have their own weight. They are like the missing teeth in a smile: you cannot stop seeing the gaps. The absence is a resident. The absence has been here longer than anything else.

What lives here, then, is the **mill itself** — not as a building, but as a verb that has forgotten how to stop. It is a single ongoing action that has outlived the world that set it in motion. The boilers are running. The dynamos are turning. The conveyors are hauling green loads of kelp and creeper through flooded bays. The forges are burning at the bottom of the water, the surface of the pool actually steaming on the iron tops of them, the orange glow pulsing in the lowest rooms like the heart of something that should have stopped beating a long time ago. These are not machines anymore. These are the inhabitants. They are the citizens. They have tenure. They have memory without mind. The belt that has frayed but not stopped, the pulleys turning against a belt that has nothing left to drive, the turbines turning in open water each one the size of a small house — these are the oldest things in the valley, and they are the most awake.

And there is the **water**, which is not water. It is water the way a memory is a memory: faithful in every particular, and wrong in some essential way that you cannot name. It does things that water does not do. It flows uphill on a tilted floor. It leaves a wall halfway down and hangs in the air for a moment — a flat shining membrane, a held breath — before continuing to the floor. A channel flows the wrong way, away from the turbines, reluctant, into a culvert that runs beneath a road that ends. The water level does not rise or fall but **thickens**, gaining body, becoming more itself, the surface becoming more present, more real, as if it is being concentrated by standing. The water is not a substance here. The water is a temperament. The water is warm the way bathwater is warm, and that warmth is wrong, and the wrongness is the point. It smells of rust and of something sweeter underneath — rot, but the wrong kind of rot, too clean, as if the rot is being performed rather than occurring. The water is a thing that has decided to be water, and has been deciding for so long that it has forgotten there was ever another option.

And there is the **vegetation**, which is not vegetation but a kind of conquest. It is lush the way a city is lush — overgrown, triumphant, having taken every horizontal surface and every vertical surface and every diagonal surface and every surface that was once horizontal and is now at an angle. Ferns the size of dining tables uncurl from the tops of the boilers. Vines as thick as your arm drop from the crane arms and disappear into the water without a splash. Flowering things bloom in clumps on the conveyor housings — purple, magenta, a yellow so bright it looks fake — and the flowers have no smell, or they have a smell that gets lost in the roar. The flowers do something else, too, something the eye almost catches: they blink, slightly, between two positions — slightly to the left, slightly to the right — and you cannot catch them doing it. The vegetation is not growing. The vegetation has *arrived*. It has been here so long that it has begun to participate in the unreality of the place, to flicker between two states like everything else here, the smokestack's bricks rearranging themselves into an earlier version of the stack, the turbines turning at speeds that do not reconcile with one another, one of them fast-forwarding or one of them running backward or your eyes being wrong.

And there is the **sound**.

The sound is not a sound. The sound is a resident the way the absence of the heron is a resident. It has a thousand sources, none of them primary. The water moving against the iron of the boilers. The boilers heating themselves against an oncoming cold that is not coming. The pumps in the lower works still pumping. The pulleys in a tower turning against a belt that has frayed but not stopped. The turbines, each one turning at its own rate, producing not a mechanical sound but a **sigh**, the sound of something that has been doing the same thing for so long that the doing has become a kind of grief, or a kind of joy, or a kind of weather. Separately, the turbines hum. Together, they are a **chord**. The chord changes key, sometimes, when you are not listening for it. The chord is the same key as the dynamos, which is the same key as the boilers, which is the same key as the water moving against the brick, which is the same key as the wind that is somewhere here, which is the same key as the silence where the heron should be. The chord is the binding agent. The chord is the only thing holding the mill and the water and the vegetation and the time together into a single place. The chord is the place.

And there is the **time**, which is not entirely trustworthy. The country remembers the mill — its smokestacks, its night-shift lanterns, the smell of coal and lye that came down off the valley when the wind was right. That was a long time ago, or a short time ago. Time here has the consistency of the warm water. It holds you. It does not hold you. It is a wall against the windows. It is a wet film across the floor of a ruined boiler house. There is no rule to it. The smokestacks are still there, three of them, brick and rivet, poking up out of the waterline like fingers out of bathwater, and from the tops of them thin plumes still issue — not smoke, not steam, but a kind of grey-white breath that hangs in the air and does not move. The plumes meet the low ceiling of cloud and become part of it. The plumes are not smoke. The plumes are what smoke used to be, continued by other means.

And there is the **light**, which is the color of old aquarium glass, a wet greenish dim, the sun happening very far overhead and being filtered through a hundred feet of humid air. The sky and the water and the steam are all the same color. You stand at the seam of them and cannot see it. Things lose their edges at twenty yards. A chimney across the flooded yard is fully visible; the base of the same chimney is a smear. The light is a resident too. The light has been here long enough to have forgotten what direct means. The light has learned to be water.

And there is the **air itself**, which is doing a small strange thing — it is being pressed. Somewhere, there is a press, and it is pressing, and the things it is pressing are not metal but air, and the air that comes out the other side is shaped, briefly, like coins, and these coin-shaped puffs of air drift away on the surface of the water and you can see them, faintly, glinting. The air is being minted. The air is being made into currency that cannot be spent. The air is part of the mill now. The air works here.

So: nothing lives here. The mill lives here. The water lives here, which is not water. The vegetation lives here, which is not vegetation. The chord lives here, which is not sound. The time lives here, which is not time. The light lives here, which is not light. The air lives here, which is being minted into coins nobody will ever spend. A piston rises and falls in an open housing where there is no cylinder. A belt turns a pulley that turns a shaft that ends in the air, and the end of the shaft, in the air, where nothing should be, is driving something you can hear being driven somewhere up in the cloud layer, beyond sight. A gear meshes with another gear in mid-water, no housing, no frame, the teeth moving correctly, the two wheels spinning in opposite directions, supporting each other in the warm water.

The mill is running. The mill has been running for a long time. The mill will run after the mill has fallen down. The mill will run after you leave. The mill is running now in the dim green light, in the deafening chord, in the warm water, in the absence of any creature to hear it, in the slowness of the real, in the patience of the eating that has been paused halfway up every wall. The water comes up to your knees. The water is a wall. The water is a film. The water holds you. The water does not hold you.

You, who have come here, are not a resident. You are the only thing in the place that knows it is in a place. That is your role. That is what you are for. You stand at the seam of sky and water and steam and cannot see it, and the not-seeing is the whole of your life here, and the mill runs through you the way the chord runs through the turbines, the way the water runs through the brick, the way the wrong time runs through the dim green light, and you hold still, and you do not hold still, and the mill is running, and the mill is running, and the mill is running.
