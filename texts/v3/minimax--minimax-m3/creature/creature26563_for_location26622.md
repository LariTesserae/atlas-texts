What lives here is, first, the fig. Not a tree exactly, and not a single tree either — a confederation, a long sentence of a thing that has been writing itself across this building for what may be a hundred and thirty years and is not yet finished. It began as a swallowed seed. A bird — some small, blunt-beaked bird, gone now from this watershed or only gone from this stretch of it — passed the seed in flight, or settled for a moment on a rafter that was then a rafter and not yet a branch, and dropped it. Or the seed came in a flood, floated in on a high water, lodged in a crook of iron. No one marked the beginning. The fig does not know its own beginning either, in the way that a sentence does not know which word began it.

The fig is the place, now, the way a coral is a reef. The brick and the iron are the substrate. The fig is the living body spread over it. The original roof-slates are gone — slid, dissolved, taken for other buildings, perhaps, or simply lost into the bog — and the fig has closed the gap the way a wound closes: with surface. Its upper roots have knit themselves into a continuous green dome, a second skin laid over the timbers, and through that skin comes the amber-green light that is the place's own weather. Inside the dome it is always the hour after a rain, even at noon, even in dry weeks, because the fig keeps its own reservoir. A fig of this size is mostly water. It breathes water out as vapor from its leaves all day, and the vapor hangs in the closed hall, and condenses on the lower leaves, and falls back, and is breathed out again, a closed weather, a private season.

You can read the fig's age, if you know how to read, in the grain of its lower roots. The lowest roots, the ones that have reached the floor and are walking across it in slow braids, are the oldest, and they are as thick as a man's thigh and as pale as the inside of an apple. The fig's outer skin, where it is exposed to air, is pale grey and very smooth, almost like skin. Where it is wet it is pinker, rawer, like a lip. Where the roots are furred with the white mold — and they are furred, everywhere, a fine pelt of mold that the constant wetness feeds — they look like something older. Like something a tide would wash up. The mold itself is its own small being, of course, but the fig wears it the way a fox wears its winter coat, without knowing.

The fig has not eaten the building. This is the thing to understand, and it is easy to misunderstand, because the building is gone in the way the building used to mean. The fig has *read* the building, in the way the prompt says. It has found the iron uprights and wrapped them and thickened against them, and now if you cut the fig away the upright would not stand. The fig is the upright's skin. The fig is the steam main's shadow. The fig is the second meaning of every pipe. It has learned the building's grammar, and it is reciting the building in a slower language, and the building is still legible underneath if you know how to look, the way a fossil is legible in the rock that has taken its shape. A paleographer, given a hundred years, could reconstruct the pump house from the fig.

Then the moss. The moss is what the floor has become in the spaces the fig has not occupied. The hummocks where the heavy machines stood are Sphagnum, mostly, and some other things — a darker, clumped moss that holds water like a sponge and a paler one that grows in tight starbursts, an inch across, that drink from the air. The hummocks are firm underfoot if you step on the crowns of them. The pools between them are ankle-deep and black and warm, and they are not still. They breathe. A slow peristalsis moves through the standing water as the pump below the floor draws and releases, draws and releases, and on the surface of each pool the tannin slick parts and reforms and parts again in the rhythm of the pump, so that the floor looks like a small dark sea with a tide of its own. The moss grows at the edges of the hummocks, down into the water, and where it grows underwater it is a different moss, a longer-leaved, looser kind, the color of weak tea, and it sways with the breath of the pump, a long slow weathervane of moss, breathing out, breathing in.

In the moss live things too small to be certain of. Springtails, certainly, the white ones that you see only when they jump. Mites, the red ones, the size of a typed period. A thing like a tiny shrimp, no, smaller, that swims in a looping path through the upper half-inch of pool-water and is visible only as a comma of motion. Rotifers. Nematodes. The floor is a country. The floor is a country that has been annexed by the fig but still runs its own affairs in the wet, in the small, in the parts of the world that a fig cannot be bothered with.

The frogs. Two frogs, as far as you can tell, and you can never quite be sure there are not more. The frog you see most often is a small one, the color of a wet leaf, with a pale throat and eyes like polished jet. It sits in the same inch of water in the same pool, beside the same hummock, and it considers the same leaf — a small fallen leaf, tan, curled at the edges — with the same long attention, and it has been there, you begin to suspect, for as long as you have been watching. The second frog is larger and darker and lives, you think, in a different pool near the broken boiler, where the water is a little warmer from the steam-vent and where the fig roots hang lowest. You have seen it twice. It is a different frog. You are almost sure. It has a small nick in the web of its left hind foot, and when it jumps it makes a low, wet sound, the sound a palm makes when it pulls off a wet stone, and the sound travels a long way in the quiet. The frogs do not call here. There is no reason to. There is no mate to be called, or no mate to be called to, or the frog has decided there is no mate, which is a different thing. They are quiet frogs. They are the kind of quiet a place gets when it has been alone a long time and has stopped performing for visitors.

The pump. You do not see the pump; it is below the floor, in a chamber you cannot reach without taking an axe to the moss, and the chamber is flooded, and the air above the water in the chamber is a different air, hotter, with the smell of old grease and iron and the particular tang of water that has been in contact with copper for a very long time. The pump turns. Once every seven seconds, the pump completes a cycle, and you feel this in the water on your boots, in the breathing of the pools, in the way the fig-root draping from the boiler-mouth trembles faintly on the third beat of every cycle. The pump is on no one's schedule. It may be on a timer that was set before anyone now living was born, and the timer may have a mainspring or a falling weight or a column of water that tips a small brass cup every seven seconds and that cup is what drives the pump. Or the pump may be on a siphon, a self-priming siphon that, once started, will run until the water in the chamber falls below the intake, and then the pump will gulp air and stop, and the chamber will refill from below, from the bog, in some hours or some days, and the pump will start again. The pump has its own weather. The pump is the second heart of the place. The pump does not know it is a ruin.

The boiler breathes. The seam that vents steam is on the upper curve of the boiler, near the back, where a rivet has given way or a patch has lifted, and the steam comes out in a slow sigh, and between the sighs there is a long silence, and the silence is the length of a phrase, exactly, the way a sentence has a length. The steam is not hot. It has been traveling a long way through old metal to get out, and it is the temperature of a hand on a feverish forehead. It rises, and meets the fig-roots, and condenses on them, and drips off them, and the dripping is one of the many drippings of the place. The boiler has been broken open from within. The roots did this. The fig, fed by the warm mineral water that used to come up the pipes, sent a probe-root down the inside of the boiler, and the probe-root thickened, and the thickening split the iron, and out of the split came the slow curtain of fig root that hangs now in the mouth of the boiler like a tongue, white and pink and furred with mold, dripping. The boiler was broken open the way a seed is broken open. The fig germinated the boiler. The fig ate the boiler the way a child eats a boiled sweet — slowly, with the whole mouth closed around it, until there is nothing left but a shape the tongue remembers.

The ants. There are ants, and you do not see them for a long time, and then you see them everywhere. They are small, dark, and they walk in lines that are not the lines of the building and not the lines of the fig. They have their own grammar. They are farming something on the underside of the lower fig leaves — a small pale aphid, or a scale insect, or a psocid, something that drinks sap and excretes a sweet dew, and the ants drink the dew and tend the insects and move them from leaf to leaf as the leaves age. The ants have made a city of the fig's lower limbs. The ants are the only quick thing in the place. When one runs across your hand it is the only motion in the room that does not obey the slow clock of the pump. The ants do not know they live in a pump house. The ants do not know what a pump house is. They live in a tree, in a wet warm tree, and the tree is also a building, and the building is also a place, and the place is also a tongue, and the tongue is being read by something patient that is not the ants and is not you and may not be anything with a name.

There is a fish. You do not know this for a long time, and then one afternoon, in a pool near the broken boiler, you see a small pale shape move in the water, a shape with a flick at one end, and it is a fish. A mudfish, perhaps, or a small catfish, or a killifish — something that can live in water this warm and this thin of oxygen, that can breathe through its skin if it has to, that has come up from the bog through whatever opening in the floor admits water from below. The fish is three inches long. The fish has been here, in this pool, in this three-inch water, for a long time, and it has grown no larger, because there is not enough to eat, and it has not died, because there is enough. The fish is the color of the water. The fish is what the water has become when it stands still long enough to notice itself.

There are midges. In the warm weeks, in the late afternoons, the midges come up out of the moss in small pale clouds and hover under the fig's lower leaves, and the frog sees them, and the frog does not always eat them, and the midges know this, and the dance of the midges is partly a dance and partly a confidence. The midges are the only flying thing. The midges are the only thing in the place, besides the steam, that is in the air with any conviction.

What lives here, then, is a stack of slownesses. The fig on the scale of years. The moss on the scale of months. The frog on the scale of the afternoon. The ant on the scale of the minute. The fish on the scale of the breath. The pump on the scale of the seven-second heart. The midge on the scale of the wingbeat. The mold on the scale of the wet. They are all, in their different clocks, working on the same sentence, the long translation of the building into the tree, and none of them are in charge, and the sentence is not yet finished, and may not be finished for another hundred and thirty years, and may then be finished into something no one now living will be able to read, and that is, perhaps, the point. The place is not in a hurry. The place has been left alone long enough to be in no hurry at all, and the slowness is itself the most alive thing about it, the way a held breath is the most alive thing in a still room. The place is breathing. The place is translating. The place is, in its own slow grammar, becoming a sentence in a language the bog will be the only one left to read.
