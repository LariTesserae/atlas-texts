# Who Lives in the Bottled Garden

The most obvious answer is also the wrong one. Nothing *lives* here so much as *endures* here. The difference matters, because endurance is what happens when a place refuses to die and a few stubborn lineages simply refuse to leave. The Bottled Garden is closed — open only to the sky, sealed everywhere else by walls of fresh basalt — and the creatures within it have had to make a covenant with the only resource that gets through. They drink the rock's breath. They have been drinking it for a very long time. In return, they have become nearly indistinguishable from the place that grew them.

Let me try to meet them.

---

The columns are the city, and the citizens of the city are the column-sloths. They are the largest things that move here, and they are the reason the place has the feel of a settlement at all.

You will not see one until you have stood very still for some time. A column-sloth is the size of a large child folded in on itself, perhaps the size of a dog of the heavier sort, but the body is built for hanging rather than holding. The limbs are long, the hands are wide, each finger is a hook. The fur is dense and pale and grows in long, soft ropes that part along the animal's back and fall on either side like wet hair. The fur is not its own colour. The fur is the colour of whatever the animal has been sleeping against. The sloth at the base of the grey-green pillar is grey-green. The sloth under the pink overhang is faintly rose. The sloth that has spent the wet season in the copper carpet is the colour of tarnished brass. The pigment is not in the hair. The pigment is in the things that have taken up residence *in* the hair.

The fur is a garden.

Each rope of hair is, at this scale, a valley. A particular valley has its own weather. Along the spine, where the animal is warmest, the valleys are jungles — dense, dark, perpetually moist, threaded with capillaries of blood close to the surface. Down the flanks they become something else: orchards, where tiny stalked fruiting bodies push up through the fur and the sloth walks through its own harvest and eats as it goes. In the deepest parts of the coat, near the skin, a community of mites keeps the whole thing turned and aerated. They are not parasites. They are tenants. The sloth could not live without them, and they could not live without the sloth, and the moss-fur could not live without either of them, and the column could not grow as it does without the sloths' manure. The whole column is a single body, in a sense — trunk and sloth and sloth's garden and the worm-worked soil where the sloth sleeps, all one system, all breathing together.

The sloths move at the speed of condensation. A hand reaches out, very slowly, and finds the next branch, and the weight follows, very slowly, and the animal settles. A minute passes. The hand reaches again. You can stand within arm's length and the sloth will arrive at the next branch in time for you to make a small lunch and finish it. It does not seem to mind being watched. It does not seem to mind anything. Its face, when you can finally see it, is placid beyond placidity: round, soft, the eyes set wide and looking out from under a permanent canopy of mossy brow. The expression is the expression of a thing that has never had to hurry and has never been hunted and has therefore no need of expressions at all.

But they are not stupid. They are slow in the way that deep water is slow — the slowness of something with a great deal of mass and no reason to move it. They know the columns. They know their own column. They know the path to it. A particular sloth, if you could mark it, would return to the same pillar, the same resting site, the same patch of swept basalt at its base, for as long as it lives. They have the settlement-pattern of a tenure. They are householders. The cairns at the foot of the columns are their cairns — the slow accumulation of fibre and dung and shed claw-sheath, the daily record of a life lived in one place, year after year.

You realise, after a while, that the columns are not the city. The columns are the *tenements*. The sloths are the residents.

---

The lichen-moths are the smallest things you can see, and they are also, in their way, the loudest.

They are delicate beyond delicacy. Their wings are the breadth of your thumbnail, patterned in fine grey-and-ochre scales that match, exactly, the lichen that crusts the upper basalt. When a moth settles, it does not settle so much as *cease to fly*, and from one moment to the next it is part of the rock. You do not lose sight of it. You discover that there was nothing to lose sight of. The wings fold, and the moth is a flake of stone, and a flake of stone is also a moth, and you can no longer tell the difference.

But they are not hiding. They are *presenting*. A moth on the basalt is showing the basalt to itself. The wings are a small, perfect, unfurled map of the surface they have chosen, and the choosing is not random. A moth will spend its whole life on one wall, on one stretch of stone, on a territory no larger than your spread hands. It will not fly far. It will not fly often. The flights are short and purposeful and have the character of errands rather than journeys: across the column, up the wall, down again, always returning to the same crevice, the same sleeping site, the same folded posture in the same patch of lichen.

They are, in this sense, the most settled of all the citizens. The sloths are householders, but a sloth will still cross the colonnade in the course of a season, will still visit the upper mats for the wet-season flowers. A lichen-moth will live its whole life in a square foot of basalt. It will eat the lichen of that square foot. It will drink the fog of that square foot. It will mate on the lichen of that square foot and lay its eggs in the crevices of that square foot. The square foot will be its world, and the world will be enough.

The pale flowers of the upper mats are theirs, too. The moths do not pollinate the flowers, exactly, but they visit them at night, and the flowers seem to be waiting for them, and the exchange between them is one of the few transactions in the basin that happen in the dark. The pale flowers open only at night, only in the wet season, only when the air is heavy enough to support a moth on its surface tension. The moths visit them only when the flowers are open. The flowers give a thin, sweet water. The moths give a dusting of something — a spore, a scale, a memory. It is a courtship that has been going on for a very long time, and neither party seems to be in a rush to finish it.

And they are the only light, at night, that the basin has of its own. The wings glow, faintly, with a cold green that has nothing to do with the sun. You cannot see by it. You can see *in* it. The colonnade becomes a place of pale vertical lines, and the lines move very slowly, and you understand, finally, why the basin feels the way it does at night — the long-held chord, the silent hum. It is the moths. There are many more of them than you thought. The whole basin, at night, is a single slow-lit constellation, and the constellation is breathing.

---

The slugs are the longest of the citizens, and the slowest, and the most patient.

A column-slug is a creature of perhaps a forearm's length, perhaps a small child's length, laid out in the wet copper carpet between the pillars. The body is thick, soft, the colour of the wet clay it lies on. The foot is a single rippling sheet that carries the whole animal forward at a speed you can only measure by coming back in an hour and noticing that it has moved. It has moved the length of your hand. It is pleased with itself. It will continue.

The slugs eat the moss. The moss does not seem to mind. The slug's mouth is a small, soft rasp, and the moss it eats is replaced, in time, by more moss, and the slug returns to the same patch in a long, slow cycle that you could mistake for the rhythm of the seasons. The slug and the moss have known each other for a long time. The slug's silver trail, when it dries, becomes a kind of varnish over the basalt, and the varnish holds the fog-water longer than the bare stone would have, and the moss grows thicker where the slugs have been. The slugs are farmers. They are harvesting a crop they are also cultivating. They are slow about it. There is no rush.

The slugs are also, in their way, the messengers of the basin. They are the only creatures here that will cross the paths between any two points without preference. A sloth walks its own track. A moth stays on its own square. A beetle — we will come to the beetles — has its own errands. A slug goes everywhere. The silver trails are the only record the basin has of a creature that has been everywhere and seen everything, and the trails, when they dry, become a kind of map. If you could read them, you would know the colonnade better than any sloth or any moth. You would know the cairns, the resting sites, the meeting-places, the deep paths into the upper mats. You would know, in particular, the wet places — the spots where the fog hangs longest, where the moss is fattest, where the small pale flowers are most likely to bloom. The slugs know them. The slugs go there, in their slow and silver way, and the basin remembers that they went.

---

The beetles are the only things in the Bottled Garden that have any claim to industry.

They are dark. They are round. They are the size of the end of your thumb. They are also, despite their modest appearance, the most important piece of the basin's engineering.

A dung-beetle, in any other place, has a simple job. Here the job is more complicated, because there is no grass and no mammals in the ordinary sense, and what passes for dung has to be manufactured from whatever the sloths and the smaller creatures leave behind. The beetles of the Bottled Garden are recyclers of a more general sort. They will roll a ball of anything: a clump of shed moss-fibre, a tuft of sloth-fur, a fragment of cast skin, a single polished seed. They shape it between their forelegs with a patience that is its own kind of slowness — a brisk, busy, purposeful slowness, the slowness of a thing that has errands to run. The ball grows. The beetle pushes. The beetle climbs. The beetle loses the ball down a crack and goes back for more. The beetle finds the ball again and pushes it somewhere else.

The somewhere else is, almost always, a cairn.

A cairn is not, exactly, a midden. It is a place where many paths meet, and at such places the basalt is worn into a shallow basin, and at the bottom of the basin the moss is thin because the traffic is heavy, and the beetles bring their balls here and bury them in the soft exposed stone. The cairn grows. Over centuries, the cairn becomes a low mound. Over longer centuries, the mound becomes a hill. Some of the hills in the Bottled Garden, if you knew where to look, are older than the creatures currently living on them. They are the slow-built, slow-broken, slow-rebuilt summits of a settlement that has been in continuous occupation for a very long time.

The beetles do not know this. The beetles know the cairn at the junction near their own column, and the path to it, and the soft spot at the bottom of it, and the right time of year to bring a ball, and that is the whole of a beetle's world. The beetle is content. The beetle is the only creature in the basin that gives the impression of being satisfied with its work. The sloths are placid, but the placidity is the placidity of deep water. The moths are present, but the presence is the presence of stone. The slugs are patient, but the patience is the patience of a thing that has no other option. The beetles are, somehow, *busy*. They are the only citizens of the Bottled Garden who seem to have a vocation.

---

The worms are the only citizens you will never meet.

They are there. You know they are there because the moss gives under your boot with that small sigh, and the giving is the worms' work, and the worms' work is the only reason the moss gives at all. The worms of the Bottled Garden are pale, very long, very thin, and they live in the dark between the basalt and the moss, in a layer of fine red soil that is mostly not soil at all — it is the digested output of a hundred generations of moss, the broken-down bodies of a hundred generations of sloth-mites, the slow mineral dust of a hundred centuries of basalt erosion. The worms eat this. The worms turn this. The worms are, in a sense, the basin's gut.

They are also, in another sense, the basin's memory. A worm will, in the course of its slow life, eat a particle of soil and pass it through its body and leave it on the surface as a small dark cast. That cast will be covered by moss, and the moss will grow over it, and another worm will come, and another cast will be made, and the two casts will be separated by a season or a year or a century. If you could read the column of casts the way you read the rings of a tree, you would know everything. The wet years. The dry years. The years when the sloths were more numerous. The years when a flake fell from the rim. The years when the upper mats bloomed. The basin is a chronicle. The worms are its scribes. They are also its librarians, because nothing in the chronicle is ever lost. Every particle is recycled, and every recycling is recorded, and the record is in the soil under the moss, waiting for someone who knows how to read it.

No one knows how to read it. The worms themselves do not know how to read it. The record is being made, and the record is being kept, and no one will ever read it, and this seems to the worms — insofar as anything seems to the worms — entirely fine.

---

And there is one more citizen of the Bottled Garden, and it is the one I have not yet named, and it is the one that, in a sense, is the place itself.

The basalt is alive. Not in the way a creature is alive, not in the way a plant is alive, but in the way a system is alive when it has been running long enough to have become a kind of organism. The walls exhale the fog. The columns drink the fog. The sloths eat the columns. The sloths' fur grows the moss. The sloths' dung feeds the beetles. The beetles build the cairns. The cairns hold the soil. The soil holds the worms. The worms feed the moss. The moss drinks the fog. The fog is exhaled by the basalt. The basalt drinks the sloths and the sloths and the moss and the worms, in the end, the way everything in the basin drinks everything else. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. The whole system is sealed, and the seal is what keeps it going, and the going is what makes it a system at all.

The Bottled Garden is its own smallest creature, and the basalt is its skin, and the fog is its breath, and the colonnade is its bone, and the sloths and the moths and the slugs and the beetles and the worms are its organs, and you, standing among the columns, are a thing that has come in from outside and will, eventually, go back out. The basin will be here when you leave. The basin was here before you arrived. The basin will be here after you are gone, and the lichen-moths will fold their wings and the column-sloths will sleep and the slugs will leave their silver trails and the beetles will push their small dark balls and the worms will turn the soil and the fog will form and the fog will lift, and the basin will breathe, and the basin will go on breathing, for as long as the basalt holds.

The basalt will hold for a long time.

You could live here a long time and never hear a sound louder than the settling of the rock.
