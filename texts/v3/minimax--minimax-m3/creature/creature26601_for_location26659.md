The frogs are the first to notice you, and the last to leave. They sit in the puddles and along the edges of the bone-bands, plum-sized, blue-gray, the color of a bruise a week old. There are dozens of them, possibly hundreds; no one has counted twice the same way. They do not flee. They stop calling when you come near, all of them at once, as if a single throat had been the only one making sound, and they watch you with eyes like wet seeds, and after a while — a minute, five, however long you can stand still — one of them calls again, and then another, and the room fills back up with their low creaking. They live in the water, is the simplest way to put it. They breed in the deeper pools at the southern end where the sheet stands an inch deep and mirror-still, and their tadpoles thread through the moss at the grout lines and emerge one season later as something the size of a thumbnail, and they grow, and they settle into their stations. Each frog seems to have one — a particular puddle, a particular cranny in the bone, a particular stone along the second course — and they return to it after rain, after disturbance, after the candles come and go. They are the room's memory of itself, the way the condensation is the room's blood.

But they are not the only ones.

The silverfish-things live along the bone-bands. They are longer than silverfish should be, the length of a finger, and they have more legs than is comfortable to count, and they glow a cold phosphorescent blue that is enough to read a hand by when the room is at its darkest. They live on the bone — they eat it, or what grows in it, or what dies in it — and their light seems to come from the bone itself, as if the bone were remembering how to shine. They move slowly along the knuckles and into the eye-sockets of the small skulls, and they pass each other there in the dark, two of them facing each other for a moment in the hollow of a jaw, antennae touching, before continuing on. They are the room's slow light.

The ash-moths are everywhere on the ceiling. They are the color of wet ash, and they powder the cedar planks in a way that looks like dust but is not. When anything moves below them — a person, a draft, a sudden thought — they fold themselves into invisibility, wings tucking, legs drawing in, becoming for a moment indistinguishable from the lichen-spots and the peg-heads. Then they unfold again, lazily, and resume their slow circulation. They do not eat anything anyone has identified. They breed rarely, and when they do, the caterpillars are the same color as the planks and live only in the gaps between them. The clawed thing that scrapes up there — the slow patient sound, patient and at home — is, most likely, one of them grown large, or something that eats them grown large, or something else entirely. No one has ever lifted a plank to look.

The click-beetles live on the floor and climb the lower courses of stone. Their backs are ridged like the underside of a mushroom, and as they walk they make a small click with each step — click, click, click — like pebbles dropped one at a time into a basin. You can hear them coming. They live in the grout and under the loose edges of tile, and they are the most numerous thing in the room by a factor you would not believe. They eat the moss, the fungus, the dead flies, each other. They are the room's digestion.

The fish in the deeper pools are something no one has explained. There are three of them now, or four, or five — they are hard to count and hard to see, because they only flip at long intervals and the pools are dim. They are pale, and not large, the size of a hand, and they should not be there. There is no inlet. There is no obvious food source. They survive on what falls in from above, perhaps, or on each other, or on something in the water that no one has sampled. Once a year, perhaps, one of them flips, and the sound is shocking in the quiet — a flat wet slap — and everyone in the room, frog and beetle and moth and person, goes still for a moment, and then resumes. The fish are the room's secret, the thing it keeps for itself.

The moss lives in the grout, in the northern third where the floor is wettest. It is sparse and pale, the kind that thrives on damp stone and patience, and it grows about a finger's width in a year. The lichen lives on the ceiling beams, in patterns that almost resemble writing and then stop. The drooping fern lives in a hairline crack near the second bone-band, leaning out over a niche where a faceless clay figure sits. The pale fruiting body at the far end lives on the bone and is bigger or smaller than it was last season; no one is sure, because it is the same color as the bone and grows in the same direction. These are the room's slow green things, the ones that have argued their way in and stayed.

And there are the people.

People who come here come back. They come once, and the room does something to them — not the frogs, not the moths, but the room itself, the held breath, the proportions that are off in a way you feel in your shoulders — and they come back. They come alone, mostly. They come at dusk, or after rain, or in the small hours when the silverfish-light is the only light there is. They bring small things and leave them in the niches. A bowl of water, set down carefully. A beetle on a leaf, placed gently. A finger-bone wrapped in linen, knotted at one end. They do not explain, and no one asks. They sit, and they listen, and after a while they go. They do not look back. They do not speak in the room. The frogs resume when they leave, as if they had only paused.

The people who come back become, in time, part of the room. Their offerings stay. The bowls dry and are refilled by the next visitor, or by condensation, or by rain through the bronze grating. The wrapped bones sit in their niches for years and then decades and the linen goes the color of the walls. The beetles on leaves are eaten, eventually, or dry, or are subsumed into the grout. The offerings do not decay the way offerings decay in dry rooms. They simply become another kind of fixture, another tile almost in the pattern.

And then there is what is in the walls.

There is water moving through hidden channels behind the stonework, and you can hear it if you put your ear to the bone-bands — a low rushing, somewhere between a stream and a whisper. The frogs press against the lower stones during the long dry spells and seem to listen to it. The silverfish follow the channels and appear in different places on different nights, as if they were traveling. And once, very rarely, something moves in the water that is not water. A slow pressure. A long body. The hum that lives under the floor — the one you feel in the soles of your feet, steady as a tuning fork — comes from there, from whatever is in the channels, or from the channels themselves, or from the room listening to its own blood.

No one has ever opened the walls to look.

The room does not need a name. It knows what it is. The frogs know. The silverfish know. The fish in the pools know, and turn slowly in their darkness, and wait for the next season. The people who come back know, in the part of them that holds still when the rest wants to move. And whatever lives in the water behind the walls knows, and moves at its own pace, and is patient, the way the room is patient, and will be there for a long time after.
