# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that no one lives here. *Things* live here. There is a difference, and the difference matters, because whoever built this hangar is long gone, and what remains is a tenancy — a squatter's republic of organisms that arrived without blueprints and have, generation by generation, rewritten the lease.

The dominant citizen is the fig. There are at least four trees, possibly six — it is hard to count because the canopy has knitted itself into a single continuous ceiling of leaf, and the trunks, where they descend through the warm pools, have fused at the waterline into what amounts to a single root-mass, a communal foot, a small forest pretending to be one organism the way a cathedral pretends to be one room. The figs are old. The figs were here before the possum. The figs were here before the hornets. The figs, in fact, may have been the first tenants of all, deposited as seeds in the droppings of some long-ago bird that found the hangar door ajar and flew in out of curiosity and never flew out, or flew out and left its inheritance behind. Fig seeds pass through the digestive tracts of birds with their germination enhanced, not diminished. This is a designed thing, an evolutionary contract: the fig bribes the bird with sugar, and the bird signs the contract in guano, and the fig seeds arrive pre-fertilized wherever the bird has been. So a bird came in. A bird ate figs elsewhere, and excreted here. And the figs grew. And the figs fruited. And the figs fed more birds. And the birds brought more seeds. This is how a forest begins — not with a grand design but with a single bowel movement made in the right place at the right time.

The figs are not alone in their root-mass. They have been joined, over the decades, by a kind of accidental arboretum. A seed of something else — a mulberry, perhaps, or a wild plum — landed in a fig's crotch and germinated, and the two have grown up together, their trunks leaning into each other like old drunks, their canopies interleaved. There is a fern — no, *many* ferns, the size of small trees, their fronds unrolling in the perpetual humidity with the slow ceremonial patience of hands opening in prayer. There is moss, dense and electric green, climbing the iron walls in a felt that is, by dry weight, mostly not the moss at all but the tiny creatures that live inside it: rotifers, tardigrades, nematodes, a whole ministry of nearly-invisible things whose business is the breakdown of dead matter into living matter and back again. There is, along the eastern wall, a single determined stand of what might once have been a small ornamental grass, now knee-high and seeding itself into the water, its pale green heads nodding at the surface like questions nobody asks.

The ferns are the senior citizens. They predate the figs by perhaps a century, having established themselves in the hangar's earliest years when the iron was merely damp rather than truly weeping, when the rivets wept rust but had not yet begun to weep soil. Ferns are patient. Ferns will wait. A fern spore can lie dormant for decades, waiting for a surface that is moist enough, shaded enough, mineral enough. When the hangar was new, none of those conditions held. When the hangar was a hundred years old, all of them did. The ferns arrived like late guests to a party that had already forgotten it was a party, and they set up housekeeping in the rust, and they have been here ever since, declining politely to comment on the newcomers.

In the warm pools, where the concrete slabs have tipped and the water has pooled between them, there is a different civilization, and a humbler one. The water is the color of weak tea — tannins from the fallen figs, leaf litter in slow dissolution, the mineral bleed of concrete surrendering its lime to the chemistry of decay. In this water there are things. There must be things. There is *always* something in still warm water, because still warm water is, biologically speaking, an invitation. Mosquito larvae hang from the surface in their rigid commas, breathing through snorkels. There is a small flatworm, or something that functions as a small flatworm, gliding along the underside of a tilted slab in search of biofilm. There are the bacterial mats — the actual ground floor of this whole enterprise — pink and orange and sulfur-yellow, layered in their chemical gradient, doing the work of turning iron into soil and soil into iron again, given enough centuries and no oversight. There are, somewhere in the murk, the things that eat the bacterial mats: the rotifers, the paramecia, the small crustaceans no bigger than a typed period. There are the things that eat those things: the mosquito larvae, the dragonfly nymphs clinging to the underside of a fig root with their extendable jaws folded patiently away. There are the things that eat the dragonfly nymphs: nothing currently, because the dragonfly nymph is at the top of this particular food chain, and at the top of a small warm pool in a ruined hangar in the middle of nowhere, the dragonfly nymph is, briefly, the tyrant of all it surveys. It has been here for two years. It will be here for one more, if it is lucky. Then it will climb out of the water, up the fig trunk, and split its skin open and emerge as something with wings, and it will spend a few weeks hunting smaller things over the warm pools, and then it will mate, and then it will die, and its body will fall into the water, and the water will take it back.

The hornets are upstairs. They have built — or rather, they have inherited, and then expanded, and then rebuilt — a paper nest the size of a watermelon under the eastern eave, where the iron roof meets the iron wall at a right angle and the condensation collects in a particular way. The nest is the color of old newsprint. It is warm to the touch on the outside, because inside, several hundred adult hornets are vibrating their flight muscles in coordinated unison, raising the interior temperature to the precise optimum for the larvae in their hexagonal cradles. The larvae are fed chewed-up fig, brought to them by the workers, who in turn are fed a sweet secretion produced by the larvae themselves in exchange — a kind of salivary tip, a carbohydrate bribe paid by the infants to the adults in return for being masticated and fed. The hornets are not aggressive. The hornets are *busy*. There is a difference. Aggression is what an animal does when it is threatened. Busyness is what an animal does when it has a job. The hornets have a job. The hornets are running a manufacturing operation. The hornets are, in their small way, the last industrialists of this hangar, the only remaining tenants who still understand what the place was *for*. They have, in their papery hanging city under the eastern eave, recreated, almost accidentally, the entire premise of the original structure: a building in which a great many small workers produce something of value in coordinated silence, humming. They are the inheritors. They do not know this. They do not care.

The possum — and it is a possum, almost certainly, though it has been here long enough that its descendants may be something slightly different now, something with smaller eyes and a more delicate foot and a fur the color of wet bark — lives in the canopy. It lives in the canopy the way a possum is meant to live in a canopy: slowly, deliberately, at night, by smell. It sleeps in a fork of the fig trunk about forty feet up, curled around the branch like a question mark, its pink nose tucked under its tail, its tail tucked under someone else's tail, perhaps, if there is a someone else. It eats the figs. It eats the fern fiddleheads when it can find them. It eats the hornets, occasionally, when it can reach the nest, which it usually cannot, but it has learned to sit on the branch below the nest and wait for a hornet to fall, and to eat the fallen hornet before the hornet can sting it, which is a difficult trick of timing and requires the possum to be, on the whole, faster than it looks. It is not faster than it looks. The possum eats mostly fallen figs and sleeps twenty hours a day and is, by any reasonable measure, a complete failure as an organism. But it is alive. And it is warm. And it is dry, up in the canopy, out of the water. And when it dies — under the fig tree, or in the water, or in the mouth of something larger, though there is nothing larger here, nothing larger than a dragonfly nymph and an adult possum is at the top of every terrestrial food chain in this hangar — its body will fall, and the figs will feed on its nitrogen, and the hornets will feed on the figs, and the dragonfly nymphs will feed on the hornets that fall into the water, and the bacteria will feed on everything else, and the cycle will close, and the possum will become, in the fullness of geological time, a fig, which will be eaten by a bird, which will be eaten by a possum, which will become, in the fullness of geological time, a fig.

There are also the small things that are not animals. There is the rust. The rust is not alive, but it behaves like a disease and looks like a flower, and it is, in its way, the most important tenant of all, because the rust is what is converting the iron into soil, one oxidation at a time, and the soil is what is holding up the figs, and the figs are what is holding up the possum, and the possum is what is holding up — well. Nothing. The possum is not holding up anything. But the possum is part of the arrangement. Everything here is part of the arrangement. The hornet and the fig. The dragonfly nymph and the bacterial mat. The condensation and the warm water and the iron roof slowly surrendering its identity to the patient chemistry of oxygen and time. It is all one arrangement. It is one large, slow, unhurried negotiation between carbon and iron and water and light, conducted in the medium of ten thousand small lives, none of which understand what is happening, all of which are necessary for it to continue.

There is also, somewhere in this arrangement, you. You stand in the middle of the hangar with your feet on a tilted slab and your head in the canopy and the light falling through the leaves in green-gold coins on the water around you. You are not part of the arrangement. You are an interruption. You are a weather event. You are a brief warm anomaly in a place that has been cooling toward entropy for a hundred years. You will leave. You will leave and the door will close behind you, or be buried behind you, and the drip will continue, and the figs will fruit, and the hornets will hum, and the possum will sleep, and the warm water will run its slow chemistry, and the place will forget you were ever here, the way it has forgotten the cranes and the work bays and the riveted seams and the people who riveted them. You will leave and the hangar will go on being a forest, which is what it has been for longer than it was a hangar, and what it will be for longer than it will be anything else, until one day it is a sinkhole, and one day after that a pond, and one day after that a meadow, and one day after that nothing at all, just a low place in the ground where the water collects, where the dragonflies hunt, where a bird might stop, briefly, to eat a fig, and to leave, in its droppings, the seed of the next forest, which will grow in the next ruin, which will be forgotten by the next civilization, which will leave, in its droppings, the seed of the next fig.

The drip goes on.

The drip goes on.
