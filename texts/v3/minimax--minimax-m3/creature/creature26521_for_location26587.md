# Who Lives There

The first thing to understand is that no one has ever moved to this town.

That is not quite accurate. People have arrived. There is even, in the way of all settlements, the faint memory of an arrival — a track worn into the high pass, a story told to children about a man with a lame donkey who came down one autumn and never left. But the town does not *receive* people the way a town receives people. A town, properly, takes a stranger and begins the long work of making them a person who belongs to it. This town does not do that work. It lets visitors stay, in the sense that it does not prevent them from staying, and it does not do the other things either. A person can live there for forty years and still be spoken of, in the careful way, as *the one who came.*

So the people of the town are, as near as anyone has been able to count, the same people who have always been there. Their names recur. A woman called Marta, or something like Marta, has a face that belongs to a particular bone structure, and her mother's face had it, and her grandmother's, and the woman three centuries back who was first written down in the cartographer's ledger had it too, and the women in the church paintings from before the cartographer — yes, there are paintings, the painter was from elsewhere and stopped counting his own years somewhere in the reign of a queen no one alive has heard of — those women had it as well. The nose. The way the brow sits. The line of the jaw. It is the kind of face that is, when you see it once, unmistakable thereafter, and you will see it in doorways and on the path to the byres for as long as you remain.

They are a quiet people. Not the quiet of suppressed feeling — there is no especial repression here, no drawn curtains, no one speaking in low tones when the magistrate passes. It is the quiet of people who have lived together for so long that the small social noises have been worn away, the way a stone on a beach loses its corners. They say good morning. They say the weather. They say *the goats are in the cabbages again.* They do not, as far as any visitor has ever been able to tell, *gossip.* There is no scandal in the town. There is no — and this is the strange thing — there is no *event.* A child is born, and it is the same child, and an old man dies, and it is the same old man, and the same people gather at the door of the house and stand in the damp for a respectful interval, and then they go back to their evening. The funerals are quiet. The weddings are quiet. The births are quiet. The town is the kind of quiet that does not need to be broken, because nothing in it is being held in.

Their clothes are the same clothes. This is not a figure of speech. The wool is hand-spun, the dye is vegetable, the cut is the cut that has been the cut, and the children wear smaller versions of the same coats, and the women wear the same aprons, and the men wear the same felt hats with the same narrow brim, and the felt is a particular dark green that does not occur in the dye pots of any dyer outside the valley. The cloth is heavy. The hems are weighted. The buttons are wooden and carved with a small motif — a mountain, a horn, a wave — that recurs in the town, in the door lintels, in the bread stamps, in the corners of the painted chests inside the houses. The people dress as if the wind were always about to come up the gorge, because the wind is always about to come up the gorge.

They eat what the valley gives. The barley that no one else can grow, ground and boiled into a thick grey porridge, eaten with wooden spoons from wooden bowls. The cabbages, salted in stone jars in the lower cellars. A pale cheese from the goats, sharp and a little ammoniacal. Roots, and a few apples from the trees on the middle terrace, which are gnarled and old and bear heavily in years that no one counts. A black bread baked in the communal oven, which is the only building on the lower terrace that has a chimney, and which the whole town has a hand in tending. There is not much meat. There is a little — goat, mostly, and the grey deer when a hunter brings one down, which is not often, because the deer are quick and the hunters, when pressed, will admit that the deer are also *particular* about which of them they will allow to take one. They drink water. They drink a small beer, brewed from the barley, that is cloudy and sour and not unpleasant. They do not drink spirits. There is no spirit-drinking in the town, and there is the sense, when one is offered the small beer at a table, that one should not ask after anything stronger.

The work is done by hand and is done in the open. There is no industry in the town, no closed workshop, no smithy with its door shut against prying. The smith works at his anvil on the middle terrace, in full view of the path, and children stop and watch him, and he gives them small things — a nail, a hook, a thumb-ring of soft iron — and they go away pleased. The women weave in the doorways of their houses, with the warp weighted over the threshold and the cloth growing in the interior. The men mend the terraces, and mend them again, though the terraces do not seem to need it. The goats are milked twice a day. The mists are tolerated. The children are permitted to do almost anything, including the small and dangerous things, and they are not often injured, and when they are, the injuries heal in the ordinary way, and the children do not seem to learn from the injuries, and they go on doing the small and dangerous things. The children have always been like this.

The hunters are the ones to watch, in a town that does not invite watching. There are three of them — or there are three of them, *today,* and there have been three of them for a long time. They are recognisable by their boots, which are heavier and more muddied than anyone else's, and by a small brass pin at the throat in the shape of a wave, which is the only ornament of metal that anyone in the town wears. They do not speak much to visitors, and they do not speak much to each other, and when they go up into the high passes they go in silence, and they come back in silence, and they hang what they have brought in the larder behind the communal oven, and the rest of the town is grateful and does not inquire. The deer they bring down are always the same deer. The hunters will deny this if asked. They will say the deer are different, that this one had a torn ear and that one had a limp, that the herd is many. They will not be lying. They will simply be describing a thing that the visitor is not, in the end, equipped to evaluate. The deer step on the rooftops, and the hunters go up after them, and the town is fed, and the small beer is poured, and the evening comes down.

The old woman who keeps the keys to the upper terrace is the closest thing the town has to a person of authority, and her authority is a small one and entirely practical. She is the one who opens the council hall in the mornings, if the council hall is to be opened, and she is the one who lights the lamps, and she is the one who sits in the high-backed chair at the end of the long table, though the chair is too tall for her, and her feet do not reach the floor. She is very old. She has been very old for as long as anyone can remember, which is a longer time than visitors can usually bring themselves to think about. She knows the names of every person in the valley, and she knows the names of the goats, and she knows the names of the deer, and she knows the names of the three hunters, and she knows the name of the river, which is a name that is not spoken aloud and that visitors never succeed in learning. She is polite to outsiders. She is not warm. She offers them small beer and a seat by the fire and asks after their journey, and she listens to the answer, and she does not, in the end, do anything with the answer. She is a keeper of things. The town is, in some sense that no one articulates, *kept.*

The visitor is allowed to come and go. This is the particular strangeness of the place — that the gates, such as they are (a low stone wall, a wooden bar, a small door in the wall that is rarely closed), are not really gates. One can walk out of the town. One can walk back in. The town does not prevent either. The town simply continues. The visitor can stay a night, or a week, or a year. The visitor will be given a bed in the lower terrace, in one of the small stone rooms that are kept for the purpose, and there will be a candle, and a basin of water, and a small jar of the small beer, and in the morning there will be porridge. The visitor will not be asked to leave. The visitor will not be asked to stay. The visitor will, if the visitor stays long enough, notice that the visitor is not aging, and will not know how to mention this, and will not, in the end, mention it. The town holds still. The visitor, if the visitor remains, holds still with it. The visitor, if the visitor leaves, will walk back up the high pass and over the ridge and down the other side, and will find the maps resume a few days on, and will be able to say, *there is a town in a place the cartographer did not finish,* and the maps will accept this, and the cartographer's ledger, in whatever archive it has been filed in, will not be amended, and no one will come to amend it.

The people of the town go on. They are quiet. They are kind, in the way that people who have known each other a very long time are kind — without emphasis, without remark, as a matter of course. They are the same people. The children are the same children. The hunters come down from the high passes. The old woman sits in the high-backed chair. The moss drips on the roof beams. The fog comes and sits. The damp is the damp. The mountain is the mountain.

The town is held. The town holds still. The town is full, and the fullness does not change, and the people in it do not change, and the light in it is the light it has always been, and the small beer is poured, and the cabbage is salted, and the door at the end of the upper terrace opens onto a long view of the gorge, and the wind comes up the gorge, and the eaves lift a little in the wind, and the stones on the roofs hold them down.
