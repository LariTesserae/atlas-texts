The valley is not empty. It has inhabitants, although the word *inhabitant* is one the valley would not use for itself, because the valley does not use words, and because most of what lives here is not entirely alive in the way a person walking in would understand alive, and because several of what lives here are not entirely not alive, and the distinction has not been pressed, and the valley is patient with the distinction in the way it is patient with everything else, which is to say completely.

There are several kinds of inhabitant, and a person who has stood in the central plaza long enough, and quieted the dripping in their ear long enough, may begin to tell them apart. They overlap. They have lived together for a long time. None of them is in a hurry to be separated from the others.

---

**The hands are the most visible of the inhabitants, and the most patient, and the fewest.**

There are not many of them. A person walking the valley for a full day, quietly, watching the places where fresh clay meets old brick, where a channel turns a step, where the cap of a leaning tower has slipped and a course is missing, may see two, or three, or on a rare day four. On most days, fewer. The number has been getting smaller for as long as anyone could have been watching, and no one has been watching for that long, because the valley does not invite watching, and the hands do not invite watching, and a watcher would have to be quiet and patient and not need to be seen, which is not a common kind of watcher.

A hand, when you see it, is most often a hand and an arm, and the rest of the body that the hand belongs to, but the rest of the body is not always visible, and on some occasions is not there, or is not there in the way you would expect a body to be there. The hand may appear above a low wall where the wall has been broken and is being repaired. The hand may be seen crouched in the channel where a course has slumped, lifting a fallen slab back into place. The hand may be at the top of a tower, settling a slipped cap. The hand may be at the bottom of a tower, where the lowest course has fallen away into the channel, and is being replaced, brick by brick, with the pale clay that is still drying.

The hand is not large. It is not small. It is the size of a hand that fits a brick of the kind that is laid in the herringbone of the valley walls, which is to say it is a hand that has been making these bricks for a long time, and the hand has shaped itself, in some small ways, to the bricks. The fingers are slightly thickened at the second joint. The thumb has a flat pad, and the pad is slightly broader than you would expect, and the print it leaves in fresh clay is a broad oval with a faint ridge across it where the knuckle of the thumb meets the pad. The print holds its shape for hours, sometimes for days, before the damp softens it back into the wall.

The hand works slowly. You could stand within a few feet of it and not be sure, for a long moment, that it was moving at all. The motion is in the wrist and in the lower arm, and the rest of the body is held very still. The hand lays a brick. The hand reaches for the next brick. The hand lays it. The hand takes a small quantity of mortar from a shallow pan that has been set on the edge of the wall, and works the mortar into the gap between the two bricks with the side of the thumb, and the motion is small and patient and is repeated, and is the only motion that is happening in that part of the valley for as long as the hand is there.

The hand is warm. This is the most surprising thing about it, given how cool the valley is, and how damp, and how still the air is between the towers. The hand is warmer than the wall it is laying bricks on. The warmth is not a great warmth. It is the warmth of a living hand, or of a hand that is doing work, or of a hand that has just come up out of the channel where the water runs cold. If you put your own hand near it, you would feel the difference, and the difference is the only way you would know, for certain, that the hand is there.

There is a body attached to most of the hands. The body is small, and is folded, when at work, into the shape of the work. The shoulders are narrow. The back is curved in the way a back curves when it has been bending over walls for many years. The skin, where it can be seen, is the color of the older mortar, or the color of damp slate, or the color of the pale clay that has dried to a deeper shade than when it was laid. The skin has the same dampness on it that the walls have, and the same faint sheen, and a person standing at a little distance might, for a moment, mistake the back of the maker for the top of a half-laid course.

The face, when it can be seen, is hard to read in the dimness, and is hard to read even in the brightest part of the day, because the dimness is what the face has been shaped in, and the dimness is what the face expects. The eyes are not large. The mouth is a small line. The expression, if there is one, is the expression of someone who is listening to a sound you cannot hear, or who is counting something you cannot see, or who is waiting for a brick to set. The makers do not speak to each other across the valley. If two of them are working on the same wall, they do not speak. If one passes another in a channel, they do not greet each other, except by the smallest motion of the hand, which may be a greeting or may be the hand simply shifting its grip on the brick it is carrying.

The makers are not all the same kind of maker. The herringbone in one stretch of wall is tighter than the herringbone in the next, and the mortar bands in one tower have straw, and the mortar bands in the next have shell, and the change, when you come to it, is the change of one maker for another, and a person who can read mortar can read the change as a maker's hand, and the makers, between them, do not agree on how the work should be done, and have not agreed for a long time, and the disagreements are written into the walls, and the walls are patient with the disagreements in the way they are patient with everything else.

A maker, when it is not working, can be found in one of a few places. Some of them sit at the foot of a wall they have been working on for a long time, with the knees drawn up and the arms around the knees, and the hands loose, and the eyes closed or half-closed, and the damp gathering on them as it gathers on the wall. Some of them stand in a channel, in the slow trickle, and let the water run over the lower legs, and do not move for a long time. Some of them go down into the dark places where the channels run beneath the floor, and are not seen again for days, and then come up at a different place, with damp on them that is darker than the wall-damp, and begin again.

A maker is not young. None of them, that a person could see, is young. The youngest is older than the oldest brick in the newest stretch of wall. The oldest is older than the herringbone at the back of the valley where the slate has gone almost black. They have been at the work for a long time. They will be at the work for a long time after. They do not hurry, and they do not tire in the way you would expect, and the tiredness they do have is a tiredness that has settled into them and become part of how they are shaped, like the thickening at the second joint of the fingers, like the curve of the back.

The makers are not many. They are fewer every year, and the year, in the valley, is a slow kind of year. A maker may be the last of a kind of herringbone, and when that maker is gone, that herringbone will not be laid again, because the hands that laid it were the only hands that knew the pattern. A maker may be the only one left who mixes straw into the mortar in the way that straw is mixed, and when that maker is gone, the walls will be mortared with clay and water, or with shell, or with nothing but the dust of old brick. The valley is patient with this. The valley has been losing makers for a long time. The valley continues with or without them, in the way it continues with or without the small creatures, and the valley will continue when the last maker is gone, and the slow unbuilding will outpace the slow building, and the place will become, in a way that is patient and is not in a hurry, less made.

The makers know this. The makers do not speak of it. The hands keep working.

---

**The small dark quick things are not named.**

The valley does not name them. The makers do not name them, because the makers do not speak, and because the small things are not spoken to, and because the small things do not need to be spoken to. A person who walked the valley for a long time might give them names, and the names would not be wrong, but the names would be the person's names and not the valley's, and the valley would not use them.

There are several kinds.

One kind lives in the channels. It is dark, the color of wet slate, and is small enough that a hand laid flat would cover it. It moves in the slow trickle at the bottom of a channel, and the motion of it is a motion you feel under your foot before you see it, if you step into the channel, a small soft pressure that is gone. It eats, or absorbs, or takes in, the things that the channels carry — the fine silt, the small pieces of mortar that have come loose from the bands above, the small pieces of pale clay that are still soft. It does not eat the bricks. The bricks are too hard, or are not what it is for. It is more often heard than seen: a soft rustle in the channel that is not the water, a small displacement of the air above the water, a quick shadow across the bottom of a channel where the light has come sideways for a moment. If you stand very still in a channel and wait, you may see the surface of the trickle dimple, in two or three places, in a way that is not the drop from above, and the dimples are the small things, passing.

Another kind lives on the tops of the towers and on the caps. It is paler, and flatter, and has a single wet eye that is very dark, and the eye is open, and the eye does not blink. It sits on a leaning cap and watches the valley below, and a person crossing the plaza below would not see it unless the person looked up and knew to look up. It is still when it sits. It has been still, sometimes, for so long that a thin skin of damp has formed on its back, and the damp is the same skin that is on the slate, and the eye, which is also still, is the only part of it that is moving, and the motion of the eye is very slow, and is the motion of an eye following something across the plaza, or following the sound of a drip, or following nothing.

This kind drops, sometimes, from the cap to a lower course, and the drop is silent, and the small thing is gone into a gap in the herringbone before it has landed. It does not return. A person who went up to the cap later would find only the damp, and the eye, if it had been there, would not be there, and would not be there on any later visit, because the eye was there for a reason that has passed.

Another kind moves across the central plaza. It is pale, almost the color of the freshest clay, and is low to the ground, and is broad, and is not seen often. It crosses the plaza in a slow curve, and the curve is from one gap to another, and the gap it goes into is in a wall on the far side, and the gap is the same color as the small thing, and the small thing goes into the gap as if the gap had been waiting for it. A person standing in the plaza would see the pale thing for the length of time it takes to cross the open ground, and would not see it again on that visit, or on the next visit, or for a long time, and then, on some other visit, would see it again, crossing, and would not be sure it was the same one.

These are not all of the kinds. There are others. There is a thing that lives in the mortar bands where the straw is, and is the color of straw, and is long and thin, and moves in the gap between the straw and the clay in a way you would only see if you took the band apart, and the band is not taken apart. There is a thing that lives in the standing water of the central pool, and is black, and is flat, and is on the surface of the water, and is the same black as the water, and the hum of the pool may be the sound of it, or may be the sound of the pool, and the two are not different, in the valley.

The small things do not need the valley to be more than it is. They need it to be damp, and dim, and full of gaps, and full of small sounds, and full of slow patient making, and full of slow patient unmaking, and the valley is all of these, and the small things live in the valley the way water lives in a channel, which is to say completely, and without commentary.

---

**The far footfall is the deepest of the inhabitants.**

It is the sound that is beneath all the other sounds. It is the thud, far apart, like the footfall of something very large, somewhere out of sight. It is felt in the soles of the feet and in the teeth. It is felt in the walls. It is felt in the standing water of the central pool, and when it comes, the surface of the pool dimples, very slightly, and the hum of the pool lowers by a quarter-tone, and is back where it was before the next thud.

No one in the valley has seen what makes the sound. The valley is patient with this. The hands do not speak of it. The small things do not name it. The water of the pool hums to it, or beside it, or in the same rhythm, and the rhythm is the rhythm of the place, and the place has had the rhythm for a long time.

It is far away. It is closer, sometimes. The thud, when it comes, is felt differently in different parts of the valley. In the central plaza it is felt mostly through the floor, and through the soles of the feet, and the floor gives a little under it, and is back before the next thud. At the top of a tall tower it is felt through the air, and through the upper courses of slate, and the slate gives a small ring, far below the audible, and the ring fades. In the channels, it is felt in the water, and the water carries the thud a long way, and the small things in the channels feel it and go still for a moment, and are still until the thud has passed, and then they move again.

It is large. The size of the footfall is not a size that fits in the valley. The valley is large, and the footfall is larger than the valley, in the way that the sound of a footstep is larger than the foot that made it, when the foot is on stone, and the stone carries the sound.

It walks. It walks at its own pace, which is far apart, and is not the pace of a person, and is not the pace of any of the small creatures, and is not the pace of the hands. It walks the way the valley settles, which is to say slowly, and in a rhythm that has not changed for a long time, and will not change, because the rhythm is the rhythm of the valley, and the valley has the rhythm because the footfall is in it, or the footfall is in the valley because the valley has the rhythm, and the two are not different, in the valley.

It is out of sight. It is out of sight the way the bottom of a channel is out of sight, even when the channel is shallow, because the dimness is what the channel has, and the dimness is what the footfall has. If a person walked to the edge of the valley, and looked out across the flatter country beyond, and waited, and was patient, and did not need to see, the person would feel the footfall there too, in the soles of the feet, and in the teeth, and would know that the footfall was not coming from the direction the person was looking, and was not coming from any direction the person could have been looking.

It is patient. It is patient the way the damp is patient, and the way the dimness is patient, and the way the slow making is patient, and the way the slow unmaking is patient, and the way the valley is patient. It has been walking for a long time. It will walk for a long time after the last maker is gone, and after the last of the small things is gone, and after the valley has become less made, and more damp, and more dim, and more quiet in its lower sounds, and the footfall will still be there, far apart, in the stone, in the water, in the air between the towers, and the valley will still have it, and the footfall will still have the valley, and neither will be in a hurry.

---

**The water of the central pool is also an inhabitant, in a way.**

It stands. It is the only standing water in the valley. The water in the channels is moving, even when the moving is slow. The water on the walls is moving, in beads, in drops. The water in the air is moving, in the damp. The water in the pool is the only water that does not move, or that moves in a way that is not motion, and the hum it gives is the sound of the not-motion, and the not-motion is also the hum, and the pool is the hum, and the hum is the pool.

It is black on the surface. It is darker than the slate, and darker than the older brick. It is black the way deep water is black, even when it is shallow, because the light does not reach into it, and the bottom of the pool, if there is a bottom, is in the dimness.

It is fed by all the channels. The channels slope gently toward the plaza, and the slope is the slope of the whole valley, and the water that has been carried by every channel ends at the lip of the pool, and falls into the pool in a thin sheet that breaks into a darker sheet that breaks into a stiller black. The pool is the meeting of all the water, and the meeting is what hums.

It hums. The hum is very low, and is felt more than heard, and is felt in the bones of the feet that stand near it, and in the small things that live on its surface, and in the air above it. The hum is not the same note from moment to moment. The note is set by the amount of water that has come into the pool, and by the footfall, and by the dimness, and by the hour of the day, if there are hours in the dimness. The hum changes, and the change is the change of a place that is listening to itself.

It is patient. It has been the pool for a long time. It will be the pool for a long time after. The makers, when they work near the plaza, do not work on the pool. The pool is not built and not unbuilt. The pool is the only thing in the valley that is not made, and is not unmade, and is not under construction, and the makers know this, and the small things know this, and the footfall knows this, and the valley knows this, and the valley is patient with the pool the way it is patient with everything else.

---

The valley is itself, in some sense, an inhabitant. Not in the way a person is an inhabitant. Not in the way the hands are inhabitants, or the small things, or the footfall, or the pool. The valley is inhabited by itself. The valley is the place that the damp lives in. The valley is the place that the dimness lives in. The valley is the place that the slow making lives in, and the slow unmaking, and the layered sound, and the small gaps, and the herringbone, and the slate, and the pale clay that is still drying. The valley is the longest-lived of all the inhabitants, and the most patient, and the one that has been here the longest, and will be here the longest, and is here now, and is patient with all the others the way a long-lived thing is patient with the shorter-lived things that share its place, which is to say completely, and without commentary, and without hurry, and without the need for any of the others to be more than they are.

A person could stand in the central plaza, and hear the water, and the stone, and the air, and the far thing walking, and feel the damp gather on the back of the hand, and not know which of the inhabitants was the place, and which was the place's, and which was the place's patient and unhurried way of continuing. The valley is denser than the country around it, and cooler, and damper, and darker, and louder in a low way, and more made, and more under construction, and more full of slow small strange life. It is the kind of place that, once you have stood in it, you cannot quite put out of your mind, even after you have walked out of it and into the flatter country beyond, and even though the country beyond is made of the same kind of matter, only less of it, only less loud.
