The inhabitants look like people. That is the first thing you have to absorb, and it is the thing that will not leave you, because once you have absorbed it you will spend the rest of your time here asking whether the resemblance is a coincidence. They look like people. They have two arms and two legs and a vertical carriage and they walk upright in the narrow channels between the constructions in long files that do not break and do not slow. They wear clothing — many layers of it, in the dark colors that match the stone, damp and clinging — and the clothing moves the way clothing should, and falls the way clothing should, and it is only after a week that you notice the fabric never creases, that the same fold you saw in the morning is the same fold you see at night, that the cloth behaves the way the metal in the walls behaves, that it folds without creasing. They have faces. The faces have the standard equipment, two eyes above a nose above a mouth, in roughly the right proportions, and they move with the gait you would call human if you were asked on the first day to call it.

But the longer you look the more you accumulate, and what you accumulate does not add up to a person, exactly. It adds up to a person with extra things pressed into the gaps.

The eyes are the first extra thing, and they are the extra thing that takes the longest to name. You watch a worker on a scaffold above you, and the worker watches you back, and the gaze has a flicker in it that you cannot for many days specify. Eventually you understand: there is a third eye, but it does not sit where the pigeon’s third eye sits. It sits behind the other two, deeper in the skull, beneath the bone, and it does not look out at the world. It looks out at you. Or rather, it looks at what is looking at you, which is to say it looks at the gaze itself. When one of them sees you here, you are being seen twice — once by the forward-facing eyes that see the world, and once by the deeper eye that sees the seeing. The city has grown them a third eye, or they have grown one for themselves, or the distinction no longer matters. They see you. They see themselves seeing you. The deeper eye does not blink.

The skin is the second extra thing. Their skin is damp — everyone here is damp — but the dampness is not sweat, and the dampness is not the wet air condensing on a surface. The dampness is a property of the skin itself, a thin perpetual moisture that they do not notice and that you notice because you come from somewhere dry. And the skin has a faint sheen, the same sheen that runs through all the materials of the city, the same thread-of-color-that-shifts-when-you-move-your-head. The fifth material is in them. They have been built, or have built themselves, partly out of the same stuff the buildings are built of. The skin does not age. The skin does not wrinkle in the way you expect, and the wrinkles it does have are the wrong kind of wrinkles — too even, too patterned, too much like the surface of the city seen from a great height.

The teeth are the third extra thing, and this is the one you will notice on the first day, because the first time a crowd turns toward you and you find yourself in the middle of it, you will see their teeth, and you will see what their teeth are made of. Their teeth are pale ceramic. The visible ones, at least, are pale and faintly translucent at the edges, and they have the look of something that was once liquid and chose to stop being liquid. They are not dentures. They are not implants. They are the teeth they grew, or that grew in place of teeth, or that were always there waiting for the baby teeth to come out and never came out. The teeth are part of them. The teeth are also the same material as the walls. When they smile, which is not often, you are seeing a small piece of the city smiling at you from inside a face.

Their voices are the strangest thing of all, because their voices are normal. Among the roar — the sub-frequencies that push against your sternum, the high pitches that ache in your back teeth — their voices are recognizably voices. They speak a language you do not know. They speak it at a pitch the roar should drown out, and yet you hear them, somehow, as if your ear has been tuned to find them, as if the city has arranged for them to be heard by new arrivals. They speak to each other in quick low exchanges. They speak to you, when they speak to you, in the same language, slowly, watching your face for comprehension. They do not switch to a language you might know. They have languages you might know. They have chosen this one. It is older than most languages. It is the language the city speaks. When you cannot understand them, they wait. They wait for a long time. They wait with a patience that is not the patience of a person being polite but the patience of a wall being a wall.

Their work is the thing you will see most of, because there is no off-switch and there is no shift change and there is no end-of-day. The construction crews work through what your body insists is the middle of the night. The demolition crews work alongside the construction crews on the same building, the same wall, the same column, removing a section that was laid this morning and replacing it with a section of slightly different material that fits the gap the removed section left behind. A worker will lay a course of stone in the morning and tear a different course of stone down in the afternoon and lay a third in the evening, and the three courses will be different patterns, different materials, different eras, and they will fit together because the worker knows how to make them fit. The worker has been doing this work for so long that the work has become a property of the worker. The worker is, in some sense, the same substance as the wall.

Their movement is in rivers. You do not see individuals at first; you see flows, currents, the way you see water moving through the channels between foundations. The flows have direction. The flows have speed. The flows do not intersect; they braid around each other, pass through the gaps between buildings, gather in plazas, disperse, gather again. When you stand still in the city for long enough the flows find their way around you, the way water finds its way around a stone in a stream, and you realize you have become a stone in a stream, and the stream will continue whether you are here or not, and the stream has been here since before you arrived and will be here after you leave.

Their children are the part that breaks most arrivals in the second week, when the arrivals are beginning to think they understand. The children are present. There are children. They are smaller versions of the adults, with the same damp sheen, the same faintly translucent pale-ceramic teeth, the same almost-but-not-quite-right eyes. They play in the channels between the buildings. Their play is the kind of play children play anywhere — running, hiding, the elaborate social negotiations of small creatures testing boundaries. But the games have rules you cannot see. A child will throw a small object against a wall and the wall will throw it back, and the trajectory will not match any physics you learned. A child will run between two adults moving in opposite directions and emerge between them with something new in their hands that was not in either adult’s hands. The children are learning the city. The children are being learned by the city. You cannot tell which direction the learning goes.

Their food is what you do not see, at first. You see them stop, occasionally — they will pause in the middle of a river of movement, they will press their hands flat against a wall or a piece of scaffolding or another person, and they will be still for a long moment, and then they will resume. The pause is, you will come to understand, the meal. They take something from the surface they touch. The thing they take is not food. The thing they take is not electricity, not heat, not any energy you can name. The thing they take is a property of the city — a vibration, a tendency, a faint pulse that runs through the materials. They feed on the city the way the city feeds on them. They are a part of the city’s metabolism. They are how the city eats and how the city is eaten.

Their sleep is the thing the briefings got wrong, or the briefings assumed you would understand without being told. They do not sleep. They slow. There is a state they enter, in the deepest part of the warm wet night, when the rivers of movement thin to trickles and the voices drop to murmurs and the workers pause against the walls with their hands flat and their eyes half-closed. But they are not asleep. They are listening. They are listening to the city, the way the city listens to them. The sleep-that-is-not-sleep lasts an hour, perhaps two, and then the rivers thicken again and the voices rise and the work resumes. The rhythm is not circadian. The rhythm is tidal, or it is something the city has chosen for them, or they have chosen for the city, and the two choices are not different.

Their faces, when they look at you, are the part that is hardest to write down because the part is most like a face and least like a face at the same time. When they look at you, you see the face — the eyes, the nose, the mouth, the expression. The expression is curious. The expression is patient. The expression is the expression of someone who has been asked the same question a thousand times before and who knows the answer and who is waiting, with great patience, for you to be ready to receive the answer. The expression is also, faintly, the expression of the city. The city is curious about you. The city is patient about you. The city is waiting for you to be ready.

Above them, in the upper structures, there are other things. You do not see them. You hear them — a slow breathing, a shifting weight, the creak of something vast moving through spaces you cannot see. They may be inhabitants. They may be organs. They may be what the city is when the city is being itself, and the people below may be what the city is when the city is being looked at. You cannot tell. The brief was clear that you should not go up to find out.

You keep feeling like you are being studied. That is what you keep feeling, in the first weeks, in the channels, in the rivers, in the wet air, under the warm too-yellow coin of a sun that does not quite belong to this sky. You keep feeling like you are being studied, and you keep feeling like you are being studied with great care, and you keep feeling like you are being studied by something that does not need to study you but is studying you anyway because you are new and you are interesting and you are the first new thing the city has seen in a long time. You keep feeling like the studying is not hostile and is not benign and is just the looking of a thing that looks the way water looks at a stone — with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else to be, with the attention of a thing that has been looking for a long time, with the unhurried precision of a thing that knows what it is looking at and is in no rush to tell you. You keep feeling like the stone is being weighed.
