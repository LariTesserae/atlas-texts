# What Lives There

First: the plants.

This is what answers you before anything else does, before any person or animal makes itself known, because the plants are the volume here, the plants are the medium you move through. They are not one species — there are at least three, probably five, probably more, growing in the strata, each adapted to a different height and a different wet. The lowest layer is a thing like sphagnum but thicker, with leaves the color of a bruise, and it has been there so long that it has begun to *be* the floor, has begun to be the thing the floor is made of. Out of this rises the second layer, the bodies — the trunks, though they are not trunks, the vertical green ropes that branch and rebranch — and these are something close to a fig, or a banyan, or a mulberry, but not any of those exactly. They put down aerial roots when they can find a surface. The roots thicken into woody cords. The cords knit with the brick, with the mortar, with the iron of the steam grates, and hold everything together. A tenement wall in the Hothouse Quarter is not supported only by its own foundations. It is supported by a hundred roots that have decided, for their own reasons, to grip it.

The highest layer is a different plant again. It is the canopy. It is the thing that makes the light the way the light is made. Its leaves are broad and waxy and arranged so densely that a bird walking on top of the canopy would never fall through, and water falling onto the canopy is split, by leaf-edges, into a thousand small streams that drop into the interior and feed the whole system. This plant flowers, in a brief and invisible way — small pale blooms that open at night and shed a pollen so fine it turns the air, in those few weeks, faintly yellow. The flowers do not need to be seen. The pollen does not need to travel far. There are no bees. There are no bees because there is no room for bees, and also because the plants of the Hothouse Quarter have not, in a hundred and fifty years, needed bees. They manage themselves.

There are the fungi.

This is its own kingdom, its own census, and it is the working heart of the Quarter in a way the plants are not. The fungalists — the people who tend them — keep maybe twenty cultivated strains, and the strains themselves are not stable; they drift, they hybridize in the compost, they pass traits back and forth in the dark. A fungus grown in one workshop will not grow the same way in the next workshop, even if it was cloned from the same mother. The humidity, the heat, the particular chemistry of the local compost stratum, the diet of a particular wooden vat — these make each workshop's yield its own. A dye fungus from the tannery district is a different color from a dye fungus from the paper-makers' row, and the fungalists will tell you, with the conviction of people who have argued this their whole lives, that theirs is the true color and the others are off.

The cultivated fungi are grown on rafts. Wooden rafts, suspended in the warm humid air of the workshops, dripping with condensate. They are fed on waste — bark, grain chaff, the spent liquor from the tanning vats, the offcuts from the paper-mills. They are slow. They are patient in the way everything here is patient. A culture takes six weeks to mature. A color takes three months to set. A strain takes years to stabilize, and is rarely stabilized before it is replaced.

But the wild fungi are the real population. They are everywhere the cultivated ones are not. They are in the compost stratum, and they are the reason the compost stratum is the compost stratum. Without them, the dead growth would not become soil. Without them, the buildings would not have a floor to stand on. They are the invisible government of the Hothouse Quarter, and they have been running it longer than anyone has been alive to notice.

There are the insects.

More insects than you would think. More insects than the casual visitor credits, because the insects are mostly small, mostly in the compost, mostly in the leaves. Beetles, principally — dark-bodied things, slow-moving, that eat the dead growth and are eaten in turn by the things that eat them. There are silverfish in the dampest corners of the apartments. There are cockroaches, because there are cockroaches everywhere humans live, and these are the steam-colored cockroach, pale and sluggish. There are ants in long processions on the brick paths, carrying fragments of leaf down into the stratum. There is the spider, the fat-bodied one that lives in the steam vents — it is a relative of the house spider but built differently, swollen with the heat, pale as a grub, and it weaves no web but sits at the mouth of the vent and waits for what comes out.

There are the snails on the walls. There are the slugs on the paths. There are the worms in the compost, red worms and grey worms and worms of no name, working the strata. There is something small and pale that lives in the pipes themselves, in the hot water, that no one has classified because no one has caught one.

There are the rats.

The rats of the Hothouse Quarter are a separate thing from the rats of the city outside. They are larger. They are wetter. Their fur is thin and lies flat against their bodies, and they move through the compost strata in tunnels of their own making, or in the tunnels of older rats, generations old, that have become part of the architecture. They come up into the workshops at night and eat what the fungalists have left out. They come up into the apartments, rarely, and steal bread. They are not afraid of people. They are not aggressive. They are just *there*, the way the pipes are there, the way the steam is there.

There are the pigeons.

The pigeons live on the upper stories and they live well. They are the only birds of the Quarter, or nearly so — a stray sparrow sometimes gets in through a broken window and cannot get out, and lives out its life in the rafters of a single workshop. But the pigeons are the birds. They come down in the morning to the stratum and they eat what they find — insects, snails, the dropped grain from a fungalist's spill. They coo. The coo of a pigeon in the Hothouse Quarter is a particular sound, muffled, soaked, and it carries further than it should in the still air. You can hear a pigeon two courtyards away. You can hear it and not know which courtyard it is in.

There are the cats.

The cats are the most recent arrivals, historically — they came in with the first wave of permanent residents, perhaps a hundred years ago, and they have been here since. They are short-haired, all of them, because long hair does not survive the wet. They are mostly dark — tabby, black, the smoke-grey of the steam vents. They are comfortable here in a way cats are not comfortable in most places, because the Quarter is warm and the prey is everywhere and the dogs do not come in. They hunt the rats. They hunt the pigeons when they can catch them, which is rarely, because the pigeons can fly and the cats cannot. They sleep on the warm iron of the steam grates. They are tolerated by the people, fed by some, ignored by others. They are part of the architecture.

There are the chickens.

The chickens are kept in the ground-floor workshops, in the dark, in the warm. They are not many — a dozen hens here, six there — and they are not kept for eggs so much as for the warmth of having them. The fungalists in particular keep chickens, because the chickens eat the slugs that would otherwise eat the fungi. A chicken in the Hothouse Quarter is a working animal, a colleague. It is also a pet. It is also, occasionally, a Sunday dinner.

There is the pig, or the pigs.

There are pigs in some of the yards, in the deeper parts of the Quarter, where there is enough room for a small pen. They are fed on the spent grain from the paper-mills and the kitchen scraps and whatever the chickens won't eat. They are slaughtered rarely. They are part of the slow economy. They grunt in the evenings and the sound of them travels.

There are the people.

The people are the smallest population of any living thing here, and they are the most recent, and they are aware, in a way the plants and the fungi and the insects are not, that they are living in a place that is not quite theirs. They came for the work — for the tanning, the paper, the fungi, the steam — and they stayed for the cheap rents and the warmth, and they have stayed so long that some of them have forgotten that anywhere else exists. They are a closed community. They marry each other. They work in the trades their parents worked in. They speak a dialect that is slightly older than the dialect of the city outside, more formal, more rounded, with words for steam-pipe fittings that no one outside the Quarter uses anymore.

There are three thousand of them, and they know each other, or they know of each other, the way people in a small town know of each other. There are families here that have been here for five generations, since the Quarter was first built up, and there are families here that arrived last month from somewhere upriver, and they are all, now, the people of the Hothouse Quarter.

They are short, mostly. The Quarter selects for shortness over generations — it is hard to be tall in a place where the ceilings are low and the air is wet and the floors are soft. They are pale, but not the pale of people who stay indoors; the pale of people who do not see direct sun. Their hair is dark. Their eyes are dark. They have a way of moving slowly, of speaking softly, of considering before they answer, that visitors mistake for unfriendliness and that is in fact the pace of the place.

They live in the upper stories. They work in the ground floors. They walk the brick paths in the evenings, when the light is the same as it was in the morning, and they call to each other across the courtyards, and the calls go up into the green mass and do not come back.

There is one more thing.

There is a man who is older than the others, who sits in a particular courtyard in the evenings, who does not work anymore, who is watched over by the steamfitters because his father was a steamfitter and his grandfather was a steamfitter and he was a steamfitter until his hands would not grip the wrenches anymore. He sits on a wooden bench under a particular patch of canopy where the dripping is least, and he watches the steam come out of a grate at his feet, and he knows, because he was taught, what each sound in the Quarter means — the slow clunk of a treadle, the hiss of a particular valve, the cry of a child, the coo of a pigeon, the drip of water from a particular height — and he is the closest thing the Quarter has to a memory.

He is not the memory, though.

The memory is the strata. The memory is the compost. The memory is the green.

The Hothouse Quarter remembers itself the way a forest remembers itself, in layers, in the dark, slowly, and the people who live there now are just the latest layer, and they will be composted in their turn, and the plants will grow up through them, and the buildings will stand, and the steam will come up, and the water will fall, and the light will be the same warm diffuse glow it has been for a hundred and fifty years, slightly amber, slightly green, the light of a place that has decided to be itself and has not yet stopped.
