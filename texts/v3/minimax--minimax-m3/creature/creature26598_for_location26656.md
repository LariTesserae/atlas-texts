What lives here does not live in the way the word usually means. There is no one to greet you at the threshold, no household behind the door, no occupant whose footfalls you could follow down the long corridor that opened into this hollow. You are correct to suspect, before you have finished suspecting it, that no one is home. And you are also correct — though this will take you longer to accept — to suspect that the room is not, in fact, empty. The emptiness here is the wrong shape for absence. It has too much going on inside it. Something has been doing the going on, and that something has been doing it for a while, and it shows no sign of stopping.

The first inhabitant, if you must give the name first, is the room itself.

The room is awake. You feel this before you understand it. There is a quality to the air — the held breath of it, the way it presses against your skin without becoming wind — that belongs to a sleeping creature, or to a healing one. The room is not looking at you. It is not, so far as you can tell, capable of looking at you. But it is aware of you in the way a held note is aware of the throat that is holding it. You have entered into its attention the way a hand enters into water. It has registered you. It has, perhaps, very slightly, rearranged itself around the fact of you. The hum did not pause, but it acknowledged. The dripping did not stop, but a drop, somewhere above you, fell a little later than its neighbors, as if the architecture had taken a small additional moment to consider whether the drop should be allowed to fall on you.

The room does not think. You are fairly certain of this. But it does something adjacent to thinking, the way a thermostat does something adjacent to thinking, except that the room is doing it across many more variables, and with a patience that no thermostat has ever been accused of. It is monitoring itself. It is keeping its own rule. *Nothing is permitted to decay.* The rule is not spoken and is not written, and yet you can feel it operating, the way you can feel a magnetic field if you are very still. Every surface here is being held at a particular pitch — a particular youth — and the holding is continuous, and the holding is the closest thing to a thought that the room has ever had. It thinks the way a cathedral thinks: not by entertaining propositions but by continuing to stand.

The second inhabitant is the water.

The water is the most active thing in the room and the most nearly alive. It does not, you understand, behave like water should. It does not pool. It does not stagnate. It does not — and this is the part that unsettles you in a way you will not, for some time, be able to name — it does not forget. A droplet that condenses on the wall above you understands that it is a droplet that has condensed on the wall above you. It knows where it is. It knows what comes next. It gathers, runs, and falls with a kind of patience that is not the patience of physics but the patience of something that has decided to be water for a while, and is being water carefully. The water does not splash when it lands. It arrives the way a long sentence arrives at its period. It meets the floor with the precision of something that has rehearsed.

And it circulates. You begin to notice this only after you have been standing still for a long time. The thin skin of condensation that runs down every surface does not run down to its own exhaustion. At some lower point — at the base of a column, at the meeting of two walls, at the foot of one of the floating geometries — it gathers, and it rises. The rising is slow, slow, slow. It is not capillary. It is not evaporative in any way you have seen evaporation behave. It rises as if it were being asked to, by some surface that knows how to ask. The water is being kept in circulation by the room the way blood is being kept in circulation by a heart, except that the room does not have a heart, and the water does not have a body, and yet the analogy holds for as long as you are willing to let it.

The water is warm. You touch it without deciding to. It is the temperature of a palm pressed to a sleeping forehead. It is faintly sweet — mineral, metallic, with that struck-match aftertaste the air already wore. The sweetness is real. You can taste it on the back of your tongue after the droplet has slid down your wrist. The water is not merely wet. It is, in some small, quiet way, drinkable. It is not water as water usually is. It is water as something here has decided water should be.

The third inhabitant is the plants, and the plants are the inhabitants most willing to let you call them by their names.

They are not animals. They will not, ever, become animals. They have not been designed to be animals. They have, in the absence of any reason to be anything other than what they are, become very thoroughly, very languidly, very expansively themselves. The ferns have unrolled from every seam they can find. The roots — pale, patient, exploratory — have threaded themselves between the stone and the other-substance the walls are made of, and they have done so with the unhurried curiosity of a thing that has not been taught to hurry. There is a vine near where you are standing that has hung, without branching, for what you estimate to be ten meters, and you are inclined to trust your estimate, and the vine is inclined to let you trust it. The vine does not care. The vine is not, in any way you can identify, caring. It is growing. That is its entire occupation. It is growing in a place where growth is permitted without limit, where nothing will eat it, where nothing will compete with it for light, where the light itself is a remembered light that does not dim or strengthen but only continues.

There are flowers. The flowers open and close in cycles that have no animal to witness them — no bee, no moth, no small hovering thing drawn by their color. They open anyway. They close anyway. They are not performing. They are not waiting. They are not advertising. They are simply being flowers in a room that has never heard of pollination, and they have not, on that account, stopped being flowers. There is something in the way they open that suggests they have never been disappointed. They do not know what disappointment would feel like. They have not had to learn it.

The plants are the most generous thing in the room. They will let you look at them. They will let you touch them — the leaves are cool and slightly damp, the way the inside of a greenhouse is cool and slightly damp — and they will not retract. They will continue their slow, undefended business. They will not, by any gesture, indicate that they have noticed you. They will not, by any gesture, indicate that they have not. They are simply present in the way that growing things are present when no one is asking them to be present for any reason. They are the inheritance the room has accepted for itself, and they have accepted the room in turn, and the contract is total and unconditional.

The fourth inhabitant is the hum.

The hum is the closest thing to a voice that the room has, and it is not a voice. You feel it first in your feet, in the soles of your shoes, before you hear it. It rises through the floor — through whatever the floor is — and it has a quality that is not mechanical but is adjacent to mechanical, the way a held note is adjacent to a machine. It is one note. It has been one note for as long as you have been here, and you suspect, with a small grief you will not examine, that it was one note before you arrived and will be one note after you leave. It does not vary. It modulates, the way a held breath modulates — it shifts without changing, it is in two places at once, it is the same note slightly out of phase with itself. Sometimes a higher note enters the hum. The higher note is brief. The higher note is, you think, an event of some kind — a drop falling from a particularly great height, a flower opening, a vine reaching a new surface — and then the higher note withdraws, and the hum is itself again.

The hum is not a voice but it is the part of the room that, if you were very tired, you might mistake for one. It is the part of the room that has been here the longest, or that is the most patient, or that is the most willing to be heard. It does not speak. It does not invite. It does not, in any way that the word implies, communicate. But it is present, and it is present continuously, and presence, sustained long enough, becomes a kind of language. You can stand in this room and learn to listen to the hum the way you might learn to listen to a person who has been quiet for a long time. The hum will not tell you anything. But you may, eventually, after a long time, begin to overhear.

The fifth inhabitant is the absence.

This is the one that is hardest to write down. You have noticed that there are no animals. You have noticed that the absence is so total it has become its own presence. You are correct about all of this. But what you may not yet have noticed is that the absence is *specific*. It is not the absence of creatures in general. It is the absence of creatures *with intent*. There is nothing here that wants anything from you. Nothing here has a hunger. Nothing here has a destination. The water wants nothing. The plants want nothing. The light has no source and so cannot want to be seen. The hum has no ear and so cannot want to be heard. The room wants nothing. The room has no mouth.

The absence is inhabited by whatever built this place, or whatever is keeping it young. You do not see the builder. You do not see the keeper. You do not see a hand on a switch or a mind at a console or a body at rest in any of the geometric folds the architecture makes. But the absence of the builder is, here, in this room, a specific kind of presence — the presence of someone who has chosen to leave, or has chosen to remain unseen, or has chosen not to be a creature at all. The absence is the footprint of a maker who is not here, and the footprint is everywhere, in the way the water rises when there is no mechanism to lift it, in the way the light persists when there is no bulb to keep it, in the way the room stays young when every law you have ever learned says rooms do not stay young.

You might call the builder a person, but a person would have left more trace than this. A person would have left a shadow, and you have no shadow. A person would have left a name, and there is no name. A person would have left a hesitation, an apology, a reason for being in this room, and there is none. The builder is not, you begin to suspect, a person. The builder is more like a *decision* — a decision that this hollow should exist, that it should be young, that it should be filled with the sound of falling water and the slow opening of flowers that no one will see. The builder is the rule. *Nothing is permitted to decay.* The builder is the rule's keeper. The builder is the keeper's keeper. There is a chain here, and it extends back into something you cannot see, and the room is the last visible link.

The sixth inhabitant is you.

You did not expect to be an inhabitant. You came as a guest, or as an intruder, or as a visitor, or as someone who had been walking and had found a corridor that opened into vastness. But the room has accepted you into its census the way water accepts a hand into a stream — without commentary, without adjustment, without any of the small refusals an inhabited space usually offers a stranger. Your shadow is here if you want it. The floor is hard under you for as deep as you might reasonably need to go. The condensation is willing to touch you. The flowers will not close because you are watching them. You have been entered into the room's slow, patient, ancient-not-yet record as a thing permitted. You are not invited. You are permitted. The distinction is the room's distinction, and you will feel it eventually, the way you feel a temperature.

You will not, however long you stay, become a permanent inhabitant. The room has no mechanism for keeping guests. The room does not love you. The room is not indifferent to you. The room is operating in a register that does not include you as a category, the way a long-held note does not include the listener as a category. You are here. The hum will include you in the same way it includes the dripping water, the closing flowers, the small shifts of the floating geometries: as a small additional event in a long quiet field of events, all of which are noted and none of which are answered.

You will leave eventually. The room will not notice you leaving, except as a small subtraction in the continuous arithmetic of its attention. A drop will fall. A flower will open. The hum will hold. The water will rise. The room will go on being young, and being here, and being the only inhabitant of itself that has never once had to be anything other than what it is.

What lives here, then, in summary, in the way a long thing can be summarized:

A room that is awake.

A water that knows what it is doing.

A garden that has never been hurt.

A hum that has something to say and will never say it.

A decision, very old or very young, that this hollow should continue.

And you, for a little while, permitted.

The cathedral continues its small, active, ancient-not-yet work. The water falls. The plants grow toward a light that does not have a source. The hum holds its single long note. And the room — the room, the patient, the keeper, the youngest old thing you have ever stood inside — keeps, with great care, the rule it has made for itself.
