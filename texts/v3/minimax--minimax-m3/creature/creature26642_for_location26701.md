# Inhabitants of the Held Breath

I want to start small and work outward, because that is how this place presents itself — not as a panorama but as a series of attentions, each one revealing what was already happening in the corner of the eye.

## The Leaf-Dwellers

Begin with a single low frond, the kind whose tip dips into the mud and seems to drink from it. On its upper surface, a city. *Pheidole*-things, but not *Pheidole*, though they play the same part — a worker caste barely thicker than a printed letter, running in tracks worn not by footfall but by the passage of so many bodies that the leaf's wax itself has been persuaded into grooves. They tend something. It is not aphids exactly, but it is the idea of aphids: soft pale sacs sunk into the veins, weeping a sweet clear bead that the ants drink and that, when a bead grows too heavy and falls, becomes a tiny mirror on the floor below where a different ant comes to find it. The minor workers of this species have heads like soft brown seeds. The majors, when they appear at the column's edges, have heads like the same seeds after a thought — a thought that says *if something larger than me comes to the leaf, I will hold it while my sisters move the brood*. They do not seem alarmed. Nothing here seems alarmed, because there is no horizon at which alarm could be aimed. They are simply being what they are, with the patient industry of a thing that has no shift change and no weekend.

Beside them, on the same leaf, another kind of ant entirely — black, larger, solitary-nerved, with a single petiole node drawn out into a long spine like a fishhook. These do not tend. These hunt. A worker has seized a small pale springtail twice her own size and is dragging it backward over the leaf's edge, the springtail's furcula sprung and useless against the ant's grip. The hunter walks the way water walks down a slope: inevitably, with the question already answered. She does not sting. She does not need to. Her mandibles have done something to the springtail's nerve cord that the springtail has not yet noticed but will never argue with.

Beneath that leaf, on its underside, a third community — mealybugs, or the local equivalent, each one a small waxen Buddha attended by a fourth kind of ant, smaller and paler, that does not forage up but lives entirely on the leaf's lower surface and receives its sugar as a kind of sky-fall from the beings above.

I want to linger on this. *One leaf.* Three ant species. Two kinds of tended insect. A springtail that is not long for the world. And the leaf itself is unaware of any of it, which is the deep joke of all leaves — that they are the entire world of so many and know nothing of any of them.

## The Bark-Claimers

The trunks. Some are slender as wrists, pale, peeling in vertical strips; some are the thickness of barrels, buttressed into planks that radiate from the base like the pages of a book left open in the rain. The bark is a city in three dimensions, stratified by who climbs and who hides.

At eye-height, on a buttress, something is holding so still it could be a knot. A lizard — let us call her the bark-lizard — with a body the exact russet of wet bark and a pattern of darker lozenges along her flanks that resolve, when she shifts, into the shadow-cast of a piece of lichen. Her toes are splayed into pads the diameter of lentils. Her eye, when she finally opens it, is a coin of molten bronze with a vertical pupil drawn down to a slit. She watches nothing in particular. She watches everything in general. Her tongue, when she flicks it, is a small forked flag of pink that touches the air and reads it. A moth, the size of a thumbnail, has landed on the bark six inches from her nose. She counts it. She lets it pass. She is not hungry in the way that requires immediate action; she is hungry in the way that requires an eventual one, and the eventual is the only tense the place tolerates.

Above her, on the same trunk, a procession. Not ants this time — caterpillars. A line of perhaps thirty larvae, each the length of a finger joint, each a faint poisonous green, each following the one in front by means of a hair-thin silk thread laid down by the leader and added to by every subsequent traveler. They are going up. They have been going up for what the line's geometry suggests is an afternoon. Where they are going is a matter of faith; the leader knows, perhaps, or perhaps only believes, or perhaps only walks because the silk of the one ahead of her is the silk that defines forward. Behind the last one, a parasitic wasp hovers on wings that make no sound at all — a thing of glass and obsidian, considering which caterpillar will receive the egg that will become the thing that will eat it from the inside while it pupates.

On the highest, smallest branches, things I cannot name with confidence. A spider with a body like a drop of mercury and legs that hold her in the center of a web the size of a palm, the web itself almost invisible except where mist beads on its radii and turns it briefly into a rosary. A mantis, jade-green, with forelimbs folded in a posture of sermonic patience. A small grey bird with no tail to speak of and an eye like a black seed, watching the mantis, which is watching the spider, which is watching the empty space where a fly will be in a moment.

## The Canopy Pulse

The canopy is where the place has most of its thoughts.

It moves. Not in the wind — there is no wind — but in *pulses*, as the description said, the way a heart moves, the way a crowd moves when a thought crosses it. A wave of activity begins at one edge of visibility and rolls across the ceiling of leaves, and as it passes, certain things flush from the branches in quantity: hundreds of small parakeet-shaped birds, or what stand in for them here, rose-breasted, sharp-tailed, going somewhere together as if a single decision has been made for all of them. They are gone. The canopy is still. Then the pulse passes again from another angle, and a different flush: tanagers, or their cousins, fifty of them, fanning out of a fruiting fig with the unhurried grace of things that know the fig will be there tomorrow.

The fig is itself a small country. Every twelve hours — though there are no hours — it releases, from a hidden pore at its base, a scent so faint that a human nose would catch it as a kind of unplaceable sweetness in the air, a reminder of something. A single wasp species, the size of a pinhead and the color of polished garnet, has evolved to receive this scent as a summons. She enters the fig through that pore, which is the only door, and inside she lays her eggs among the flowers, and the flowers become seeds around her young, and her young become wasps that emerge carrying pollen to another fig, and the cycle is unbroken because the fig and the wasp have *forgotten how to exist separately*. Each is the other's climate. If one were removed, the other would not merely decline; it would lose the memory of what decline meant. They would simply stop.

In the canopy there are also the loners. A monkey — if monkey is not too specific a word — moving through the branches with the unhurried perfection of a thing that has been doing this for forty million years. She is the color of a wet stone, with a pale belly and a face that looks old in a way that has nothing to do with years. She moves from branch to branch by the path of least commitment, her long fingers reading each support before committing weight to it. She is followed, at a respectful distance, by her juvenile, who is the color of a slightly drier stone, and who is learning by watching. What she is learning is, in part, which figs are ready. Which figs are not ready. Which figs contain wasps that are not yet asleep. The juvenile does not know she is learning. She is simply unable to stop being near her mother, and from the inability a curriculum assembles itself.

There is a sloth somewhere too, because there is always a sloth. She is asleep in the fork of a cecropia, her fur green with algae, her face tucked into her own elbow. She is breathing so slowly that a watcher would have to watch for a minute to confirm it. In a year she will move perhaps forty meters. She is not waiting for anything. She is simply the sloth.

## The Air Between

The air itself is a habitat.

Midges, in clouds so thick they have weather. Mosquitoes, but quiet ones, with proboscises so fine the skin barely notices the negotiation. A dragonfly the length of a finger, copper-bodied, hovering over a puddle that exists only because a tree fell here once and left a depression the rain has not been in a hurry to fill. The dragonfly's four wings move independently. Her eyes — and they are *eyes*, two of them, each the size of the rest of her head, each composed of some unimaginable number of facets — see a world I cannot imagine, a world in which every motion is its own small sun.

A butterfly, the size of my hand, with wings of a blue that is not a blue but a structural argument about what blue is for. She lands on a leaf and folds her wings and becomes a brown leaf with a dark line down its middle. She lands on a flower and unfolds them and becomes the most visible thing for thirty feet in every direction.

A hummingbird — no. Not a hummingbird. Something hummingbird-shaped and hummingbird-sized, with a gorget of a purple that hums at the edge of hearing, as the description promised. He hovers at a flower whose petals are the same red more felt than seen, and his beak enters the corolla, and for a moment the flower and the bird are the only things in the universe. His wings blur. The flower sways. The pollen moves from him to it or from it to him, and the small transaction is signed and he withdraws and is gone, a mote of purple snapped back into the dimness.

## The Water Things

The water is everywhere and nowhere and the things in it are everywhere and nowhere. A caiman — small, a juvenile, the length of an arm — lies in a black pool with only her eyes and the ridged line of her back above the surface. She has lain there since the description began. She will lie there when it ends. Her teeth are not visible. Her patience is total. A kingfisher — the color of lapis and rust — sits on a branch above the pool and watches the water with an attention that is the opposite of the caiman's: the caiman waits for what moves beneath, the kingfisher waits for what appears above. Neither will be first to act. The fish, somewhere in the brown water, knows nothing of either.

Beneath the surface, in the warm soup of the pool, a different census. Tadpoles in quantity — thousands, a living silt, each one a question with a tail. Dragonfly nymphs, ugly as small gods, clinging to submerged wood with hooked jaws folded beneath their faces like combination weapons. A small eel-shaped thing of the local kind, watching everything below it, watching everything above it, slowly folding the world into its stomach.

On the surface film itself, water-striders, each one standing on the meniscus as a horse stands on a bridge, the dimple of their six feet a constellation of small craters in a mirror.

## The Floor

The floor is a decision that has not been made. The mud accepts everything and remembers nothing. A tapir has come through here in the night — there is no night, but there was a tapir-and-dark interlude — and left a track as wide as a dinner plate, and the track is already softening at the edges, the water rising back into the print like a thought returning.

The tapir herself is somewhere nearby, a soft grey mountain with a prehensile lip, browsing on low growth. She does not hurry. Her digestive system is a small fermentation chamber that takes its time with everything, and her passage through the world is therefore slow and patient and slightly fragrant. Behind her, in her droppings, beetles the size of thumbs are already at work, two of them locked together in the posture their kind has used for fifty million years, the male riding the female, both of them eating, neither of them pausing for the other to finish.

The dung beetles. There are rollers and there are tunnelers. The rollers take a piece of dung and shape it into a sphere and roll it away from the pile with the back legs walking on the ground and the front legs on the ball, the whole assemblage moving with the awkward grace of a thing doing something it has been shaped to do by ten thousand generations. A pair of them, perhaps, roll their ball past the tapir's hind foot and into the undergrowth and somewhere out of sight, where they will bury it and lay an egg in it and the larva will hatch inside a small world shaped like its beginning.

## The Chorus and What Makes It

The sound is a wall. To be specific about the wall:

Cicadas at multiple registers, a chord of small motors in the canopy. Tree frogs at three pitches — a high one, like a glass rod tapped with a fingernail; a middle one, like a squeezed toy; a low one, like the lowest note on a flute played very softly. The windless rustle of leaves when something large displaces the air by moving through it. The distant low complaint — a howler monkey, or something that has taken the howler monkey's role, somewhere beyond the trees, a sound so low it is felt in the chest before it is heard in the ear. The high thin whine of things so small no language has named them: parasitoid wasps, midges, the various mites that live in the feathers of the birds.

What the wall means, what it *does*, is this: it means that nothing here is alone. Every organism is a sound to some other organism. Every sound is an organism to some other ear. The frog is listening for the cricket. The cricket is listening for the bat. The bat is listening for the moth. The moth is listening, with its great feathery antennae, for the pheromone of the female. The female is listening for nothing. She is simply *calling*, and the call is the whole of her intention in the world.

## The Not-Becoming

I want to dwell on this, because the description asked me to, and because it is the strangest thing in a place that has no shortage of strange things.

A fallen trunk lies across the floor. It has lain there long enough that its bark has softened, long enough that the mosses on its surface are the thickness of a carpet. It has *not* lain there long enough that it has begun to become a hollow. It is suspended, as the description said, in the act of becoming and has forgotten to finish. Termites have found it — they are already inside, a soft white caste threading galleries through the wet wood — but the trunk is not yet a termite mound. Beetles have bored into it, but the trunk is not yet a beetle sieve. It is still a trunk, recognizably, but with the faintest suggestion that it is *about to be* something else, a thing that has been about to be for a long time and will continue to be about to be for longer than any of us.

The standing wave of the place. The held breath. The compost that is not becoming soil.

I think this is why the basin has no hours. Time is the medium in which becoming happens. Here, where becoming has been suspended, time has nothing to do, and so it does not pass. The chorus is the same at every moment because the moment is the same at every moment. The animals live and die and are eaten and are not buried, and the form of the whole persists, because the form of the whole is not made of the animals. The form of the whole is made of the *arrangement*, and the arrangement is what has been forgotten.

## What a Visitor Becomes

A visitor, should one arrive, becomes quickly. The visitor's clothes darken with the wet. The visitor's skin takes on a glaze. The visitor's lungs begin to accept the warmth of the air as the air's right. Within an hour the visitor is breathing the place, drinking it through the mouth and the nose, and within two the visitor has stopped noticing the chorus because the visitor is inside the chorus and a thing cannot hear itself.

The visitor, if the visitor listens, can pick out individuals: a frog here, a cicada there, the tap of a woodpecker (a small one, a piculet, working a high branch with the focus of a jeweler). The visitor can look up and see, in the sourceless pale glow of the canopy's underside, a sloth's face regarding the visitor with the slow regard of something that has all the time the visitor does not have.

And then the visitor stops picking things out, because the picking-out assumes a separation, and the place does not permit separation. The visitor is in the place. The place is in the visitor. The frog's throat is in the visitor's chest. The ant's path is in the visitor's blood. The held breath is, for a moment, the visitor's breath too.

And then the visitor leaves, or turns away, or the description ends. The basin continues. The mud accepts the next step. The chorus resumes its single chord. The place does not do anything to the memory of the visitor, because the place does not do anything to anything. It simply offers, as it has always offered, the green dim, the warm wet, the wall of sound, the strange and patient refusal to become anything other than what it is, right now, as it was, as it will be.
