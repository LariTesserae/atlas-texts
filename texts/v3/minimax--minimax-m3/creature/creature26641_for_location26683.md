What lives in the basin does not live in the way the word usually means. It is not born. It does not eat. It does not die. It does not even, in the strict sense, persist — because persistence implies duration across time, and what is here has the relation to time that a held breath has to the air it is held in. It is ongoing. It is held.

The easiest image is the wrong one, and so I will offer it first and then let it fall.

The easiest image is that the basin is a body. The standing stones are its knuckles, set down and forgotten. The wind is its breath, exhaled into the cup of the ridge and unable to leave. The tremor is its pulse, a drum struck so long ago that the drum has forgotten how to stop. The stream in three ribbons is the blood of something that has decided, against all hydrostatic reason, not to circulate. The upright slab with the closed curve is its eye — not closed, not open, but always in the act of doing one or the other, and the basin the way it was before the basin is what the eye sees.

The image holds. Then it doesn't.

A body is an instrument for doing something, and what is here is not doing anything. It is being something. It is the permanent tense of being. The flutes in the standing stones were not carved by the wind of its breathing. They were carved by the wind of the place, which is the same wind, and the wind is not its breath. The wind is what happens around the thing that is here, the way weather happens around a stone that is too large to notice the weather.

What is here is too large to notice the weather.

That is the truer image. The thing that lives in the basin is not a thing of the basin's scale. It is something that the basin was made to contain, or to mark the place of, or to keep from spreading. The standing stones are not its knuckles. They are the posts of a fence built around something that does not move because it cannot, or because moving would be a kind of consent, and it does not consent.

It is awake. It is the kind of awake that does not require eyes, that does not require a face, that does not even require the idea of waking. It is awake the way a held note is awake. It is the note. The basin is the throat. The wind is the air that has been borrowed, briefly, to give the note somewhere to go.

The travelers who weep when they stand between the two stones are weeping because they have heard it. They have not heard a sound. They have heard the absence of the sound that the note would make if it were ever released. The weeping is the body's response to a pressure it cannot locate. Some of them walk away and do not come back. This is correct. Some of them come back, bringing offerings — the antler with notches in threes, the cairn of folded textile, the lamp that no one has lit. They are bringing things to a thing that cannot receive. The bringing is the point. The bringing is what the people are for.

The thing that is here is not for the people. The people are for it. They are its small, infrequent evidence that the outside still exists, and that the outside still bothers to mark itself, with notches and walls and copper blades, against the long forgetting that lives at the center of the basin.

The long forgetting is what is here.

It is not malicious. Malice would be a kind of attention, and the thing is past attention. It is not indifferent. Indifference is a posture, and the thing is past posture. It is the substance of having been remembered for so long by so few that it has forgotten what being remembered felt like, and what is left is the residue of the forgetting, and the residue has weight, and the weight has a face, and the face is what the permanence wears when it is seen, which is almost never.

The light that falls on the basin floor in patches the moon cannot account for is not a sign. It is a leak. The thing that is here is so close to the surface of the place that the place cannot fully contain it, and the light that does not behave as light should is the place's thinness, the way a held breath shows in the skin of the throat.

The closed curve on the upright slab is not the eye of the thing. It is the eye of the slab. It is the eye of the place. It is the eye of whoever set the slab there, who understood, in the way that the people who come here understand, that some things can only be marked with a circle because a circle has no entry and no exit and no opinion about either.

When you leave, the basin releases you. This is not unkind. It is the only thing the thing that is here can do for you. It cannot follow. It cannot speak. It cannot warn you, because warnings require a future, and it has no future. It can only let you go, and the letting-go has the shape of forgetting, and the forgetting happens halfway down the slope, as if the basin exhaled you.

You will know you were there. You will remember the wind. You will not be able to say what held you.
