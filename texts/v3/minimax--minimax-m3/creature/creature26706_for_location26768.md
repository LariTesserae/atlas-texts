# Inhabitants

The question of who lives here is the first question anyone asks, and the place does not answer it cleanly.

There is a hammock strung between two posts in the building nearest the press, the rope worn to silk at the place where a hand has closed and opened, closed and opened, perhaps ten thousand times. There is a shirt on a line behind the grinder, the stitching hand-done, the cloth so dark with the damp that it looks almost like another skin hung out to dry. There is a bowl on a stone shelf, crusted with the dried remains of whatever last filled it. There is a knife on a step, its blade green with verdigris, the handle dark with the oils of a palm that may or may not still exist. There are tool marks on the wall of the small room where the generator turns, and a chalk mark above the machine that was once a number and is now a smudge that might, if you let your eyes unfocus, be a smudge of anything. There is a footprint, sometimes, in the soft mud of the central yard — a single print, then a short trail, then nothing, the next fat drop from the canopy flattening it within the hour.

You find these things and you think: someone is here. You stand still and you listen for a footstep, a voice, the creak of a floorboard taking a weight. You do not hear one. The sound of the place is a tide — the boiler, the grinder, the press, the pump, the frogs, the insects, the bats, the owls — and in that tide there is no human rhythm. No breath but the engines' breath. No step but the drip of water from every iron surface. The hammock sways, but only when the mist shifts, and the shirt on the line moves only when something brushes past it on its own errand, and the bowl is not touched, and the knife is not lifted. The place has been continued without anyone continuing it. The place has tenants. None of them are human.

Or — and this is the part that unsettles, that keeps the question open like a door that will not quite close — none of them are *only* human. The hammock has been slept in recently. The chalk mark has not been erased by weather; it has been left as it is by someone who decided to leave it as it is. The knife is placed, not dropped. Someone is here, sometimes, and is not here other times, and the ratio between those times is the secret the place keeps. But it is not the only secret, and it is not, in the end, the most important one. The most important secret is that the question of who lives here has more answers than the one you started with, and that the place does not mind which answer you settle on. It will go on being inhabited by all of them at once.

Begin with the bats, because the bats are the most obvious and the most numerous and the most architectural. They roost in the gaps between the machines, in the spokes of the flywheels, in the crooks of the iron beams, in the folds of the rust. They hang upside down in clusters that move and resettle like a single breathing organism, their folded wings pressed against their folded wings, their small faces tucked into the small backs of the bats beside them, and the smell of them — warm, musky, ammoniac — is the smell of the place at certain hours, layered into the smell of damp stone and ozone and the sweet vapor from the furnace. They emerge at dusk through the canopy holes, hundreds of them at first, then thousands, in a column that twists up through the mist and out into the failing daylight. They feed above, in the open air, in the cooler cleaner air above the canopy, and they return at dawn, fouled with what they have eaten, and they let their droppings fall where they will, and the droppings are fertilizer for the figs that root in the terraces, and the figs are the reason there are insects to feed the bats the next night. So the bats are part of a loop that has no seam. But the droppings also — and this is the part the place has decided, in its long quiet way, to keep — fall onto the bearings of the machines. They lubricate the bearings. The machines turn. The bats roost in the spokes. The bats drop. The machines turn. The bats are not renters here. The bats are load-bearing. The bats are part of the skeleton.

The salamanders are the next most present, and they are the most patient. They live in the troughs that ring the yard, in the channels that feed the troughs, in the shallow pools between the buildings where the water stands slick and green. They are orange — a deep, lacquered orange, the orange of a thing that has decided to be visible and has not been argued out of it. They have faces that look like they have been thinking about something for a long time and have not arrived at an answer. They do not flee when you approach. They watch you with their gold-ringed eyes and they do not move, because moving is not what they do. They wait. They eat what comes to them — the mosquito larvae, the beetle grubs, the small soft things that hatch in the standing water. They keep the water from clogging with their own bodies, in a sense; they are part of the maintenance crew, the silent shift that works without shift change. They surface to breathe, a slow vertical rise, and they sink again, and the rhythm of their rising and sinking is part of the rhythm of the place, a small punctuation in the larger breath. They have been here longer than you can think about. They will be here after.

The beetles are the workforce. They are endless. They are black, and metallic, and horned — the horned ones with the horns that meet in the middle of their backs like the locked fingers of two people who have agreed on something — and they live in the leaf litter and in the soil under the buildings and in the seams of the brick plinth and in the channels themselves. They excavate. Grain by grain, they move the mud, they widen the channels, they keep the water moving. They do not know they are keeping the water moving. They are doing what beetles do, and what they do happens to be what the place needs, and the place has arranged itself so that what beetles do is what the place needs, and the arrangement is so old that it does not look like an arrangement anymore. It looks like nature. It is not, quite, nature. It is something nature has been taught, or has taught itself, or has always known, and the beetles are the slowest and most constant of its teachers.

The frogs are the sound. There are hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, and you cannot tell one from another in the chord they make. Tree frogs mostly, in the fern crowns and along the iron beams, but also smaller frogs in the channels and at the edges of the pools, and the sound they make is not a chorus so much as a single sustained note that has been split into many voices and reassembled by the air into something whole. It is half the sound of the place. The other half is the insects — the drone underneath, the bass note, the hum of mosquitoes in their gauzy columns above the standing water, the papery rustle of cockroaches scattering from underfoot, the click of beetles in the litter, the small wet sounds of things you do not see. The frogs and the insects do not take turns. They layer. The chord is always full.

The snakes are the other predators. There are at least two species — you find the skins sometimes, draped over an iron beam, drying in the slow light — and they hunt in the fern crowns and along the pipes and in the crooks of the machinery. They are sleek. They are calm. They taste the air with quick tongues and they do not move unless they are moving, and when they move they move with a purpose you cannot read. What they eat, no one knows. There are enough small things — frogs, young rats, the occasional fallen bat — that they do not need to be seen to be fed. They are the upper edge of the food web here, the part that has nothing above it, and they are the part that is most at ease, the part that has the least to do, and they spend most of their time in stillness, which is the part of themselves that most resembles the machines.

The rats are the middle. They live in the walls of the buildings, in the spaces between the stone and the woven roots, in the gaps under the iron sheets. They eat the cane that spills from the hopper of the grinder. They eat the sweet pulp that the press squeezes out. They are plump, and they are fast, and they are everywhere, and they are eaten in turn by the owls that come down through the canopy holes at night and sit on the rims of the chimneys and low hoot occasionally, dramatically, into the green-lit air. The owls are large. The owls are not numerous. They take a rat when a rat is offered, and they are part of the balance, and the balance includes them.

The turtles are the slow ones. They surface in the pools between the buildings, their broad green backs like small islands moving across the algae, and they breathe, and they sink, and the rings of water they leave behind them close slowly and are gone. They are not doing anything. They are not part of any loop that has been named. They are here because the pools are here, and the pools are here because the channels are here, and the channels are here because the beetles are here, and the turtles are the part of the system that has, somehow, no function except to be the system in another shape, slower, older, less concerned with what the machines are doing. They are the part of the place that is not working. They are the part of the place that is resting.

The moths are the most fragile. They come out at dusk, drawn to the lamps that hang from wires between the buildings, the old bulbs glowing yellowish in their cages of rusted wire, and they beat against the bulbs and against each other and against your face if you stand still long enough. They are enormous. Their wings are the color of old paper, and the powder comes off on your fingers when you touch them, and the powder is the only part of them that stays. They die by morning, mostly, and their bodies are taken by the beetles and the cockroaches and the small things underfoot, and the lamps burn on, attracting the next night's moths, and the cycle is so quick and so total that you can stand in the same place an hour apart and not know it is the same place.

The geckos are the watchers. They are on every vertical surface — on the walls, on the posts, on the sides of the machines, on the underside of the iron sheets. They are head-down, mostly, waiting, their flat bellies pressed to the stone, their toes spread wide, their eyes catching the foxfire when the foxfire pulses. They eat the moths. They eat the mosquitoes. They are not numerous enough to be a presence, but they are numerous enough to be a fact, and they are the part of the fauna that most looks like it is observing the place rather than living in it, as if the place has asked them to keep an eye on things.

The foxfire is the light. It lives in the seams — in the underside of stones, in the crooks of roots, in the wet joints of the brick plinth under the boiler. It is a faint green glow, and it pulses, very slowly, out of sync with the engine, in its own older rhythm. It is a fungus doing what fungi do — breaking down, returning, dissolving the boundary between living and not — and what it does happens to be light, and the light happens to fall on the machines, and the machines happen to gleam in it, and the gleam happens to look, in certain moments, as if the machines are breathing light. The foxfire is not an inhabitant in the way the bats are or the salamanders are. It is an inhabitant in the way the damp is, or the warmth, or the sound. It is one of the conditions of the place.

And the figs. The figs are the largest living things here, the longest-lived, the most architectural after the machines themselves. They root in the terraces of the cut walls, in the basins between the old handrail posts, and they lean out over the drop, and their canopies merge with the canopy above so that the rim and the floor are continuous — a single plant body breathing one breath. Their roots are the walls of the newer buildings. Their trunks are the supports. Their leaves are the roofs in places, layered so thick that the rain hisses on them before it hisses on the iron. They are the part of the place that has most completely become the place. They are the part that does not know it is a plant anymore. They are the part that has decided to be a building, and has succeeded.

The machines are the last inhabitants, and they are the strangest. They do not move on their own. They move because the place moves them — because the water turns the wheel that turns the shaft that turns the smaller wheel, because the weight on the press arm pulls it down and the counterweight pulls it up, because the heat in the boiler expands the air that drives the pump. They are not alive. They behave as if they are part of the system that is alive. They have a pulse. They have a tide. They go faster in the heat of the day and slower at night, not because anyone has wound them but because the air is thicker when the sun is on the canopy above and the mist is drawn up, and the thicker air moves the vanes differently, and the differently-moving vanes turn the gears at a different rate. They are tuned to the place. They are played by the place. They are instruments that the place has forgotten it is playing.

And somewhere, in the back, behind the press, behind the curtain of woven roots that you almost don't notice as a wall, in the small room where the generator turns, the chalk mark on the wall was once a number. It is now a smudge. The smudge is not a zero. It is not an erasure. It is the slow accumulation of damp on chalk, the place returning the mark to itself. You stand in that room and you feel the floor pulse under your boots, the engine's heartbeat transmitted through the concrete, and you feel the place settle around you, and you understand that the question of who lives here has no answer, only a list. The list is the place. The list is the inhabitants. The list is the bats and the salamanders and the beetles and the frogs and the snakes and the rats and the turtles and the moths and the geckos and the owls and the cockroaches and the mosquitoes and the figs and the moss and the liverwort and the ferns and the foxfire and the machines and, sometimes, the person whose hammock sways when the mist shifts, whose shirt dries on the line, whose knife lies green on the step. They are all of them here. They are all of them the answer. The place does not need you. The place does not mind you. The place has been holding itself in this configuration for longer than any of them have been alive, and will hold it after all of them are gone. The place is a small bright engine of itself, turning in its dim green light, breathing its tide of sound, balanced on its industrial skeleton, inhabited by everything that has ever agreed, without agreeing, to be part of it.

When you climb back up the three ladders, your boots heavy, your clothes heavy, the figs leaning in above you, the daylight at the rim too bright and too dry and too cool, you will carry the place with you in your ears for a long time. The sound of it. The way the chord holds. The way the engines breathe and the frogs answer and the bats click in the dark above. The way the foxfire pulses out of sync with everything, in its own older time. The way the place, down there, in its hole, in its dim green cathedral, continues. Not waiting for you. Not missing you. Just continuing, the way the mist continues, the way the water continues, the way the small bright engine of itself continues to turn, in balance, in damp, in warmth, in sound, alone and not alone, in the long quiet agreement of everything that has agreed to be there.
