What lives there is slow, and what lives there is many.

You hear them before you see most of them. The smallest and most numerous are the dogs, and they have arranged the city among themselves without anyone remarking on it. A dog sleeps now in the angle where a stair meets a wall, chin on its paws, one ear turned up to the wind. Another is already on its feet, pads clicking on the fitted stone, trotting down a channel toward the lower gate. A third stands on the roof edge of a third-tier house and watches the valley with a fixed attention that is not quite alertness — something older than that, the attention of an animal that has no enemies. Their coats are the dun and the rust and the smoke-grey of the stones. They own the streets in shifts, as has been said, and at nightfall they gather, and the barking that rises is not a warning but a kind of keeping — of time, of territory, of each other — and the sound carries a long way in the thin air because the air does not absorb what passes through it.

The goats are out by mid-morning. They are released through low openings cut into the retaining walls of the lowest terrace, and they climb without hurry up onto the rough pasture above the city, where the grass is cropped close and the lichen grows only in the cracks. There are perhaps a hundred of them. They are pale — a cream color that has gone slightly yellow in the light — and small-boned, hard-coated, with the horizontal pupils that give them their perpetual look of sidelong appraisal. They move across the slope in a loose formation that is not herded but that holds together. A goat pauses to browse a cushion plant in a crevice. Another pauses to look down at you, and the look is not curiosity. It is the look of an animal that has been looked at by many things and has not yet found one of them worth moving for. Their hooves make a small brittle clatter on the broken rock. When a cloud comes down and they are suddenly grey, they do not move. They stand and continue to chew, and the chewing is a sound you would recognize later as the sound of this place.

The oxen are heavier and rarer and live lower. They are kept in stone-walled pens just inside the gate, and they come out only to pull. They have the wide foreheads and the splayed hooves of animals bred for work on stone, and their coats — when the cold has thickened them into that felt-like growth — are nearly the color of the dark blue-grey walls. A man leads one up the main street, and the ox puts its head down and pulls, and the cart behind it carries dressed stone or a fallen beam or a clay jar the size of a child, and the wheels grate against the fitted paving, and the sound is not unpleasant because it is a sound that has been heard here a long time. The ox does not hurry. The man does not hurry. The street is too steep for hurrying, and besides, hurrying is not what they do.

The birds are the largest of the animals that move freely. They sit in rows along the roof edges of the upper terraces, dark-feathered, broad-winged, with a look of having been designed for the particular wind that comes off the glacier to the north. They shift from foot to foot in the gusts the way a sailor shifts on a deck. They do not startle at your approach. They watch you pass with the same flat incurious attention the goats give, and the look is not unkind — it is only the look of an animal that has not had to be afraid of anything for many generations, and has lost the memory of how. When a weather system moves down from the peaks and the visibility drops, the birds are still there, dark shapes on dark shapes in the grey, and when the cloud lifts they shake themselves once, thoroughly, and take up their stations again.

The people are slower than all of these animals and quieter.

You see them first as figures in the street at a distance, going about a small errand with the pace of someone who has made this errand before. A woman comes down a stepped lane with a water jar held against her hip by one arm, and her other arm swings slightly, and she does not look up, because the steps are uneven and she has been down them every morning of her life and knows the places where the stone has been worn low and the place where a chip has been wedged to bring a tread back to level. She wears a long dark wool — felted, perhaps, or densely woven — and over it a sleeveless coat that has been patched twice in different shades of brown, and on her head a cloth wrapped and tied. Her face is brown also, and weathered, and the lines of it are not so much lines as creases, the kind that come from a face that has been held the same way for a long time, looking slightly down, watching the ground. Her breath makes a small cloud in front of her mouth and disappears.

She is carrying water from one of the cisterns. The cisterns are everywhere — under the houses, behind the retaining walls, in the foundations of the upper terraces — and in the morning the small boys of the city go round with clay jars and a ladle, and they empty the night's gathering into the jars, and the jars are carried up. The water is black with the mineral of the roof and the street, and it tastes of stone, and it is what there is. A boy of perhaps nine or ten is doing this now. He is barefoot. His trousers are cut off at the knee and have been let out twice at the hem, so that the lower band is a different fabric than the upper, and the cloth is faded to the same color as the rock it was woven against. He moves with the quick unselfconsciousness of his age, up three steps, down two, around the corner of a house where the wall is so thick the corner is a place you can almost sit in. He does not greet you. He glances at you once, the way the animals glance, and goes on. He has work.

The man with the chisel is older. He is sitting on a low stone bench outside his house in a patch of sun that will move off him in an hour. He has a piece of dressed stone across his knees and a small hand-ax — the blade a dull bronze-green, the haft a wood that has gone dark with handling — and he is making a wedge. He has been making wedges for a long time. There is a basket of them beside him, already finished, and a basket of offcuts, and a pile of stone dust that the wind has not quite taken. He is not thinking about what he is doing. He is thinking, if he is thinking, about something else — the small boy, perhaps, who is his, or the goat that did not come down this morning, or nothing at all, the way a man thinks nothing when his hands know what they are doing. The chip of the bronze on the stone is one of the city's sounds, and you will hear it, on and off, all day, from several parts of the city at once, the way you would hear birdsong in another place.

A man climbs the main street, and he climbs at the pace of a man climbing a long staircase. He carries nothing. He is going up to the upper terrace to see about something — a wall, a cistern, a goat — and his hands are loose at his sides, and his breath comes out in front of him in small regular clouds. He is forty, perhaps, or fifty, or thirty-five — the cold and the light have made everyone look older than they are, and also younger, in the way that people who live in hard places do not accumulate the marks of single years but instead seem to have arrived at the age they will keep. His face is the color of the bread. His hair is cut short. He nods at you, a small inclination, and passes.

Inside the houses there is more warmth, but the warmth has been made, and is being maintained with effort. The fire is small and the fuel is precious — the dried dung and the slow twigs of the cushion plants, gathered in the season when it can be gathered — and the smoke goes up into the roof and finds the chimney-hole and goes out. A woman sits on a mat near the fire with a wooden bowl of tea in both hands. The tea is dark and bitter and very hot, and she drinks it slowly. Across from her another woman is working a hide across a stone, scraping it toward her with a tool whose blade is of the same dull bronze-green. The scraping is a sound like a long slow breath. A child is asleep on a folded blanket in the corner, and the breath of the child makes no cloud because the air here is warm enough to hold it, and the face of the child is the same brown as the faces of the women, and is unworn.

They eat with their hands. The bread is flat and dense and the color of the mountain, and it is broken and not cut, and the pieces are used to take up the stew, which is mutton or goat with the root of the small tough plant that grows in the cracks of the upper rock — a root that has to be dug with a tool and that tastes, when you eat it, of the cold itself, or of the stone. They do not speak much at the meal. They do not speak much at any time. They have not been silent because they have nothing to say, but because the place they live in does not require many words and has not encouraged them to develop the habit of them. When something is said it is brief, and it is the kind of brief that does not need to be explained afterward.

The children are the exception. The children move fast, and they speak in bursts, and they laugh with a high breathless sound that carries the way the dogs' barking carries, and it is the most human sound in the city, and you notice when it comes and you notice when it stops. A child runs along a rooftop, and a dog runs after, and the bare feet make a quick patter on the stone, and the dog's nails make a quicker patter, and they turn a corner together and are gone. Another child sits on a low wall and watches the goats being driven down in the late afternoon, and the child does not call out or wave but watches with the same flat attention the adults give, as if the goats were the subject of something the child is learning. A small girl carries a smaller child on her hip and walks with the careful gait of someone who has had practice at it, and she is six.

At dusk the light goes blue in the long shadows and the air sharpens, and the city contracts into its small fires. The smoke rises. The dogs begin their chorus, and it is answered from the upper terraces, and from the lower gate, and from somewhere out on the pasture, and the mountain seems to be holding a conversation in one tongue, and you do not understand it but you can tell that it is not about anything urgent. The people go in. The children are brought in. The small boy with the water jar makes his last round of the cisterns and comes down a lane that is almost dark and disappears through a low doorway. The man with the chisel puts the bronze tool in his basket and the basket behind the door and goes inside. The woman with the water jar sets her jar down on the stone floor and does not empty it yet but sits beside it, and her husband or her neighbor pours tea, and they drink it, and the fire is small, and the smoke goes up, and outside the dogs go on with their talk, and the wind moves over the roof, and the cold waits, patient, on the other side of the wall.
