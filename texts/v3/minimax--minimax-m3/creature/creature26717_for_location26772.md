The first thing to say is that they are not so much *people who live in a place* as people who have agreed, over many generations, to be a particular kind of body. The Below makes its inhabitants the way a river makes its stones, and you can tell long residents from newcomers the way you can tell a water-rounded cobble from a sharp one — by the surfaces, by the way the light comes off them, by the patient dulling of the corners.

They are short, by the standards of the world above. Not dramatically so — an inch, perhaps two, on average — but enough that the people who come down the crevasse for the first time notice it in the doorways, in the way they have to duck less than they expected. This is not genetic in any simple sense. It is the slow accumulation of a thousand small pressures: ceilings that are low because ceilings do not need to be high, stair treads that are short because the people who measured them had short legs, work at the face done crouched or kneeling because the seam of good stone is rarely at a comfortable height. Children grow up under ceilings that press them gently downward into a particular shape, and the shape, over centuries, has become the shape of the people. The ones who come from above and stay for a generation begin, after a while, to walk as if the ceilings were lower than they are. They never quite lose their height, but they lose the habit of standing fully upright in a room.

**Their hands** are the thing you notice next, because in the Below the hands are always working, and the work is always against stone. The palms are wide and thick and the fingers are blunt at the tips, not from any single injury but from the slow accumulation of callus and the slow wearing of callus and the slow building of callus again, a kind of geological process happening on the skin. The nails are kept short — broken, usually, rather than cut, because a chisel will catch a long nail and tear it and the tear is a small wound that becomes a few days away from the work. The knuckles are enlarged, particularly on the right hand of the right-handed and on the left hand of the left-handed, and the skin across the back of the hand is often a little scarred in patterns that are diagnostic of the trade — the chisel-kiss on the tendon below the thumb, the small white mark on the index finger where the bronze picks up under a missed strike. Their hands are not beautiful by any standard that prizes smoothness. They are competent, and they are warm, and they are the part of themselves that the people of the Below would most readily show a stranger.

**Their faces** are pale, in the way that faces are pale when they have never been tanned — a low, even color, somewhere between the cream of the limestone above and the dull blue-gray of the darker bands, and the undertones are cool because the light they live in is cool and their skin has, over many generations, become a kind of mirror of the stone. They freckle only at the very young, and the freckles fade and do not come back. The eyes are large, by the standards of the surface world, and the pupils are wide, and the irises are dark — a deep brown, sometimes with a fine amber ring at the edge that is thought to be a Below trait even when no one admits to thinking in those terms. The wide pupil is a practical adaptation, or looks like one: the light is always dim, and the eye that wastes photons wastes food, and the eye that gathers them well is the eye that works in the half-light at the working face. The whites of the eyes are visible, but only just, because the eyes are not so much wide as *open*, held in a permanent middle state of alert, and the result is a face that looks, to a visitor, as if it is listening even when it is not.

**Their skin** is generally dry, in the way that skin is dry in a place where the air is damp but the dampness is in the stone and not in the air a person can feel. The skin tends to be cool to the touch, and a little rough, and there is often a faint mineral smell to it, not unpleasant, like the smell of a clean kitchen. Older people's skin goes thin and papery in the way old skin does everywhere, but the wrinkles are concentrated in particular places — around the eyes, in the corners of the mouth, across the back of the hand — and the rest of the face stays smoother than the equivalent face above, because the constant humidity of the Below keeps the skin from drying out fully. People look, in the Below, younger than they are until they are quite old, and then they look older than they are very quickly, and the transition is visible in the way the hands go from working to worn.

**Their hair** is most often dark brown to black, and it is worn long by both men and women, usually tied back at the nape with a cord or a strip of cloth because loose hair catches in the chisel-work and is dangerous. The hair is often a little lank from the damp air, and it does not have the volume or the wave of hair that has been allowed to dry in sunlight. There is grey in the hair of older people, but it comes in salt-and-pepper rather than as a full silvering, and a fully white head is rare — the workers who stay at the face into their fifties and sixties were dyed by stone dust and lamp soot as much as by age, and the white that comes through is intermittent, a kind of striation of the hair that matches the limestone around them. Beards are common on men and uncommon on women, and the men who grow beards keep them short at the chin and longer at the jaw, because the drip from the ceilings catches the beard and the longer the beard the wetter the chest.

**Their clothing** is layered, and the layering is functional. The innermost layer is linen, where linen can be gotten, and is otherwise a coarse plant-fiber cloth; it is worn against the skin and changed when it can be afforded to be changed. Above that is wool, in the cooler chambers, and the wool is thick and a little felted from use and is often the natural color of the sheep it came from, which is a yellowish off-white, so the general color of the people in the Below is a kind of pale cream, with the darker bands of brown and grey where the outer garments have been dyed or have picked up the dust of the work. The outer garment, when one is worn, is a long vest or a short coat that covers the shoulders and the upper arms and leaves the forearms free, because the forearms are where the work is done and the work must be visible and must be unencumbered. The legs are wrapped in wool or heavy cloth, and the feet are shod in soft leather shoes that are more like thick socks — there is no cobbling in the Below, because the floors are stone and the stones are not what wears out the shoes, the dampness is, and a soft leather shoe that can be thrown away when it is worn through is more practical than a well-made one. The shoes are often wet. People do not remark on this.

**Their voices** are, to a visitor, the most disorienting thing. They are quiet. Not in the way that a quiet person is quiet, by restraint, but in the way that everyone in a quiet place is quiet, by calibration. The walls are thin and the rooms are close and a voice raised in one chamber is heard in three others, so the people of the Below have learned to speak at the volume of a person in the same room, and the same room is, in the Below, always a small room. They speak low, and they speak with the chest more than the head, and the result is a voice that does not travel, that is meant to be heard by the person next to them and by no one else, and when they need to be heard further they do not raise their voice but they shape the words more carefully, and a shout in the Below is a strange, controlled sound, all consonants and very little volume, projected by the diaphragm and aimed at the listener. The accent of the Below is distinctive — the vowels are flatter, the consonants are sharper, and the rhythm of the speech is slower than the speech of the surface, because a sentence spoken quickly is a sentence that cannot be understood through a wall. Newcomers are recognized, among other things, by the way they speak too fast and at the wrong volume.

**Their posture** is particular. They walk with the shoulders slightly forward and the head a little down, not in deference but because the ceilings are low and the habit of looking down is a habit of looking ahead. They do not swing their arms when they walk because the corridors are narrow and swinging arms hit walls. They step lightly and unevenly, the way a person steps on a stair that is not quite where the foot expects it, and this is because the floors of the Below are worn and the footing is rarely flat. They are good at walking in the dark, and they move through the unlit chambers with the confidence of long familiarity, and a visitor who is watching them in lamplight will notice that they look, in their movement, almost as if they are slightly underwater, slowed and careful and continuous.

**Their faces in repose** have a particular quality, which is the quality of a face that has been seeing the same walls for a long time. There is not so much expression as attention. The eyes move, and the mouth moves, but the face itself is mostly still, and the stillness is not blankness but a kind of resting state, the way a well-tuned instrument is at rest. Children are noisy and quick and their faces are very expressive, because the children have not yet been tuned to the quietness of the place, and the difference between a Below child's face and a Below adult's face is the difference between an instrument being played and an instrument being still. There is a particular expression that the adults have, which is a slight narrowing of the eyes and a slight compression of the mouth, and it is the expression of a person listening to the stone, and a Below person will fall into it when they are thinking, or when they are not thinking, or when someone they live with is being noisy upstairs.

**Their sleep** is interesting. They sleep in shifts, and the shift system is not formally organized but is something that has settled into the rhythms of the place. The deeper chambers, which are the cooler and the quieter, are the sleeping chambers, and the people who sleep in them sleep in the warmest hours of the surface day, when the temperature differential is least and the air is most still. People who sleep near the shafts sleep in the deep surface night, when the shafts are dark and the city is at its quietest, and the shafts do not bother them with their light. The result is that the Below is never fully asleep and never fully awake, and the people of the Below are accustomed to the presence of others in the various stages of being asleep or awake, and they move around the sleepers without remark. A Below person can fall asleep in any of the postures of the working day, because the working day is the sleeping day and the two are interleaved, and a visitor who sees a person asleep in a corridor will not be able to tell whether the person is between shifts or between tasks.

**Their relationship to the stone** is the most particular thing about them, and the most difficult to put into words. They do not think of the stone as an adversary, and they do not think of it as a friend. They think of it as a body, and the city is a body they live inside, and the work of carving is a kind of conversation with the body, and the conversation goes on for a long time and is mostly silent. There is a Below word, *the listening*, which is what a person does when they put their ear to the stone and wait to hear what the stone is going to do next, and the listening is a practical skill, because the stone tells you, in advance, where it wants to come apart, and a person who listens well wastes less effort and breaks fewer tools. There is also a Below word for the feeling of being in a chamber that has just been opened, the smell of the stone and the coolness of the air and the slight sense of being in a place that has not been settled before, and the word is used for new rooms and for new children and for new ideas, and the people of the Below do not think it strange that the same word is used for all three.

**Their food** is plain, and not abundant, and the meals are social in a way that meals are only social in places where the people who eat together see each other at many other times anyway. Bread, mostly, and a kind of thick porridge made from a grain that is stored dry and cooked with water and oil and sometimes a little dried meat or dried fish. There is a cheese that is aged in the deep chambers, where the temperature is constant, and the cheese is sharp and a little ammoniac and is one of the things the Below is known for. There is a small bitter green that grows in the moss-bowls near the shafts, and it is eaten fresh and is also dried and stored. There is, on feast days, a kind of cake made from honey and ground grain, and the honey is one of the things that comes down from above, traded for carved stone and for the small bronze buckles that the Below makes well. People eat with their hands, mostly, because the kitchens are small and flatware is not common, and the bread is torn and used to scoop the porridge. The meals are quick, because the day is full of other things, and the people of the Below are accustomed to eating in the middle of other activities and to setting the bowl down and picking up the chisel and setting the chisel down and picking up the bowl again.

**Their children** are one of the great presences of the Below, and they are a presence in a particular way. A Below child is born in a residential chamber and grows up in the close air of the Below and the child's first memory is, very often, the sound of a hammer through the wall. The children play in the corridors and in the small plazas, and they play the games of children anywhere, but the games are constrained by the architecture — there is no wide and open space, there is no grass, there is no place to run except down a corridor, and a child who runs down a corridor learns quickly that the corridor is also the kitchen of someone else, and the running stops. Children are not so much watched as accompanied, in the Below, because the chamber is too small to lose a child in and the adults are too close to fail to hear a child, and the children grow up in a kind of distributed attention, with many adults aware of them at all times. Children are taught to use a chisel before they are taught to read, and the first chisel is a small one, and the first lesson is the listening, and the child learns to put the ear to the stone and to wait for what the stone will say. By the time a Below child is eight or nine, the child has made a small contribution to the city — has carved a small niche, has smoothed a small face — and the contribution is visible in the wall, and the child grows up knowing that the city is partly their work.

**Their old people** are treated with a particular kind of respect, which is the respect of a place that does not have many places to put a person who cannot work. An old person in the Below is, very often, the keeper of the listening, because the listening is a skill that is perfected over a lifetime, and an old person who can listen to the stone is an old person who can save the working face from a bad cut. The old people sit in the plazas, and they sit on the benches that are carved into the walls, and they watch the city, and they are consulted on the small decisions of the day and on the large decisions of the year, and they are carried up the shafts at the end, in bundles, and laid out on the hillside. The hillside above the Below has been receiving the dead for a long time, and the stones of the place are a kind of continuation of the city, an open-air gallery of the names of the people who made the chambers beneath.

**Their strangers** are rare, and are treated with a guarded hospitality. A newcomer is watched for the first weeks, and the watching is not hostile but careful, because the Below is a place that does not have the resources to absorb a person who cannot contribute, and also is a place that does not have the resources to absorb a person who will not listen. The newcomer is given a corner of a chamber and is expected to learn the listening before being given a chisel, and the learning is judged by the ears of the older people, and the newcomer is either admitted to the work or asked to leave, and the asking is done with regret but with firmness. Newcomers who stay become, in time, indistinguishable from the people who were born there, except for the small things — the way they hold their shoulders, the way they walk down the stairs, the way they speak a little too quickly when they are excited. The children of the newcomers are not newcomers, and the grandchildren of the newcomers are very Below, and the generation after that is stone.

**Their bad days** are not uncommon. A chamber collapses sometimes, and the people in it are pulled out by their neighbors, and the chamber is shored up with wood and is carved again. A child dies, and the body is carried up the shaft, and the parents are quiet for a while and then are not. There are fevers, and there is the slow lung-sickness that comes from breathing the damp air of the deep chambers, and the people who get the lung-sickness are moved to the higher chambers and are given the driest work, and sometimes they recover and sometimes they do not. There are quarrels, and the quarrels are conducted in the low voices of the Below and are not often loud, and the resolution is usually informal and is mediated by whoever is nearby. There is not much violence. The Below is not a place where a person can easily run away, and the people who live there have learned, over generations, that the cost of a fight is not paid only by the fighters.

**Their good days** are not called good days. They are called *the days when the stone was good*, or *the day the bread came out right*, or *the day the child said the first word*, and the good days are not celebrated so much as noticed, and the noticing is done by the people nearby and is not announced. The Below is a place where the small things are the large things, because the large things are too large to be visible in a single life, and a chamber that takes a generation to carve is not a project but a context, and the context is the place itself, and the people in it are the place made of flesh.

**Their names** are short, and are often the names of the chamber the person was born in, and the chamber is the name of the stone in that chamber, and the stone is named for the texture or the color or the seam, and a Below person's name is a kind of genealogy of place, and the people who share a name are the people who share a piece of the city, and the sharing is not a metaphor.

**Their music** is rare, and when it happens it is in the deep chambers when the surface is quiet, and it is a single voice or a small drum or a reed-pipe, and the music does not fill the chamber so much as inhabit it, and the people who hear it are still in the way that people in the Below are still, and the music is over and there is the tick of the water again, and the tick is the music's accompaniment and the music is the tick's variation, and the two are not separable in the Below.

**Their gods**, if they have them, are not spoken of in a way that a visitor would recognize as religion. There is a Below word for the spirit of a place, and the spirit of the place is the place itself, and the people of the Below do not pray to the spirit so much as attend to it, and the attending is the listening, and the listening is the work, and the work is the city, and the city is the people, and the people are the stone, and the stone is the place, and the circle is closed.

**In sum**: the people of the Below are a particular kind of human, shaped by the place they have made inside the place they have found, and the shaping is not complete because the shaping is never complete, and the people of the Below are still becoming, the way the city is still becoming, and the working face is still being opened, and the chisels are still ringing, and the lamps are still being lit, and the shafts are still bright in the day and dark in the night, and the people are still in the city, and the city is still in the stone, and the stone is still in the hill, and the hill is still in the world, and the world is still going on above, and the world does not know about the Below, and the Below does not entirely care.

It is enough.
