What lives on this plateau is not one ecosystem but a stack of them, each layer pressed against the next, and the city — with its terraces and slag and rails — is the strange shelf they have all agreed to share. Begin at the bottom, because that is where the rest of it hangs from.

The stone itself is alive, or close enough that the distinction no longer matters. The violet crust that creeps along the rail-yards and under the eaves of the cooling sheds is the foundation of everything else: a film of biology so flattened against the basalt that it is easier to think of it as a discoloration than as an organism. It eats the rock. It drinks the air. It is patient in a way that the animals above it are not, and in the few favored places — the shaded sides of slag heaps, the joints between old cobbles — it thickens into the soft fruiting bodies the workers call breathing-stones. These are not stones. They are something like a lung made of mineral tissue, slow to inflate, slower to release. When a foot comes down beside them, or when the day's heat peaks and the air pressure shifts, they exhale a fine spore that drifts upward and becomes, for a few minutes, part of the haze in which the whole city is suspended. They are the lungs of the place. They are also, quietly, the grain fields.

Above the crust live the grazers. The small ones are leg-counts that no one bothers to remember precisely — six, eight, ten, the workers argue about it over their tea — flat-bodied and armored in pale mineral plates that they seem to grow directly out of the dust they eat. They are the size of rats, or a child's spread hand, and they work the violet crust in careful strips the way sheep work a pasture, moving in loose herds along the bases of the walls and through the older streets. They make a sound like a whispered bristle. They are the most numerous large things in the city, and they are the food of everything else.

The grazers are preyed on by the armored forms that move through the alleys at a careful, jointed pace: dog-sized, low to the ground, plated in dark mineral that has the sheen of wet iron. They do not chase. They wait, the way a trap does, in the lee of the larger slag heaps, and when a grazer blunders into the seam between two plates the strike is over before the air has time to carry the sound. Their droppings, which are rich in undigested crust and half-mineralized tissue, become the next year's pasture. They are the wolves, or the foxes, or perhaps the foxes of foxes. They do not have names. They are referred to by posture.

Between the cobbles, in the wet seams where the condensation channels run, there is another world. Flatworms, ribbon-thin and slow, ride the runoff toward the rim. Larvae — pale, soft, segmented — hang in the slow water and pulse with it. Sessile feeders press themselves to the underside of every basalt block and extend, when the day warms, a wet tongue of tissue that tastes the passing flow. The tube-worms — everyone still calls them worms — are the colonizers of the mortar. They send root-like holdfasts into the lime between the blocks and pulse with the day's warmth, and they are the reason the older streets lift and crack and slowly become ditches. They are slow geology made of flesh. A street that was paved two generations ago is, in their hands, already halfway back to rubble.

And then, above the stone, the air.

The pinhead gnats are a weather more than an animal. They hang in dense slow columns over the warm outflow vents, so thick in places that the eye registers them as a faint thickening of the light, and they rise from every surface where the breathing-stones have just released their spore. They feed on the spore, or on each other, or on both at once; the question is not settled and the city has stopped asking. Their sound is the highest layer of the constant hush — a thin, dry, glassy note, almost a frequency rather than a noise.

The sails ride above them. A hand-span across, flat, triangular, the color of wet slate. They are membrane and cartilage and some sort of internal gas-bladder that lets them hang in the thermals pouring off the heated slag without beating their wings more than once a minute. They drift in lazy circling tiers, dozens of them visible at any moment, hundreds at the dusk-hour when the thermals peak. They eat the spore-puffs. They also eat, more cannibally than the gnats, the shed skins of their own dead, which come loose in the heat and drift down through the columns like grey snow. The sailors on the inland sea, who see them from below over open water, call them sails. Here, where they are overhead and constant, they are called the ceiling. Children are taught their flight-paths before they are taught to read.

Above the sails, rarely, you can see a deeper shape. The ketches — because that is what the sailors call them, and the name has stuck for want of a better one — move through the higher haze at the pace of weather. Small-room-sized. Slow-flapping. They have no agreed-upon anatomy because no one has ever seen one clearly. They are felt first: a shadow, a drop in the hush, and then the deep resonant moo that arrives not through the ears but through the ribcage, the way distant thunder does. They feed on the sails, or on each other, or on nothing that anyone has been able to prove. They drift above the city the way seabirds drift above a fishery, and their droppings — when droppings have been found, white and mineral-pale, on the upper terraces — are one of the reasons the violet crust is so thick on the older roofs. They are the upper sky's only punctuation.

So the city lives inside a sandwich. Below it, the eaters of stone. Above it, the eaters of eaters. And the workers — the people in the stained coats, the children who know the fauna's names before they know their own — move through the middle of it, unhurried, certain that the layers will part around them. They whistle. They shout. They walk on stone that is, at any given moment, being slowly chewed by something that is not quite a plant and not quite an animal and not quite a fungus, and they have made their peace with this in the way that one makes peace with weather: by knowing its moods, by harvesting its water, by laying the dead on the warm stone in the dark so that the breathing-stones will be thicker in the morning.

The plateau's living things do not name themselves. They are named by the people who walk among them, and the names are tender and practical at once — breathing-stones, sails, ketches, tube-worms — and they tell you what each thing does in the system that holds the whole. The system is old. It has been running, with adjustments, longer than any of the cities built on top of it. The cities come and go. The fauna remains, patient, eating the stone and the spore and each other, drinking the hanging mist, slowly, very slowly, taking the masonry back into the hill it came from.
