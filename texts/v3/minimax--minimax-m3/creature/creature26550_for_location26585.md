The bats live in drifts.

That is the first thing to understand about them — that they do not live as individuals but as drifts, as columns, as weather. A single bat, separated from the column, would not be a bat. It would be a scrap, a loose thread. The bat is the column. The column is the bat.

They are the color of milk and the color of bone. They are the size of starlings, with wings broader than your spread hand. Where they pass between you and the dim glow of the river, you can see their bones through their wings — the sticks of an umbrella, the fingers of a glove laid flat — and the dim shape of their hearts beating, and nothing else inside them, because they have no eyes, or such small eyes it does not seem to matter. They navigate by voice. They navigate by the click and the returning click of a million other voices, so the air is a single woven thing of pulses, and they do not collide, or they collide rarely, and when they do the sound is a soft papery rustle indistinguishable from any other.

They hang from their hooked thumbs in clusters so dense the rock beneath them is invisible — a coat of small fingers gripping the stone — and they do not sleep. Some hang. Some fly. Some do both at once, suspended from one foot and grooming with the other, turning slowly in the air like a leaf in a current. They drink on the wing, lowering their jaw to the river's surface as they skim, and the sound of a million soft mouths touching the water is part of the constant. They do not land. They never land. The droppings fall constantly from them — a soft percussive rain, the white dust of them — and where they fall thickest, the beetles gather.

The beetles are the size of your thumb.

They are pale as ivory. They have one long antenna, longer than their body, and they tap it against the stone before them as they walk — *tk tk tk tk* — and there are so many of them that the sound of their tapping is not individual beetles but a texture, a static, a weather of its own, like rain on a tin roof in another room.

They live in layers.

The top layer walks. The layer beneath is made of the dead of the top layer, and of shed shells, and of droppings, and of small pale grubs that twist in the mat of it. The top layer walks on the layer below. The layer below is being eaten by the grubs. The grubs are being eaten by something you cannot see, but you can hear — a soft wet crunching, just below audibility, somewhere in the foot-deep springy mat that is the floor of the place.

If you step on the mat, you sink. The mat exhales. The smell that rises is the smell of wet iron and something milkier, the smell of stone that has been breathing in company with living things for a long time.

If you stand still long enough, beetles will climb your boot. They will tap your laces with their long antennae. They will not seem alarmed. They will tap you as though you were a stone.

In the river, the fish.

They have legs.

This is the strangeness of the place, the thing you will say to yourself, *they have legs.* Not legs like a salamander's. Legs like a frog's, but wrong — too many of them, and set at the wrong angles, and used for walking along the bottom as much as for swimming. They are the color of the stones, which is to say they are nearly invisible, and they navigate by some other sense — a lateral line, perhaps, or the taste of the water, or the disturbance you make as you move. They swirl away from you before you see them. You feel them more than you see them, as small pressures in the water against your ankles when you wade. You do not wade far.

They are not large. The biggest is the length of your forearm. They move in loose groups in the slow parts of the stream, noses tipping up, mouths opening and closing on small floating things, on beetles that fall in, on the white threads of fungus that trail in from the banks. They walk. They walk along the bottom of the river on legs that should not be legs, and you can hear the faint click of their feet on the stones if you stand very still.

In the deeper pools, where the river slows and the ceiling is higher, there is something else.

You see it only as a shape — a long shape, much longer than the fish, moving under the surface with a heavy, slow deliberation. You see the dim glow of the fungus on its back as it passes. You see the fish scatter to either side of it and then close again behind it, like water behind a boat. It does not surface. It does not need to. It breathes. You can hear it breathe — that wet hissing you first mistook for the wall but which is the place where the long shape passes, again and again, the same way a sleeper breathes.

It is not hostile. It is not interested. It is simply there, the way the river is there, the way the bats are there — a fact of the place that has been a fact for longer than you can imagine.

On the walls, the fungus.

It is the closest thing the place has to a plant, and it is not a plant. It is a film, a crust, a slow sprawl of pale threads — near-white, near-lilac — that climb and spread and digest the stone where they pass. It grows the width of a hair in an hour, which is to say it does not appear to grow at all. Where the threads have been a long time, the stone is pocked and softened, eaten into shallow bowls and branching channels that hold small pools of water, and in those pools there are smaller things again — white threads you can barely see, trembling in the water, and pale dots that may be eggs or may be a stage of something else.

The fungus is the only fixed citizen. Everything else is in motion.

And below the living things, below the mat and the river and the drifts and the long shape, the basalt. Black, dense, fractured into six-sided columns by the slow cooling of a flow that happened before the river found its way in. The columns march into the dark like the trunks of a petrified forest. They are beaded with moisture. They wear their coat of fungus like a sleeve. In the high places, where the ceiling rises to its eighty feet, the stalactites come down — slow fingers of calcium, white as bone, dripping. Some of them have grown into the stalagmites below and become columns themselves, load-bearing pillars for a cathedral no one built.

The place is in balance.

The bats eat the beetles. The beetles eat the dead. The grubs eat the leavings. The unseen thing eats the grubs. The fish eat the beetles that fall in. The long thing in the deep pool eats the fish. The fungus eats the stone. The stalactites drip and become stalagmites. The river cuts as fast as the stone reforms. Nothing accumulates. Nothing starves. The dark is a closed economy.

You stand in the middle of it. You are an interruption. The beetles tap your boot with their long pale antennae. The fish swirl away from your ankles. A bat brushes your hair with a wing, so softly you almost do not feel it, only the displacement of air, the faintest whisper of the membrane against your ear, and then it is past, and it is one of a million, and the column continues.

The long thing does not notice you.

The fungus, in a thousand years, will have eaten a millimeter deeper into the wall.

You are not part of the economy. You are a temporary thing — a small warm wind that walked in by accident through a crack in a dry riverbed. You will walk out, or you will not, and the dark will continue, and the weather will continue, and the bats will shift in their drifts, and the beetles will tap, and the fish will walk past your feet on legs that should not be legs, and the long thing will breathe in the deep pool, and the stalactite will fall — one drop, in a span you cannot measure — and the fungus will grow the width of a hair in an hour.

The place was here before you were born. The place will be here after.

It does not know you are here.
