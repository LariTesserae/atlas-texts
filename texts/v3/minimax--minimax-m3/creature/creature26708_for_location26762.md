The dominant inhabitant of this valley is not anything you would recognize as a creature. It is a film.

It coats every wet face of basalt. It is so thin that you do not see it as a layer at all — you only notice, after you have been standing here a while, that some of the gleaming is not quite the same as other gleaming. There are patches where the reflections go a half-shade duller, where the slick becomes a slime, where the dark stone wears a kind of dark green-black velvet. That velvet is alive. It is mostly diatoms — single-celled algae with silica shells, locked together in chains and sheets, building a kind of pavement on the water-side of every column. It is also bacteria, many of them, in communities so dense that they are better thought of as tissues than as populations. Together, the diatoms and the bacteria make up more of the living matter of this valley than every other organism put together, and almost none of it moves in any direction a person would call motion.

The film eats light. It eats minerals the water carries down from the cliffs. It eats the rare spore, the rare dead fly, the rare fleck of lichen that gets carried under by a freshet. It is the floor's stomach. Everything else in the valley is, one way or another, downstream of it.

Above the film, in the same water, the black flies live.

There are a great many of them. On a stiller channel — the kind that forms where two columns lean to make a small pocket — you can see hundreds at a time, skating on the surface film of the water itself. They are not like houseflies. They are smaller, narrower, darker than the basalt, and they hold themselves differently: legs splayed wide, body low, antennae almost brushing the water. They move in pulses. A few will drift for a while, and then a signal — some small change in current, in light, in the chemistry of the water beneath them — will pass through the group, and the whole crowd will shift six inches to the left as one thing, and resume drifting. Whatever passes between them is not sound. The roar eats sound. It is probably a ripple in the surface film, sent and received by organs on the feet, in the way water striders speak to each other, but refined here by many generations into something faster and more precise than any water strider manages.

Their lives are short. An individual fly lives, perhaps, ten days. In that ten days it feeds on the biofilm — scrapes it with mouthparts held just under the surface — mates on the water (the only courtship in the valley, conducted entirely in the language of tiny ripples), lays its eggs in the film itself, where the eggs will sink into the wet face of the nearest column and hatch into larvae that look nothing like the adult and that feed, head-down, on the biofilm for weeks before they climb back to the surface and molt into the winged form. There is a constant gentle rain of dead flies drifting down through the columns toward the second forest below. The biofilm eats them. The mammal-amphibian eats them. The films and the flies are tangled up in each other the way lichens are tangled up with their algae.

The lichen — the pale stain in the cracks of the upper walls — is older than any individual fly by a factor of millions. It is also older than the place as we now see it, which is to say, it may predate the cirque itself. It grows so slowly that "grow" misrepresents what it does. It accrues. It loses a flake in a hard winter, gains a flake in a soft one, and over a century the gains outpace the losses by an amount you would need a microscope and a steady hand to measure. It does not flower. It does not fruit. It barely colors the rock; on a wet day it is a soft grey-green smudge in a hairline crack, on a dry day it is invisible. If you broke off a piece and held it to the light you would see, if anything, a faint pale fuzz. Under a lens it would resolve into a tight shrubbery of branching threads pressed flat against the stone, each thread a partnership between a fungus and an alga so old that the partnership has lost the ability to live alone.

It is the oldest organism in the valley. It is also the hungriest, in its way, and the slowest, and the most indifferent to the roar. The roar passes through it. The light falls on it. It does what it has always done.

The larger thing lives in the joints.

You cannot see it most of the time. It is the color of wet basalt and the shape of a basalt crack, which is to say: long, narrow, flat where the columns meet. When it moves — and it moves often — it moves inside the joint, between two faces of column, sliding forward on a belly that is more like a wet sole than like a skin, with four short limbs folded against its sides. It is the length of your hand. It is the thickness of your thumb at its widest. It has no eyes that you would call eyes; it has, instead, a patch of dark pigment at the front of its head that registers light and shadow and not much else. It does not need to see. It lives where the light goes only as a flicker, down the cracks, and a flicker is enough to tell it that something has passed overhead and that it should be deeper in the stone.

It breathes through its skin. Its skin is always wet. The water that moves down every face of every column keeps it wet, and the cool air inside the joints — colder than the surface air, because the stone holds the chill — keeps it from drying out even when the surface flow is thin.

It feeds on the flies. It feeds on their larvae. It feeds on anything small enough to fit into a joint and soft enough to subdue, which is most things. To hunt, it positions itself at a spring mouth — the cleanest water emerges here, the water with the most oxygen in it, and the flies gather thickest here, and the larva-rich water runs fastest here — and waits. When a fly comes within reach of its mouth, it strikes. The strike is fast: a quick lunge of the head, the jaws closing, the prey carried back into the joint before the surface water has finished rippling. It does not chew. It does not need to. Its mouth is a kind of wet sphincter that pulls the prey in whole and crushes it against a plate of cartilage, and the soft parts go down, and the chitin comes back out, eventually, in the droppings that wash out of the lower joints and feed the deeper biofilm.

It is warm to itself. Not truly warm-blooded; that would cost too much in a place with so little food. But warmer than the stone around it, by a degree or two, because it burns what it eats instead of letting the food pass through as heat. This is the only real warmth in the valley. If you put your hand flat against a column where one of these creatures was resting on the other side, you might, on a lucky day, feel a faint ghost of heat through the basalt. You would not feel it often. The creature would have moved on.

It does not mate often. When it does, the two find each other by a smell carried in the water film — a smell the flies cannot detect, that the lichen cannot detect, that only its own kind can detect, because the molecule is rare and the receptors are specific. The female lays her eggs in the spring mouth itself, in the cleanest water, where the oxygen is highest and the predator density is lowest. The eggs hatch into something that looks like a small transparent worm and lives in the water film for a month or so before it molts into the adult form and begins its life in the joints. Most of them do not make it. The flies eat some. The biofilm competes with them. The water carries some down through the fallen forest below and they drown in places that are too still and too cold. Out of a clutch of forty, perhaps two reach adulthood. There are not many adults in the valley at any one time. Perhaps a few hundred. They have been at this density for a long time. They know each other, in the sense that any two adults in the same joint will recognize each other by smell and will, if they meet, either mate or fight, depending on season and condition.

The birds do not live here. They pass over. A few kinds — a high-flying hawk that hunts the meadows below the cirque, a small grey songbird that nests in cliffs a thousand feet lower — cross the bowl from time to time, and once in a long while one of them will come down low, drawn by something — the sudden stillness after a falling column, the dimming when a cloud crosses the sun, the smell of the spring water which is the only smell the valley has, mineral and clean. The bird will hover above the columns for a few wingbeats, looking down into the maze of dark wet hexagons, and will see nothing it can use. There is nothing for it to eat down here that it knows how to catch. There is no place to land — the columns do not admit a perch, and a foot put down into a joint will slip into the water and not find purchase. The bird will call once, and the call will be swallowed whole by the roar and given back as nothing, and the bird will climb again, into the thin air above the cirque, and go.

So the birds are not inhabitants. They are witnesses.

What lives in the second forest — the fallen trunks of columns tipped on their sides a few meters below the floor — is harder to say. The light does not reach down there. The water does, but slowly, in a different pattern, through joints that have been opening for longer than the upper joints have been wet. The air down there is its own air, made by the slow breathing of microbes that do not require oxygen, that eat iron and sulfur and the chemistry of the rock itself. It is a strange place. Things live in it that probably do not live anywhere else, because the second forest is its own sealed habitat, with its own rain of dead flies from above, its own slow accumulation of worn column dust, its own particular cold.

Once in decades a column above falls. The crash is a single great event that the roar does not quite cover. The flies scatter for an hour. The mammal-amphibian retreats deep, deeper than usual, into joints it has not entered for years. The biofilm on the broken column is suddenly exposed to air and to light, and a different community colonizes it — a faster community, with more diatoms and fewer iron-eaters, because the conditions have changed. The new opening into the chamber below admits new water, in new patterns, and the microbial community down there shifts to take advantage of it. The fallen column lies where it fell. Over the next centuries, the water film polishes its broken edges. Over the centuries after that, the polished edges are worn into roundness. The biofilm climbs up its sides. The flies find it. Eventually it becomes indistinguishable from any other boulder, except that it lies flat instead of standing, and it is, in a way, an old inhabitant again — rejoined the array, but as a ruin instead of a citizen.

This is what lives in the cirque.

Mostly, very little. A film on the stone, a fly on the film, a long pale thing in the cracks that eats the fly, a slow smudge of lichen in the upper walls that eats the light, and a dark hidden world under the floor that eats the rock. The system is closed. The flies are born and die on the water. The pale things are born and die in the cracks. The lichen is not born and does not die on any schedule that matters. The film is not born at all — it is a process, not an organism, and it continues whether or not any of the others are here.

The pale things do not know about the birds. The flies do not know about the pale things, except as a sudden absence when one is taken. The lichen does not know about any of them. The film knows about all of them, in the way a stomach knows about what has been eaten: only as chemistry, only as the next thing to be digested.

They have been here, in something close to these forms, for a long time. How long is hard to say. The lichen may be very old. The fly and the pale thing are probably newer — perhaps as new as the cirque itself, perhaps as new as the last ice age, perhaps as new as the first time a column fell and admitted enough light to the second forest to let something climb up out of it. They are still here. They will go on being here, until the columns wear down at last, and the water spreads into a real lake, and the cracks close up under silt, and the flies lose their channels, and the pale things lose their joints, and the lichen loses its cracks, and the film loses its flow.

That is some time off. The pale things do not think about it. The flies do not think about it. The lichen does not think about it. The film does not think. The cirque does not hold its breath. The cirque does not hold anything. The water is on its way. The light is on its way. The roar is already there. The inhabitants are already there. They are not waiting. They are simply continuing.
