The shrimp-like things come first, because they are the most numerous and the least particular. They drift in loose, shifting curtains from chamber to chamber, their pale segmented bodies just visible against the darker stone the way a hand of cards is visible against a green table — distinguishable only because there is so many of them, and the edges of their group are soft, and you cannot say where one ends and the next begins. Up close — closer than the water lets you get without disturbing them — a single one is a small, careful piece of engineering: a curved carapace the color of old cream, a fan of fine legs that beat in continuous rippling waves, antennae longer than the body trailing behind like the tail of a kite. The legs do not so much propel the animal as hang it in place, and what the animal is doing, mostly, is feeding. The water here is warm and carries a fine broth of organic matter — shed skins, dissolved minerals, the slow exhalations of every other living thing in the place — and the shrimps walk through this broth with their fine combs out and let it pass across the combs. They are sieves that move. They convert the indistinct murk of the place into small pellets of mud that drift down and settle on every horizontal surface, thickening the floor by an imperceptible fraction each year, smoothing the carved channels, leveling the cisterns from the bottom up. They have done this for so long that the floors are no longer quite the floors the carvers made. They are the floors the carvers made plus several lifetimes of shrimp. The shrimps themselves do not seem to know this. They feed. They molt. They drift. When a lamp, lowered from above on a long line, suddenly illuminates a column of them, they do not scatter — they simply continue feeding, and the light catches the pale commas of their eyes (small, dark, present but vestigial, more a memory of an eye than an eye), and the antennae wave a little faster, and then the lamp is hauled up again and they are back in their brown dusk. Generations have passed beneath lamps without learning what lamps are.

The eyeless fish move differently. They are larger — hand-length, some of them, and a few longer — and they have weight and intent. They travel in loose columns through the doorways and along the corridors, and when they come to a junction they do not hesitate in the way the shrimps do not hesitate, but for opposite reasons: the shrimps cannot deliberate, and the fish have nothing to deliberate about. The current tells them where to go. They face into it, or with it, and they let the architecture of the place decide the rest. Up close a fish is a soft, deliberate thing, white-grey, almost the color of the mineral crust it lives among. Its head is blunt and patient. Where eyes would be there is only smooth skin, and the skin there is a little thinner, a little more translucent, and if you brought a lamp down you could see the faint dark loops of vessels beneath it — the remnants of an apparatus that has not been used in so many generations that the apparatus has become a kind of internal scar. The fish finds its way by the currents in its skin, by the taste of the water, by the faint pressure-changes that arrive when its snout nears a wall. It eats the shrimps. Not constantly, not with the single-minded appetite of a predator in a hungry place, but steadily, the way a cow eats grass — one mouthful and then another, the population of shrimps always slightly ahead of the population of fish, the equilibrium tilted but not toppling. The fish are more curious than the shrimps, or rather they are less indifferent. If you reach a hand into the water and hold it still, a fish will sometimes come and nose along your fingers with a small, dry pressure, tasting the salts of you, deciding you are not food, moving on. They do not fear. There is nothing here that has had reason to develop fear in a long time, and the fish have lived here so long that fear would have to be reinvented from scratch, which is not how evolution works. They are calm. They are the calmest large animals you will ever meet. They are calm in the way a piece of furniture is calm.

In the cisterns there are worms. The cisterns are deep, narrow stone basins, some of them still watertight after a fashion, some of them breached and connected to the general flow. The ones that are still closed have a different water in them — older, stiller, with a faint sulfurous thread of chemistry that the open chambers do not have. Into this quieter water the worms have built their crowns. A worm at rest is a pale tube, flesh-colored, barely visible against the pale stone of the cistern wall, and it sits in a hole it has excavated over years and it extends, very slowly, a frilled crown of red gills into the water. The crown opens like a small, soft hand. Water passes through the hand. The worm takes what it needs and withdraws the hand, and the hole is just a hole again, and the water is still. If you watch one for a long time — and you can, because nothing else will disturb you while you watch — you can see the rhythm: an emergence, a waiting, a withdrawal. The rhythm is not fast. The rhythm is the rhythm of a creature that lives in a place where the seasons are measured in crust-growth and the news is the occasional collapse of a far wall. The worm does not know there is a place above it. The worm does not know there is anything above it at all. The worm has been in its hole for longer than the hole has been a hole, and will be in its hole for longer than you will be anywhere.

The animals with hands are the ones you watch longest. They are not large — a big one would span a man's palm — and they are not beautiful in any conventional sense, but they are very busy, and they are the closest thing the place has to an heir. They have a carapace, segmented, dull brown, that they wear the way an old carpenter wears his apron: as a record of what they have been doing. On the front of the carapace there is a pair of appendages that have become, over the long patient history of their kind, hands. Not human hands — too many fingers, the fingers arranged in two combs rather than one, the joints bending in directions that would not be comfortable for a thumb — but hands in the sense that they pick things up, and they set things down, and they decide where things go. The animals walk the floors and walls and ceilings of the place on legs that grip stone as well as a human foot grips a ladder rung, and what they pick up is mostly mineral crust. They scrape it. They carry pieces of it in their hands to the edges of chambers and drop them into the current, and the current carries the pieces away, and the place is, by a fraction, more open than it was an hour ago. They also pick at one another, and at the smaller animals, with the unhurried attention of creatures who are not in a hurry and have learned that hurrying is not how things get done down here. Two of them will meet on a ledge, and there will be a long, careful negotiation of antennae and hands, and one will give the other a small piece of food it was carrying, and they will separate. The transaction takes forty minutes. Nothing the animals do takes less than forty minutes. They are slow in the way that glaciers are slow, which is to say they are not slow because they are weak but because the time-scale they live in is a different one from the time-scale you were built for. A wall falls. The animals cross the new doorway before the rubble has settled, but they cross it at their own pace, and their pace has not changed in ten thousand years.

There are other things, smaller and less central. There are things like snails, with shells that have become, in some cases, indistinguishable from the mineral crust — a snail at rest on a wall is hard to pick out from the wall itself, and the place has learned to use this, and so have the snails. There are flat things that cling to the underside of every overhang and breathe through their skins and let the current bring them what they need. There are larvae — of what, exactly, is not clear; of the fish, of the shrimp-like things, of the hand-things — that hang in the water column in pale drifting clouds and are eaten by everything larger than themselves and so do not come to rest until they are ready to become something else. The place is full of larvae the way a forest in late spring is full of seeds: a standing bet on the future, distributed everywhere, most of them never becoming what they were meant to become, but enough of them doing so to keep the system going.

None of these animals know that they live in rooms. They move through doorways because the current funnels them there, or because the food is slightly better there, or because that is where the burrow of their mother was and they have not had reason to leave. They do not know the difference between a wall and a floor and a ceiling; to them the place is a continuous three-dimensional habitat of stone and water and small edible things, and the fact that the stone was once carved is irrelevant to them except insofar as the carving created surfaces they can grip and channels they can shelter in. They are, in their own way, respectful of the place. They do not damage it. They polish it, slowly, with their traffic, and they scrape it, slowly, with their hands, and they let the crust grow back in the places they have cleaned, and they scrape it again. They are maintaining it. They do not know they are maintaining it. They are simply doing what their kind has always done, in this place, in the way that this place has always been done in.

A larger animal moves through the place occasionally, but rarely. There is a thing — soft, dark, longer than a man — that surfaces from deeper water once in a while and threads its way through the chambers with a slow, heavy deliberation, and the fish scatter before it, and the shrimps compress into denser curtains, and the hand-things retreat into their burrows and wait. The large animal eats. It eats a certain number of fish, a certain number of shrimp-things, a certain number of hand-things, and then it goes back down, and the smaller animals resume. The visit is a weather event. The visit is a typhoon passing through a village: significant, remembered (in the sense that bodies remember), but not the central fact of life. Life here is the central fact of life, and it goes on.

What you would notice, if you were lowered slowly into this place with a lamp and patience and enough air, is that the place is not empty of feeling. It is full of feeling, although the feeling is not for you and not of you and not in any idiom you share. The shrimps feel the current. The fish feel the pressure of water against their sides and the particular tug of a fellow-fish beside them in the column. The worms feel the chemistry of the cistern water and know when to open and when to close. The hand-things feel one another across a ledge, across a doorway, across a chamber, in a slow, attentive, persistent way that is not social in any sense you would recognize but is not nothing either. The place is a society. It is a small, dense, ancient society of creatures who have lived together in these carved rooms for so long that the rooms have become part of them and they have become part of the rooms, and neither one can be understood without the other. The builders are gone. The builders have been gone for so long that the mineral crust has begun to bury their faces. But the village they built is still a village, and is still inhabited, and the inhabitants go about the long business of living in it with the unhurried attention of creatures who expect nothing to change and are mostly right.
