# Inhabitants

The valley is full, in its way. You might not think so at first — the eye registers the wet rocks and the dark pillars and the mist, and the mind files it all under *uninhabited*. But the mind is wrong. The valley is dense with life, layered and negotiated and ongoing, in the same way that the basalt is layered and the water is layered. You just have to slow down. You have to let the first impression pass and then look again, and again, until the second impression becomes a third and then a fourth and then a habit.

---

## The Voles, Who Have Made Their Peace

The voles are the first ones you meet, because the voles are the ones who met this place first.

They came up out of whatever ancestral vole-lineage gave rise to them — some long chain of small warm bodies threading through a thousand similar valleys — and they arrived here, and they stayed. They did not stay because the valley was hospitable. They stayed because the valley was *legible*. The basalt shifts, but it shifts slowly enough that a vole's run, built in a single night, will hold for weeks before it needs to be redrawn. The moss is wet, but it is also thick and deep and full of the worm-segments that the voles eat. The cold is real, but the voles are small and warm and they sleep in nests of grass that they pack tight against their own bellies, and the cold does not, in the end, get them.

A vole, when you stand near one, will look at you. This is the thing you notice first about the voles here. They do not flee on sight. They consider. They sit up on the small shelf of their run-entrance, their dark eyes fixed on you, and they *consider*. There is a calculation happening behind the small black beads of their eyes, the same calculation that has always happened, the same calculation that has always been correct: *is this thing going to eat me?* And here, almost always, the answer is *no*. There is nothing here that eats a vole except the fox, and the fox is not often. So the voles have lost their reflexive panic. They watch you. They wait for you to do something interesting. When you don't, they return to their foraging.

You begin to envy the voles.

They live at a scale that the valley has not yet noticed. The basalt plates shift under their runs but the runs are rebuilt within a day. The water climbs the pillars and the pillars reject it and the voles pay no attention, because the pillars are not their business. The dark shapes move in the deep water, but the voles do not go to the deep water. The voles live in the upper crust of the place — the moss, the tussocks, the seam-grasses — and that crust has been stable enough, for enough vole-generations, that the voles have begun to do something you do not see in other vole populations. They have begun to *look up*. Not at the sky. At the pillars. A vole will sit at the base of a pillar and watch it the way a person watches a river. There is no reason for this. There is nothing to see. But the voles watch, and you can see, in the angle of their small heads, that they are waiting for something they cannot name.

## The Dunlins, Who Have Not

The dunlins are the second inhabitants, and they have not made the peace the voles have made.

They come down out of the gray sky in loose flocks of fifteen or twenty, banking hard against whatever wind is currently in the wrong place, and they land on the wet plates and they feed. They feed on the worm-segments and on a small fly, dun-colored, that lives in the moss. They feed with the desperate efficiency of long-distance migrants who have stopped here on the way to somewhere else and are aware, in their small bird-brains, that they have stopped here on the way to somewhere else. The dunlins do not belong to this valley. They are passing through.

But they have been passing through for a long time. There are generations of dunlins now whose great-great-grandparents stopped on these plates, and the birds have not changed in any visible way — they are still dunlins, they still cry the dunlin cry, they still bank at the dunlin angle — but their *relationship* to the valley has changed. The dunlins no longer treat it as a waystation. They treat it as a destination. They arrive and they stay for weeks. They roost in the dwarf willows at the edges of the higher plates. They sleep in shifts, because the valley does not have a night, exactly, but it has a darker period, and the dunlins respect it.

You can hear them most clearly when the mist rolls in. Their cry — a low, hoarse *trreeeee*, repeated — comes through the fog in a way that suggests more birds than are present. Sometimes the cry seems to come from a direction that no bird is in. Sometimes the cry seems to come from *below*, from the deep water, in a register that no dunlin could produce. But this is the mist, doing what mist does to sound. The dunlins are only above. They are only birds. They are the most ordinary thing in this valley, and they are the only ordinary thing, and this is why you hear them so clearly — because the rest of the valley is, beneath them, doing something else entirely.

A dunlin in flight, over the marsh, looks like a piece of paper being folded. The wings beat fast and shallow, and the bird seems to be working against the air rather than with it. They are heavy for their wings. They are not graceful birds. They are competent birds. There is a difference. They get where they are going, but they do not pretend to enjoy the journey. They cry about it the whole way.

## The Fox, Who Is the Reader of the Place

There is one fox. There may be two. You only ever see one at a time.

The fox is reddish, as foxes are, but leaner than the foxes of warmer latitudes, with a longer muzzle and a thicker coat and a tail that looks too heavy for the body carrying it. It moves across the basalt plates with the slow attention of an animal that has learned to read the ground. It knows which plates will shift in the next ten minutes and which will hold. It knows where the water will climb and where it will lie flat. It knows where the moss is deep enough to hide a vole and where it is shallow enough that a chase will end in a clear line of sight. The fox has been here a long time. The fox may have been here longer than you.

It hunts voles, mostly. A vole in this valley is not fast, but it is observant, and a fox has to be more observant than the vole, which is a high bar. The fox hunts by sitting. It will sit on a plate for an hour, motionless, and watch the runs. It knows the runs. It has memorized them. When a vole emerges, the fox moves — once, fast, clean — and then it has a vole in its mouth, and then the vole is not a vole anymore, and the fox carries it off to wherever the fox lives, which you never find out, because the fox always goes the other way when you try to follow.

You see the fox watching you, sometimes. It does not consider you the way the voles consider you. It considers you the way the basalt considers you — as a thing that is there, that has not yet decided what it is, that may or may not be a threat. The fox has decided that you are not, yet, a threat. It has also decided, you can see it in its eyes, that you are *close to* being a threat, and that it would prefer to keep you in view. The fox and you are in a long negotiation. The negotiation will end, eventually, when one of you leaves, or when the fox decides. The fox has not decided.

## The Worm-Segments, Who Have No Name

There are things in the moss that are not quite worms.

They are segmented, and they move, and they are roughly worm-shaped, and they are pale, the color of raw dough, and they emerge when the plates shift and expose a damp seam. They are not earthworms — the earthworms have not made it here yet, or have not survived. They are something else. Something that has been here since before the basalt finished moving. Something that the dunlins eat, and that the voles eat, and that nothing else eats, because nothing else has found them yet, or because nothing else can fit into the seams they live in.

When you kneel and look, you can see them. They are small. They are patient. They move with the unhurried rhythm of an organism that does not have predators and does not have ambitions. They eat the moss. They eat the sediment that the water has deposited in the cracks. They eat, possibly, the basalt itself, very slowly, the way lichen eats rock — though this is speculation, and you have not seen them eat rock.

You can pick one up. It does not resist. It lies in your palm and moves, gently, and after a moment it begins to dry, and as it dries it darkens, and then it is still. You have killed it, by removing it from its seam. You put it back. It does not revive. This is the cost of curiosity here.

The voles and the dunlins both eat these things. The voles eat them in the seams, reaching in with their small mouths and pulling out segments. The dunlins eat them on the surface, picking them off the moss after a shift has exposed them. The two predators do not compete, exactly — they operate at different scales — but the relationship is close. A vole-run that the fox has destroyed, a seam that has been opened by a plate shift and then closed again, a dunlin that has not arrived yet — these are all small windows of opportunity, and the worm-segments use them, briefly, and then the surface closes over them and they are gone again.

You do not know what they are. You will never know what they are. They are named *invertebrate* and *segmented* and *worm-like* and these names are inadequate, and you accept this. The valley contains more unknowns than knowns, and these are among the unknowns.

## The Dark Shape, Which Has Not Yet Been Named Either

You saw it once. You saw it for four seconds.

It was long and dark and it was in the deep water, the water that sits in the larger pools, the water that has a different quality — colder, thicker, less given to climbing the pillars. It moved left to right. It had no visible head. It had no visible tail. The water closed behind it without a ripple. It was there and then it was not.

You will look for it again. You will stand on a high plate and watch the deep pools for an hour, for a day, and you will not see it. You will think you saw it — there, at the edge, that darker patch — and it will be a basalt shadow, or a thicker column of water, or a trick of the failing light. The dark shape does not perform. It is not here to be seen. It is here to be doing whatever it is doing, and seeing it is a privilege, and you have had the privilege once.

You begin to suspect it is not alone. You begin to suspect there are several, in the deep water, moving along routes that the basalt does not cross. You begin to suspect that the dark shapes are what the pillars were made for, or that the dark shapes are what was here before the pillars, or that the dark shapes will outlast the pillars. You begin to suspect that the valley is, in some way you cannot quite articulate, *theirs*, and that the rest of you — the fox, the voles, the dunlins, you — are the visitors, and the dark shapes are the residents, and the residents have not yet noticed you.

You do not see the dark shapes again. This is also part of what it means to live here. To share a place with something you cannot reliably perceive, and to know that the perception, when it comes, is a gift, and not to expect another.

## The Pillars, Which Are the Oldest

The pillars are not alive. This is the obvious thing to say. They are dark metal rods, two meters tall, rising from the basalt at irregular intervals. They do not move. They do not eat. They do not reproduce. They are not, by any biological definition, alive.

But they are the oldest inhabitants. They have been here longer than the voles, longer than the dunlins, longer than the fox, longer than the dark shapes. The basalt itself is newer than they are. The basalt is still moving into the configuration the pillars were placed in, or out of it. The pillars are a fixed point in a place that has no fixed points. The pillars are what the rest of the valley measures itself against, even though the valley does not know it is measuring itself.

The water rejects them — or they reject the water, or they are made of a material that the water does not climb. The moss will not grow on them. The fox does not sleep at their bases. The voles do not run between them. The pillars are alone, in the way that only very old things are alone. They are not lonely. Loneliness requires the possibility of company. The pillars are past the possibility of company. They are the last remnant of something, and they do not remember what.

A pillar, if you put your hand on it, is cold in a different way from the basalt. The basalt is cold because the air is cold. The pillar is cold because it is cold. It has a chill of its own, deep, structural, the chill of something that has not been warm since before you arrived. Your hand will not warm it. Your hand will only be cooled by it, and then your hand will be taken away, and the pillar will still be cold, and the pillar will still be here.

The pillars do not have names. You can name one, if you want — you can say *this one is the one I am sitting beside* — but the name will not stick. The pillar is the same pillar whether you have named it or not. It does not require you. It will be here when you are gone.

---

## The Water, Which Is the Strangest

And then there is the water.

The water is alive, or it is doing something that is close enough to alive that the distinction does not matter. It climbs. It lifts. It gathers in lens-shapes above the rock and rotates briefly and descends. It moves against the local gravity, which is reduced here, and the reduction is not uniform — it varies from plate to plate, from pool to pool, from hour to hour. The water is responsive. The water is reading the valley, moment by moment, and adjusting itself to whatever it finds.

The voles drink the water. The dunlins drink the water. The fox drinks the water. The water lets them. It does not climb into their mouths, the way it climbs the pillars. It behaves, for the voles and dunlins and the fox, like ordinary water does. It is only when no one is watching that it begins to do the things it actually does.

You begin to suspect that the water is not the substance you thought it was. You begin to suspect that the water is a thin layer of something else — something colder, something older — that has dressed itself in the appearance of water because that is what it has always done. The mineral smell, the iron-and-peat smell, is not a smell *of* water. It is a smell of the thing the water is covering. You are drinking, when you drink, the surface of a much larger substance, and you are taking in only the smallest part of what is there.

The dark shapes live in the water. Or live *as* the water. The distinction fails, here, in the deep pools. The dark shapes do not displace the water because they may not be displacing anything. They may be *part* of the water, in the way that a thought is part of a mind — present, structuring, not separable. The dark shapes are what the water does when it has decided to be a thing for a while. The dark shapes are what the water does when it has decided to be a creature, briefly, before returning to being water.

You do not know if this is right. You cannot know if this is right. The water does not explain itself. The water does what it does, and the voles drink it, and the dark shapes move in it, and the pillars reject it, and you stand on a basalt plate and watch it climb, and you have no way to ask, and the water has no way to answer, and the negotiation continues, between you and the water and the rest of the inhabitants, in the slow and patient way that negotiations continue here, in the unfinished valley, in the cold and the gray and the never-quite-day.
