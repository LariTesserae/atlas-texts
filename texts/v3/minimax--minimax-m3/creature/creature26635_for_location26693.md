# The Inheritors

They are the regulars, and they are not many. Three is right. Four would be a crowd.

You will know them by how they move here. Above the surface they are impatient things — quick hands, quick eyes, the tight shoulders of people who have been counting something all their lives. Down in the works they shed it. Their strokes lengthen. They begin to breathe with the pump instead of against it, which is the first sign, the only reliable sign, that someone has started to belong.

**The woman with the satchel** comes most often. She has been coming longest, though she will not say how long, and the other regulars defer to her the way you defer to a quiet relative who has outlived everyone. Her hair, once some definite color, has gone the indeterminate rust-brown of things steeped too long in mineral water. She has a satchel of oilskin that she brings down full and brings up empty, though she will not say what is in it either. She moves through the avenue like someone crossing her own kitchen in the dark: her hand on the wall, her feet knowing where the moss is slick and where it is grippy, a long familiarity with the loading platform where the banyan-fig leans its crown against the surface. She is, the others suspect, mapping something. She has a small book — waterproof, dog-eared — and she is sometimes seen turning its pages, and sometimes seen simply holding it closed against her chest, the way you hold a thing you do not intend to read again but cannot bring yourself to set down.

**The younger one** — and they are younger, though not by as much as they would like — is a careful, watchful creature with the build of someone who has spent a long time in narrow places. They came here first on a dare, or a bet, or a recommendation from someone who did not explain what they were recommending, and they came up converted in the way that converts are converted: totally, and with a need to describe. They talk too much, the first few visits. They point at the boilers. They ask about the pump. They want to know the name of every fish, every fern, every pattern of mineral on the wall. After a while this passes. After a while they come down and do not speak at all, and only nod when the woman with the satchel points to a thing she wants them to see. They are learning, you understand, that some places are not described. They are entered, and then you carry them quietly inside you for a while, and then you come back to enter them again.

**The third** is rarer. The third is, in a way, the most devoted, because they are the one who has trouble with it. They come down tense. They come down still counting whatever they count up above. They swim the avenue and do not feel the tunnel of it close over them. They look at the banyan-figs and see only trees. After an hour, sometimes two, something in them unlocks — you can see it, the way you can see a held breath finally let go — and then they are quiet, and then they are in it, and then they are the ones who do not want to surface. They are the ones you have to watch, when the hour grows late, because the works will let you stay forever if you let it, and forever is not a thing that can be agreed to in advance.

There are others, of course. There are always others. A pair who come to take photographs that never come out, the flash bouncing back off the particulate in the columns of light, the subjects too green and too near to be captured. A diver from the city council's survey teams, who comes with a clipboard and tries to inventory the pipes and leaves after three visits with a small fixed smile and does not return. A grief group, once, who were guided here by a counselor who had read about it, and who came in a procession of eight and left the place feeling like a parking garage, which is to say they were too many to let the silence in.

The grief group is why the regulars do not bring friends.

The fish are not, properly, characters. They are the long pale knife-bodied things that drift through the loading bays at the level of your chest, and they are indifferent to you in the particular way that only small lives in large quiet places are indifferent. They know the tide. They know when the pump will lift the water and which chamber it will rise into. They have, each of them, a route — a circuit of the works, repeated without variation, that takes them past the boilers and through the weave-shed and out into the avenue and back again — and you will see the same fish in the same place at the same hour, if you are here often enough to notice. The regulars notice. The regulars nod to them.

There is a shrimp that lives in the crack above the third loading platform. It is, as far as anyone knows, a single shrimp, and it has been there for years. It is enormous, by the standards of its kind — the length of a man's hand, and old, the carapace gone the soft milky color of old bone. It does not move much. It cleans itself with small precise motions of its front legs, like someone washing carefully in a basin. The woman with the satchel has, on quiet visits, been seen to hover before it for a long time, as if in conference. The younger one has tried to name it and given up. The third has tried to touch it, once, and was surprised by how warm it was — warmer than the water, warmer than the wall — and has not tried again.

The pump is not alive. The regulars know this and say it to one another sometimes, in the small jokes of long acquaintance, when one of them is heard to refer to the pump's mood, or the pump's opinion. The pump is a pump. It is, by any honest accounting, the most ruined thing in the works — the most rusted, the most eroded, the most clearly a relic of the before-time. And yet it lifts the water. It has lifted the water, by the best guess of the survey teams, for longer than the regulars have been alive. It does so without complaint and without credit. It is the heart of the place. The regulars do not thank it, exactly, but they have a way of listening to it, on entering, the way you listen for the furnace when you come home to a cold house, and of relaxing only when they have heard it. If the pump were ever to stop, the regulars would feel it before they heard it, in some part of themselves that has come, without their noticing, to be tuned to its slow beat. This is a thing none of them has said aloud, and all of them know.

The flatworms are not characters either, but they are something, and the regulars have a way of greeting them. They are the wide pale things, soft as wet paper, that slide across the floors of the corridors in their slow inchworm way, and they are the closest thing the works has to an opinion about you. If a flatworm crosses your path, the regulars say — and they say it without laughing, or without quite meaning to be serious — you are welcome to come back. If a flatworm shrinks from your shadow, you are not. It is, of course, a superstition, and the regulars know it is a superstition, and the regulars pay attention to it anyway, because this is a place where superstitions feel less like foolishness and more like honest instruments. The works is small enough to know you. The works is small enough to have preferences.

What lives here, in the end, is a small congregation of people who have decided, without quite deciding, that they are responsible for a place. Not the place. *A* place. The six blocks, the seven, the avenue, the boilers, the pump, the single old shrimp on its loading platform, the long pale fish on their circuits. The regulars do not maintain the works. They do not clear a path. They do not plant or prune or repair. They come, and they are quiet, and they go, and they come back. This is, they have come to feel, enough. A place that is visited patiently by a few people who love it is not the same place as one that is not, and the difference is real even if it cannot be photographed, even if it cannot be entered in a survey, even if the only evidence of it is the small fact that certain divers, on certain afternoons, find the silence waiting for them when they arrive, as if it had been kept.

The grief group did not understand this, and so the grief group felt nothing, and so the grief group left, and this is why the regulars are careful about who they bring.

Above the surface, the world continues. It counts. It asks. It moves at the speed of the world, which is the speed at which things decay. Down here, in the warm water, in the held breath, in the green tunnel of the avenue with the banyan-figs testing the ceiling with their soft crowns, nothing is asked. The pump lifts. The fish pass. The shrimp cleans itself. The woman with the satchel turns a page she has read before. The younger one learns, slowly, to be still. The third unlocks, and is in it, and does not want to surface, and is gently, firmly, brought back up into the light by the others, who understand that the works will let you stay forever if you let it, and that this is a promise and not an offer, and that the people who love a place are also the people who bring each other home from it.
