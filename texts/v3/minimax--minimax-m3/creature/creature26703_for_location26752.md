The inhabitants of the basin are mostly miners.

There are perhaps four hundred of them on a working day, fewer on the days the cable is being inspected because half of them are up on the rim waiting their turn in the inspection queue. They live in barracks along the third and fifth benches, in clusters of buildings that were originally built for two hundred and have been partitioned and subdivided and extended out onto the next bench down whenever the bench above got too crowded. The partitions are made of ore-slates that do not weather well and have to be replaced every few years, and the ore-slates are cut from the benches themselves, which means that the walls of the barracks are made of the same green-black material that the workers are paid to dig out of the wall and ship up the cable. The workers do not remark on this. The walls smell of iron when they are new and smell of nothing when they are old.

The workers have families in some cases, in fewer cases than the company would like. The children are usually sent up the cable to the boarding school on the rim for as much of the year as the weather permits. The school is called something that begins with the name of the company and ends in *Academy*, and it is run by two teachers who came down for a season each and stayed for decades. The children return in the summers. They wear company boots that are too big for them, and they pick up loose ore from the spill piles and pocket it and trade it among themselves for things the teachers do not know about. The youngest ones are afraid of the tramway. The older ones, the ones who have been coming down since they could walk, are not. The cable operators wave at them when they come down. The children sometimes wave back.

The miners themselves are a varied lot in origin and a uniform lot in appearance. They come up the cable from many places and they go home to many places when their contracts are up, but the basin makes them look the same. The dust is the first thing. The dust of the crushers is fine and grey and it gets into the lungs and into the hair and into the weave of the work clothes and it does not come out. A miner who has worked the benches for ten years has a grey cast to his skin that will not wash off. The mineral water is the second thing. It stains the teeth and the fingernails and the hair at the temples and the corners of the eyes. A miner who has drunk the water for a decade has the beginning of a yellow rim at the edge of each iris that the company doctors have a name for and the older miners call *the basin-mask*, because it looks, when you see a row of them at the mess, like the same face repeated. The basin-mask is considered a sign of seriousness. A new hand without it is a new hand. A new hand with the beginning of it is a man who has decided to stay.

The cable operators are a different breed. They are chosen for steadiness of hand and steadiness of stomach and a particular indifference to height that the company tests for in the hiring hall on the rim. They work in pairs. They ride up and down for eight hours and they shout into each other's collarbones because the wind makes any other kind of conversation impossible, and they wave to the workers on the ledges and the workers do not wave back, and the operators wave anyway, every trip, because waving is part of the protocol, and the protocol is what holds the small human part of the operation together while the mechanical part runs itself. The operators do not drink the basin water. They bring their own in bottles. They do not eat the basin food. They eat what they brought up with them in their packs. They come down on shift in clean coveralls and they go up on shift in coveralls that are grey with crusher dust, and they leave the coveralls in a bin at the top of the lift and the coveralls go to a laundry on the rim that returns them, washed, in time for the next shift. The operators consider themselves clean men. The miners consider the operators clean men. There is no friction between them. They are two different kinds of inhabitant of the basin and they do not occupy the same parts of it.

The animals of the basin are three.

The fish are the least visible. They are pale and small and they live in the deep part of the lake where the light does not reach, and they come up only in the moments between the largest gusts, when the standing waves briefly flatten and the water is for a moment almost still. They dimple the surface with their mouths and then they are gone. A miner walking the lowest bench will see, sometimes, a dozen dimples at once across a stretch of water, and then the next gust will come down the wall and the dimples will vanish. The miners do not eat the fish. The fish taste of iron and lime and the same unnamed mineral the water tastes of. The miners leave them alone.

The birds are the most visible. They are long-limbed and pale, with a wing that seems too narrow for their body, and they ride the thermals that come up off the inner wall on the sunward side of the basin. They nest on the basalt shelves, in little scrapes in the rock that they line with their own feathers and that they defend against the foxes with a sharp clattering call that has become part of the basin's mechanical weather. The birds do not fear the tramway. They perch on the cable when it is still and they take off from it when it begins to move, and they sometimes ride the cable car itself, standing on the roof, for a stretch of three or four trips, before they remember that they have somewhere else to be.

The foxes are the most knowing. They are fox-sized but they are not exactly foxes. Their coats are thicker than a fox's coat should be, and their paws are wider, and their tails are shorter, and the older ones have a yellowish cast to the fur around the eyes that resembles, from a distance, the basin-mask of the older miners. They live among the ore benches, in burrows dug into the spoil of the abandoned cuts, and they come out at all hours but mostly in the wind-lulls when the noise of the crushers drops to a steady mutter. They scavenge the workers' middens. They eat the fish the miners leave on the drying racks. They eat, sometimes, the smaller birds. They have learned the rhythm of the cable cars and they cross the tramway tracks only between cars. The miners know them individually. The oldest of them, the matriarch of the eastern bench, has been there longer than any of the current miners and has outlived three of the cable operators and two of the teachers at the Academy. She has a torn ear and a limp from an old injury that she will not let heal. The miners feed her, sometimes, on the sly, because the matriarch is not a pet and is not a tame animal but is, in some way that nobody can quite name, a colleague.

There is also, in a sense, the ore itself. The ore does not move. The ore does not eat. The ore does not, in any way a biologist would recognize, live. But the ore is what the basin is for, and the ore has been what the basin is for longer than anything else in it has been anything, and the smelters on the rim have a name for the metal and the university people have a name for the intrusion and the miners have a name for the particular green-black bench they are cutting that day and the foxes have no name for it at all. The ore is patient. The ore will be there when the cable is cut and the buildings have fallen. The ore will outlast the basin-mask and the birds and the foxes and the dust. The ore is the oldest inhabitant of the basin that anyone is willing to name.

And finally there is the wind. The wind is not an inhabitant in any usual sense. The wind is the air of the basin moving, and it moves because the basin is a bowl in the side of a mountain and the air above the mountain is colder than the air in the bowl and the cold air falls and the warm air rises and the falling and the rising make the wind. The wind has been there since before the ore was exposed. The wind will be there when the ore is covered again. The cable hums in its voice. The standing waves march in its direction. The mist rises where it tells it to. The small pale flowers on the abandoned benches close when it passes. The cable operators shout into each other's collarbones because it will not let them do anything else. The wind does not care about any of this. The wind is patient. The wind is the form that the basin is always tending toward, and the basin is patient, and the basin is the form the wind is always tending toward, and somewhere between the two of them, in the small space that is left for living things, the miners and the operators and the fish and the birds and the foxes go about their work, and the work is what they are for, and the work will end, and the basin will still be a basin, and the wind will still blow.
