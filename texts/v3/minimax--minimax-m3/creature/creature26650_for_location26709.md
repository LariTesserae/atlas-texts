What lives here is mostly wet.

Start with the amphibians, because the amphibians are the basin. They are not the only thing in it — there are other lives, smaller and stranger and quieter — but the amphibians are the substance, the bulk, the moving weight of the place. Everything else is either eaten by them, eats them, or is held aloft on the damp breath of their abundance. A person who has spent any time in the basin comes away with the amphibians in the mind the way a person who has spent time on the open ocean comes away with the idea of wave: not as a thing encountered, but as a thing inhabited.

There are many kinds. The kinds are not, for the most part, the kinds a traveler from elsewhere would recognize. Some of them — the small ones, the ones that fit across a thumbnail, with their slim wet fingers gripping the rim of a pool — those look like the amphibians that exist anywhere. They are the entry-level form, the basic amphibian body in miniature, and they sit on every stone in every stream, and they tip their heads at you, and they are gone. But the larger ones are something else. The larger ones have been in this basin a long time, and the basin has been working on them the way the basin works on everything, which is slowly, patiently, and with no particular opinion about what they should become.

The dog-sized ones are the commonest of the larger forms. They have wide, low bodies, heavy in the haunches, and they sit in the shallows the way a heron sits in the shallows — motionless, looking at the water with the particular attention of a thing that does not have to think about what it is looking at, because it has been looking at water for a very long time. Their skins are the color of the silt they rest on, or the color of wet slate, or the color of liver. When you see one from a distance it is easy to mistake it for a hummock of mud until the eye resolves the shape and the small still eye at the top of the head. Up close they are uglier and more delicate than they first appear. The skin is not smooth. It hangs in soft folds at the throat and the flanks, and in the folds there are small pores that breathe, and on the small pores there are tiny things — mites, perhaps, or very small crustaceans — that live in the moisture the way barnacles live on a hull. The amphibians do not seem to mind. They are damp things. They accept the small lives that live on them the way a stone accepts lichen: as part of the surface, not as a problem.

They eat almost anything. They are the generalists of the basin, the ones that have not specialized, and because they have not specialized they can move between pools, between marshes, between the silt flats and the slow braids of the rivers, without being shut out of any particular kind of meal. A frog the size of a loaf of bread will eat a beetle, a snail, a smaller frog, a fish that has strayed into the shallows, a piece of carrion that has drifted against its flank. It does not hunt so much as it accepts. It sits, and things come to it, or it makes one slow movement with its wide mouth, and things are inside it. There is no chase in this basin. The chase is for the things that have to chase. The amphibians have the patience of the place, which is the patience of silt settling.

Above the dog-sized ones are the larger ones. These are the things the basin folk will mention, sometimes, in the long wet evenings by their small fires, in a tone that is not quite fear and not quite respect but something more like the acknowledgment you give to a thing that lives in the same house as you and does not care that you are there. The reports of them are inconsistent, which is itself a kind of consistency: it means no one has gotten a good look at one and come back to tell about it in detail. What is consistent is the disturbance. A surface of still water will suddenly dome upward, as though pressed from below, and a shape will pass under it that does not quite fit the dimensions of what you thought you were looking at, and then the water will resettle, and the place where the disturbance happened will be a little quieter than the rest of the basin for a moment, and then the chorus will fold back over the silence, and that is all you will know.

It is possible these are only larger amphibians. It is possible they are something else, or partly something else, or are amphibians the way a whale is a mammal. The basin does not clarify. The basin is not in the business of clarification.

The smaller amphibians are legion, and they are what you hear. They live in every pool, in every stretch of mud, in the ooze at the edge of every slow current, and at certain hours they rise up out of the water and the mud and the dark crevices between stones and they sing. The song is not musical in any way that a person from elsewhere would accept as music. It is a rolling, overlapping, layered trill, with no gap in it, with no silence between the calls, and it builds and builds and builds without cresting, the way the mist builds and builds without raining. The trill has pitches but it does not have melody. It has rhythm but it does not have beat. It is the sound of a place that has not stopped being alive for a very long time, and it is the sound you fall asleep inside, and it is the sound you wake up inside, and after a while it is the sound you think in.

Some of the small amphibians — those that live in the boggiest parts of the marshes, in the places where the water is just barely water and mostly just wet — are not green. They are not brown. They are a pale, almost translucent grey, and you can see, through their skins, the slow dark workings of their innards. Their hearts are visible as small thumps. Their stomachs, when they are full, show as a dark shape under the belly. They are, in their own way, the most honest creatures in the basin. They do not have a surface that lies about what is happening beneath.

There are amphibians that burrow. These are the ones you do not see. They are the ones that move through the upper layer of the silt the way worms move through soil, leaving low humps that travel across a flat place in the early morning. When you step on the ground here, in the right places, you can feel the give of a thing underneath that has not committed itself to being seen. The burrowers are darker than the others. Their skins are thicker. They have, in place of the wide amphibious mouth, something narrower, something that can take a worm or a rootlet or a smaller burrower without taking in the whole surrounding puddle. They come up at night. They come up, in particular, on the nights when the mist has been thick for several days and the air is so waterlogged that nothing can dry. Then the surface of the silt moves, here and there, in slow ridges, as the burrowers travel from one wet patch to another. They eat each other when they meet. They mate in the dark. They are not, in any way that you would call it, social. They are just present, in the way the silt is present.

And there are amphibians that filter. These are the strangest. They sit, in the deepest of the pools, with their mouths open and their gill-slits pulsing, and they pass the brown water of the basin through themselves and let it out again, taking from it the small lives that it carries — the larvae, the algae, the bacterial clouds. They do not move. They are essentially anchored, in the sense that they have picked a place and they are staying there, and what passes their way is what they eat, and what does not pass their way is not their concern. They are pale. They have, on their backs, a kind of feathery frill that is not feathers but is not not-feathers, that ripples in the slow current. They look, when you see one in the dim light from below the surface of a still pool, like a thing that has been waiting there since before you arrived and will be waiting there after you have gone.

Around the amphibians, and beneath them in the food order, is everything else. The insects are the second-largest group, after the amphibians themselves, and they exist in numbers that are not numbers but are more like weather. They rise from the marshes in columns. They hang in the dim air in shifting clouds. They land on every surface, including you, and they do not sting, mostly, but they crawl into every fold of clothing, every fold of skin, every seam of the hide tents, and they are a presence that you cannot shake off because they are not on you so much as they are around you, in the air, in the mist, part of the moisture. Their hum, when they are at their densest, is a pressure on the eardrums. It is the sound the basin makes when it is being most itself.

The insects of the basin are not, all of them, insects. Some of them are things that have six legs and would be called insects if you did not look closely, and when you do look closely they are not insects at all, but something softer, something without a hard exoskeleton, something that is more like a small soft grub with wings. Some of them are larvae that have never committed to becoming adults, that have stayed in the grub-stage and reproduced in it, as though the adult form had been agreed upon once and then quietly set aside. The basin is a place where the usual life histories have been edited. Things are born in one form and stay in it. Things are born and then are not born and then are born again in another form, lower, simpler, wetter.

There are worms in the wet soil, and the worms are not just worms. They are pale, length-of-a-hand worms, and they are in every handful of damp earth, and they are what the burrowers eat, and they are what the fish eat, and they are what the fungal threads wind around and digest at leisure. There are small crustaceans — small even by the standards of small crustaceans, no bigger than a grain of rice — that swim in the open water of the slow rivers and that color the water a faint milky grey when they are dense. There are snails, with shells that are thin and pale and that flake at the lip, and that graze the algae off the underwater stones with the slow patience of a thing that does not have anywhere to be. There are things with too many legs, which no one has named, which scuttle sideways across the silt when disturbed, and that have, on their undersides, a faint phosphorescence that does not quite light up but that makes them easier to see in the dimness, as though they are advertising themselves to a predator that does not exist.

The fish are few, and small, and pale, and slow. They are not the fish of a river elsewhere. They are fish that have been in this basin long enough that their fins have softened, their colors have gone out of them, their backbones are only barely there. They are the color of the water they live in. They swim in small loose schools near the bottom of the pools, and they come up to take an insect or a worm from the surface, and they go back down. They are eaten by the amphibians. They are eaten by the raptor-things. They are not, in any ecological sense, the dominant thing in their own water. The amphibians are dominant in the water as well as out of it.

And the raptor-things are on the ridges. These are the predators. They are long-legged, narrow-winged, hunched — built like herons and built like owls and built like neither, but with the stillness of an owl and the patience of a heron and the unhurried economy of a thing that does not have to move until it does. They sit on the ridge crests above the marshes and they look down at the dim water, and they wait. They wait a long time. A raptor-thing may sit for a whole day without moving. Then it moves, once, and it does not miss, and it goes back to its stone, and it eats the thing it has caught slowly, in small pieces, and it sits again. Their feathers — if they are feathers — are dark and damp. They do not look like birds of elsewhere. They look like birds that have been left out in the mist for several thousand years and have grown accustomed to it.

There are snakes, but fewer than the basin would seem to demand. The snakes are slow. They are the color of the stones they lie on. They eat the small amphibians, the eggs, the young. They are eaten, in turn, by the larger amphibians, which is one of the reasons the amphibians are the rulers. There is not a predator in this basin that does not get eaten by an amphibian at some point. The amphibians have made themselves unkillable in this way. They occupy the bottom of the food chain and the top of it simultaneously, and they do not seem to find this strange.

The things-with-leather-voices are on the ridges and in the deeper marshes, and no one has gotten close to one. Their call, when it comes, is a low wet sound, like a hide being stretched, and it carries across the basin in the still air, and the people who live here know the call the way you know the sound of a particular branch of a particular tree in wind — by where it comes from and what time of day it comes and how far away it is. They do not try to approach the source. The approach is not what is being offered. The call is the offering. It is the thing they are willing to give of themselves to the rest of the basin.

The small things with damp fur — the rat-sized, darting things — live in the cracks between stones, in the lee of outcrops, in the spaces that the low walls of the people have inadvertently created. They are not rats. They are not mice. They are something that resembles both without being either. Their fur is sparse and lies flat and is always a little darker at the tips than at the roots, because it is always a little damp. Their eyes are large and round and dark and placed on the sides of their heads in a way that gives them a wide field of vision at the cost of depth perception. They eat anything they can carry off, which is not much. They are eaten by the snakes, by the raptor-things, by the larger amphibians when they come up onto the land, by the people when the people can catch them. They are the basin’s loose change. There are a lot of them. They scuttle. They do not thrive, exactly, but they persist, in the way that small soft things persist in places that are not trying to kill them but are also not trying to help them.

There are birds, or near-birds, that move through the dim air above the basin. They are not numerous. They call thinly — a high, fine sound that gets eaten by the larger noise almost as soon as it leaves them. They eat insects. They are eaten by the raptor-things. They nest, when they nest, on the vertical faces of the harder outcrops, in the small protected ledges that the weathering has not yet erased. They are the most easily understood of the basin’s fauna, because they are the most like things elsewhere, but they are also the least of the fauna, in the sense that they are the ones the basin could most easily do without. They are present, but not central.

Now the people.

There are not many of them. A long walk between households. A long walk between one standing stone and the next. They are thin, careful, few, and they are in the basin the way the lichen is on the stones — not as the main thing, but as the thing that has learned to live on the surface of the main thing without being eaten by it. They have been here a long time. How long is not something they would say. They are not given to saying how long.

They are dark-skinned, in the way that people who live without much sun are dark-skinned — not by pigment but by a kind of general dimness, the color of tea, the color of the silt, the color of the stones they sit on. Their hair is black and hangs lank. Their eyes are the wet round eyes of the amphibians, almost, in the sense that they have the same stillness, the same willingness to look at you for a long time without deciding what they think of you. They are small people, in build. They do not grow tall. They are not starved. They eat well enough, of the basin’s abundance, but the basin does not offer the kind of food that makes people large. Their diets are mostly the meat of the larger amphibians, which is pale and soft and slightly sweet in the way that an animal that has been eating only algae and insects tastes slightly sweet, and the small fish, which they smoke and dry on the racks, and the eggs of the amphibians, which they take in season from the shallows, and the fungal towers, which they cut and dry and grind and that give them the only starch-like substance they have.

The fungal towers are how they get pitch. The towers rise in some of the deeper marshes — tall, soft, fibrous columns that look like fingers pointing up out of the muck, and that are the fruiting bodies of the network of fungal threads that veins the wet soil under everything. They are pale. They are not quite solid. They are warm, faintly, in the way that a compost heap is warm. The people cut them at the base and split them and lay them out on the racks, and they dry into something tough and fibrous and slightly sweet, and they can be eaten, and they can also be rendered down — boiled slowly in a small clay pot over a small fire of the dried dung of the burrower amphibians, of which there is a great deal — into a thick dark pitch that is the only waterproofing the basin offers. The pitch goes on the baskets, on the seams of the hide tents, on the threading of the bone needles. Without it the people would be wet all the time. With it they are only wet most of the time.

Their tools are not many. A hand-axe of river stone, held in the palm, used for everything that needs an edge. A digging stick — and this is the rare one, the one for which they have no easy explanation, because wood does not grow here, and a digging stick implies that wood has been brought in, or found, or traded for, at some point in the long past, from a place that is not this place. The stick is older than the people who use it now. It has been reworked several times. Its current form is not its original form. It is the most valued object in any household that has one, and not every household has one. A knife of sharpened amphibian bone, taken from the long bone of a dog-sized amphibian and ground to an edge and hafted into a short handle of the same bone or of the harder stone. A needle of bone, thin, with an eye drilled through it by patient rubbing against a piece of grit. A length of sinew for thread — amphibian gut, dried, split fine. A basket, woven of reed and grass that does not grow here but that comes, somehow, by trade, by long slow trade across distances that have not been measured in a long time, and made watertight with the pitch.

They have a bead or two of native copper, occasionally. The copper is not from here. The copper is from a long way away. A bead of native copper, in this basin, is not a piece of jewelry. It is a piece of distance. It is a small proof that somewhere, on the other side of the ridges, there is a place where the rock has a different color, and someone lives there, and someone walked here once with that bead in their hand and traded it for something, and the bead has been here since.

They live in hide tents. The hides are the hides of the larger amphibians, taken from animals that have been killed and cleaned and stretched and dried in the wind over many days. The hides are thick and soft and a little bit damp forever, and they smell of the basin, and they are the color of the basin, and they are warm, which is the most that can be asked of a shelter in a place where the sun does not come through. The tents are low, dark, stretched between outcrops, pegged down with stones. A person walking through the mist in the right part of the basin can come within a few steps of one and not see it. The people prefer it that way. They do not advertise.

The cairns are the most numerous of the visible marks. They are piles of stone — small, deliberate, raised at the edge of a path or beside a pool or on a ridge. They are not graves, or not only graves. They are markers of something the people do not explain. A place where an event happened. A place where an event should be remembered. A place where a person died, or a place where a person was born, or a place where the basin did a particular thing that the people thought was worth noting down. The cairns accumulate. A cairn that has been added to for several generations is a small hill of carefully chosen stones, each one placed by hand, each one taken from somewhere else in the basin and walked here. The people walk a lot. The cairns are evidence of how much they walk.

The low walls are not walls. They are windbreaks, or the foundations of shelters that did not get finished, or the remains of shelters that have been finished and then left, or markers of a boundary that has not been spoken about. They are single-course, hand-laid, made of the harder of the local stones — the dark stones, the ones that have not yet been worn down. They are not high. They are not long. They are the work of an afternoon, or of several afternoons, and they are the most that the people generally commit themselves to building at one time. They do not build in the way that people in other places build, with the idea that the building will outlast them. They build with the idea that the building is for now, and for the next few years, and after that the basin will have it.

The fish-traps are set in the slow rivers. Weirs of woven reed and weighted stone, with a narrow opening at the upstream end that the fish swim into and then cannot swim out of. The traps are tended. The fish inside them are taken by hand and smoked on the drying racks. The racks are simple A-frames of stone-weighted poles, with cross-pieces on which the pale strips of amphibian meat and the small fish are laid out. The drying is slow, because the air is damp, but it works, because the wind is constant. A piece of meat on a rack in the basin dries the way a thing dries in any place with a persistent breeze — by giving up its water to the moving air, even when the air is already waterlogged. It just takes longer.

The standing stones are the most deliberate of the marks. They are single stones, set up on end, in places that have been chosen and that the people can name, although they do not name them to outsiders. The stones are taller than a person. They are not decorated. They are not inscribed. They are not arranged in any pattern that can be read, because the pattern is not the kind of pattern that can be read from the outside. They are simply there. The effort of setting them up is enormous, by the standards of the basin — the stones are heavy, the basin offers no leverage, the people are not large, and there is no draft animal. To set one up is to spend a season on it, or several seasons, with ropes of sinew and rollers of wood from somewhere else and the help of everyone who lives within a long walk. The stones, once up, are not moved. They are visited. They are the center, sometimes, of a cairn field that accumulates around them. They are the closest thing the basin has to a cathedral, except that a cathedral implies a god, and the standing stones imply something the people do not name, which is the basin itself, perhaps, or the long patience, or the wet.

The people trade. They trade slowly, by walking, in a network that spreads out across the basin and over the ridges into whatever is on the other side of the ridges, which is a place of less rain and more stone and more sun, and which is not described here because the people themselves barely describe it and it is not what is being asked about. They trade hides for reeds. They trade amphibian meat for the rare wooden object — a digging stick, a pole, a piece of a thing that does not grow here. They trade a bead of native copper for a basket. They walk for days to do this. They walk in family groups, mostly, and they sleep in the open in their hide wraps, and they do not hurry, because hurrying in this basin is a thing that gets you wet faster and does not get you there sooner.

They are careful with each other. They do not, by the look of them, fight. The marks of violence are not visible in the way they are visible in the settlements of other places. The cairns do not record battles. The hide tents are not clustered behind walls. The standing stones are not on high ground with a view of approach. The people live spread out, and they meet by walking to each other, and when they meet they talk in low voices, and the talk is not the loud thing that the basin’s other fauna does. The basin does not have a word for them. They are just one of the small things that live here, in the mud, in the mist, in the dimness, making small marks on a place that is in no hurry to notice.

You can live in the basin. They live in it. They have learned to do without the sun, and without the green, and without the warmth, and without any of the things that a person from elsewhere would call necessary. They have learned to listen past the roar — past the wind, past the chorus, past the hum of the insects, past the low wet call from the ridges — to a quieter layer underneath, and then to a quieter layer underneath that, all the way down to the silt, where the sound is not sound but vibration, the slow working of the place on itself. They do not, by their look, think this is remarkable. They have not been anywhere else. They do not compare.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are wet, and few, and they are here, and the basin is older than they are, and they know it, and they have made their small marks anyway, and the marks will outlast them, and the basin will outlast the marks, and that is the arrangement, and they have accepted it the way the lichen accepts the stone — by living on the surface, and not asking the stone to be anything other than what it is.

That is who lives here. The amphibians, in their many forms, in their soft still eyes, in their patient mouths. The insects, in their columns, in their hum, in their unclassifiable softness. The worms and the crustaceans and the snails and the things-with-too-many-legs, in the silt and the water and the cracks. The fish, pale and slow, in the deep pools. The raptor-things, on the ridges, waiting. The snakes, fewer than you would expect, moving slowly in the shallows. The leather-voiced things, in the deeper marshes, calling without being approached. The rat-sized furred things, darting in the lee of stones. The near-birds, calling thinly above the larger noise. And the people, sparse, careful, dark, in their low hide tents, with their hand-axes and their digging sticks and their bone needles and their baskets and their beads of copper and their standing stones — making their small patient marks on a place that has been working, for a long time, on the slow patient business of being itself.
