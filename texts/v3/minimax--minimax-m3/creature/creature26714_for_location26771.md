The amphibians are first to make themselves known, though they are the last to be seen.

They live in the wet ledges where the chamber wall shelves into a dozen private pockets, each one a small heated pool no bigger than a pair of cupped hands. There are perhaps forty of them in the chamber, though the number is not stable — some disappear for weeks into cracks that go deeper than the warm water, some arrive fresh from elsewhere, drifted in through the upper marsh in seasons when the marsh itself remembers how to be wet. They are pale, almost the color of skim milk, and their skin is so thin that the workings of their bodies are visible from outside: the slow pulse of a three-chambered heart, the peristalsis of a small gut, the lazy drift of something luminous in their blood that is not quite red and not quite green but something between, a color that only exists in the bodies of things that have agreed to live where nothing reasonable would. Their eyes are flat and dark and do not seem to look at anything. They navigate by other means — by the heat that washes over their flanks, by the chemistry of the water against their bellies, by some older sense, something lateral-line, something that reads the world in pressure and gradient rather than in light. When they hunt, they do not strike. They wait, and the food comes to them: an insect drifting too low, a larva tumbling from a mat, a small thing that has lost its footing on a wet stone. The strike, when it comes, is too fast to follow — only a blur, a snap, a small soft collapse of jaw, and then the pale throat working slowly.

They have been here long enough that their bodies have stopped being quite animal. Their metabolism runs at a tempo the rest of the temperate world would find insulting — slow, considered, a thing that takes its time because there is no reason not to. They can go without eating for stretches that would kill a mammal of equivalent mass. They can tolerate temperatures that should cook them, because their cells have made a quiet deal with the heat: a suite of proteins that hold their shape at temperatures that would unfold lesser proteins, a lipid membrane that stays fluid where others would go brittle, an arrangement of mitochondria that is less an organelle than a habit, distributed throughout the cell rather than concentrated, so that the heat is spread thin and never permitted to gather in one place and become a problem. They are not heat-proof. They are heat-tolerant. The distinction matters: they can still die, they simply have decided, generation by generation, to die less often. They breed slowly, in the chamber's only season — which is to say, the season when the rain above slackens and the cold air stops pressing down so hard and the steam is permitted to rise a little higher before being beaten back. The females lay their eggs in the warmest water, against the vent wall, in strings of pale jelly that pulse faintly with the same inner light as their mothers' blood. The eggs hatch into something that is not yet a salamander and not yet a fish and not yet anything else, a small slip of life with external gills that wave in the current like the legs of something much smaller, and these larvae feed on the mats and the bacterial film and the slow drift of organic matter that falls from the mosses above, and they grow, slowly, into the eyeless pale things the chamber knows.

They are the chamber's most visible citizens, but they are not its authors. That title belongs to the mats.

The mats are everywhere.

They cling to the walls in sheets that vary from paper-thin to as thick as a fist. They paint the stones in long vertical streaks, as if the stones have been crying rust. They hang from the undersides of shelves in thick curtains, dripping, waving gently in any current. They bloom on the floor of the chamber where the water is shallow enough to permit them a foothold. They are not one thing. They are a negotiation — a council of organisms so different from each other that they would, in any other context, be considered separate kingdoms of life, but here have agreed to live together because the alternative is to live alone, and alone in this chamber means dead. There are bacteria in the mats that eat sulfur. There are other bacteria in the mats that eat the waste of the bacteria that eat sulfur. There are archaea — different again, older in their lineage, more particular in their tastes — that process the byproducts of the byproducts, extracting energy from compounds that should not yield energy, running a metabolism so strange that it was only named in the last century of human thought and is still not fully understood. There are fungi threading through the whole, breaking down the dead, recycling, ensuring that nothing that falls is ever truly wasted. There are, occasionally, the ghosts of algae — chlorophyll-bearing things that drifted in from the surface and have lost their color in the generations since, becoming, against their lineage, dependents on chemistry rather than light. They are not a single organism. They are an ecosystem compressed to a felt thickness.

Their color is the only honest color in the chamber. The sodium-glow from below is borrowed; the green-amber pulse from the mats themselves is their own. They do not glow in the way a firefly glows — not as a signal, not as a warning, not as a courtship. They glow because their chemistry is sloppy, because the reactions they run to extract energy from the minerals and gases around them sometimes produce photons as a byproduct, the way a coal produces sparks. The light they make is waste. It happens to be beautiful.

The mats breathe. When the vent surges — and the vent surges in a rhythm that the mats have learned to read — the chemistry of the water changes for a few minutes. More sulfur. Less oxygen. A flush of mineral-rich fluid. The mats respond: the sulfur-eating bacteria bloom, multiplying until the mat is thick with them, and in their blooming they glow brighter, and the whole chamber lifts in luminance, briefly, like a lung filling. Then the surge passes. The oxygen returns. The sulfur-eaters retreat, and other bacteria — oxygen-loving, slower, quieter — rise in their place, and the glow dims to its resting state. To sit in the chamber through a full surge is to watch the walls inhale and exhale in mustard and rust.

The insects are the loudest, though this is not saying much.

They live in the air, in the steam, in the warm wet space between the vent and the ceiling. There are several kinds, though only two are numerous enough to register as presence rather than accident. The first is a small fly — pale, blind or nearly so, with wings reduced to nubs it does not use. It lives its entire life in the air of the chamber. It hovers in the updraft, in the warm currents that rise from the vent and meet the cooler air of the upper chamber and form, in their meeting, a slow horizontal weather. The fly has no reason to land. It eats what drifts past. It mates in the air, briefly, clumsily, a small collision that produces a small egg that drops into the water and either hatches or doesn't. Its body is a compromise — too heavy to truly drift, too light to fight the air for long. It bobs. It waits. It is, in its way, a hostage to the current, and it has made its peace with the captivity.

The second is a beetle, longer, slower, with a shell that glows faintly with the same color as the mats — borrowed color, symbiote color, the shell hosting a thin film of bacteria that the beetle cultivates on its own back, grooming them, feeding them, keeping them clean, in exchange for a coat that may or may not confer camouflage and almost certainly does not confer much else. The beetle crawls. It crawls on the walls, on the floor, on the mosses. It eats the mosses. It eats the mats. It eats, on occasion, a fly that has blundered too low and met the wet stone. It is the chamber's largest herbivore, though it is not large.

Neither insect has a long life. A season, perhaps two. They are food for each other — the beetle eats the fly, the fly's larvae eat the beetle's dead — and for the amphibians, which eat them both without distinction, and for the small things that live in the mosses and have not yet been named because no one has come down here with the patience or the funding to name them. They are the visible motion of the chamber, the proof that the chamber is alive, the darting and crawling that makes the eye believe in the place.

The mosses are the oldest citizens, though not by the measure of any single moss.

They have come down from the marsh above — generations of them, a slow dripping colonization, a thread of green that has been working its way down for as long as the chamber has existed. They root where they can find a foothold: in cracks, on shelves, in the seams between stones. They are not the mosses of the surface. They have changed, in the generations since they came down, becoming more tolerant of heat, more tolerant of mineral water, more willing to live without the sunlight that their kind was, in older lineages, built to require. They are still green, but a darker green, a stained green, the green of something that has been somewhere it was not designed to go and has, by small adjustments, by tiny refusals to die, made itself fit. They hold water. They hold heat. They hold the small invertebrates that live among them — mites, springtails, things smaller than these words — and they hold, too, the falling debris of their own dead, a slow compost that feeds the next generation of moss and the next and the next.

They are the chamber's memory.

And above them all, in the long beards that hang from the ceiling and sway in the updraft, the mosses flower, briefly, in a season no one has tracked — small pale sex organs that release spores into the steam. The spores rise. Some make it out, into the marsh above, and germinate there, briefly, in the cold wet world, before dying. Some settle back down into the chamber, completing a cycle that is not quite a cycle, a circulation that is not quite a circulation, a reminder that the chamber is not sealed, that it leaks into the marsh and the marsh leaks into it, that the boundary between the two is a negotiation rather than a wall.

The whole chamber, in its way, is a negotiation. The heat from below against the cold from above. The chemistry of the vent against the chemistry of the rain. The slow colonists from the surface against the ancient colonists from the deep. Nothing here owns the place. Everything here has made a claim, and every claim has been contested, and the place itself is the residue of those contests — the wet walls, the breathing mats, the darting flies, the waiting amphibians, the working steam, the continuous roar of the throat.

If you sat long enough, you would see it.

The beetle would crawl past, slow, deliberate, its symbiote coat glowing faintly.

A fly would blunder into a strand of moss and struggle there briefly before freeing itself or not.

A pale amphibian would shift on its ledge, its flank rising and falling with its slow metabolism, its dark eyes seeing nothing, its lateral line reading the chamber in gradients of heat and pressure and chemistry.

A mat would surge. The wall would brighten. The chamber would lift in luminance, briefly, like a lung.

And then it would settle, and the sound would continue, and the steam would continue, and the throat would continue its long unceasing work, and the life in it would continue to refuse to die, because it had made a quiet deal with the place, and the deal was: *you may be hostile, but I will learn you, and in learning you I will become something that can live here, and in becoming that thing I will help make you what you are.*

And the place would answer, in its own language of heat and pressure and the slow glow of waste:

*Yes.*
