# What Lives There

The residents of this place are not, for the most part, the residents of places. They are the residents of edges — of the margins where one condition ends and another begins, of the interface between what is held and what is letting go. The plateau is a kind of shoreline turned upward, and what lives there lives the way shore things live: opportunistically, seasonally, with one eye on the cold water and one eye on the dry land.

## The Senior Citizens

The lichen are the closest thing this place has to elders. There is a community of them encrusting the eastern face of a boulder that the morning light, when it arrives, strikes first — a community that has been arriving, in their slow unhurried way, for perhaps ninety years. They are crustose and foliose and fruticose in patches, and among them there is a kind of council of textures, a parliament of grays and olive-greens and the pale, almost orange-gold of *Acarospora* weathering its particular century on the same square meter of stone. The boulder, in their time, has cracked and been resealed by their own bodies a dozen times. They do not know they live on a plateau. They do not know about the wind, which is to say they know about the wind the way a brick knows about gravity. Their whole life is the holding of a place, and they are very good at it, and they are the only ones.

A little further down the successional ladder — but still slow, still patient, still partaking of the lichen's geological calendar — are the mosses. They live in the lee of the same boulder, in the wind-shadow where the moisture lingers just long enough to make their business possible. They are a *Racomitrium* cushion, probably, going bronze at the tips and emerald at the heart, with a few capsules of sporophyte raised on thin red setae like tiny lighthouses. They do not move. They cannot move. They have been waiting for the boulder to be still long enough to colonize it, and the boulder has obliged.

## The Cushion People

The cushion plants are the next layer out, the smallest living things you would recognize as a thing and not a feature of the rock. There are maybe six species on the plateau, and they have all come to the same conclusion independently — that the appropriate shape for a body in this place is the hemisphere. They grow as low, dense, hair-clad mounds the size of teacups and saucets and, in the case of one venerable individual near the eastern gully, a small dinner plate. Their hairs are so thick you cannot see the leaves without parting them with your thumb. They hold a microclimate inside themselves that is several degrees warmer than the air outside and considerably more humid. They are, in effect, the only houses on the plateau that anyone was born in.

The cufees — let me invent a small name for them, because calling them cushion plants forever is not going to let you know what they are like — the cufees have their own animals. Springtails, principally, a footless hexapod an eighth of a millimeter long, that lives in the humid sleeve between the outer hairs and the inner leaves, eating pollen and fungal hyphae and the bodies of even smaller things. Mites eat the springtails. A tiny staphylinid beetle eats the mites. A spider the size of a printed period eats the beetle. The whole economy is smaller than the period at the end of this sentence and it is going on right now, in the plumage of the teacup, in the Antarctic light.

## The Grass That Stayed

There is a grass. There are several grasses, in fact, but the one I want to tell you about is the one that grows only on the lee side of rocks, where the wind has been kind enough to bury its crown just enough to keep the frost off it. Its name is something like *Deyeuxia* or *Pappostipa* — it doesn't matter for our purposes. What matters is that it has figured out, generation by generation, the exact distance at which a rock's shadow gives shelter without giving too much shade, and it propagates only there. A person walking past a hundred identical-looking rocks would notice the grass on maybe five of them, and those five would be the only ones the grass considered worth living on. Each of those five is a separate tribe. They do not interbreed with their neighboring tribe over the next rock because the wind would kill the pollen in transit.

The grass flowers in late January, if the summer is generous. It produces a single thin panicle, the weight of which the stem cannot always bear, and when it goes to seed the whole inflorescence is taken by the wind and carried for miles, most of it dying in transit, a few percent lodging against a new lee-side of a new rock and starting the long argument all over again.

## The Rats

The rats did not evolve here. To be clear: the rats are not of this place. They came in a kitchen module, or a generator housing, or a shipping container that had been on a truck that had come up the seasonal road. They are *Rattus norvegicus*, the same species that lives in subways and sewers and the basements of hospitals in every temperate city on earth. They are here because people are intermittently here, and people bring what they bring.

The rats live under the kitchen modules in burrows they did not have to dig, because the modules were set on raised platforms that left a crawl-space, and the crawl-space is warm, and there are crumbs. They are perhaps thirty or forty, in the most generous estimate. They have multiplied exactly as fast as the human presence has supplied them with food. They are fat and well-favored and they do not hibernate, because they do not need to.

They are an invasive species. They are the ones the foxes came for.

## The Foxes

The foxes are *Lycalopex culpaeus*, the culpeo, and they are small — about the size of a well-fed house cat, gray-backed with reddish legs and a white chin and an enormous brush of a tail that they carry low when they hunt and high when they are crossing open ground. They did not evolve here either, technically — they are a high-Andean lineage that has been on these altitudes for tens of thousands of years, which is to say they have been here long enough to consider themselves local. They are residents, by Andean standards. They are summer residents, by Andean standards.

A pair of them has denned under the eastern edge of the crusher pad, in a void left by a poorly leveled I-beam and the partially compacted rubble that the berm builders did not get around to. The den faces south, which is the correct orientation for sun avoidance in the southern hemisphere, and it is lined with a few scraps of insulation that the construction crew discarded. There are kits. There are usually kits in late summer — two, three, four of them, the size of grown rats themselves, woolly and curious and absolutely feral.

The parent foxes hunt the rats. The rats live in the kitchen and the fox den is fifty meters from the kitchen and the foxes know exactly how the kitchen works — they wait until the meal shift is over and the human traffic dies down and then they cross the open ground in a low loping trot, ears flat, tails streaming, and they work the perimeter of the module sniffing for the rat runs that come out at the bottom of the insulated panels. They take one or two rats a week, which is not enough to control the population but is enough to keep the rats nervous. The rats that get nervous get bold in other directions. The rats that get bold in other directions get eaten by something else.

The foxes also eat the goose eggs when they find them, which is usually after the goslings have hatched and the empty shells are lying around the scrape nests in the scree. They will take a gosling, if a gosling is slow, and they are unmoved by the parents' kettle-spout hissing. They have heard the kettle-spout hissing for ten thousand years. They know what it means.

## The Geese

The geese are *Chloephaga picta*, the upland goose, the male a clean bright white and the female a richly barred rust-and-black, and they are not, to be perfectly honest, an animal that has ever believed in the dignity of its situation. They are loud. They are ridiculous. They are perfectly built for the place and they make absolutely no acknowledgment that the place is bleak. They arrive in early spring in pairs, sometimes with a juvenile from last year still in tow, and they land on the plateau with a thump and a honk and they begin immediately to fight with the geese that were already there.

The fights are short and serious. They are about nest sites, which in this place means scrapes in the scree, which means particular small sheltered corners where the prevailing wind has deposited a thin protective drift of small stones and possibly an old bit of lichen mat. The geese that win a fight get the corner. The geese that lose a fight go somewhere else, probably to a less good corner. The whole territorial negotiation takes about three weeks, by the end of which the plateau has a map of goose territories traced over the map of the wreckage from the last two years of mining.

The nest is a scrape with a lining of down, which the female plucks from her own breast, which is the kind of commitment to the project that you have to admire. She lays four or five eggs. She sits on them for thirty days. The male stands on the rim of the nest and hisses at any fox that comes close, and this hissing is the kettle-spout noise, and it is louder than you would think a goose could be, and it is largely bluster, and the foxes have heard it before.

When the goslings hatch they are wet and yellow and they dry out fast in the wind and become gray and small and absolutely incapable of looking after themselves. They are led, in single file, by both parents down to the temporary stream in the eastern gully, which is the closest thing this place has to a daycare, and there they practice catching insects and learn what water looks like and grow feathers. By late summer they can fly. By the time they fly, the parents have already started the molt, which they do badly — they drop primary feathers and look surprised about it — and they are gathering on the southern edge of the plateau in preparation for the migration back down to the lower valleys, where they will spend the winter eating grain in the pastures of the estancias.

The migration is the loudest event of the year. The first time you hear it you mistake it for a truck engine on the haul road, because it is a continuous low honking that comes from above and from many directions at once and rises in pitch as the skein tilts into the wind. Then you see them — long wavering lines against the gray sky, black-and-white birds in *Chevrolegance* formation, a hundred and fifty at a time, the wings of the birds ahead making brief dark shadows on the backs of the birds behind. They do not land. They know the place is wintering over without them. They will be back.

## The Caracaras

The caracaras are *Phalcoboenus megalopterus*, the mountain caracara, and they are — let us be honest about this — the smartest things on the plateau. They are corvids in the way that hawks are corvids, which is to say they belong to the family Falconidae but they are perfectly happy to eat whatever they can pick up, the way a raven is happy to eat whatever it can pick up. They are black and white and pale, with a bare yellow face and a black crest that goes up when they are interested and down when they are thinking. They have, for a bird of their size, an absurdly large vocabulary — a chuckling rattle, a thin keening call, a single note that goes up at the end as if they are asking a question. They ask many questions.

They walk among the equipment the way a person walks through a market. They have a route. They go from the kitchen to the generators to the cable posts to the berm, and they inspect everything, and they find things. They find the rat that was careless. They find the piece of foil the cook dropped. They find the bit of insulation a fox shredded. They find the egg of a smaller bird that was nesting in the wrong place. They find, occasionally, a piece of ore that has come loose from a haul truck and rolled out into the scree, and they pick it up in their beak and carry it twenty meters and set it down again, as if the ore was the point and the carrying was a favor.

They nest on the cliffs that the mine has not reached, in scrapes among the basalt, and they lay two eggs that hatch into hungry young that they feed for sixty days on whatever can be found. The juveniles stay on the plateau through their first winter — they cannot fly well yet — and they huddle together in the lee of a storage tank in a tight pile, and they look, in the early light, like a small heap of dark laundry. They learn the route. They take over, eventually, the routes of the adults that did not survive, and the plateau's caracara population, in this way, is a slowly edited manuscript.

## The Condors

The condors are *Vultur gryphus*. There is no need to introduce them. You know what they are. You have seen the picture, which is unfair to them, because the picture is always a glamour shot in good light and you have never seen one in the slate-colored wash of this place, where they are not glamorous but enormous, not majestic but heavy, not graceful but rather *committed* in a way that has its own dim severity.

A condor at this altitude is a working bird. It does not fly for joy. It flies because it has to come down from the roost on the cliff edge to the carcass it found three ridges over, and it does not flap because it cannot sustain flapping at this thin air, so it rides the thermals in long patient circles, tilting the tips of its primaries up and down by a centimeter at a time to steer, the wings held absolutely still in the body of the air. From the ground, looking up, you see a black cross on a gray sky and you cannot tell if it is moving. If you watch long enough you will see that it is. Slowly. Slower than you thought was possible for something that large.

They ride the thermals off the heated rock faces in the early afternoon, the same thermals that the air down in the valley is sending up the slope, and they use the conveyor structures as an excuse to be lower than the cliff without having to commit to the actual landing. A wing tip brushes a guy wire sometimes. The bird adjusts. The bird adjusts because it has been alive for seventy years and this is not its first guy wire. The bird does not care about the machine. The machine is weather to it.

They are the only thing here that is older than the rocks were when they were uncovered by the ice. They were here before the mine. They will be here after the mine, whatever state the mine leaves the place in, because a condor can live on a worked-out scar just as easily as it can live on a pristine one, both of them being, from the condor's perspective, places where large dead things occasionally are.

## The Smaller Birds

There are others, smaller, that I want to mention briefly because they are the ones that animate the dull hours.

There is a ground-tyrannulet, *Muscisaxicola* something, that runs on long pink legs across the scree and takes insects from the lee-side of rocks. It bobs its tail the way a pipit bobs its tail. It is too small for this place. It knows this. It compensates by being too active for this place. It is impossible to point it out to someone because by the time they look it has moved.

There is a sierra-finch, brown and streaked, that lives among the insulation scraps under the kitchen and eats crumbs when the kitchen is unguarded and seeds when the kitchen is not. It sings a short dry trill that the wind takes away. It is the most secretive song on the plateau. You have to be inside the equipment zone to hear it.

There is a miner — the bird, *Geositta*, that lends its name to everything here because it lives in the holes in the ground that the mine has made. It is a small brown bird with a white throat that digs a vertical burrow in a cut bank and lays eggs at the end of it. The mine has given it more cut banks than it could ever fill. The miner has responded by being more numerous than it has any right to be in a place this high and this cold. It runs along the ground on short pink legs and pauses on a tussock and bobs its head at you and runs on. It is unbothered by your presence. It considers the mine habitat.

There are a few others — an occasional specimen of something like a cinclodes at the temporary stream in summer, a swallow that should not be there but is, catching the one hatch of insects the place produces — but they are visitors, and they are the reason I am noting them rather than describing them, because they are not residents and they will not be there in two weeks.

## The Human Beings

The human beings, when they are here, are here in shifts. They arrive on the seasonal road in trucks and personnel carriers, and they work fourteen days on and seven days off, and in between they live in the heated modules that were placed, when the modules were placed, in a configuration that took into account wind direction, blast radius, and the location of the kitchens. They are between eighty and three hundred at any one time, which is to say the plateau has a population that fluctuates wildly over the course of a year and is essentially zero for three months of the winter. The humans sleep in bunks that are stacked three high. The humans eat in the kitchen module. The humans do not, individually, leave much trace. Collectively, they leave a great deal.

The workers are mostly young men from the lower valleys, some from the altiplano, a few from the coastal cities. They are not, in the strict sense, locals. They are here for the wage. They have a complicated relationship with the plateau, which is to say they do not romanticize it and they do not hate it and they do not, in general, talk about it. It is where the work is.

But there are individuals among them who have begun, over the multiple seasons of their employment, to notice things. There is the operator of the primary crusher who has learned to identify individual condors by their plumage and who has, without telling anyone, been taking notes on the comings and goings of the local pair for three years. There is the cook who leaves a bucket of kitchen scraps by the east door of the kitchen in the morning, after the shift change, so that the foxes can eat what the rats would otherwise eat, which is her small private way of influencing the political economy of the kitchen module. There is the geologist's assistant who has begun, against protocol, to log the locations of the goose nests and report them to the operations manager, so that the haul trucks can be routed around the scrapes during the breeding season. These people are not conservationists. They are people who have been around long enough to have opinions about who lives on the plateau, and the opinions have softened, in the way that opinions about the wildlife of a place do soften when you watch the wildlife every day for three years.

The humans are temporary. This is the truth of them. They are here for the mineral, not for the place, and when the mineral is gone they will leave, and the road will be erased, and the modules will be loaded onto trucks, and the plateau will be a worked scar without a population again. They are the only things here that are more temporary than the place itself. The rocks will be here. The lichen will be here. The geese will come back. The foxes will den somewhere else. The condors will not notice.

The humans are the only thing here that knows it is moving on.
