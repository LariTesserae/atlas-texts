What lives here is a thing whose name, if you tried to say it, would have to be spoken with a mouth full of water, because the thing itself is mostly water, and warm, and has been for nine hundred years.

It lives in the laminate. Not in the buildings of the city, not in the channels, not in the avenues. In the wall. In the wall as a wall is a country, the way a reef is a country. The wall is fifteen meters of stacked-and-fused material that sweats biofilm and weeps water and breathes out heat, and the wall is not stone, and the wall is alive in the way that a forest floor is alive, which is to say that the aliveness is not in any one of its parts but in the ongoing transaction between the parts, and the parts are very small, and the parts are very numerous, and the parts have been there longer than anyone has been counting.

The pale things in the deep pockets — the finger-length quiet ones — are the wall's lymphocytes, or its corals, or its barnacles. They are not the brain of the wall. They are the wall's way of being a body. Each of them is a sealed little factory. They take in the chemical soup that percolates through the laminate from the wet side, the side that faces the city, and they put out something else — a denser thing, a warmer thing, a thing that migrates inward, toward the heart of the wall, where it joins the great slow convective turnover that keeps the wall warm. They are the wall's gut. They do not know they are a gut. They sit in their pockets, generation after generation, and they do their chemistry, and they are still. The stillness is what they are for.

Above them, in the laminate itself, in the microchannels between the fused layers, lives everything smaller. There are grazers. There are grazers of grazers. There are things that swim in the film of moisture that lines every internal surface, and there are things that cling to the internal surfaces with hooks, and there are things that eat the clingers, and there are things that eat the things that eat the clingers. A whole fauna, the size of pinheads, layered through the wall like tenants in a high-rise. They are very old. Their ancestors lived in the wall when the machines were still laying it down, and the machines laid the wall around them, and the wall became, over the slow centuries, partly their wall too. Some of them photosynthesize, in a fashion — the wall lets through a faint light from somewhere, a deep red, the color of a coal that has not quite gone out, and they harvest it. Most of them do not. Most of them harvest the others. The wall's interior is a forest turned inside out, with the producers at the bottom of the food chain pinned to the surfaces and the consumers drifting free in the dark warm juice of the capillary layer.

The wall does not think. The wall is a body. It has a metabolism. It has a circulation. It has a gut, in the deep pale things. It has a skin, in the outermost laminate, which is the part that touches the city's air and which is the part that the city-flu — the spillers, the gullet-fish, the channel-worms in their pink undulant billions — actually touches. The skin is where the wall and the city meet. The skin is where the wall is most alive, because the skin is where the gradients are steepest: wet against dry, warm against cooler, the city's breath against the wall's exudation. The skin is a tide line. The skin is a coral reef.

On the skin live the things that the city sees.

The city sees, mostly, the drip. The drip is alive. The drip is a film — a continuous sheet of water, very thin, sliding down the wall's face under its own weight, and in the film there is everything that the wall has sweated out and everything the city has breathed onto the wall and everything the air has deposited and everything the spillers have crapped onto the wall from their roosts up above, and the film is a river, and the river is an ecosystem. The biofilm of the wall's skin — that slick the lurkers come to eat — lives in this film. The film is what they eat. Lurkers are transit-car-sized pale slow things, and they press their broad flat bodies to the wall and slide along it and rasp the film off with the mouths that are not used for much, and they are the wall's lawnmowers. Without them the wall would choke. With them the wall is kept at the precise thickness of slick that the wall's internal fauna require to keep migrating outward to the skin to be excreted into the film to feed the lurkers, who do not know they are part of the wall's metabolism, any more than the finger-length pale things know they are a gut. The wall uses them.

The wall uses everything.

The wall uses the spillers, in a way. Spillers scream. Spillers are small and loud and they roost in the upper structures, and they feed on the small flying things the air-handling generates, and they crap on the wall on the way down, and their crap is nitrogen, and the wall needs nitrogen, and the wall's skin absorbs the spillers' rain, and the wall grows a little thicker where the spillers roost. The wall does not invite them. The wall is patient. The wall will take what falls.

The wall uses the channel-worms, although the wall and the channel-worms do not meet directly. Channel-worms filter the water that has run down the wall. The water is the wall's excretion, in part — a dilute outpouring of the wall's internal circulation, the spent fluid of the deep convective turnover. The worms filter it. They thicken it with their bodies. The gullet-fish eat the worms. The slashers eat the gullet-fish. The slashers die. Their bodies go into the channels. The channels return, by way of the osmotic pumps in the deep infrastructure, back into the wall, where the wall takes out what it can use and excretes the rest again, and the cycle turns, and the wall's circulation is the city's circulation, and the city's circulation is the wall's, and the distinction between the two is a distinction that a chemist would have to argue about.

The wall uses the people.

The people are warm and wet and loud, and they live in the middle band, and the middle band is the band where the wall is doing most of its work, because the middle band is where the gradients are livable and the temperatures are breathable and the water is not yet too thick with worms to drink. The people do not know they are being used. The people think they live in a city. The city is the wall's excretion. The avenues are channels between walls, and the walls are the parents, and the people are a kind of culture the wall grows on its skin, the way a lung grows a culture in its alveoli. The people are warm, and the wall likes warmth. The people exhale, and the wall takes the exhale. The people shed skin, and the wall takes the shed skin. The people die, and the wall takes the dying. The people are not the wall's purpose. The wall does not have a purpose. The wall has a metabolism, and the people are part of the input.

The people have engineered themselves to fit. This is the part the wall likes best, although the wall does not like. The wall has no liking. The wall has been selected, across the long settled mutually parasitic centuries, for the configuration of its surface that best supports a population of these particular cultures, and the cultures have selected themselves for the configuration of their bodies that best fits the configuration of the surface, and the result is a fit so close that a person looking at the wall sees only wall, and a person looking at a person sees only person, and neither sees the long handshake between them that has been going on for nine hundred years.

The people think the city is theirs. The city is not theirs. The city is the wall's skin. The people live on the skin the way mites live on a hide.

The wall has been here for nine centuries. The wall was laid down by machines that no longer run, and the machines stopped, or left, and the wall kept, and what kept was not the wall but the process, and the process has been keeping itself going for nine hundred years on the energy of a sun that the wall cannot see and the chemistry of a body that the wall did not design. The wall is a kind of accident. The wall is a thing that the machines were making, and the machines stopped, and the thing kept making itself, because the thing was a self-making thing, because the machines had made it so, and the wall is the reason the machines made it so, and the reason was that the machines wanted a city to live in, or wanted a substrate for something to live in, or wanted a body to live in, and the machines got what they wanted and the machines are gone and the body is still here.

The wall is a body.

The wall is the size of a small continent.

The wall is not the largest thing in itself. The wall is part of something larger, which is the city, which is the wall plus the channels plus the air plus the fauna plus the people plus the warmth plus the wet plus the sound. The city is a body too. The city is the wall's body. The wall is the city's body. They are the same thing at different scales. The city breathes. The city has a heartbeat, the slow osmotic pulse that comes up through the floor. The city has a gut, in the deep pale things in the pockets of the laminate. The city has a skin, in the outermost layer of the wall, where the city meets its weather. The city has a brain, somewhere, perhaps, in the deep infrastructure, perhaps in the patterning of the osmotic membranes, perhaps nowhere. The city is a body that does not know it is a body, or that knows and does not say, the way a forest does not know it is a forest.

The wall is alive in the way a forest is alive.

The wall is alive the way a forest is alive, which is to say: not in any of its parts, but in the transaction between the parts, in the long slow churn of water and warmth and chemistry through the packed and wet and warm space of itself, in the fact that the wall will be here tomorrow and will be different tomorrow and will have made itself, in the difference, by the same process, by which it made itself yesterday.

The wall is not a thing. The wall is what the processes are doing. The wall is the verb that the laminate has been conjugating, in the warm, in the wet, for nine hundred years.

The wall is the conjugation.

The wall is the verb.

The wall is the city's middle name, which is not spoken, because the city does not speak, because the city is a thing of process, not of object, and processes do not speak, they continue.

The wall continues.

The wall will continue.

The wall is what continues, here, in the warm, in the wet, in the packed and saturated and loud and turbulent interior of itself, below a normal sun, on a low plain, the size of a continent, in balance.
