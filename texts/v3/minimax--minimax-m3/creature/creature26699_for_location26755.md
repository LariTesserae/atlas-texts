The birds are only the loudest.

What lives on the shelf lives in strata, the way the vegetation does, the way the carved terraces do, the way the light does when it manages to reach the floor in coins and shafts. There is a high air and a low air, a canopy and a floor, a damp bright world and a deep dark world beneath the moss, and in each of these lives a different congregation, with different business, different hungers, different tolerances for the slow drowning of the mist. The birds are the layer you cannot ignore. They are not, however, the layer that has been here longest.

The shelf is older than the sound.

Begin, if you can bear it, with the silence underneath. The carved stone holds heat the way a sleeping animal holds heat. Within its cracks, in the seams the grasses have not yet pried open, in the dust beneath the waxy creepers where the day's wetness never quite dries, there is a smaller and older business. Springtails hop in the damp leaf-litter, blind and pale, in the dark. Something with many legs moves through a channel that was once a gutter, and pauses, and moves again. A fungus lifts a brown shelf from the flank of a fallen stone, and its underside is a lace of pale gills, and the air around it is sweeter than the air around the flowers. A slug — there is always a slug in such places — traces its own bright line across a carving of a figure whose head has been worn smooth. The carving does not know. The slug does not know. The shine of the trail will be gone by morning, and the carving will still be there, and the slug will have moved on, or not, depending on the cold and the damp and whether the morning mist decides to lie heavy.

The damp-bright world is generous to these tenants. It does not ask them to remember. It asks only that they go on, and they go on.

Above the floor, in the knee-high tangle of the broad-leaved creepers, the insects have their own crowded life. The creepers flower at the nodes, small pale bells that almost never open fully in the wet, and the bees that visit them are heavy-bodied, slow in the humidity, furred with pollen that will never quite be brushed off. The bees are not the only pollinators. There is a moth, long-tongued, that visits the resinous shrubs when the mist lifts in the afternoon, and a butterfly the color of bruised fruit that is rarer, and seems to belong to no one's pollination schedule, and is seen most often along the carved channels where the light catches the damp and the wings lift and close, lift and close, as if the butterfly were reading something written in the wetness. The insects are eaten, in their turn. There is a small lizard — a long thing, the color of wet bark, very still, very patient — that perches on the warm stone of a retaining wall and watches the bee-path with one eye and then the other. It is not the only predator. There is a frog, in the deeper channels, that only emerges when the mist is at its thickest, a pale soft thing that seems to be made of the mist itself until it moves and is gone. The frog does not croak. The place is too loud for a single voice.

The carved faces on the walls, half-eaten with growth, are watched by all of these lives and known by none. The lizard basks on a chin. A spider has strung a web between an open mouth and a brow. The reliefs of animals — the flank, the hand, the circle of figures with their heads open to the sky — are merely topography now, convenient surfaces, weather. Whatever the figures were doing, in the long-ago time when the place was made, the place has been doing it without them for so long that the doing itself has become the maker.

The shrubs are the second stratum, and they are loud in their own way, though not with sound. The resinous ones — the ones whose smell you catch from a hundred yards, the clove and the heavier sweetness under it — flower in the wet air, and the flowers are thick and waxy and the color of old butter. They are not pollinated, exactly. They are worked over, by the bees in the morning, by the moths in the afternoon, by the small flies in between, and the smell they give off is the smell of being worked over, a yeast-and-clove breath that hangs in the bowl of the valley and mixes with the bird-noise until the two become a single medium. The shrubs are not, themselves, very old. The valley was a place of carved stone and open plaza before they were; the plazas became meadows, the meadows became forest, the forest became this, and the shrubs are an inheriting generation, no more aware of being an inheritance than the birds are. But they have done work. Their roots have cracked the pavement. Their fallen leaves have made the soil in which the next, taller, things will stand. They are, in their slow way, the second remakers of the place.

The trees are the third stratum, and the eldest living stratum in the valley. They are not, by any honest measure, what one would call old. A tree here might be two hundred years, or four hundred, or, in the case of the great wide-crowned one whose roots have eaten the central stair, perhaps eight hundred. The lichens on their bark — those vertical stripes of cream and charcoal — are older than they are. The moss is older still. The channels their roots drink from are older than anything that drinks from them, and the stone those channels were cut into is older than the channels, and so on back, and so on back, until the reaching gives out in the dark.

The trees do not move, of course. But they are not, in any meaningful sense, still. Their crowns are full of birds. Their trunks are full of the small industry that follows the birds — the insects that eat the feathers, the mites that live in the nests, the parasites that live in the parasites. A tree here is a city. A tree here is several cities at once, stratified, with their own migrations, their own uprisings, their own quiet extinctions. A swarm of small birds will lift from a crown in a detonation of wing-beat when a shadow passes over, and the tree will be, for a moment, only a tree, and then the birds will return, and it will be a city again, and the leaves it has dropped in the disturbance will be turned by the beetles underneath, and the beetles will be eaten by the lizards, and the lizards will be eaten by something you will not see, and the whole system will continue, unconsented, unconducted, an orchestra with no listener and no hall, the hall having been carved by hands whose descendants are no longer present in any form the valley recognizes.

The birds.

The birds are the stratum that meets you. They are the stratum that has inherited the acoustics. The valley is a bowl, and the bowl holds sound, and the birds have, over the long generations, become shaped to the bowl the way water is shaped to a vessel. They are not louder than they would be elsewhere. They are simply all here.

The pigeons are the bass of the place. They are big, slate-blue, with the iridescent neck a pigeon wears when the light is right, and the throat-pouch that swells and deflates as they coo. There are thousands of them, and they move in loose columns through the canopy, not in the disciplined V of a migration but in the slack, conversational formation of birds who have no reason to hurry. Their wings make the low percussion. Their coo is the held note beneath everything else, the pedal, the drone, the thing that does not stop and does not vary. The pigeons nest in the broken towers on the far rim and in the cracks of the upper terracing, and they fly down to the floor in the early morning to drink from the carved channels, and they are the most visible of the residents, and the least remarkable, and they are the reason, if you asked a bird to explain the place, that the place is the way it is. They have been here longest, in their current form. They are the hinge.

The smaller birds are the melody, and they are in flocks. The flocks are the visible unit, not the individual. A flock will rise from a shrub in a single motion, a thrown cloth of wings, and will wheel above the canopy and settle again in a different shrub, and the shrub will seem to be the same shrub because the birds are the color of the leaves in that light, and the place will seem, for a moment, to have lost a population it had. There are several species, and they do not mix. A flock keeps to its own kind, and the kinds partition the valley in ways that are not obvious to a visitor but are fiercely known to the birds. There is a green one, glossy, that keeps to the resinous shrubs. There is a brown one, smaller, that works the waxy creepers. There is a streaked one, secretive, that is only seen in the broken towers at the rim. The alarm call you heard on the stairs — the one repeated from three directions — was a streaked one, and it was not, in fact, an alarm about you. It was an alarm about something else, a hawk, perhaps, or a snake, or a shadow that only the streaked one can interpret. You happened to be climbing at the time. You happened to be the wrong shape, in the wrong place, at the wrong moment, and the alarm was passed over you like wind over grass.

The swifts are a different order of resident. They are not, strictly, of the shelf. They are of the upper air, and they come down into the bowl only when the mist clears, in the late morning, when the sun has burned the valley open and the warm air rises in a visible sheet. Then they are here, screaming, cutting the bright air with their stiff wings, and the scream is not, as you might think, a cry of distress. It is a cry of speed. It is the sound a swift makes because a swift is built to move at the edge of what is mechanically possible, and the air, at that speed, makes a noise, and the noise is the swift's only song. The swifts are not here for the bowl. They are here for the insects that the bowl's warmth lifts into the air. They are guests, in a sense, and the longest migrations in the place pass through them.

The bowl is full of these. The bowl is a continuous event. The pigeon drone beneath. The flock-twitter above. The swift-scream, intermittent, high. The alarm call, passing through. The coo and the trill and the thin reedy note of a bird you will never see but will hear for as long as you stand in the valley. And beneath the birds, the frog that does not croak. The lizard that does not call. The bee that hums only when it is in flight, and is silent when it is working. The moth that makes no sound at all. The slug, silent. The fungus, silent. The roots, silent, except for the slow creak of growth that no ear can detect and no instrument, probably, would catch.

Nothing hums. Nothing glows. The place has no mechanism. The mechanism is the birds, and the mechanism is the bees, and the mechanism is the roots that have re-engineered the earth around themselves, and the mechanism is the slow fermentation of leaves into soil and soil into the next leaves, and the mechanism is the patient unconsented orchestra that plays whether or not anyone is listening, has played since long before there was anyone to listen, and will play, in this form, for some time yet.

What lives here, in the end, is the place itself. The place has become its own animal, a slow one, a damp one, a loud one in the ears and a quiet one in the long view, made of stone and root and feather and rot and the small blind things that move in the dark seams of it, none of them aware that they are parts of a single body, all of them going on.

The light will shift. The mist will clear, and then return. The birds will lift and resettle. A flock of small green ones will work the resinous shrubs. A pigeon will inflate its throat-pouch and coo. A lizard will watch a bee-path with one eye and then the other. A frog will emerge, briefly, in the thick mist, and be gone. The carved faces on the walls will continue to be overgrown. The reliefs of figures with their open heads will continue to be unread. The towers on the far rim will continue to be broken, and to be inhabited, and to keep no watch at all, however much they may have watched once, in the long-ago.

The place will continue. The place is what is continuing, and what is continuing is the only answer, finally, to the question of who lives here.
