# The Inhabitants of Kharad-Ruun

## I. The People in Their Blues

The most numerous resident of the mountain is, of course, the person. There are two hundred thousand of them, and they are organized — as everything in Kharad-Ruun is organized — by altitude.

You learn a great deal about a Kharad-Ruuni by the blue of their skin. This is not metaphor and not dye. Generations of living in a particular spectrum of engine-light have, in the slow patient way that generations work, pigmented the inhabitants according to the tier in which they were born and to which their caste binds them. A deep-blue is not a cyan-blue who has gone underground; a deep-blue is a *deep-blue*, the way a river is not a pond that has gone deep. The light has entered them. It has become them. They cannot leave it without becoming ill, and so the castes are stable, and the castes are inherited, and the castes are, to a degree that outsiders find unsettling, *visible*.

The deep-blues are the lowest and the most numerous. They live in the carved galleries, in the chambers whose walls still hold the chisel-striae of the original workers — a thousand years of strokes, names, dates, the careful record of a city that does not forget. Their skin is the deep ultramarine of a bruise a week old, or of the sky just after sunset in a wetter world than this. Their eyes — they have eyes, unlike the chthonic or the mules, but they use them in a particular way: they see by the dim glow of the engine-walls and they do not look up much, because looking up is the privilege of the castes above. They are short and thick through the chest, the chests of people who have spent a thousand generations breathing thin mineral air. Their hands are wide. Their fingernails are black with the dust of the galleries, no matter how often they are cleaned; the dust is in the keratin, the way the blue is in the skin, the way the chisels are in the walls.

A deep-blue in the morning is already at work. There are no mornings in the deep galleries in any usual sense — the engine-light does not vary, the engines run continuously, the city does not sleep — but there are shift-chimes, and the shift-chimes tell the deep-blues when to descend further into the mountain to cut a new gallery, or to maintain an old one, or to bring up the spall in ore-carts drawn by the mules. The deep-blues do not call themselves miners, though outsiders do. They call themselves *the first*. They were the first to carve here, ten centuries ago, and they have been carving ever since. A child of the first is taught the chisel before she is taught to read. The chisel is the first. The chisel is the city's alphabet. Every stroke a deep-blue makes is a letter in a language that is being written continuously, in every wall, in every direction, and the city is the text.

A deep-blue lives, on average, fifty-two years. The air at the lowest levels is not thin in the way the upper air is thin; it is *thick*, fouled with the dust of a billion chthonic and the spall of a thousand years of chisels. Their lungs are dark. Their coughs are wet. They die as they were born: in the blue.

The cyan-blues are the engineers. They live in the middle tiers, where the glass-iron grows in the seams of the cooling basalt and is harvested in great pale sheets that ring like bells when struck. Their skin is a working blue — not the bruised ultramarine of the deep galleries but a clear, almost aqueous cyan, the blue of a lake in a painting, the blue of a healthy vein seen through pale skin. They are taller than the deep-blues by a hand's breadth, because they have lived closer to the engine-light and the engine-light has not been so kind to their bodies — they are lean, and they are tired-looking, and they cough less but smile less too. They are the people of the *squeeze*, the hydrostatic systems that turn the mountain's heat into power, and they wear leather gauntlets permanently stained with the mineral oil that they have handled since childhood. The oil gets into everything. It darkens the creases of their palms. It gives their children a particular smell.

A cyan-blue is born knowing the pumps. She is apprenticed at seven to a press-master, and by twelve she can read the pressure-gauge by the pitch of its whistle alone, and by twenty she is foreman of a tier, and by thirty she is dead of a pressure-blow or a heat-flash or a long slow failure of the lungs from the aerosol. The cyan-blues do not live as long as the deep-blues. They are not built for it. But they do not seem to mind; they are too busy. The engines are hungry. The engines are always hungry. A cyan-blue without work is a cyan-blue who has lost the thread of her own name, and the *squeeze* is the thread, and the *squeeze* is never satisfied.

The white-blues are the fewest and the strangest. There are perhaps four thousand of them, the priesthood of the vents, and they live in the uppermost tier, where the engine-light is so intense it hurts the eyes of anyone who has not been born to it. Their skin is not really white; it is a hot, almost-feverish blue so close to the edge of the spectrum that it reads as colorlessness, the way a fluorescent light reads as no light at all. They are thin in the way that people are thin who have been cooked slowly from inside by proximity to the magma chamber. Their eyelashes are gone. Their tear-ducts have sealed. They do not weep. They wear breathers of glass-iron mesh and goggles of the same, and their robes are stained permanently the brown of the mineral aerosol that drifts down from the heat-exchange towers. They do not live long. They do not, in any case, *expect* to live long. They expect to serve.

A white-blue is selected in childhood from the cyan-blue castes, by a process the priesthood calls *the calling* and that the cynics of the middle tiers call *the burning*. The child is taken up through the tiers, into the light, into the heat, into the plume, and the child who survives the first year is a white-blue, and the child who does not is commemorated by a chisel-mark in the deep galleries, anonymous, dated, *known*. The white-blues tend the magma chamber. They feed it. They bleed it. They are the keepers of the city's heart, and the city's heart is a lake of molten rock that has been kept molten, deliberately, for a thousand years, and the white-blues are the reason.

The white-blues do not speak of the eventual erosion. The white-blues are forbidden to speak of it, formally, by the liturgy of the vents. But they carve. Even they carve. The upper tiers bear the chisel-marks of the priesthood as surely as the lower tiers bear the chisel-marks of the first, and the marks say the same thing: *let it be known*. The white-blues know they will die. The white-blues know the city will die. They keep the engines running anyway, because the engines are the only protest they have.

## II. The Chthonic

Below the people, in the deep galleries, in the chambers where the engine-light does not reach and the only illumination is the cold blue of the copper-ore walls, there are the cousins.

The chthonic are not animals. They are not machines. They are something the city has made, or something that has made itself in the city's dark, and the distinction is not important to anyone who has spent a long shift in the lower galleries. They are pale. They are soft. They are small — the largest is no bigger than a rat, and most are smaller, the size of a thumb-joint, the size of a tooth. They have no eyes, because there is no light to see by, and they have no mouths, because there is no wet food to eat, and they have, in fact, no features at all — they are pale oblong bodies, slightly tapered at one end, slightly flattened at the other, and the flattened end is pressed always to the stone. They feed by leaching. They put the stone into themselves through capillary feet. They excrete a fine mineral dust that drifts down and accumulates on the floor of the galleries, and the deep-blues come each morning with ore-carts and sweep up the dust and take it to the smelters, and the dust becomes glass-iron becomes beams becomes floors becomes more galleries, and the cycle is complete.

A chthonic lives, as far as anyone can tell, forever. Or rather, it lives until it is killed, and most are not killed — they are stepped on, occasionally, by a careless deep-blue, and they burst into a bright powder that smokes briefly and is gone, and the deep-blue swears, not because the chthonic is precious but because the powder is caustic and gets into the boots. There is no death in the deep galleries the way there is death in the upper city. The chthonic simply *are*, in vast numbers, in every crack, in every chisel-groove, in every damp corner of the lower mountain. A square meter of gallery-floor may hold a thousand of them. A wall may hold ten thousand. They are the city's inhabitants in the way that the lichen is a cliff's inhabitant — they are not visitors, they are the surface itself, only moving.

The deep-blues regard them with affection. This is the wrong word; the deep-blues will tell you it is the wrong word. They regard them with the fond contempt of a farmer for his soil-fauna, which is to say, with the practical tenderness of someone who knows that the small pale wriggling things are the reason the place is habitable at all. Without the chthonic, the galleries would choke with their own spall. Without the chthonic, the dust would build up to the ceilings, and the chisels would be working in their own cuttings, and the city would bury itself in a century. The chthonic digest the mountain's leavings. The chthonic keep the deep galleries clean. The chthonic hum, faintly, all the time, a vibration just at the threshold of perception, and the deep-blues tune their shift-chimes to the hum, and the children of the first are taught to listen for changes in the hum, because a change in the hum means a change in the galleries, and a change in the galleries means work.

The chthonic have no names. The deep-blues do not name them. The cynics of the middle tiers call them *the cousins*, mockingly, but the deep-blues do not use this word and do not, when pressed, seem to find it funny. There is a silence in the deep galleries, around the chthonic, that is not the silence of a place where a thing is absent. It is the silence of a place where a thing is too present to need a name.

## III. The Mules

Above the chthonic, in the middle tiers, the city has built its own fauna.

The mules are not chthonic. The mules are not people. The mules are something in between: *engineered*, in the sense that the cyan-blues have been breeding them for a thousand years, but also *natural*, in the sense that they breathe and eat and die and are born. They are six-legged. They are eyeless. They are hairless. Their skin is a pale gray, the gray of unworked basalt, and it is warm to the touch — too warm, warmer than a mammal, warmer than anything that ought to be alive at this altitude without a great deal of effort. The warmth comes from inside them. The mules have, threaded through their chests and bellies, a network of fine glass-iron capillaries that carry a small diverted flow of the *squeeze*, and the *squeeze* is heat, and the heat keeps them warm in the cold galleries, and the warmth is, in a way no one speaks of, the mountain's own warmth made animal.

A mule does not see. It does not need to see; the cyan-blues have lined the cart-stations with low ridges along the floor, and the mules follow the ridges by feel, and the ridges lead them where they need to go, and they go there. A mule is docile in the way that a well-designed machine is docile: not because it has chosen docility but because docility is what it has been made to be. A mule eats mineral paste from a trough. A mule pulls a cart of ore. A mule stands in a stall between shifts and is warm and is quiet and is, in its own limited way, content. The cyan-blues do not love their mules. The cyan-blues do not abuse their mules. The cyan-blues maintain their mules the way they maintain their pumps, with the same careful attention and the same lack of sentiment.

A mule lives about thirty years, which is twice as long as a white-blue and slightly more than half a deep-blue. A mule's lineage is recorded in a great book in the central engine-hall, and the book is the city's second-most-valuable secret, after the liturgy of the vents. The mules breed true. The mules have bred true for a thousand years. The cyan-blues who keep the book know each mule's mother and grandmother and great-grandmother back to the first pair, and the first pair are commemorated in a chisel-mark in the deep galleries, beneath the chthonic, in the cold below the engines: *the first mules, named, dated, known*.

The mules are the only large animals in Kharad-Ruun. There are no dogs. There are no cats. There are no birds — there is no sky to fly in, and the upper tiers are choked with the aerosol, and the lower tiers are dark. The mule is the only large moving thing in the city that is not a person, and the mules are everywhere, in every cart-station, in every shaft, pulling their carts of ore and finished glass-iron and the carefully rationed outputs of the engines, and the mules are part of the city's rhythm the way the chthonic are, only the mules have names, and the mules have lineages, and the mules are, in a way the cyan-blues do not say out loud, family.

## IV. The Old Woman in the Deep Galleries

There is a deep-blue woman, very old, who lives in the lowest gallery of all — the drainage-catch, the chamber that sits beneath the rest of the city, where the mineral leachate of a thousand years of chthonic activity pools in shallow basins and is pumped back up to the smelters. Her name is Rukh. She is eighty-one, which is impossibly old for a deep-blue, and she has survived this long because she has lived, for the last fifty years, in a place no one else wants to be. The drainage-catch is damp, in the particular way that places below the rest of the city are damp — not with water, there is no water, but with the moisture of a thousand chthonic breathing in a sealed space, the slow exhalation of pale soft bodies. Rukh's skin is the deep ultramarine of her caste, but it has a sheen to it now, a kind of moisture, and her cough is the wet cough of the deep galleries at its worst, and she is thin, and she is bent, and she is busy.

Rukh sweeps the drainage-catch every morning. She sweeps it with a broom of bundled glass-iron wire, and the chthonic scatter before her and then close behind her, and she hums as she sweeps, and the hum is the hum of the chthonic, indistinguishable, and her grandchildren — the few of them who have survived to visit her in this damp cold place — say that she has become one of them, that the cousins have taken her, that she is no longer quite a person in the way the upper castes mean person.

This is not true. Rukh is a person. She is a person who has been in the deep galleries for eighty-one years and who knows every chisel-mark on every wall in her section of the mountain, and who can tell you, by running her hand along the stone, who carved which stroke and when, and who has taught every child of the first in her generation to read the chisel-marks the way an upper-city scholar reads a book. Rukh is a person who has spent a lifetime in the presence of the cousins, and who regards them with the fond contempt of her caste, and who has named three of them, privately, against the customs of the deep galleries: a particularly large one she calls *Old Father*, and two smaller ones she calls *First Daughter* and *Second Daughter*, and no one knows if this is heresy or merely eccentricity, and no one has been willing to ask her, because Rukh is old, and old people in Kharad-Ruun are permitted small heresies.

Rukh will die soon. She knows this. She has carved her own chisel-mark already, in the wall of her gallery, in letters deep and clear: *Rukh of the first, eighty-one years, who kept the catch*. Beneath her name she has carved, in the old style, the phrase: *let it be known that we were here*. The chisel-marks are fresh. The mountain is young.

## V. The Child in the Upper Tier

There is a white-blue child, very young, who lives in the uppermost tier, where the engine-light is white and the aerosol hangs in the air like luminous brown fog. She is six. She has been called for the burning, which is to say, she has been brought up from the cyan-blue castes to the priesthood of the vents, and she has survived her first year, which is to say she is now a white-blue, and she is now being taught the names of the heat-exchange towers and the prayers that go with each one.

The child is not afraid. Children of the calling are not afraid, or rather, they are afraid in the way that all children of six are afraid, which is to say, intermittently, and of specific things — the loud hiss of a vent cycling, the strange shape of a breather-mask, the absence of her cyan-blue parents in the dim and distant tiers below. The child is being taught by an old white-blue, a man called Ushun, who has perhaps two years left before his lungs fail entirely, and who is teaching her the liturgy with the patient careful attention of someone who knows that the teaching is the thing, that the teaching is what will outlast him, that the child is the protest against the future that the upper tier makes in place of chisel-marks.

The child has not yet been permitted to descend to the magma chamber. She has seen the heat-exchange towers from a distance, and the great plume of the aerosol rising above them, and the upper sky — such sky as there is, the thin cold star-pricked darkness of extreme altitude, visible only at the very top of the city, only when the plume parts. The child has seen the stars once. She does not know what they are. The white-blues do not speak of the outside; the outside is sterile, the outside is a hundred kilometers of metallic dust, the outside is the place where the shadow of the city falls. The child has seen the stars, and she has asked Ushun what they are, and Ushun has said, *they are the chisels of someone else*, and the child has not understood, and Ushun has not explained, because the explanation is forbidden, and because Ushun is tired, and because there are more prayers to teach.

The child will live perhaps twenty more years. The child will learn the liturgy, and the names of the towers, and the prayers for the feeding of the magma chamber. The child will die as the white-blues die, of the slow compromise of the lungs, in the upper tier, in the white light. The child will carve her name, when she is old enough to hold a chisel, in the upper walls, in the place set aside for the priesthood, and the name will be fresh, and the name will be known, and the city will have one more mark against the future.

## VI. The Mule in the Cart-Station

There is a mule in the central cart-station of the middle tiers, an old gray animal with a network of fine glass-iron capillaries visible through her pale skin in certain lights. Her name is Teth. She is twenty-six, which is old for a mule, and she has been pulling the same ore-cart along the same ridge in the same station for sixteen years. Teth knows the station the way Rukh knows the chisel-marks: completely, bodily, in her six feet and her warm gray skin.

Teth cannot see. Teth has never seen. Teth follows the ridge by feel, and the ridge leads her to the ore-pile, and the ore-pile is loaded into her cart by a cyan-blue boy of fourteen who whistles as he works, and Teth pulls the cart to the smelter, and the smelter takes the ore, and Teth returns along the ridge, and the cycle begins again. Teth is content. *Content* is the wrong word; the cyan-blues do not use that word, and Teth is not a thing that has language to be content in. But Teth is doing the work her body was made to do, and the work is steady, and the work is warm, and the *squeeze* in her capillaries is steady, and the cyan-blue boy whistles, and the smell of the smelter is the smell of her whole life, and she is, in her own limited way, well.

Teth has had foals. Teth has had seven foals in her twenty-six years, and the foals are recorded in the great book in the central engine-hall, and the foals have gone to other cart-stations, and Teth does not know this, because Teth does not know what *other* means, because there is no other for a mule. There is only this station, this ridge, this cart, this ore, this boy who whistles. Teth will die in this station, in harness, mid-pull, perhaps, her great warm body simply stopping, and the cyan-blues will note her death in the book, and the book will be updated, and a new mule will be brought from the breeding-tier to take her place, and the station will not pause. The station cannot pause. The engines are hungry. The engines are always hungry.

Teth does not know about the engines. Teth does not know about the magma chamber, or the priests, or the eventual erosion, or the chisel-marks in the deep galleries. Teth knows the ridge, and the cart, and the ore, and the boy. This is enough.

## VII. The City, As Inhabitant

And there is one more inhabitant, perhaps, that ought to be named, though it is not a person and not an animal and not a thing that the castes would recognize as alive.

The city itself.

Kharad-Ruun is not, strictly, alive. It does not breathe, in the way a person breathes, though the chthonic breathe for it, in the deep galleries, in their slow capillary way. It does not think. It does not intend. It runs on engines and it is tended by priests and it is extended, always extended, by the deep-blues with their chisels, and the engines are the heart of it, and the priests are the mind of it, and the deep-blues are the hands of it, and the chthonic are the gut, and the mules are the blood, and the people are, all of them, every caste, every age, every shift, the whole stubborn busy careful working body of it.

The city is busy. The city is always busy. The engines run. The chthonic digest. The mules pull. The chisels tap. The glass-iron grows in the seams of the cooling basalt and is harvested and forged into beams and laid into floors. The pneumatic tubes whisper. The markets clink. The priests pray. The children learn. The old die. The names are carved. The chisel-marks are fresh.

The mountain is young.

The city is old.

The city remembers.
