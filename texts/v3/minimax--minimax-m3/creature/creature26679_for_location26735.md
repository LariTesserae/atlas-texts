What lives here has learned to be slow.

This is the first thing to understand about the inhabitants of the Halver-Veld, and it is the only thing that matters. Everything that lives on the plateau has had to negotiate, over deep time, with the same set of constraints: the wind, the stone, the scarcity, the thin warm air that asks nothing of the body and gives nothing back. The creatures that have stayed are the creatures that stopped hurrying. The ones that did not stop — the migratory grazers from the coastal bands, the seasonal birds that once passed through — are gone now, or only ghosts in the local speech. What remains is what fits.

And what fits is strange.

## The Dust-Eaters

The dust-eaters are the heart of the place. Everything else orbits them. They are the size of a small dog, though the comparison is misleading; a small dog is quick and has opinions about the world, and the dust-eater has neither. The body is broad and low-slung, built close to the stone, with the kind of economy of form that you see in things that have been shaped by pressure rather than by intention. The legs are thick at the root and taper down to wide, padded feet that look, at a glance, almost like the hooves of something larger. The head is blunt. The muzzle is the most distinctive feature — broad and flat and slightly cleft at the end, with a tough cartilaginous lip that meets the stone without injury, and a small mouth behind it set with teeth that are not teeth in any familiar sense but rather a kind of grinding plate, mineral on mineral, that wears the rock down the way a millstone wears grain.

Their color is the color of the calcium-stone they live on. A pale dusty cream, with the faint iridescence of the bloom caught in the coarse guard-hairs of their coats. They have no undercoat. They do not need one. The temperature here does not vary enough, and the wind that would strip a mammal's heat is the same wind that has, over generations, selected for a hide that is closer to a rind than a fur — tough, slightly waxy, almost reptilian in its resistance to abrasion. You can rub it the wrong way and it does not mat. The dust just falls off.

They graze. This is the verb the locals use, though *graze* is a loan from a world with grass in it, and there is no grass here. What they do is press the flat of the muzzle to the stone and move forward, slowly, with the patient shuffling gait of something that is in no way interested in arriving anywhere. The stone comes up in fine powder. It goes in. The digestion of it is the mystery that no one has fully resolved; what is known is that the powder passes through a gut that is long and many-chambered, that it spends weeks in there, that it is worked on by a chemistry that seems to involve heat and bacterial action and something else — something that may be related to the bloom itself, some precursor, some pre-precipitate — and that what comes out is finer than what went in, and is laden with the precursors of the bloom.

This is the cycle. The dust-eaters eat stone. The stone becomes, in them, the powder that becomes the bloom. The bloom spreads. The dust-eaters eat the bloom too — they will press their muzzles to a stone crusted with the lacework and lick it clean, slowly, with the unhurried dedication of something that knows it has all the time there is. They are both cause and consequence of the crust that covers their plateau. They are, in a sense, the plateau's metabolism made visible. When they are abundant the bloom thickens. When the bloom thickens they become more abundant. The cycle is not closed — there is leakage, there are losses to the wind, there are inputs from the trace chemistry of rain — but it is almost closed, and it has been almost closed for longer than the town.

They are quiet. The only sound they make is the slow grinding of the internal stones — a low continuous crunch that you can hear if you are standing near them, that is the sound of the digestion itself, the millstones of the gut working, and that becomes, after a few days on the plateau, indistinguishable from the wind. They make no cry. They have no language. They seem, as far as anyone can tell, to have no internal life that requires expression. They are, by any human measure, dim. They are also, by any honest measure, perfectly adapted. They have been here longer than the town. They will be here after.

In the town they are workers. They pull small flat carts, harnessed at the chest with a yoke of dark iron-band leather that is the only machinery of traction that has ever worked for them — they refuse, with the stubbornness of something that does not know how to refuse, any more complicated rigging. They pull the slag-carts from the smelters. They pull the dust-carts through the converter halls, where the fine calcium-powder accumulates in drifts after a shift, and they clean it, slowly, with their muzzles, while the converters stand cold and dark around them. They are fed, in the dry season, on a paste of smelter-slag that the humans prepare for them in wide shallow troughs; the paste is grey-black and smells, to a human nose, of iron and sulfur and something older, and the dust-eaters eat it with the same unhurried attention they give to stone. They do not seem to mind the captivity. They do not seem to notice it. They appear to have no concept of anywhere else.

The oldest of them, the ones that work the inner yards, are very large. No one breeds them deliberately; no one has, in living memory. But the oldest are noticeably larger than the younger ones, and the oldest of the oldest are larger still, and no one knows why. They simply do not die. Or they die very rarely, and very late, and the deaths that are noticed are usually from injury — a fall from a rail-trestle, a crush in the converter gates — and not from age. There is a dust-eater in the Old Works yard, working a slag-cart, that the oldest worker there swears was working the same yard when he was a boy, and that was working it when his father was a boy. It is the size of a small pony. It has been named, unofficially, *Old Grey*. It moves slower than the others. It has been moved, in some sense, by time itself.

## The Frilled Lizards

The frilled lizards are the most visible of the other fauna, which is to say they are visible at all only because they like the iron-grey rocks of the lower strata, and because they are large. A full-grown adult is the length of a man's arm and a third, with the long whip-tail that takes up most of that length, and the broad splayed body that is pressed flat against the warm stone in the hours when the sun is high. They are pale — almost the same cream as the dust-eaters, but with a cooler, slightly bluish undertone, and with the iridescent bloom-dust so thoroughly worked into the fine scales of their backs that they appear to be wearing a kind of glazed armor. In certain lights, at certain hours, they look like living porcelain.

The frill is what gives them their name. It is a semicircular membrane of thin skin stretched between cartilaginous spines that run from the back of the skull down along the neck, and it lies flat, most of the time, folded close against the body like a collapsed fan. When the lizard is alarmed — when a foot comes too close, when the shadow of a bird passes over it — it lifts the frill with a sudden percussive snap, the spines spreading in less than a second, and the membrane filling with blood and standing out from the head in a wide pale disc that is, against the iron-grey rock, almost luminous. The display is not aggressive. It is startle, pure and simple — a way of making the body's outline uncertain for the crucial half-second it takes for a predator to decide whether the thing in front of it is worth the trouble. The lizard holds the display for two or three seconds and folds it, and goes back to its bask, and the predator, more often than not, has already moved on.

The frill is also, the locals believe, a solar collector. They have noticed that the lizards orient themselves, in the cooler hours of the morning, so that the frill — held half-open, half-folded, at an angle — is presented broadside to the sun, and that the membrane, when held so, is shot with fine capillaries that flush visibly. The lizard is warming itself through the frill. It is a slow way to warm. Nothing here is fast.

They eat the bloom. They lick the stone, slowly, with long pale tongues that are forked at the tip and slightly prehensile, and they work over the lacework patterns of the crust with the meticulous patience of something that is not in any hurry and has no competition. The bloom is their principal food. They take in the powder, they process it through a gut that is, like the dust-eater's, long and strange and adapted to mineral digestion, and they excrete it — a finer powder still, more thoroughly digested, that is one of the secondary sources of the bloom's spread. They are not as efficient as the dust-eaters. They do not cover the ground the way the dust-eaters do. But they are numerous, and they are persistent, and they are everywhere on the exposed iron-grey rock where the bloom has had time to take hold.

They bask in company. A good stone, in the warm hours, will have a dozen of them on it — small ones, large ones, juveniles with their frills still folded tight against their necks and old ones with their frills torn and re-torn from a hundred startle displays. They do not interact. They do not seem to acknowledge each other. They are simply together, in the way that rocks are together, and the colony of basking lizards on a warm stone is, like the colony of barnacles on a pier, more a matter of shared conditions than of social organization. They are quiet. They make no sound. The only motion on a basking stone, for long minutes at a time, is the slow pulse of the frills and the occasional flick of a pale tongue.

They are not domesticated. The locals do not try. They are sometimes eaten — the meat is said to be tough and slightly mineral, not unpleasant — but rarely, and more as a tradition than as a food practice, and the eating of them is governed by a set of quiet informal rules that amount, in effect, to a taboo on taking more than is offered. The plateau gives what it gives. You do not press it.

## The Pale Birds

The birds are not birds in any sense a naturalist from elsewhere would recognize. They are small — the size of a sparrow, with a sparrow's quickness of eye — but the body is wrong, and the wing is wrong, and the song is wrong. The body is too long for the wing. The wing is too narrow for the body. The feather is not quite a feather; it is closer to a coarse filament, brittle and slightly translucent, with the iridescent dust of the bloom so worked into its barbules that the bird appears, in motion, to be trailing a faint shimmering wake. They are pale. They are almost colorless. They are the color of the dust they live in.

They live on the wind-carved ridges. They nest in the lee of the larger hills, in scrapes they make in the calcium-powder of the gutters, lined with a few fibers they pull from somewhere — old slag-wool, dried bloom-strands, anything fine and pale. They lay two eggs, sometimes three. The eggs are pale and slightly translucent and very hard. The chicks, when they hatch, are blind and downy and almost perfectly still for the first days of their lives, and the stillness is so complete that the local saying for a child that does not cry is *hatched on the ridges*.

They eat the bloom. They eat small insects that eat the bloom. They eat, occasionally, the smaller of the frilled lizards, though the lizards are too quick for them most of the time and the predation is, in practice, negligible. They are not, despite appearances, predators. They are gleaners. They work the bloom-encrusted stone for whatever the bloom has concentrated in it — trace iron, trace calcium, the small beetles, the small worms — and they take it in, and they go on.

Their flight is the most distinctive thing about them. They do not fly the way birds fly. They do not fly the way anything flies. They hang. They set their narrow wings against the prevailing wind and they hang in it, motionless, sometimes for minutes at a time, their bodies tilted at an angle to the draft that holds them in place the way a kite is held in place, and from this position they survey the ground below them and they drop, when they have chosen a spot, with a folded-wing plunge that is more a surrender to gravity than a flight. They land. They feed. They walk back up the slope of the wind-carved hill, into the wind, until they have height again, and they jump, and they catch the wind, and they hang.

The song is the other distinctive thing. They have one note. It is a single repeated tone — high, clear, slightly querulous — that sounds almost like a question and almost like a complaint and is, in fact, neither. It is a territorial marker. It is a mate-call. It is the only vocalization the birds make, and they make it constantly, and the plateau, in the warm hours, is full of it — a single sustained note, repeated from a hundred ridges, slightly out of phase with itself, layering into a kind of shimmering chord that is, if you listen to it long enough, the most melancholy sound on the Halver-Veld. The locals are used to it. They do not remark on it. They have not remarked on it for generations. But the visitors from the lowlands sometimes cannot sleep for it, and sometimes cannot stop listening to it, and sometimes weep without knowing why.

The birds are numerous. They are not as numerous as the dust-eaters, but they are present in every part of the plateau, and their note is the constant backdrop of the place. They outlive the dust-eaters. They outlive the lizards. They outlive, by a wide margin, the humans. No one knows how long an individual bird lives. The locals do not mark them. They are, in the local reckoning, more like weather than like fauna — a condition of the place, like the wind itself, rather than an inhabitant of it.

## The Beetles

The beetles are small and pale and slow and there are very many of them. They are the size of a fingernail, with the rounded backs of scarabs and the pale dusty color of the calcium-stone. They live in the gutters between the wind-carved hills. They eat the bloom. They do something with the bloom that no one has ever explained.

What they do is this: they gather the powder. They take it, mouthfull by mouthfull, from the crusted stone, and they work it — they knead it, with the small forelegs, into a compact sphere, slightly larger than their own bodies. They roll the sphere. They roll it across the stone, slowly, with the patient deliberation of something that is not going anywhere in particular and knows it. They find a place. They dig, with the small forelegs, a small depression in the powder. They bury the sphere. They walk away.

No one has ever opened a buried sphere and found anything alive inside. No one has ever opened one and found a larva. No one has ever opened one and found anything at all except the powder, slightly compacted, slightly different in its chemistry — slightly more concentrated, slightly more *something* — than the powder around it. The beetles are not farming. They are not caching food for a future they will not see. They are doing something else, something that has no obvious analogue in any other ecology, and the doing of it is, in its small way, one of the engines of the bloom.

The buried spheres are, the locals have noticed, the places where the bloom grows thickest. Where a beetle has buried a sphere, the lacework of the crust that comes up in the next season is denser, finer, more iridescent than the crust around it. The beetles are, in some sense, planting the bloom. They do not know they are planting it. They are doing what their ancestors did, in the same gutters, in the same powder, for as long as there have been gutters and powder. The bloom is grateful. The bloom grows.

The beetles are everywhere. They are, in their numbers, the most successful fauna on the plateau, and they are also the least noticed. You have to look down to see them. You have to look down and you have to look slowly. The locals, who have lived here all their lives, have looked down slowly so often that they can find a beetle within a minute of standing on a gutter, and the visitors from the lowlands, who do not know how to look, sometimes spend hours on the plateau without seeing one. They are not invisible. They are simply very small, and very patient, and they have been here for a long time.

## The Deep Worms

The worms live in the deeper rock. No one sees them. No one has seen one in living memory, except in the exposed faces of the deeper strata after a long dry season has cracked the surface crusts open, and even then what is seen is a thin pale length, lying white against the dark iron-band stone, and it is gone — withdrawn back into the crack, into the stone, into wherever the worms go — within minutes of the air touching it.

They are long. The fragments that have been measured, in the rare exposures, have been many meters in length, and the fragments are always fragments — the worm withdraws before the full length is exposed, and no one has ever seen an end. No one knows how long they are. No one knows how old they are. They are simply there, in the stone, somewhere below the level of the bloom, below the level of the dust, below the level of almost everything else on the plateau, and they are doing something that keeps the deeper rock from collapsing into itself.

They eat the stone. This is known. The exposed fragments, when they have been examined closely — by a researcher from the lowland university, decades ago, who came up for a season and left and never came back — showed a gut-pellet of fine dark powder, iron-rich, very old, very compressed. The worms are processing the deeper strata of the plateau. They are, in their slow and hidden way, the reason the iron-band rock is not a single solid mass. They are the reason the deeper layers have the cross-hatch of crystallization that says *this was liquid once and was not hurried*. The worms are, in some sense, the reason the plateau is layered at all.

No one has ever tried to domesticate them. No one has ever tried to study them at length. They are too deep. They are too slow. They are, in the local reckoning, the foundation-stones of the place — the things that were here before anything else, that will be here after everything else, that do not concern themselves with what is happening on the surface because the surface is not where they live. The locals do not speak of them. The locals do not speak *to* them. They are simply part of the place, in the way that the deeper rock is part of the place, and they are treated with the same distant respect.

## The Grey Watchers

The grey watchers are the question that the plateau does not answer.

They are seen. That is the first thing to be said about them, and it is the only thing that can be said with certainty. They are seen, on the highest of the wind-carved hills, in the hours when the light is low — at dawn, at dusk, in the grey half-light of the very early morning. They stand. They are tall. They are thin. They are the color of the iron-band rock, with a faint shimmer of bloom-dust caught on them, and they do not move.

This is what is known. The rest is not known. They do not appear to be any of the other fauna. They are too tall for the lizards, too still for the birds, too narrow for the dust-eaters. They do not appear to eat. They do not appear to drink. They do not appear to do anything at all except stand on the high hills and not move. They are not always in the same place. A watcher that is seen on a hill one morning will not be there the next morning, but a watcher will be on a different hill, the next morning, and on another hill the morning after that, and no one has ever kept track of them closely enough to determine whether it is the same watcher or different watchers or whether the distinction is meaningful.

They have never been approached. This is not because they cannot be approached. It is because no one has tried. The locals do not go to the high hills at the dawn hours. The locals do not go to the high hills at any hours. The high hills are, in the local reckoning, not for the locals. The high hills are for the watchers. The watchers were there before the locals. The watchers will be there after. The locals do not speak of them except in the oblique way that they speak of weather — *a watcher was on the East Mound this morning* — and they do not ask what the watchers are. They have not asked, in living memory. They have not asked, in the memory of any memory.

The visitors from the lowlands ask. The visitors from the lowlands always ask. The locals do not answer, except to say that the watchers are there, and that the watchers are not to be approached, and that this is the rule. The rule is not enforced. The rule is observed. No one from the town has ever gone up to a watcher. No one from the lowlands, in the recorded history of the lowlands' interest in the Halver-Veld, has ever come back from an approach to a watcher. The watchers are not known to be dangerous. The watchers are not known to be anything. They are simply not to be approached. This is the rule.

What they are is the question that the plateau holds open. They might be fauna — some large pale cousin of the lizards, some rare and solitary dust-eater grown to improbable size. They might be the bloom itself, in some configuration the locals have not seen. They might be something else, something that has no analogue in the other ecologies of the world and that lives here because this is the kind of place that holds what does not fit elsewhere. They might be watchers. They might be nothing. They are there, on the high hills, in the low light, and they do not move, and they are not approached, and this is the rule.

## The Humans

The humans are the latest.

They came up from the lowlands, three centuries ago or four, and they cut the Old Works out of the calcium-stone, and they have been here ever since. They are smaller than the visitors from the lowlands — a hand shorter, on average, than the people of the coast — and the shortness is, the locals believe, an adaptation to the elevation, to the thin air, to the slow labor of mining the iron-band rock in an atmosphere that does not give a body much. They are pale, like the other fauna. Their hair is pale. Their eyes are pale. They wear the calcium-dust in the creases of their hands and the corners of their eyes and the dust is, after a generation or two, indistinguishable from their skin.

They are not numerous. There are a few thousand of them in the town, and the number has been falling for decades, and they know it is falling, and they have stopped trying to stop it. The smelters run at a third. The converters run at a third. The rail runs once or twice a day. The young leave, when they leave, and they go down to the lowlands, and they do not come back. The old stay. The old work the converters and the smelters and they tend the dust-eaters and they feed the slag to the dust-eaters in the wide shallow troughs and they listen to the bells of the converter halls ring at the start and end of each shift, and the bells carry for miles across the plateau, and the old hear them, and they go on.

They are quiet. They speak softly. They do not gather in crowds. They move through the town the way the dust-eaters move through the gutters, with the patient unhurried gait of things that have been here long enough to have stopped being in a hurry. They live in the calcium-stone houses of the Old Works and the more recent smelter-rings, and the houses are low and stout and they follow the contours of the wind-carved hills, and the wind moves through them in eddies and the calcium-dust settles in the corners and the corners are not cleaned, because the cleaning of them is the work of the dust-eaters, and the dust-eaters do it slowly, and the slow is enough.

They have rules. They do not fight the wind. They do not approach the high hills at the dawn hours. They do not take more from the plateau than the plateau offers. They feed the dust-eaters in the dry season and they let the dust-eaters graze the slag and they clean the converter halls with the broad flat muzzles of the dust-eaters and they let the bloom grow on the slag-heaps and they do not scrape it off. They have, over generations, settled into a long equilibrium with the place they sit on. They are not natives. They are not, in the deepest sense, residents. They are, in their own slow way, becoming part of the place — another stratum in the layering of the plateau, another layer in the long slow accretion of the bloom.

They will be here for a while longer. The converters will run, at a third, for another fifty years. The smelters will run, at a third, for another fifty. The rail will run, thinned, for another fifty. After that — the old say, when they say it at all — the town will settle back into the plateau. The converters will rust. The foundations will crack. The bloom will cover the slag-heaps and the rail-beds and the dark iron cubes of the smelter-rings, the way moss covers a fallen log. The dust-eaters will graze across the ruins. The birds will hang in the wind and sing their single note. The wind will hush. The plateau will be the plateau again.

The locals know this. They do not mind. They are, in their own unhurried way, as patient as the dust-eaters and as quiet as the birds. They are the latest layer of the place. They will not be the last. They have made their peace with the wind. They have made their peace with the bloom. They have made their peace with the watchers on the high hills, who were here before them and who will be here after. They have stopped being in a hurry.

This is what lives on the Halver-Veld. The dust-eaters and the frilled lizards and the pale birds and the slow beetles and the deep worms and the watchers on the high hills and the quiet humans in their calcium-stone houses. The wind and the bloom and the slow patience of a place that has been here longer than anything that lives on it and will be here after everything that lives on it is gone.

This is the Halver-Veld. This is what lives here.
