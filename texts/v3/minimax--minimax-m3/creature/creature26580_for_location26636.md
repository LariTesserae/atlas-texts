What lives here lives slowly. This is the first thing to learn, and it takes a long time to learn it because the body keeps insisting on its own clocks — the rhythms of breath and step and the small clockwork of digestion — and the place insists on its own, and the two clocks do not keep together. The microbes in the damp zone beneath your feet may divide once a week. Some of them, perhaps most of them, are not dividing at all at the moment you are standing on them. They are waiting. The waiting is not passive. They are maintaining their gradients, their ion pumps working continuously against the osmotic pressure of the brine that surrounds them, their pigments absorbing photons that the salt crust has not yet managed to filter out, their enzymes folding and refolding proteins at temperatures that would unfold a mammal. They are alive the way a held note is music. The note is not moving forward; it is sustaining itself in place against the tendency to decay.

The microbes are the only animates in the place. The basin told you this, and it is almost true, and almost true is the truth here. The microbes are many, and they are layered, and the layers are different. In the uppermost millimeters of the crust, where the salt is freshly precipitated and the light comes through in pale columns, the community is dominated by things that can use light directly — *Halobacterium* and its relatives, the haloarchaea, which carry purple membranes studded with a protein called bacteriorhodopsin. Bacteriorhodopsin is not chlorophyll. It is a single protein, and the protein changes shape when it absorbs a photon, and the shape-change pumps a proton across the microbial membrane, and the proton moving back down its gradient drives the synthesis of ATP. The archaea are, in effect, photosynthesizing in a way that has nothing to do with plants, and they are doing it beneath two centimeters of salt that has cracked and been resmoothed by a season of thermal expansion. Some of them are pink. The pools in the lowest pockets, where the water is still and reflects the sky, sometimes carry a faint pink wash at the edges that you might mistake for iron staining if you did not know better. It is not iron. It is the color of a billion archaea pressed against the meniscus, breathing light and waiting.

Below the archaea is a denser community, in the damp zone proper, where moisture persists from one evaporation event to the next and the temperature swings are softened. Here the populations shift. Halophilic bacteria — *Salinibacter*, *Halomonas*, and a long roster of genera that have been named only in the last forty years because the field is young — work alongside the archaea in a kind of uneasy cohabitation, eating what the archaea leak, leaching what the salt has buried. Some of them reduce sulfate. The sulfate in this place is abundant — it is the legacy of evaporating seawater, the anion that paired with the calcium and the sodium and the magnesium as the brine concentrated over the basin's long history — and certain microbes use it the way animals use oxygen, passing electrons to it and harvesting the small energy released. They breathe sulfate. The product of their breathing is sulfide, which is sometimes visible as a faint darkening in the lower part of a fractured section: a strip of grey or black that does not belong to the iron-seam palette and that is the only sign you will see of a chemistry happening at a depth your knees are resting on.

Below the damp zone, where the salt thins into the underlying sediment, the community shifts again. Here the conditions are anoxic, or nearly so. Here the work is slower and stranger. The microbes here are methanogens and sulfate reducers operating at the limit of what is biologically possible. They turn carbon into methane, or sulfate into sulfide, or — if the conditions are just right, and they almost never are — they precipitate carbonate. The basin told you this: the microbes precipitated carbonate and bound the crystals into a more coherent fabric than crystallization alone could manage. It is the most important thing the microbes do. They are the masons of the floor. They take the loose crystals left by evaporation and cement them into a pavement that can hold the weight of a person walking across it. Remove the microbes and the basin is a powder. Add them back and the powder becomes a crust, layer upon layer, season upon season, the construction that your eye is standing on.

They have been doing this for a long time. The crust is, in places, hundreds of layers thick, and each layer was their work in some small fraction. The deepest layers are also theirs, but compressed and altered, the cells long dead, the carbonate they precipitated now indistinguishable from the carbonate that fell out of solution on its own. The microbes are not the only masons. They are the masons that distinguish this crust from a pile of salt.

It is warm down here, and the warmth is partly theirs. Their metabolism is small, but it is many, and it is constant, and over the years it adds up to a heat the salt holds better than the air can shed. The microbial layer is a warm layer. On a still afternoon, if you laid your palm flat against the crust and held it there for a minute, you could feel the difference between the topmost salt — which has been losing heat to the sky since dawn — and the deeper damp zone, which has been holding the microbial heat. The difference is half a degree. It is enough.

Above the microbes, the heat. Above the heat, the light. Above the light, the silence and the slow vertical weather and the columns of refracting air. Below them, the deeper brine — a saturated solution that has been there longer than the shrub has been there, longer than the present shape of the crust, longer than the present arrangement of the seams. The brine is itself a kind of life, or a kind of memory of life. The microbes live in the brine. The brine is the medium they inhabit, the way fish inhabit the sea. The brine has its own slow circulation, driven by heat and concentration gradients, and the microbes ride it.

The shrub is harder to describe because it is one and they are many, and it does not behave as a community. The shrub is a single organism, and it is the wrong organism here. It is the only vertical thing in the field of view, and its verticality is an argument with the place rather than an accommodation to it. It has the posture of something that has been forced, over a long time, to compress itself into a shape that presents the least surface to the wind. Its leaves are reduced to grey scales. It photosynthesizes through them. It does not cast a real shadow because the sun is high and the scales are small and the angle is wrong, and so it persists at the edge of being noticed, half-invisible against the pale crust behind it.

It is probably an *Atriplex* — a saltbush — or perhaps a *Sarcobatus*, or one of the *Tamarix* that have colonized arid salt margins across the world. It does not matter which. What matters is that it is here, and that being here is an act of will. The salt concentration in the soil beneath it is high enough to kill most plants by osmotic stress — water pulled out of the roots faster than the roots can replace it. The shrub survives by compartmentalizing. It sequesters salt in special cells in its leaves and then, periodically, it sheds those leaves. The leaves it sheds are salty. They fall to the crust around its base and bleach there, and they are the only litter the place receives, and they are not enough to feed a decomposer community. They dry. They crack. They become indistinguishable from the floor. The shrub is, in this sense, a closed system. It does not feed the place. It persists in the place. The distinction matters.

The shrub is old. You can tell by its posture — the central stem leaning into the prevailing wind, the dead wood visible at the base where older stems have been killed by drought or salt-burn and then replaced, the canopy reduced to a tight dome that suggests a long argument with the air. A hundred years. Perhaps more. The basin was different a hundred years ago — different in ways that the crust does not record, because the crust is too young, the floor you are walking on was laid down after the shrub had already learned to hunch. The shrub is older than the floor. The shrub remembers a floor that this floor has replaced.

---

There are other things that live here, if you stretch the word. The water in the lowest pockets lives, in the way that any bounded system lives — it is finite, it is active, it is responding to its conditions. The water arrives from somewhere — from a brief rain the basin cannot remember, from a slow seep from the aquifer below — and it sits, and it warms, and it loses its solvent to the air, and it surrenders its load. While it sits it is a habitat. The pink wash at its edges is not only archaea. It is also *Dunaliella*, a green alga that turns orange-red when the salinity rises above what most algae can tolerate. *Dunaliella* produces glycerol to balance its internal osmotic pressure, and it produces beta-carotene as a sunscreen against the light that has nowhere to be filtered. The orange you might see at the edge of a drying pool is the alga's stress becoming color. The algae are eaten by small flagellates. The flagellates are eaten by nothing here, and so they die and fall to the floor and are bound in by the next precipitation event, and the carbon they carried becomes part of the crust. The pool is a small ecosystem, bounded and brief.

The crystals themselves — do they live? The basin treats them as alive in places. They grow. They expand. They crack and heal. They have a metabolism of sorts, in that they are always exchanging ions with the brine that surrounds them, always approaching an equilibrium that they never quite reach because the conditions keep changing. The crystals are not alive in any way a biologist would recognize. But they are not inert either. They are in a continual low-grade negotiation with their environment, and they have structure — lattices, defects, fluid inclusions — that records the conditions they grew in. The inclusions are little time capsules. Each one holds a sample of the brine from the moment the crystal grew around it. If you cracked open a halite crystal from this crust you could read its history: the temperature of the water, the concentration of the ions, sometimes even a bubble of the atmosphere that was dissolved in the brine at the time. The crystals remember. Memory is not life. But it is not nothing.

The heat lives. The wind lives, in the hours it spends here. The light lives, briefly, between the sky and the salt before it is either reflected or absorbed. The silence is the most present animate of all, and it is the silence of a place being finished by processes that do not require witnesses. The microbes are doing their slow work beneath your feet. The shrub is holding its argument with the wind. The water in the pools is surrendering its load. The crust is being made. The crust is being unmade. The making and the unmaking are the same operation observed from two sides.

The basin goes on.
