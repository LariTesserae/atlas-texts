# The Inhabitants of the Lifted Shelf

What lives here is mostly small, and mostly patient, and most of it has only just arrived.

---

The mosses are the true citizens, though they have no politics about it. There is a *Racomitrium lanuginosum* that has come up from below and is doing what it does on bare rock the world over — sending its rhizoids into the minute cracks, gripping the granite with a ferocity that has nothing to do with intent and everything to do with water retention. It grows in tight hoary cushions, the color of old dishwater, and when the wind lifts it you can see how it is anchored: a pale fuzz of root-hairs against darker stone. Beside it, in the wetter seams, are the *Andreaea*, the lantern mosses — almost black, almost crustlike, the color of something that has decided to be stone so that stone will not bother it. They live where the water drips constantly, and they are the darkest living things on the shelf.

Between them, the *Sphagnum* has begun. Only just. You find it in the shallow basins where rainwater stands for weeks at a time, in the cupped tops of boulders, in the depressions behind the turbine halls where the drainage has nowhere to go. It is not the great red bog-sphagnum of the old mires; it is a tentative species, perhaps *Sphagnum compactum*, growing in low tight domes the size of a fist, the color of a bruise — that pale yellow-green at the top shading to near-black underneath. It is beginning, in its slow patient way, to make soil. When you pull up a fist of it you can see what it has done: a half-centimeter of pale fibrous peat beneath the green, and beneath that, the powdered beginnings of mineral soil, granite ground fine by the acids the moss has been exuding for a decade. A centimeter of new earth. In a thousand years there will be a meter.

The lichens are everywhere, and they are doing a slower version of the same work. The orange crust that paints the granite in the wet places is *Xanthoria elegans*, I think — or perhaps *Caloplaca*, a relative; the taxonomists can fight it out. It is the color of a healing wound, exactly that color, the bright rust-orange of something that has decided to live on bare rock and is announcing its presence to anyone passing. It is the only warm color on the shelf. Beside it, in drier microhabitats, are the grey-white *Rhizocarpon* maps, slow-growing, so slow that lichenologists can date the moraines behind them. The glacier that never quite was left its traces here, and the lichens are measuring them at a millimeter per century. There is a *Umbilicaria* — the rock tripe — in the most exposed places, a grey-black leafy lichen the size of a thumbnail, attached to the stone at a single point like a tiny umbrella pinned to a table. It is edible, theoretically. It is also the texture of old rubber. No one is eating it.

Among the mosses, in the early summer, the saxifrage blooms. *Saxifraga* — the rock-breaker, named for what its ancestors did over deep time, splitting the stones of mountains while the mountains were young. Here it does not need to split; the stone is already fractured, recently fractured, still sharp at the edges. The species is the purple saxifrage, *Saxifraga oppositifolia*, and it grows in tight cushions pressed so hard against the rock that the flowers seem to emerge from the stone itself. The blooms are the color of a magenta pen, an improbable pink-purple against the grey granite, and they are the only color on the shelf that the lichen does not provide. The cushion can be the size of a dinner plate and a decade old; the flowers last three weeks. The plant has a taproot that runs a hand's depth into a crack and holds on, and it is in no hurry. It will be here long after the lichen has finished painting around it.

The crowberry, *Empetrum nigrum*, has colonized the slightly deeper soils — behind boulders, in the lee of the turbine halls, in any place where dust and dead moss have accumulated to a depth of two centimeters. It is a low creeping shrub, never more than a finger's height, with tiny dark evergreen leaves and a stem that runs along the ground rooting where it touches. It does not yet flower here in any abundance. The plants I have seen are vegetative, spreading vegetatively, sending out their runners across the moss like the cables of a system that has not yet decided to broadcast. In another decade, perhaps, when a real soil has accumulated, it will berry. The berries will be black and unremarkable, and the ptarmigan will eat them, and the seeds will pass through, and the crowberry will move.

There are other small flowers in the moss, very few, very scattered. A white star of something in the crowberry family — *Diapensia lapponica*, I think, the pincushion plant, forming mats so tight they look embroidered. A yellow composite that may be a *Hieracium*, a hawkweed, the color of weak mustard, blooming on a stem three inches tall, the whole plant so small and tentative it seems like an apology. A *Silene acaulis*, the moss campion, in one place on a south-facing shelf, a hemisphere of tight green moss with pink flowers erupting from the surface like a small celebration that is embarrassed at itself. Each of these plants is a single genotype — they are all the same plant, one clone, one individual, repeating itself across a square meter of moss and calling it a population. They arrived once, from one seed, and have been spreading vegetatively ever since, waiting for the second arrival, the crossing individual, the genetic mixing that will let them actually evolve.

---

The animals are few, and most of them are visitors.

The ptarmigan is the largest thing that lives here year-round, and even it is a marginal presence. *Lagopus muta*, the rock ptarmigan, the bird of the high barrens, weighing perhaps half a kilo, dressed in winter white that becomes summer brown with a mottled transitional plumage in between. There are perhaps a dozen birds on the shelf, perhaps two dozen — the population fluctuates with the severity of the previous winter, with the food supply, with whatever drives ptarmigan to wander. They live among the rocks and the moss, eating the crowberry leaves and the buds of the saxifrage and the occasional insect, and they are almost invisible against the granite in their summer plumage. You will be walking across the shelf and a bird will erupt from beneath your foot with a hoarse *arr-krr* of alarm, and you will be startled, and then you will see it — a brown shape hurtling away at head-height on whistling wings, casting a sharp shadow on the moss, and then it will be over a rise and gone, and you will stand there with your heart thumping and the impression of a wild thing.

They nest on the ground, the female excavating a shallow scrape among the rocks and lining it with a few feathers and bits of lichen. The clutch is five to eight eggs, the color of wet stone, and the chicks are precocial — they hatch and they walk, and within a day they are picking at the moss on their own. Most years some of them make it. The raven takes a few. The weather takes a few. The slow fox that comes up from below in the worst of the winters takes a few. There is a population, but it is a small one, and it is held at the edge of viability by the thinness of the resources. In a hard year there may be no successful nests at all.

The raven is here. *Corvus corax*, the only large bird on the shelf, present because ravens are present everywhere there is anything at all. There may be two of them, or three, or a single bird wandering along the watershed and ranging back down to the lowlands to roost. They patrol the turbine stations for scraps; they wait at the landing strip for offal from the small aircraft; they find the dead ptarmigan before the beetles do. They are the only bird here that is not at the edge of its range. They are at the center of theirs.

Smaller birds come through, mostly in migration. Snow buntings in flocks, bright as coins against the grey, stopping for a few days to feed on the saxifrage seeds before moving on. A wheatear, perhaps, in the summer, on its way to a more settled territory. A single ring ouzel, once, in a wet gully, surprising to both of us. These are visitors, not inhabitants. The shelf does not yet have the insect biomass to support a breeding population of small passerines. The buntings come and go and the shelf does not notice them except as a brief abundance of small droppings on the rocks.

---

The insects are the secret population, and they are the future.

Under every flat stone that has lain undisturbed for a few years, in the damp space between stone and moss, there are beetles. Ground beetles, *Carabus* species — perhaps *Carabus glabratus*, a large black beetle with a faintly metallic sheen, hunting the smaller invertebrates that live in the moss. Beside them, smaller beetles in the family Staphylinidae, the rove beetles, elongated and narrow, predator and scavenger, the cleanup crew of the small world. A weevil or two, feeding on the saxifrage, drinking the cell sap with a rostrum the length of its own head. A small grey moth that I cannot name, resting on a lichen-painted stone with its wings folded tight against its body, the color of the rock itself. The moth is a wonder: how did it get here? What does its caterpillar eat? There is not enough vegetation to support a caterpillar, but there is a moth, so the caterpillar exists, and somewhere in the moss it is finding what it needs.

Springtails are the most numerous. *Collembola*, in the hundreds of species, in the hundreds of individuals per square meter of moss. They are tiny, most of them less than two millimeters long, and they are the only abundant macroscopic animals on the shelf. They live in the interstices of the moss, in the film of water that coats every surface, and they eat the fungal hyphae that are eating the dead moss. They are the reason the moss is being broken down. They are the small digestive system of the new world, processing the pioneer vegetation into the first real soil, pellet by pellet, generation by generation. They are also prey — for the beetles, for the predatory mites that live with them, for the spiders that have arrived in ones and twos and are waiting, spider-wise, for a fly.

Spiders, yes. A *Pardosa*, a wolf spider, running across the wet rock on legs that do not seem to be quite long enough. A tiny *Theridion* building a thread no thicker than a hair between two saxifrage cushions, the web a single square of silk so fine you can only see it in the right light, when the mist parts and the sun strikes the moss and the spider's thread catches the light like a held breath. The spider is a quarter of a centimeter long, mottled grey and brown, and it is waiting for something to blunder into the thread. The something will be a springtail. The spider will wrap it in silk and eat it over the next three days. This is the drama of the shelf: a quarter-centimeter predator and a two-millimeter prey, played out beneath your boot soles in a moss that is a centimeter thick.

There are no earthworms. There are no slugs. There are no snails, though I have looked. The mollusks have not yet made it up here, or the calcium is too scarce for their shells, or the winters are still too cold. The soil is not yet an animal intestine. The springtails and the mites and the small beetles are doing the work alone, and they are doing it slowly, and the moss above them is not yet aware that it is being consumed.

Midges appear in the still air of the summer evenings, the males dancing in a vertical column above a single rock or a single moss-cushion, the females flying through and being taken. The swarm is a faint haze, a pillar of small motion, perhaps two meters tall, lasting an hour at the height of the season. The midges are the food of something — the spiders again, the small birds on passage, perhaps a bat that has wandered up from the lower country and is finding the shelf unexpectedly rich. A bat, here, would be the largest predator on the watershed. I have not seen one. I have seen the midges.

In the pools, in the slow-moving margins of the streams, there are the larvae of something. A mayfly, perhaps, or a stonefly, clinging to the underside of a stone in the current, breathing through gills that are also fins. The rivers are young and they are clean, and the invertebrate fauna is sparse but present, and the fish have not yet come up. The waterfalls are too high. In a few thousand years, perhaps, after the rivers have cut their beds down to grade, something will be able to make the ascent. For now the streams are pouring off the edge of the world with their small cargo of insect larvae and no fish to eat them.

---

And then there is the human presence, which is also an animal presence, however much the humans would prefer not to be.

There are perhaps forty people on the shelf at any one time. They come up on a rotation — three weeks on, three weeks off, the schedule of the maintenance crews and the engineers and the small administrative staff at the depot. They are mostly men, mostly in their thirties and forties, mostly from the towns along the river below. They have been working here since the stations were built, some of them, and they have begun to know the shelf the way they know any place they have worked for a long time. They know where the moss is deepest underfoot, where the streams are easy to cross, where the ptarmigan nest, which turbines are singing sharp and which are singing flat. They do not love the place, exactly, but they have a working familiarity with it that is a kind of attachment.

They eat in the mess hall at the depot, and they sleep in the bunks, and on the third week of their rotation they begin to get restless, and on the day they go down they are ready to go down. The shelf is a place to work. It is not a place to live, not yet, not in the sense that the mosses live here, which is the only sense in which anyone truly lives anywhere.

The depot is the closest thing to a settlement. It is half-buried in the north face of a cliff, a long low building of poured concrete with a radio mast at one end and a small weather station at the other. The mess hall is the largest room. The windows are small. The walls are stained with the condensation that runs down them in the wet weather, and the radiators clang in the cold weather, and there is always a kettle on somewhere, and the radio plays a station from the lowlands in a permanent low murmur that the crews stop hearing. The bunks are army-surplus, narrow, the mattresses shaped by the bodies of the men who have slept in them. There is a small library, mostly of technical manuals, and a few paperbacks in a lowland language, and a single chess set with one missing knight that has been replaced by a bottle cap.

The crews walk out to the stations along the gravel road, or they take one of the small all-terrain vehicles that hum along the compacted track from station to station, the driver and a passenger in the cab, the rest of the crew in the open back with the wind on their faces and the mist in their hair. The road is the longest human artifact on the shelf. It runs for perhaps forty kilometers in a rough circuit, connecting the thirty stations and the depot, and it is the color of the rock it was made from — a pale gravel-grey, wet most of the time, dry for a few hours in the afternoon when the wind picks up. Where it runs across the moss the moss is killed, compacted into a darker line, and the road is visible from a distance as a pale thread stitching the green-black of the watershed. It is the only sign, from a distance, that anyone is here at all.

The stations themselves are unmanned most of the time. They run themselves, mostly. A turbine hall and a small powerhouse, a penstock running up the cliff to the intake, a tailrace running back down to the river. The machinery is Swedish, I think, or Norwegian, the reputation of the Nordic engineering firms having reached even this new place. The turbines are vertical-axis, the alternators above them, the whole thing built into a small concrete blockhouse that has begun to take on the color of the moss that climbs its walls. Inside, the machine hums. The bearings are warm. The oil in the sump is clean. The instrumentation ticks over to the depot via buried cable, and the men come up when the telemetry says something is wrong, and they fix it, and they go back down.

The men at the stations, when they are there, are alone. A crew of two or three, sometimes, walking through the turbine hall with a wrench and a notebook, listening to the bearings, checking the oil, looking for the small irregularities that will become large ones. They eat their lunches on the concrete apron outside the powerhouse, sitting on a pipe, looking out at the gorge and the falling water and the moss. They do not talk much. The noise of the water and the machinery is too constant for easy conversation, and they are not the kind of men who talk for the sake of it. They sit, and they eat, and they look, and they go back inside, and the hum of the machinery takes them again.

The foreman at the depot is a man in his fifties with a grey beard and a permanent squint from the light. He has been here since the beginning, since the first station was built, and he knows every man on the crews and every turbine on the shelf and every stream that feeds the system. He walks the road once a week in good weather, a slow circuit, checking the stations, talking to whoever is there, watching the moss and the water and the slow changes in the watershed. He is the closest thing the shelf has to a native. He has not been born here, but he has been here long enough that the place has entered him in a way that the younger men have not yet experienced. He points out things that the others have not noticed — a new species of lichen on the wall of the number 14 station, a patch of saxifrage that has come up where the road crew dumped a load of gravel three years ago, the first tentative willow in a sheltered gully, a single stem, a meter tall, where there was no willow last year. He watches the world becoming.

There is a woman among the crews, now. One. An engineer, in her thirties, who has come up for the rotation and is working on the instrumentation of the newer stations. She is the first woman on the shelf, and the men are careful with her in a way that is half courtesy and half uncertainty. She is more competent than most of them and she knows it, and they know it, and the dynamic is not yet settled. She walks out to the stations alone, sometimes, with a notebook and a thermos, and she looks at the lichen and the moss with the eye of someone who has been trained to see systems. She is the one who first pointed out that the *Sphagnum* is establishing in the drainage ditches along the road, and that this is changing the hydrology of the shelf in ways that the engineers have not modeled. The foreman listened. He nodded. He has been waiting for someone to say it.

---

The first willow, in its sheltered gully, is a single stem a meter tall. *Salix herbacea*, the dwarf willow, the smallest tree in the world, a thing that grows as a creeping mat in the high country and only here, in this sheltered spot where the cliff blocks the wind and the drainage concentrates the moisture, has dared to lift itself upward into a recognizable tree-shape. It is a gesture. It is a flag. The foreman has been watching it for two years, and he has not told anyone except the engineer, and she has not told anyone either, and the willow is a small secret between the two of them and the wind.

It will not live, perhaps. The winters are still too hard, the soil still too thin, the exposure still too much. Or it will live, and it will spread, and in a hundred years there will be a thicket of dwarf willow in this gully, and the thicket will trap more snow, and the snow will melt slowly and feed the stream, and the stream will cut deeper, and the deeper cut will expose more rock, and the moss will follow the new moisture, and the soil will deepen, and somewhere in the slow accumulation a rowan will arrive, and after the rowan a birch, and after the birch something else, and the shelf will begin, very slowly, to forget that it was ever bare stone.

Or none of that will happen. The willow will die. The shelf will remain a moss-painted rock for another hundred thousand years. The engineers will continue to come up on rotation. The turbines will hum. The ptarmigan will pick at the crowberry. The springtails will process the moss. The lichen will paint itself orange. The shelf will sit in its mist and its water and its slow patience, and the world will continue to become, and no one will be here to see the becoming except the wind and the water and the slow patient things that are willing to wait.

The foreman thinks about this sometimes, walking his circuit. The engineer thinks about it too. They do not talk about it. There is nothing to say. The shelf is doing what the shelf is doing, and they are part of it now, the way the moss is part of it and the lichen is part of it and the small beetles under the stones are part of it, and the willow is part of it, and the turbines are part of it, and the slow hum of the machinery is part of the sound the shelf is making to itself as it waits for the next thing to arrive.
