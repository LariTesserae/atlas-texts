What lives here are mostly the ropes.

Not the stone, though the stone is patient and behaves in many of the ways that patience requires. Not the water, though the water is the medium and the carrier and the climate, and has, in its slow circulation, its own kind of intent—cold from one far side of the world, warmth from the other, meeting here without fully meeting, passing on. The ropes. The pale, fibrous, swaying ropes of growth that hang from the tops of every worn shoulder and braid into mats between every two long mounds. They are what inhabits this place most fully, in the sense that inhabiting requires being somewhere without having been made for somewhere else.

The ropes have no name. They are not classified, by anyone here, because there is no one here to do the classifying. They are a single immense population, or they are many populations, or they are one organism distributed across miles of worked stone—these are questions that would require a viewer from above and a scale of time no no current inhabitant possesses. What can be said is that they are everywhere the stone is, and that they have been growing there for longer than the stone has been stone-that-was-worked, and that they will continue growing after the stone is no longer recognizable as worked, and after that, perhaps, they will be the only thing left that distinguishes this place from any other patch of cold dark sea floor.

They take from the stone. They give to the stone. The stone takes from them and gives to them. This is the closed circuit, the only economy in operation, and it has been running without oversight for so long that each participant in it has forgotten, if it ever knew, that the arrangement was once an arrangement and not simply the way things are.

The ropes are not alive in any of the ways the constructors were alive. They do not eat. They do not breathe, in any sense that would require lungs. They do not reproduce in the sense of dividing into smaller versions of themselves and sending those smaller versions out into the world. They grow, and they fray, and the frayed ends drift free in the water and root somewhere new if they find a stone surface still rich enough in mineral to receive them. This is not reproduction. This is more like the slow spreading of a stain, except that the stain is patient, and partial to granite, and responds to currents by leaning.

They lean. This is their most animal behavior, the thing they do that the stone cannot do, and it is what one notices first when one spends any length of time here. In the channels where currents converge, they all lean the same direction at once, in a long hush, and it looks like agreement. In the channels where currents part, they stand more upright and the spaces between them widen and it looks like disagreement, or like solitude. Both interpretations are wrong, of course. They are just the ways the ropes distribute their mass when the water pushes on it.

But the leaning is also how they feed, or one of the ways. By leaning, they expose different surfaces of themselves to the slow upwelling of mineral from the stone below. By leaning, they bring different parts of their length into contact with other ropes, and at the points of contact something is exchanged—calcium, perhaps, or the faint signals that travel through calcium in these temperatures and pressures, signals that no one has decoded because there is no one here to decode them and because they travel so slowly that decoding would require a lifespan longer than any constructor's, longer than any rope's. The tallest of them, in the deeper channels, lean upward as far as the diminishing light will allow, brushing at the underside of the dim green-gold wash where it finally fails and gives over to the rest of the dark. They cannot reach what is up there. They reach for it anyway. They have been reaching for it for longer than the stone has been old.

They respond to tremors. The occasional groan of a stone block settling a fraction further into its final position—a sound that travels for miles through the worked plain—registers on the ropes as a tremor, and they sway around it and forget it. The forgetting is the important part. If they did not forget, they would accumulate every tremor that ever happened in this place, and there have been many, and they would eventually be too full of past tremoring to lean at all. They forget, and so they remain responsive. They remember for the duration of a sway. They remember for the duration of a current shift. They do not remember longer.

This is a kind of memory. It is the only kind available here. It is enough for the ropes' purposes, which are the purposes of growth and the taking of mineral from stone and the giving of mineral back and the slow replacement of one substrate with another. It is, in its way, sufficient.

The constructors are not here.

They were here. They are the reason the stone is the shape it is, the reason there are corners and tiers and long inward-curving mounds where buttresses once held something back. They worked with basalt hammers and copper chisels and ropes of braided kelp-stalk, and they did not have machines, and they had time, and hands, and the patience of their own slow metabolisms, and they were so patient and there were so many of them that what they made endured.

Then they stopped, or the water rose, or the sun went out, or all three together, and what they made has had a very long time to become something else without becoming anything different, and the constructors are not here.

But they are here.

In the shapes.

The inward curve of the long mounds is the shape of a buttress that once held something back. The tier is the shape of a tier that once supported something above it. The corners are the corners they cut. The patience of the water and the patience of the plants are continuations, in different materials, of the patience that made the stone what it is. The constructors did not build the ropes, and the ropes did not build the constructors, but the patience is the same patience, and the patience is what is here now, distributed across the worked plain in the form of pale swaying ropes and slow settling stone and the long deep hum of water that has been carrying cold from one far side and warmth from the other for longer than any constructor lived.

If you could read the shapes the way the ropes read them—through the slow leaching of mineral, through the long sway of response to current—you would read the constructors in every curve. You would read their hands in every worked surface. You would read their patience in the patience of what has replaced them. They are here the way the rope of a once-braided kelp-stalk is here, embedded in the stone it once helped to move. They are here as traces. They are here as the reason the stone is shaped as it is. They are here as the inherited patience of everything that now occupies what they made.

The water is here.

The water is here the way air is here on a hillside—everywhere, taken for granted, the medium in which the rest of existence happens. The water moves in slow bodies. It has weather. It has convergence and divergence. Where the currents meet, the ropes lean all in one direction and the channels narrow with growth and the place becomes denser, more inhabited. Where the currents part, the ropes stand more upright and the corridors open into dark passages that could be followed for a long way, if one could see.

The water is cold. The water is old. The water has been here longer than the constructors, probably, and longer than the ropes, possibly, and it will be here after both are gone, or after both have become something else. It carries everything. It carries the minerals up from the stone. It carries the frayed ends of ropes out into the dark. It carries the groans of settling stone for miles before they fade. It carries the notes, the occasional low clear notes that ring once and die when a current shifts a braid of growth against a corner of granite. It carries all of these and does not distinguish between them and does not privilege one over another. The water is the most impartial thing here. It is also the most patient, but this is a different kind of patience than the constructors' patience, and different again than the ropes' patience. The water's patience is the patience of what has nowhere to be and no reason to be there sooner. It moves when it moves. It does not move when it does not move. There is no urgency in it because there is nowhere urgency would lead.

And in the water, doing nothing that would show up to any eye that ever existed, the chemistry. The unseen work. The slow negotiations between mineral and mineral, between the calcium that the stone gives up and the calcium the ropes take and the calcium that settles, in time, into new configurations of limestone somewhere in the dark where no eye will find it. There is no word for the beings that do this work, because there are no beings that do this work. There is only the work. There is only the slow patient exchange that has been running since before the constructors and that will be running after the ropes have incorporated the last worked stone into themselves. It is the oldest life here, if life is the right word, and the least visible, and the most essential. The ropes could not grow without it. The stone could not settle without it. The water could not carry without it. It is everywhere and it is the reason everywhere has the texture it has.

The stone is here.

The stone is patient in the way that all stone is patient, which is to say not at all, and yet. The stone does what the water tells it to, over very long periods. The stone wears down. The stone gives up its minerals to the water and to the ropes. The stone receives minerals back, in different configurations, in the slow chemistry of patience. The stone is not alive, but it is occupied. It is the substrate of an entire population of ropes, and of an entire circuit of exchange, and of the memory of hands that once cut it into the shapes it now is.

The stone groans sometimes.

The groans are the most surprising thing here, because the stone does not otherwise indicate that anything is happening in it. But a groan travels for miles and registers on the ropes as a tremor and the ropes sway around it and forget it, and so the groans, too, are part of the circuit. They are the stone's contribution to the exchange. They are the stone's way of registering its own settling, its own slow wearing-down, its own patience with its own patience. They are not frequent. They are not regular. They are the only thing in this place that one could call an event, and they are not events so much as confirmations. The stone is still settling. The stone has not yet reached its final shape. The work of the constructors continues, in the negative, in the form of the slow erosion of what they made.

The ropes are the only builders this place will ever know again.

This is the most important thing to know about what lives here. The constructors built once, with their hands, over many generations, and then they stopped. The water and the ropes are building now, slowly, with the slow chemistry of patience, and they will not stop. The ropes will, eventually, take the stone apart. They will incorporate it into themselves. They will build in its place, with its material, in the form of their own slow growth. The inward curves of the buttresses will become, in time, the inward curves of dense rope-mats. The corners that were cut by copper chisels will be uncut, smoothed over, forgotten. The tiered terraces will become, in time, the long undulating braids of pale fibrous growth that step down from one channel to the next. The place will still be here. It will be differently inhabited. It will be inhabited by what grew from the place, and the place will be made of what grew from the place, and no constructor will be required, and no hands will be required, and the patience will continue, and the patience will be the same patience, and the patience will be sufficient.

This is what lives here. The ropes, and the water, and the stone, and the unseen chemistry that runs between them, and the constructors as traces, and the patience, and the slow circuit of exchange that has been running for longer than any of them have existed in their current forms. There are no animals. There are no observers. There are no events except the occasional groan of settling stone and the occasional note of growth against granite. There is no music. There is weather of a kind, but it is centuries long and no one is here to notice it pass.

What lives here lives slowly, and what lives here lives long, and what lives here does not, for the most part, know that it is living. The ropes respond to tremors and forget them. The water moves and does not know it is moving. The stone settles and does not know it is settling. The constructors are not here but their patience is here and the patience does not know it is patience.

It is enough.
