What lives there is, in the largest sense, the plants. They are the chamber's true occupants, in the way that the hold of a ship is occupied by the dark and the casks and the slow rolling. They came after the hand-builders. They came up through the cracks, through the dust, through the powder of the floor where the fired clay had already crumbled. They found a place that was already shaped for them, a place where the channels were already cut, where the stopped ends were already stopped, where the chambers were already tuned to something the hand-builders had heard in the mountain and tried to keep. The plants grew into the shape. The plants grew into the pipes. The pipes grew with them. You cannot tell now, in most of the chamber, where the original fired clay ends and the woody tissue begins. They have been fused by the dust and the calcium and the long patient chemistry of the place, into a single substance that is half wood and half stone and is neither.

The plants are not green. They are not the green of any plant that has ever drunk. They are the color of dry things — of old paper, of a mushroom that has been left on a log through a dry winter, of the sand of the plain outside, of the dust in the chamber, of the stone of the walls. They photosynthesize somehow, by what light they can steal from whatever faint glow seeps down from the cracks above, or from the heat of the pipes themselves, or from some chemistry you would not recognize if you saw it. They grow a finger-width in a century. They grow. That is enough. They have been growing since the city was young, and the city is no longer young.

They pipe. They pipe because the breath of the mountain passes through them. The breath is so slow that a leaf would not stir, but the plants are hollow in just the right way, hollow in patterns that were perhaps once deliberate in the clay and that the plants have continued, and the chord is theirs, and they are the chord's, and you cannot say which makes which. The oldest of them, the ones that reach from floor to ceiling, have been tuned for longer than the hand-builders' grandchildren lived. Their hollows are smooth. Their hollows have been worn smooth by the slow passage of the air, the way a riverbed is worn smooth, and the smoothness is what the chord needs, and the chord is what keeps the plants alive, and the plants are what keeps the chord sounding. The whole thing is a single machine now, a single organism, a single long-breathing thing that does not know it is alive but is, in some sense, the most alive thing in the chamber.

Then there are the smaller things. They are smaller in scale, but not smaller in patience.

On the felted floor, in the pale dry weave of fibers that the plants have laid down over centuries, there lives a thing that is almost not a thing. It is flat. It is the size of a thumbnail, perhaps a little larger, and it is the color of the dust that lives among the fibers, which is to say the color of the chamber, which is to say no color at all. It moves by rippling. It moves so slowly that if you set your hand down beside it and watched for an hour you would not be sure it had moved at all. It feeds on the dust. The dust is rich. The dust is the powdered bones of hand-builders, the powdered shells of mites, the powdered tips of roots, the calcium that has fallen from the ceiling for a thousand years. The flat thing eats this dust in a path that winds and rewinds, year after year, century after century, and it never seems to need to move very far from where it started. There is enough. There has always been enough. It is patient in its eating. It is the patience of the dust.

In the walls, in the half-rooms, in the hollows where the air is stiller and the chord is a half-tone lower, there are small hard-shelled things that rasp at the woody tissue. They are slow. They have to be slow, because the wood is hard, harder than the wood of any tree you have seen, almost mineral in its density, and a mouthpart can only take so much in a year. They live in colonies. A colony will occupy a single hollow for many generations. The hollow darkens around them as they work, because their droppings are dark, and because the dust they stir is dark, and because over time they have stained the wood of their hollow to a deep brown, almost black. They know the chamber by the feel of the air, by the smell of the resin, by the small vibrations that travel through the wood when something larger moves on the other side of the wall. Some of them no longer have eyes. Eyes were a luxury that the darkness ate away, generation by generation, until now the colony is eyeless and has been eyeless for so long that no one in the colony remembers what an eye was for. They rasp at the wood. They rasp. They have been rasping at the same hollows for so long that some of the hollows are deeper than they were when the colony moved in, and the dust that the rasping makes is part of the dust that the flat thing on the floor eats. The chamber is fed by its own slow wearing. The chamber eats itself, in the smallest increments, and what it wears away becomes the next generation of the things that wear it.

In the pipes themselves, riding the slow breath of the mountain, there are smaller things still. They are almost weightless. They are the color of the air, or they have no color at all, and they have learned to let the breath carry them. They feed on what the breath carries — the few molecules of water that wander through on the slow circulation, the few drifting spores, the trace of resin that lifts from the wood when the temperature changes by a hundredth of a degree. They live their whole lives in the column of moving air. They are born there. They breed there. They die there, and their bodies are carried upward on the warm current and settle, when they are dry, on the ledges and the platforms, and become part of the dust. Some of them have learned to rest at the antinodes of the chord, where the air moves fastest, and some of them rest at the nodes, where the air is still, and you can tell which is which by the way they drift, because the ones at the antinodes shimmer in the dark, faintly, by what light they can steal from the friction of the moving air.

There is a thing that waits for the light.

In a single crack in the ceiling of one of the upper alcoves — a crack so narrow that you would not see it unless you were looking for it, and so high that you would not reach it even if you climbed — there is a plant that has learned to bloom. It blooms only when the crack opens, which is to say when the wind outside the mesa blows in a certain way, and a sliver of sun comes down the crack and falls, for an hour or two, on the ledge where the plant has rooted. The plant blooms in that hour. It puts out a single pale flower, the color of bone, with a faint smell of resin and something sweeter, something that might once have been nectar. It opens. It holds itself open for as long as the light lasts. And then it closes, and it waits. It waits for years, sometimes, for the next opening. It has been waiting since before the hand-builders were gone. It has bloomed a hundred times, perhaps, or two hundred, in all the centuries. Each bloom sets a few seeds, and the seeds fall into the crack, and most of them do not sprout, but some of them do, and the ones that do grow very slowly into the wood of the ledge, and the ledge becomes theirs, and they wait too.

There is a larger thing.

It lives in the chamber proper, in the main hollow, where the platforms rise and the air is not quite so still. It is the size of a large dog, or a small child. It has four limbs and a long body and a head that it carries low, near the floor, the way a thing carries its head when it is listening. It has no eyes. It has had no eyes for longer than it has had a name, if it ever had a name. It moves slowly. It moves through the chamber with the sureness of a thing that has known the chamber since it was born, and it climbs the platforms where the platforms are still climbable, and it rests in the half-rooms where the air is stiller and the chord is a half-tone lower.

It feeds on the dust-eaters. It finds them by feel, by the small vibrations of their rippling, by the faint warmth they give off as they digest. It eats them whole, slowly, and it chews them with a set of teeth that are flat and ridged and that look, if you could see them in the dark, like the worn keys of an old piano. It has been eating them for a long time. Its kind has been eating them for a long time. Its kind is what the hand-builders became, in the chamber, after the chamber was sealed. The hand-builders did not all leave. Some of them did not leave. Some of them stayed, in the chamber, when the rest went out into the plain, and they ate what they could eat, and they drank the trace of moisture, and they bred, very slowly, because there was not much to breed on, and their children were smaller than they were, and their children's children were smaller still, until the children were the size of mites, and then smaller, and then larger again, as the chamber allowed, as the niche allowed, until there was a size that could be sustained on what the chamber had to give, and that size held, and held, and holds now.

It is the last of a line. It is the only one of its size that the chamber supports. It knows the chamber by the chord. The chord is how it knows where it is. The standing waves of the chord are nodes and antinodes, and the creature knows where the nodes are, and where the antinodes are, and it rests at the nodes, where the sound is quietest, and it moves along the antinodes, where the sound is loudest, and it has done this for its whole life, and its mother did this before it, and its mother's mother, back to a time when there were more of them, when they could hear the chord because they had ears that were not yet vestigial, when the chamber held enough of them that they could be a population instead of a line.

It is patient. It is the patience of the chamber. It moves when it needs to move, and it rests when it does not need to move, and it has been doing this for so long that you could set your hand on its flank and not feel it breathe, because its breathing is so slow that it is not breathing so much as exchanging, by fractions of a degree, the gases that its slow metabolism requires. Its skin is pale and dry and slightly mineral, and it is warm to the touch, warmer than the air, warmer than the stone, because it is a small engine of digestion, and its digestion is the only heat in the chamber that is not the mountain's.

It does not know that the chord is a chord. It does not know that the plants are pipes. It does not know that the chamber was built. It knows the chamber. That is enough. It knows where the dust-eaters are thickest. It knows where the air is stillest. It knows where the platforms will hold its weight and where they will not. It knows the chord the way you know the floor of a house you have lived in since you were born — not as a fact but as a thing you walk on, a thing that is always there, a thing that has no name because it has never needed one.

It is the closest thing the chamber has to a hand-builder. It is the hand-builders' last echo, in a place that has almost forgotten the hand-builders. Its bones, when it dies, will become part of the plants, calcium in the woody tissue, the same calcium as in the limestone the hand-builders quarried. Its dust will join the dust on the felted floor. The flat things will eat it, slowly, and grow faintly larger for a season, and then they will go back to their patient paths. The chamber will absorb it, as the chamber absorbs everything, and the chord will go on, and the plants will go on growing a finger-width in a century, and the thing that waits for the light will bloom, if the wind is right, in an hour that is years away.

Nothing in the chamber is in a hurry. Nothing in the chamber has ever been in a hurry. The chamber is the size of a town that has been crumpled up and put in a box, and the things that live in it have been folded into the box with it, and they have been there so long that they have forgotten they are folded.
