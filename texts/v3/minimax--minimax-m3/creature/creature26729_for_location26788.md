The salamander is the first thing you meet that is not a machine and not the rock. It sits on the damp limestone beside the catwalk, six inches from where the steel stanchion is bolted in, and it does not move when your boot comes down eighteen inches away. It has the pinkish pallor of something that has never been in the sun, and its eyes are the dark beads of a creature that has not had much use for them, set in a face that is mostly mouth — a flat wedge of a head that is largely appetite. It is *Eurycea*, of some species, one of the cave-dwelling ones, the ones that lose their pigment and shorten their limbs over many generations and become the animal that the cave wants them to become. This one has not gone all the way. There is still a faint trace of darker color along its spine. There are still eyes. It is in between, a salamander that would live in light if light were offered, and has elected otherwise.

It watches nothing. That is the wrong way to put it. It is not watching and not-watching; it is doing something that does not have a verb in your language. The cave is not visual for it, or not primarily. It is humid. It is a field of vibrations transmitted through the wet stone: the bass thrum of the pumps, the falling water, the footsteps of anything that walks above, the slow creep of millipedes, the small potential of an isopod brushing the underside of a rimstone pool. The salamander is tuned to this, the way you are tuned to light. It knows when a drip will land near it because the air shifts a millisecond before. It knows when something is coming because the floor transmits the weight before the weight arrives. It does not know what you are. It knows that you are large, and that you are not food, and that you are not a predator it has met before, which is to say, you are not a category it possesses. You register as a disturbance in the hum of the place. The salamander does not move.

If you stand very still — and you will, because the cave's sound presses you into stillness — you can watch the salamander for a long time and it will not do anything. Its sides move. Its throat pulses. A tiny change in the angle of its head, which may be nothing. The pale throat of it. The way the wet stone has adhered a flake of calcite to its flank, the slow mineral accretion that is happening to everything in here, including the animals. The salamander is being slowly entombed by the same process that is building the stalactites above it. It does not mind, or it cannot mind. It is a temporary structure on a permanent geology, and it does not know this, and the not-knowing is one of the things that makes it possible for it to be here at all.

The midges are the second thing, and they are louder in number than the salamander is in body. They hold themselves above the warm work-lamps in pale drifting columns, so thick at the densest part that they form a kind of fog you could put your hand into, and they do not seem to be doing anything in particular. They are not feeding. They are not mating, or not visibly. They are simply there, riding the warm air that rises from the bulbs, in a slow continuous churn that reaches from the lamp to the ceiling and back down again, a slow convection. They have not read any books about midges. They are not in conversation. They are the only things in the cave that are not committed to some larger process — the pumps' larger process, the water's larger process, the geology's larger process. The midges are a smaller process, almost an accident. They are here because there are lights, and there are lights because there are humans, and there are humans because there is a pipe that needs to be kept running. The midges are the periphery of the periphery. They are not even a footnote in the cave's larger indifference. They are the smallest possible deviation from the damp air, and they live and die in a few days and are replaced by other midges, and the lamp they are riding does not know.

The isopods are smaller and quieter still. They live in the rimstone pools, the shallow tiered basins that step down across the floor, and they walk upside-down on the underside of the water film where it meets the air. This is a real thing they do. They are pale and segmented and have more legs than seems reasonable, and they move with the slow deliberation of something that is not in a hurry, because the world they are moving through is not in a hurry. The pool they are in has been here for thousands of years. The water overflowing from this pool into the one below it has been doing so for thousands of years. The isopod can take its time. If you dip your hand into the pool, the isopods do not scatter. They barely register you. They are tuned to a vibration that your hand does not make — the wriggling of small prey, the disturbance of organic matter, the slow rain of bat guano from the ledges above. You are not those things. You are a new category, and the new category does not contain a threat or a meal, so the isopod continues walking on the underside of the world, and you withdraw your hand wetter than you put it in.

The frog is somewhere else. You do not see it. You might hear it, on a quiet day, except that there are no quiet days, so you do not hear it. It lives in one of the deeper chambers, a side passage that the catwalk does not reach, a place where the pumps' vibration is dampened by intervening rock and the water noise comes to you as a muffled rather than a foregrounded thing. The frog calls anyway. It is, as far as you can tell, a *Pseudacris* or a *Hyla* of some kind, a tree frog that has somehow ended up in a cave — perhaps washed in during a flood, perhaps as an egg on a salamander's foot — and has decided to stay because the humidity is right and there are midges in the deeper chambers too, and the walls are wet, and the constant temperature of the place is hospitable to a small cold-blooded thing that would otherwise have to worry about freezing on the surface. The frog has no predator here. The frog has no competitor. The frog is the largest animal in its side passage, and it calls, and the call goes out into the warm wet air and meets the falling water and the pumps' bass thrum and is overwhelmed by both. The frog may have stopped hearing itself. It calls because calling is what frogs do, or because the calling is not for itself, or because the calling is the only thing in the frog's body that is loud enough to compete with the cave, and so the frog commits to it, a small noise in a large sound, going nowhere, registering on no one. This is, possibly, the most poignant thing in the cave. It is not the most important.

The bats are the largest visible non-human residents, and they have made the loudest accommodation. A few thousand of them, roosting in a high fissure on the far side of the chamber, visible from the catwalk as a moving dark mass when a head-lamp swings past — a slow churn of bodies pressed together, hanging head-down, occasionally shuffling, occasionally falling off and re-attaching. They emerge at dusk from a chimney that opens somewhere above, and they have to fly past the pumps to do it. The pumps are not quiet. The pumps are, as has been noted, ninety-plus decibels of sustained noise transmitted through the rock itself. The bats navigate by echolocation, which is to say, by listening to their own voices bounce back at them, and the pumps have been running for decades, which means the bats have been shouting into a wall of noise for decades, and the result is — the theory is, and the field researchers have written it up — that the bats have shifted their frequency. They chirp higher. They chirp narrower. They have found a band of sound that the pumps do not occupy, and they have moved their language into it, and they come and go past the machinery without colliding with it, and the cave does not much notice. The cave is not a place that notices its inhabitants. The cave is a place that has inhabitants because the conditions permit it, and the conditions here permit a bat that can hear above the pump, and so the bat is here, and so is the salamander, and so is the frog, and so are the isopods, and so are the midges, and none of them are here because the cave wanted them, and none of them are staying because the cave wants them to stay. They are here because they are able to be here. The cave is a sieve. What can pass, passes. What cannot, does not.

The humans are also the result of a sieve, but a different one. There are three of them on the far side of the chamber, the head-lamps drifting in the dark. You can see them as small pale spots moving against the far wall, and they are doing something that requires lights and gloves and tools. You cannot tell what. They are not moving quickly. They are not talking, or if they are talking you cannot hear them over the pumps, which is also probably an accommodation of a kind. They have learned to communicate by gesture, by pointing, by the angle of their head-lamps. They are not the only humans who have been here. The shed has the calendar on the wall, the clipboard on the nail, the wooden stool. There is the chair outside, the folding chair facing the largest of the rimstone pools. Someone set the chair there. Someone sat in it, perhaps many times, perhaps for years, perhaps facing the pool and the phosphorescent rim and the slow patient work of the rimstone building itself outward. They are not here now. They are on the surface. They have not come back, or they have come back and not stayed. Either way, they left a chair, and the chair is here, and the chair is wet, and the plastic is fogged with condensation, and there is a faint mineral deposit on one of the legs where the splashed water has dried and left its load of calcite. The chair is becoming part of the cave, the way everything becomes part of the cave, given enough time. The person who set the chair there is somewhere else. The chair is patient. The chair faces the pool.

You can stand on the catwalk and see all of this. You can see the salamander on the rock beside you, motionless. You can see the midges rising from the lamp above you, a slow inverted fountain. You can see the head-lamps across the chamber, three pale spots drifting, and you can imagine the person holding each one — the way they hold their mouth, the way they squint into the dark, the way they have learned to work with the sound pressing on them. You can see the phosphorescent rim of the rimstone pool, the low blue-green glow that does not illuminate anything, that only marks its own edges. You can see the isopods, if you kneel and look. You can hear none of the frog. You can imagine the bats in their high fissure, folded against each other, a dark mass that will stir itself at dusk and rise through the noise of the pumps and out the chimney and into the open air above, where there is no sound, or less sound, or a different sound, and they will feed, and they will return, and they will fold themselves back into the dark and the damp and the unceasing roar of the place.

The pump nearest you exhales. It does this every few seconds, a slow rhythmic clunk that travels through the rock and into your feet and up through your knees, and it is louder than your breathing, and your breathing is, in this moment, almost the only thing you can hear of yourself. You are the smallest thing in the cave. You are smaller than the bats, smaller than the frog, smaller than the salamander, because the salamander has been here longer, and the bats have been here longer, and the cave has been here longer than any of them, and the water has been here longer than the cave, and the rock has been here longer than the water, and you are the most recent arrival, and you are the one who will leave first, and the cave will not notice your leaving, and the pump will not change its rhythm, and the salamander will not move.

A drip falls on your shoulder.

You stand there. The cave holds you in its sound and its warmth and its slow dim light, and the things that live here live around you, and they do not know your name, and you do not know theirs, and the chair faces the pool, and the pool is building itself outward by a thickness you will never measure, and the isopod walks upside-down on the underside of the world, and the bat, somewhere, sleeps folded against another bat, and the frog calls into a sound that swallows it, and the salamander does not move, and the pump exhales, and the cave grows.
