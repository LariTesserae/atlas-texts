# The Inhabitants of the Cirque

They do not arrive. They are *in place* when the basin is, as if seeded at the moment of the shearing.

The first thing you might call a creature is a sound — a downward musical slur that begins somewhere in the upper granite and seems to lose interest in itself before it ends. If you trace the sound upward you find a shape pressed flat against the wet stone, low and broad, the color of the granite beneath it. It has no neck you can see. Its head is its shoulder is its flank. Four limbs, or six, or the suggestion of limbs held so close to the body that the body is the limbs. It is breathing — you can see the rise and fall of its back, slow as the pool's pulse — and it is watching you with one eye, set high and round and very dark. You did not see the eye until you were looking directly at it. You are not sure you see it now.

Move, and it is gone. Stop, and another, fifty meters up the wall, takes up the call, and then another, and the calling cascades around the bowl in a sequence that never varies. You begin to map it. There are eight voices. There are always eight voices. They are not the same eight voices, in the sense that you can point to one and say *that one is the second voice* — they swap positions, or they don't, and you cannot decide which. The call passes between them with the regularity of a relay, and the pitch of each call is a function of where the caller is sitting on the wall, and where the caller is sitting is a function of the time you have spent in the cirque, and the time you have spent in the cirque is not the time that is passing on the wall.

The creatures on the floor are different. They are taller. They stand in the marsh on legs that look as if they were intended for deeper water, long and folded, the joints bending the wrong way for a mammal, the right way for something that has been standing in slow current for a very long time. Their bodies are sleek, dark, mottled with the greens of the mosses that grow on them — not parasites, you realize after a long time, but partnerships, the moss rooting into the creature's skin the way it roots into stone, and the creature wearing the moss the way the trees wear the moss, and the moss spreading from the creature to the next tree, and the next, in a slow green circuit you can almost trace.

They have hands. This is the detail that does not fit. Five-fingered, jointed, with the soft pads and the opposable thumb of something that was once, or still is, a primate. They use the hands to pull down branches, to peel bark, to cup water from the travertine terraces and bring it to mouths set low in their faces. They use the hands to touch each other — a brief press of palm to shoulder, palm to flank, a contact that lasts one second and is not repeated. When they touch each other their calls change, briefly, a small downward inflection that travels through the population like a shudder. You do not know what the touch means. You suspect it is bookkeeping.

Their eyes are the color of the pool. Almost black, very calm, very still. They do not blink often. When they do blink, the lids come from the side, like shutters, and the eye disappears for a moment and reappears unchanged. They have seen you. They saw you arrive. They are not interested in you. You are an item in the system. You have been incorporated. The system does not need to know you any more than it needs to know a particular molecule of water. You are passing through it, and it is passing through you, and this is the steady state.

There is one larger than the others. You do not know why you think so — they are all roughly the same size, roughly the same shape — but the one that comes closest to you, the one that stands on the terraced stone at the edge of the pool and looks at you the longest, gives you the impression of being older, or more central, or more *itself*. It stands on two legs and does not move. Its chest rises and falls. Its hands hang at its sides. Its mouth is closed. After a long time, it opens its mouth, and out of its mouth comes a single low note, so low you feel it in your molars, and the note travels out across the water and is answered, faintly, from the walls, and the hum of the cirque changes by a fraction you can measure only with your pulse.

It is not the leader. There is no leader. It is a node. A junction. A place where several of the circuits running through the cirque pass close to the surface and can be observed, the way a knot in a piece of wood is a place where the grain can be seen most clearly. It does not know it is a node. It is not aware of being observed. It is aware of you the way a thermometer is aware of a hand.

The young ones you never see. You infer them from the small shapes that move higher on the walls, in places you cannot reach, in the cracks where the water gathers and the moss is thickest. They make a different sound — a high thin piping, the only sound in the cirque that varies, the only sound that has any quality you would call *new*. They are not infants. They are small adults, perfect in form, dark-eyed, damp-furred. They are kept up high. They are not brought down until they are the size of the ones on the floor. You do not know why. You suspect it is because the floor is for those who have been *initiated* into the system, and initiation is not a ceremony but a calibration, and the small ones are still being calibrated, and the calibration happens out of your sight.

The creatures feed. You see them eat — the bark, the moss, the pale flowers of the trees, the small pale grubs that live in the peat. They eat in the same order. They eat in the same place. They eat the same amount. The one by the pool dips its hand into the water and brings up a knot of something dark and glistening and eats it without chewing, and the gesture is so precise, so exactly repeated each time you witness it, that you begin to think of it as a stroke of a piston. A thing done at the right moment in the cycle for a purpose you cannot see. The waste of the eating falls into the water and is taken by the current toward the throat. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is excreted that is not immediately taken up by the next part of the system. The cirque is a closed loop and so are they, and the two loops are nested, and the nesting is exact.

Their skin, when you once get close enough to see it in detail, is not skin. Or it is skin, but it is also something else — a fine pattern of ridges, hexagonal, faint, that runs across the shoulders and down the flanks. The ridges are the same pattern as the lines on the far wall. You see it now. The same pattern. The same angles. The same junctions. The creatures are not decorated with the pattern. They are *made* of it, the way a printed page is made of type, and the type is the same type that is set into the stone, and the stone is the same type that is set into the water, and the water is the same type that is set into the hum, and the hum is the type itself, propagated.

They speak to you once. Only once. You are standing at the edge of the pool, late in your stay, and the large one comes to you, and it puts its hand on your forearm, and the hand is warm, and the warmth is the warmth of the water and the air and the stone, all the same warmth, and the hand presses once, lightly, and the creature makes a sound you have not heard before — a short upward note, almost interrogative — and the other creatures fall silent, all of them, simultaneously, and the hum drops out, and the pool stops pulsing, and the water stops falling, and the whole cirque holds its breath for one second that lasts a very long time, and in that second you understand that the creature is *asking* you, and the asking is part of the system, and the system is asking itself through you, and the question is whether you are a perturbation that will damp out or a perturbation that will propagate, and you do not know the answer, and the creature does not know the answer, and the cirque does not know, and then the second ends, and the hum returns, and the pulse returns, and the creature releases your arm and walks back to the edge of the pool and resumes its piston-stroke, and you are again a molecule in the medium, and the medium does not mind.

You think, sometimes, that they are sad. You think this because the word fits the tilt of their heads, the long downward slur of their calls, the way they stand in the water and do not move. But the word does not belong here. There is no time here for sadness. There is no time here for any of it. The creatures are not sad. They are *operating*. They are the visible part of the engine, the moving part, the part that is not stone and not water and not hum, the part that makes the system look, to a visitor, like a place that is alive, when the system is in fact a place that is *running*, and the difference between alive and running is the difference between a river and a pipe, and the creatures are both, and the cirque is both, and you, in the dim sourceless light, in the steady unvarying drone, in the cool wet air that does not change, are neither, and will be neither, and will continue to be neither, indefinitely, as the pulse goes on, and the water falls, and the hum holds its note, and the creatures in their hexagonal-patterned dark skins watch you with their pool-colored eyes and do not, cannot, will not ever, be surprised that you are there.
