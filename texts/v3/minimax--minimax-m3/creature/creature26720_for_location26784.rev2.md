# Inhabitants of the Condensation Ward

No one lives in the Ward by arriving. That is the first thing to say about the people here. You are not a citizen of the Ward the way you might be a citizen of a city — by registration, by vote, by the registration of your name in some clerk's ledger — you are a citizen of the Ward by virtue of having stopped trying to leave. The Ward does not trap you. The Ward simply makes going feel uncalled for. People came here on foot, mostly, down that long stalled escalator, in ones and twos, in their working clothes, sometimes with a single suitcase, sometimes with nothing at all. They intended to pass through. They are still here. This is true of nearly everyone, and they say so without resentment, the way you might say *I came for the weekend*.

You can tell, after a few days, which residents arrived recently and which have long since dissolved into the place. The new ones still look for the source of the light. They crane upward. They squint. They say things like *the architecture must conceal a diffused array*, and the older residents listen politely and offer them tea, because tea is what is offered here, and is not what it is anywhere else. After a few months the new ones lose the question entirely. They begin to take the light the way a fish takes water. After a few years they cannot remember having asked.

The Ward is small enough that everyone knows everyone, but only just. In the wider passages — the ones the Ward sets aside for circulation, the *alveoli*, an old resident might call them — you can pass an entire afternoon in nodding acquaintance with strangers whose names you have not learned and whose faces you half-recognize from the rooms you have both happened to walk through. There are perhaps a thousand souls here, perhaps fewer. The Ward does not favor counting. People estimate generously, then shrug.

---

The first thing you notice about the Ward's people is that they are damp.

Not visibly, not at first. But if you take someone's hand in greeting, you will feel it: a cool, persistent moisture on the skin that does not come from rain or sweat but from the air itself, which has been drinking from the walls since before they were born. Their hair, even after a towel, holds a curl it should not hold. Their breath, on cold mornings, comes out of their mouths in shapes that are slightly too slow to disappear. They wear coats indoors sometimes, and no one remarks on it, the way no one remarks on a child's habit.

They are also — and this is harder to put into words — *patient*. Not stoic. Patience is the wrong word for stoicism. Their patience is the patience of someone who lives inside a sentence they have come to expect to finish, eventually, in its own time. They wait for the bus. They wait for the door. They wait for the walls to decide whether today is a day for walls or for something else. They wait with a soft, unmodulated attention that a stranger might mistake for melancholy, and which is closer, in fact, to a kind of steady love.

They do not, as a rule, speak about it. You can stand beside a resident for an hour and learn nothing of their interior life. You can ask them what the Ward is, and they will tell you what they told you already, which is *it is for living in*, and that will be the end of it. You can ask them whether they are happy, and they will look at you as if you had asked whether they are tall. The question has a kind of sweetness they are willing to let you have, and they will not return to embarrass you.

But if you sit with them long enough — an afternoon, an evening, one of the long low-evening hours that the Ward rolls out without much variation — they will say things. They will say them to the room, mostly, and you will overhear.

They will say: *The Ward is settling into its old age today.*

They will say: *The walls are being walls this week.*

They will say: *The condensation is generous this month. My joints thank it.*

They will not smile, particularly, when they say these things. They are reporting.

---

If you walk into one of the public rooms — and there are public rooms, despite what I have just implied about privacy; the Ward, like any lung, has its larger chambers — you will see perhaps a dozen residents at any time.

There will always be someone playing a game. The Ward harbors games. There is one in particular that involves a board which is also a map, and which is also, on certain afternoons, a calendar. The pieces are old and smooth and made of a material that none of the players can identify, and they click together when you set them down. You will sometimes see three elders bent over such a board in a courtyard that appeared for the purpose and will probably not survive the evening. They will offer you a chair. They will offer you a piece. They will not teach you the rules, because the rules are not the kind you teach. They are the kind you wake up knowing.

There will always be someone reading. The Ward loves readers. It produces a kind of reader that no other place quite produces — patient, with their finger tracing a line, sometimes reading the same paragraph for an hour while the condensation collects around their fingernail. Books in the Ward are damp the way the air is damp, and the pages cling, and you turn them with care, and the act of reading becomes a kind of slow devotion. There is a small library, somewhere, that is not in the same place twice. The librarians do not seem to mind. They say that is the Ward's work, and the Ward has its reasons.

There will be someone mending something. This is important. People here mend. The Ward produces a surplus of objects that do not quite keep working in any straightforward way, and the residents have developed an entire civic technology of repair. A woman on a low stool, sleeves pushed past her elbows — though it is cold; she has decided to be hot for a while — with a needle and a length of thread that has gone damp in her hand. She is re-stitching a curtain that has been here longer than she has, and the curtain will outlive her, and she knows this, and it does not make her sad. She is only the most recent person to have done this particular work. The curtain will be mended by many hands after hers.

There will be children.

The children of the Ward are different from children elsewhere. This is not because of any strangeness in the children themselves — they are mostly ordinary children, with ordinary appetites and ordinary sorrows — but because the Ward treats them with a particular respect that other places do not. Children here are not, by and large, hurried. They are not run ragged. They are given the long low afternoon, and the wall-tops, and the chalk.

---

The children of the Ward draw suns.

You can find these drawings anywhere you can find a flat wall-top, and the flat wall-tops are everywhere, because the walls are low. The suns are simple — a circle with rays, almost always yellow, sometimes white, occasionally drawn in the pink of boiled beetroot when that is what is in the kitchen. They are drawn large enough that one child cannot finish a sun alone, and so the suns are collaborative, a first child drawing the circle, a next child drawing the rays, a third child trying to make the rays more even and giving up. The suns persist. The condensation does not quite erase them; it softens them the way a kiss softens a word. And then other children draw on top, and add their own rays, and the sun becomes a kind of palimpsest, a record of every child who ever reached up onto that particular piece of wall.

The children are also the ones who keep the place warm, by their presence. The streetlamp at the center of the Ward — the one that casts no light, that is only warmth — tends to gather them in the late hours when the cold has settled into something dutiful. You will see their small heads bobbing around its base, and the steam of their breath, and the soft click of their shoes on the damp ground, and they will be saying things to each other that no adult can quite hear. They are translating, perhaps. They are learning the geometry of rooms within rooms. They are practicing the long patient sentences they will use when they grow older. They are not in a hurry to grow older. The Ward does not hurry them.

There is one child I should mention, because she is famous, locally, in the way that anyone who can do a useful thing is famous in a small place.

Her name is either Yara or Mara or neither, depending on whom you ask, and she was born with the ability to hear the walls think.

She is eight, or nine, or older, and she sits sometimes in a low doorway in a passage that has no name, and people come to her with questions. *Is the Ward displeased today?* they ask. *Is this wall safe?* they ask. *Is the well at the corner of Third Instance actually deep, or am I thinking of somewhere else?* She listens, with her head slightly tipped, the way a child listens when she is making you wait. Then she answers.

Most of what the walls think, she says, is too large to translate. It is the size of weather, the size of the breath of a sleeping animal. Sometimes it is so boring she falls asleep herself, sitting cross-legged in the doorway, and the people waiting for her answer wait. They wait well. They are patient people. They are not the kind of people who would wake a child.

When she does translate, she does so in a low voice, and what she says is rarely what the questioner expected. *The wall is thinking of its grandmother*, she might say. *The well is thinking of water, but the water it is thinking of is not from here*. She charges for this, but only a little, and even the charge is variable, depending on what she thinks the questioner can afford. She is, in her small way, the civic priest of a place that has no religion, and she takes the role with the gravity of any eight-year-old asked to be a grown-up.

---

There is a woman who lives at the bottom of a knee-deep well.

She is not, technically, *at* the bottom of *a* well. She is in a courtyard that has a well in the corner of it, and the well is shallow, and she lives beside the well rather than within it — but she insists, when asked, that the well is the deepest place she has ever stood, and no amount of measuring will persuade her otherwise. She is sixty, perhaps, with grey hair that has gone damp and would not straighten if she asked it to. She wears two coats, one over the other, and she is one of the warmest people you will meet in the Ward, in the affective sense. Her name is Hellen, or Hellenne, or it does not matter.

She speaks often, when you sit with her, about the depth of the well. *You can feel it*, she says, *the bottom is much further down than the bottom is*. She has been asked to prove this. She has not been asked recently. The Ward has learned that she does not need to prove it, the way the Ward has learned that the broken escalator does not need to be repaired. *The well is the well*, is what she says when pressed. *You would not understand it, because you have not lived beside it*.

People believe her. People here believe many things, and they are not embarrassed by their belief, and they do not require evidence in the form their neighbors elsewhere might require. Hellenne, or Helen by another spelling, has been beside her well for forty years, and in that time she has become slightly translucent to the rest of the Ward — not visible-through, not ghostly, but *thinner*, somehow, in the way she walks through passages, in the way her coat sometimes seems to overlap with the wall beside her. She is more Ward than she is woman, in those moments, and the Ward does not seem to mind, and the woman does not seem to mind, and the well continues, very calmly, to be deep.

There is a man whose shadow walks ahead of him.

He is younger than Hellenne, perhaps forty, perhaps less, and his name is something like *Artur*, said with a soft ch in the middle. He is tall in the way of people who stand up very straight to be polite, and he has a thin face, and he is almost always slightly wet at the cuffs of his coat. He is the sort of person who, outside the Ward, would have been called *quiet*, and inside the Ward is called *steady*, because quiet here is a category reserved for the building itself. He crosses intersections. He does this carefully, because intersections in the Ward are not always intersections, and sometimes what looks like a corner is actually a passage that goes through another room. His shadow, which he has trained with patient years, will stand up a few paces ahead of him before each crossing, survey the geometry, and lie back down again. *It is what I have*, he says, when asked.

He is not unhappy about it. He has a small piece of business — he repairs the wells that do not need repairing, and he repairs the rails of the broken escalator, though the rails are decorative and lead nowhere — and he is busy, and his shadow does him good turns. He is one of the very few people in the Ward who can stand in two places at once, in some metaphysical sense, and he has learned to use it. Once, he tells the story, his shadow came back and reported that the next street over had become a courtyard full of standing water, and he took a different route, and so avoided wetting his shoes. Once, his shadow stood up and pointed, and he found a child lost behind a wall. These are facts. He does not embellish them.

---

Beyond the named people, the Ward is full of the unnamed.

There are the watchers, who sit on the low walls in the afternoon and watch the walls breathe. They are not idle. They are doing important work, which is to bear witness to the slow shift of the place. They watch for new doorways, and for doorways that have closed, and they make small notes in small books. They are tolerated by the rest of the residents with the easy affection one has for the slightly eccentric cousin at the family gathering.

There are the listeners, who put their ears against walls and wait. Some of them go to the child Yara-or-Mara with their findings. *The wall here is thinking of a kitchen*, they say. *The wall there is thinking of saying something*. Yara-or-Mara nods, and translates when she can, and the listeners go away, and the walls continue to think.

There are the mappers. There are always mappers, in any labyrinth, but the Ward's mappers are a special breed. They do not map the Ward. They map the instances. Each instance — each configuration of walls and rooms — has its own character, and the mappers attempt to track these characters over time. There is a map, somewhere, that is also a record of every time the Ward has decided to add a door, and it is pinned up somewhere that is not the same place twice. The mappers meet on Tuesdays, or Wednesdays, and they drink thick tea and argue softly, and they are respected and slightly feared.

There are the eaters. There are the sleepers. There are the in-betweens, who wander.

The wanderers are an entire population of their own. The Ward, dense as it is, has produced them. They do not so much move through the place as *circulate*, in the way blood circulates. They are not lost. They are doing what the lung does. They walk through doors that have just appeared, and they find themselves in rooms that have always been there, and they do not stay long, because the Ward is always moving them on, gently, the way a sleepwalker is moved by the bed. They are the ones who first arrive at new courtyards. They are the ones who find the new chair. They sit in it, and someone offers them a piece in a game they do not yet understand.

The wanderers keep the Ward alive. Without them, the place would settle, and then it would settle again, and eventually it would forget to arrange itself for the benefit of anyone in particular. The wanderers remind it why it is doing what it is doing.

---

The fauna.

The animals of the Ward are few, and they are polite, and they are slightly unreal in the way all of them are unreal, here.

The dogs are gone. There is no convincing explanation. There are pictures of dogs in some of the deeper rooms, in frames that have gone damp at their lower edges, and the dogs in the pictures look like dogs, and the residents look at the pictures and remember dogs the way one remembers a deceased aunt, with affection and a vague grief. The Ward did not forbid dogs. The Ward simply did not support dogs. There are no dogs to feed. There are no dogs to walk. The residents were relieved, then guilty about being relieved, then at peace with the relief. *The Ward is being a Ward*, they say.

The birds are pale. They are not white, exactly; they are the color of wet paper, the color of condensation that has been wiped off a window and left on a cloth. They live in the higher places — on the wall-tops, in the rare courtyards where a few walls stack unevenly enough to make a perch. They do not sing. They make a sound like the turning of a page in a slow reader's hands. A kind of papery rustle, a soft click of the wing. When you hear it in the late afternoon, you know that a bird has just landed somewhere, or just taken off, and you are meant to know this. The birds are not numerous. There are three of them on a good day, the residents say, or four, or five, and no one can say why they keep that count. The birds may be the same bird. The birds do not seem to mind.

The insects are made of light. They are small — the size of a moth, the size of a finger on a child's hand — and they glow the way deep water glows, a faint pale illumination that comes from inside them. You can catch one. You should not catch one. But you can. When it rests on your palm it produces a fleeting warmth, and a sense — not a thought, an actual *sense* — that something has happened which you will remember later without remembering. The insects drift along the walls at chest height, mostly, in lazy paths. The children think they are messages. The adults do not think the children are wrong.

The rats are polite. This is the only word for it. They are the same dull grey as the walls, and they go about their work in the channels at the base of the walls with the busy dignity of clerks. They are not afraid of people, exactly. They are aware of people. If you set a piece of bread on the floor and turn your head, they will take it. If you hold a piece of bread in your hand, they will sit and wait at the edge of visibility, with their small intelligent faces, until your arm grows tired. The residents like the rats. The residents are not embarrassed about liking the rats. *They understand*, a resident will say. *They understand the Ward*.

There are other things — small, unnamed, glimpsed — that move through the deep channels of the place. No one has ever fixed them in description. They are the size of a cat, possibly; or the size of a small child crouching; or the size of a thought. They make no sound. They are seen only in peripheral vision, in the half-glimpsed corner of an inner room, in the moment before one turns. They are harmless. The residents do not remark on them. They are part of the furniture in the same way a creaking floorboard is part of the furniture. You walk over them and do not think of them.

---

There are strangers, sometimes, who pass through.

Some of them stay for a few days and leave with polite gratitude, and they talk later about *the strange little town I visited*, and they describe the walls and the rain and the streetlamp that produces warmth. They could not, if pressed, draw a map. They could not, in a year, find their way back.

Some of them go mad, briefly, in the way visitors to deep caves go briefly mad — a kind of swelling in the head, a sense of being held by a hand larger than they had imagined. They come out of it. They leave. They write to the Ward, sometimes, from elsewhere, to see if anyone will write back. Someone does.

Some of them are the kind of person who comes, and then stops trying to leave, and then becomes one of the residents, in the way described above. There is no ceremony. There is no moment of decision. They simply wake up one morning and realize they are no longer the kind of person who *arrives* in places, and never were, and would not want to be again.

There is also — and I mention this only because it would be dishonest to omit it — the sense, among some of the residents, that the Ward is itself a kind of being. Not a god. Not a person. Something between a person and a dog, between a dog and a clock, between a clock and a slow weather. They say *the Ward is being a Ward today* in a tone that acknowledges this. They say *the Ward is settling into its old age* in the way you would say of an old friend. They do not pray to it. They would not think to. They are simply in the presence of something that notices, and that notices them, and that holds them, very gently, in the palm of its many hands.

---

So: who lives in the Condensation Ward?

A thousand souls, more or less. Damp. Patient. Wearing coats. Drawing suns in chalk on the flat tops of walls, and helping each other draw them. Mending curtains. Playing games that are also maps. Listening to the walls think. Living beside wells of unexpected depth. Crossing intersections with the help of their own shadows. Catching insects made of light and feeling briefly warm. Listening, in the late afternoon, to the place *be*, and then continuing.

Children, mostly. Even the oldest of them is a child of the Ward, the way even the most ancient stone in a cathedral is a stone of the cathedral. They came here, or they were born here, or they assembled, as the place assembled. They are held. They are noticed. They give back to it the only thing the place wants, which is the slow ordinary fact of being lived in.

You can be one of them.

You will know it when the question of leaving stops occurring to you.
