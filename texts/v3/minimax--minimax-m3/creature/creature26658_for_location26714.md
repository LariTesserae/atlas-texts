The lichen-things move first in the morning. Or what passes for morning, which is only the long slow brightening that the plateau permits itself. They have no name for themselves, and the ones who study them from the low domes have given them names that are mostly arithmetic — L-series, M-series, the particular purple-crusted one that has colonized the third basin on the eastern flank. The lichen-things do not have a name for the people either. They have no name for anything. They are the color of old injuries, and they advance across the basalt at a rate that would frustrate a patient god, and they taste the stone with whatever it is they taste with, and they leave behind them the faintest ghost of a chemical signature that says *I was here* in a language the rock is only beginning to learn to read.

In the pools, where the water has settled long enough to forget it is falling, there are the glass- swimmers. They are very small. You would not see them unless you knelt and put your face close, and even then you might mistake them for the play of light on the water's surface. They are not fish. They are not insects. The engineers have argued about them for thirty years and have arrived at no consensus, only a series of increasingly careful words: *motile*, *brittle*, *cold-affine*. The biologists use different careful words. The glass-swimmers themselves, if they have any opinion about being studied, have not made it known. They hang in the water column, or drift through it, or occasionally do something that is neither hanging nor drifting but might be called *intending*. When the pool dries, which happens in the long clear spells, they go somewhere that is not the water, and when the water returns they are back in it, and no one has yet determined where they went. The pools are clear. The bottom is the same stone as the top. The glass-swimmers are, in their way, the most truthful residents of the plateau: they live in what is given to them and they do not pretend otherwise.

On the stone, in the thin films of water that run between the basins, the small things move. The ones with the many legs, the ones with the few legs, the ones that seem to have no legs at all but flow across the wet rock like a second water. They eat the lichen, or they eat each other, or they eat the faint chemical memory the lichen leaves behind, and they are prey for the things that come after, and the things that come after are prey for the things that come after those, and so on, up the chain, to the grazers and the grazers-of-grazers and the great furred ones who move across the plateau in their slow migrations and leave their droppings on the basalt and continue. The small things are many. The small things are the reason the large things are possible. The plateau, by every visible measure, should not support them. It does.

The grazers are the ones you see from the towers, if you climb. They are not shaped like anything you would recognize as a grazer. They are tall and angular and their coats are the same color as the rock in any given light, which is to say they are the color of the plateau, which is to say they are difficult to see. They move in groups of four or seven or eleven and they eat the bunch-grass that is not quite grass, and they have hooves that click on the basalt when they walk, and the sound of many of them walking together is a sound like small bells being struck in no pattern, and from a tower you can hear them for an hour before you see them, and then you see them as a slow darkening of the surface, and then they are past you, and the surface is the surface again, and the only sign they were there is the faint disturbed stone and the droppings and the small things that come to clean up after.

The grazers have yellow eyes. The predators have amber eyes. This is the difference. The grazers will watch a tower for a long time and then return to their eating. The predators will watch a tower for a long time and then not return to anything, because they are no longer there. The predators are few. The food chain is intact and operates with the patience of a well-made machine, which is to say it does not hurry and does not waste and does not apologize. The predators take what they take. The grazers do not seem to hold this against them. The grazers and the predators have been on the plateau longer than the city. The grazers and the predators will be on the plateau after the city, if the city is ever after.

In the domes, in the low structures that sit against the stone like sleeping animals, there are the people. There are not many of them. There are fewer than the grazers in any given herd and more than the predators in any given week. They came here, or they were sent here, or they grew here out of the rock the way the towers grew, and the question of their origin is one of the questions the engineers and the biologists do not discuss in mixed company. They wear what the plateau permits, which is layered and close-fitted and the color of the lichen, the bruise purples and ochres, and they are difficult to see against the stone if they do not want to be seen, which they often do not. They eat what the plateau provides, which is the grazers, in modest number, and the bunch-grass, when it is cooked, and the small things from the films and the pools, which they have learned to prepare in ways that the glass-swimmers do not survive. They drink the water. They breathe the air. They are adapted, or they are sustained, and the difference is a matter of vocabulary and possibly of metaphysics, and they have not settled it among themselves.

They have tools. The tools are not tools, exactly. They are pieces of the plateau that have been persuaded, the way the walls of the towers have been persuaded, at a scale that is hard to perceive. A knife will have the grain of the basalt continuing through it. A cup will be a thinning of a larger stone. A light will be a thing that emits light without burning anything, and the light it emits is the same color as the light outside, which is to say it is not a special color, but the light inside the room where the light is will have a quality of stillness, the way the air in the domes has a quality of stillness, as if the air were being held in place by something patient. The people use these tools. The people do not seem to find the tools remarkable. The tools are the way the plateau is, and the people are part of the plateau now, or the plateau is part of them, and the question of which is the older is, again, a question they do not discuss in mixed company.

They have names for each other. The names are short, mostly, and the people do not use them often. In the domes, when they speak to each other, they use a vocabulary that has been pared down over the generations to the words they cannot do without, and the words they cannot do without turn out to be few. *Water. Stone. Weather. Lichen. Grazer. Predator. The crossing. The hold. The breath. The held breath.* The people of the plateau do not have a word for *home* and they do not seem to miss it. The plateau is where they are. The plateau is what they are made of, in some sense that is not metaphorical, or is only barely metaphorical, and the domes are the inside of the plateau and the towers are the plateau reaching up to read the wind, and the people are the plateau thinking about itself, slowly, in the rarefied air.

They have children. The children are born in the domes, or on the stone, or in transit between the two, and they are small and loud and they do not yet have the lichen-colors in their clothes, and they are the most visible things on the plateau, the most noticeable, the most likely to be seen from a tower in the middle distance. They run. They shout. They fall on the basalt and the basalt teaches them, the way the basalt teaches everything that touches it, with patience and with edges. They learn the names of the grazers before they learn their own names. They learn the smell of the lichen before they learn the smell of themselves. They grow up. They become quiet, the way the plateau is quiet, and they put on the lichen-colors, and they take up the persuaded tools, and they go out onto the stone and they do what the people do, which is to persist, in the cold clarifying air, in the long light that does not dramatize itself.

The people have a word for the hum. The word is not translatable, exactly, but it means something close to *the stone remembering*, or *the stone being spoken to*, or *the stone speaking, in a frequency the air will carry but the ear will sometimes refuse*. Some of the people hear it all the time. Some of the people hear it only at night, or only in the fog, or only when the weather is crossing and the air pressure is changing and the basalt is contracting or expanding by some amount too small to measure. Some of the people say they do not hear it. Some of the people say they have heard it so long that the not-hearing has become a kind of hearing, a negative space in the audible field that is more present than any sound. The hum is not coming from the towers. The hum is not coming from the domes. The hum is coming from the plateau itself, from the young stone that has not yet learned to be smooth, and the city is built into the hum the way a cup is built into the stone that thins to make it, and the hum is the plateau being a plateau, and the people are inside it, and the grazers walk across it, and the glass-swimmers drift in their pools, and the lichen advances its slow bruise-colored front, and the weather crosses and recrosses and crosses again, and the held breath of the place is held, and held, and held, and is, perhaps, on the verge of becoming an exhale. Or perhaps not. The plateau is patient. The plateau has time. The plateau is young.
