# The Inhabitants of the Drowned Plateau

The plateau is not empty. It is not even, in the way that matters, *quiet* — though the sound of it is so constant and so layered that newcomers often report, in the first weeks, a kind of low-grade madness, an inability to separate one signal from another, as if the air itself were a single sustained chord being played on an instrument too large to see. You learn, eventually, to parse it. The plateau's inhabitants have had longer to learn.

---

## The Herd-Things

The largest of the plateau's terrestrial fauna move in the day-long processions the surveyors call *traverses*, though no one knows if the animals themselves have a word for what they are doing. A mature specimen stands roughly two metres at the shoulder — a low, broad, enormously heavy creature built along a body plan that no lineage on any other continent has ever quite committed to. They have too many legs; the exact count is a matter of ongoing dispute among the naturalists, because the legs are arranged in paired banks along the body and the animals move them in waves, and in poor light it is difficult to be certain where one bank ends and the next begins. The hide is thick, rubbery, dark, and perpetually filmed with condensed moisture, so that the things seem to wear a second skin of water. The face — if face is the word — is set low, almost between the forelegs, and bears a cluster of sensory pits that the herders believe can taste the chemical content of the rain itself. The animals orient, in part, by the runoff from the smelters. They know, instinctively, which valleys are hot and which are cooling, and their routes shift across the years to match the shifting work of the industry, as if the herds and the foundries were two hands of the same clock.

The herders — and there are herders, although the relationship is more symbiotic than directive — say that the animals do not sleep, and this is almost true. They rest, in shifts, the way the rest of the plateau's fauna rests: half the herd motionless in a shallow wallow of warm runoff while the other half continues to graze, the rotation turning over every few hours so that no individual is ever still for long. The young are kept, as the text says, in the moving centre, ringed by the largest adults whose flanks bear the long pale scars of a lifetime of indifferent contact with the industrial detritus of the valley floors. The herders move among the outer animals on their own low, broad mounts — animals of a different lineage, smaller, with a shaggy pelt that repels water and a docility that is not, the herders insist, stupidity.

The herd-things are loud. They are loud in a register that is felt before it is heard: a deep, percussive thudding when they move in numbers, the footfalls of a hundred heavy bodies striking the mossy ground in something like synchrony and the ground itself transmitting the rhythm up through the legs of anything standing on it. They vocalize in long, low moans that travel for kilometres under the cloud and are used, by the watchtowers, as a rough weather-glass — when the moans carry farther than usual, the air has become dense and still, and the rain is about to come down harder.

## The Eyes in the Ferns

There are predators. There must be. The herds could not otherwise exist in such numbers, and the smaller swarming fauna could not maintain the population densities the naturalists report from the warmer valleys. The predators are difficult to study because they do not wish to be studied. They are long, low, and lithe; they carry their bodies close to the ground and move in a gait that has been described, by the more poetic of the herders, as *spilled*. They have too many eyes. The eyes are set in paired banks along the flattened skull, and each eye, it is said, can close independently of the others, so that a hunting animal can dim its own silhouette in the murk by selectively shutting down the reflective surfaces while keeping one or two open and alert. They reflect the foundry-light from ridges and are gone. The phrasing is exact: they reflect the light, and then they are gone. The herders say that you will see the reflection, and then you will look at the place where the reflection was, and there will be nothing there, and you will decide, eventually, that there was nothing there to begin with. This is incorrect. There was something there. The something is simply no longer there.

The predators are not, on the whole, dangerous to the herders, although a small number of the herders are lost each year and the lost ones are not, as a rule, recovered. The predators are dangerous to the small things that swarm in the canopy and under the rocks, and they are very dangerous to the rail-cars, which move slowly and predictably and present, the herders say with the grim humour of long acquaintance, an irresistible opportunity. A rail-car that has been still for more than an hour is, statistically, no longer a rail-car. It is a den.

The naturalists have not, to anyone's satisfaction, classified these animals. The taxonomy of the plateau does not fit neatly into the systems developed for the dry continents, and there is a long-running argument, never resolved, about whether the predators are a single species with several regional forms or several species that have convergently evolved the same flat, low, many-eyed body plan. The herders do not care. They call them all *the quiet ones*, which is a name, and which is enough.

## The Swarming Small Things

Under the rocks, in the canopy, in the warm air above the cooling vats, in the spaces between the rail-sleepers where the pale fungi are digesting the wood, the plateau teems with the small fauna. There is a great deal of it. There is so much of it that the herders and the foundry-men have largely given up trying to keep it out of the built environment and have instead, in a spirit of long accommodation, learned to live alongside it. A barracks room that is entirely free of the small fauna is a barracks room that has been recently poisoned, and the barracks-men will avoid it on that account.

The small fauna speaks. The small fauna is, at any given moment, almost certainly speaking. The conversation is constant and is conducted in registers that overlap and interleave without ever quite resolving into a single voice. There are the clickers — small, hard-shelled things that live under rocks and along the rail-ballast and that produce, by rubbing certain body parts against certain other body parts, a rapid, dry, percussive chatter that the herders use, again, as a weather-glass. The singing things produce tones that are felt in the sinuses and, occasionally, in the chest, low frequencies that the ear does not process but the body does. The glowing things — there are many of these, in many colours, though the dominant luminescence, on the plateau, is a faint blue-green or a deep red-amber, depending on the chemical content of the local runoff — produce light when alarmed, and as a consequence the air above an active herding route is, at any given moment, a slow constellation of pulses and flares, the small fauna announcing to itself, and to the predators, that it has been disturbed.

None of the small fauna is, individually, sapient. Collectively, the herders are not certain. The herders have a long list of questions about the small fauna, and a much shorter list of answers, and the answers they have are mostly of the form *we have stopped doing that* and *we do not go into that valley after dark* and *the rail-cars on the upper inclines sometimes arrive at their destination with the doors sealed from the inside, and we do not open the doors, and we reroute the cars, and we do not speak of this*. The plateau is not a place that encourages curiosity about its smallest inhabitants. The plateau encourages a respectful disinterest.

## The Blooms

The flowers that open on the cliffs after a heavier rain are not, technically, *inhabitants* of the plateau. They are *of* the plateau — they are grown from the plateau, they are an expression of the plateau, in the way that the rust on a rail-spike is an expression of the rail-line and the rain. But they are alive, and they behave, and they have come, over the long centuries, to track the chemistry of the industrial runoff with a fidelity that the naturalists find unsettling and the foundry-men find, in a quiet way, almost companionable.

The blooms are large — a mature specimen, on a particularly favourable cliff face, can reach the size of a washtub — and they are heavy with water, and they glow. The glow is a faint, cool blue-green around the older workings, where the runoff has had time to settle into something the blooms apparently prefer, and a deeper, almost red amber around the newer ones, where the chemistry is hotter and more various. The blooms open after a heavier rain and close, slowly, over the following days, releasing as they close a faint, mineral-sweet scent that the herders say is the closest thing the plateau has to a dawn. The blooms are pollinated by something — the naturalists have argued about this for decades and the argument has not been resolved, because no one has ever caught a pollinator in the act, and the blooms, which are otherwise passive, are observed to close sharply and without warning if a small animal lands on them, and the small animal is not, thereafter, observed again.

The blooms are also, the herders note, an excellent indicator of the health of a given valley. A cliff that bloomed blue-green last year and is blooming red-amber this year has, in some significant sense, changed, and the change will be reflected, in the following months, in the behaviour of the herds and the quality of the runoff in the streams and the precise character of the sound that rises from the deeper workings. The blooms are the plateau's quiet way of telling you what the plateau is doing, if you know how to read them. The foundry-men know how to read them. The foundry-men have known for a long time.

## The Foundry-Men

The plateau is inhabited, and has been for as long as anyone can remember, by people. They are a people shaped by the place in the way that any people, given enough centuries, is shaped by the place that holds them. They are not, in the main, tall. They are broad, and they are patient, and they are quieter than you would expect of people who live above a constant pulse of hammers and turbines. They have learned, over the generations, to speak at a volume that does not compete with the sound, and to listen at a volume that does not require them to ask twice.

They live in the low, windowless barracks and the slightly less low, slightly less windowless houses that have accreted, over the centuries, around the older workings. They paint the buildings a colour that was once bright and is now, by long exposure, a mineral grey. The mineral grey is, in its way, the only honest colour, and the foundry-men have come to prefer it. They are a people of the mineral grey.

They work in shifts, the way the fauna works in shifts, the way everything on the plateau works in shifts. The day is divided into the cooler hours and the warmer hours, and the warmer hours are the hours of the heavier industry, and the cooler hours are the hours of the maintenance and the surveying and the slow, patient business of reading the plateau. The watchtowers are manned, on a rotation, by the foundry-men who have the patience for the long watches and the steady eye for the slow signals that come across the murk from the next watchtower. The herders ride out, on their shaggy mounts, with the herds. The rail-men drive the slow cars along the floor of the broader valleys and up the long inclines to the higher workings. The drainage-men maintain the ditches that carry the runoff from the smelters down into the streams. The naturalists, who are few and who are listened to, maintain the long argument about the taxonomy of the predators and the blooms and the small fauna and do not expect, in their working lives, to resolve it. The archivists, who are fewer still, maintain the records of which valleys bloomed which colour in which year, and what the herds did, and what the rail did, and what the cloud did, and what the murk did, and what the rain did, and they do not expect anyone to read the records, and they maintain them anyway.

The foundry-men are not, in the main, sentimental about the plateau. They are *of* the plateau, in the way that the rust is of the rail-spike, and they do not find this troubling. The plateau is what it is. The plateau has been what it is for longer than the foundry-men have been on it. The foundry-men have been on it for a long time, and they have settled into the landscape the way the rivers have settled into the landscape, and they have reached, with the landscape, a kind of truce.

---

## The Cloud Itself

There is one final inhabitant of the plateau, and it is the most patient of them all. The cloud has been there longer than any of the others. The cloud is the reason for the rain, and the rain is the reason for the ferns, and the ferns are the reason for the herds, and the herds are the reason for the predators, and the predators are the reason for the small fauna, and the small fauna is the reason for the soil, and the soil is the reason for the ferns, and the whole of it turns, slowly, in the long warmth of the geothermal fields, and the cloud sits above all of it, and the cloud does not lift.

The cloud is not, in any ordinary sense, alive. But it moves, and it thickens and thins, and the animals have learned to read it, and the foundry-men have learned to read it, and the rail schedules bend around it, and the murk closes, and the rain finds you, and the warm air presses against your face, and the distant sound of the hammers picks up where it left off, and continues.
