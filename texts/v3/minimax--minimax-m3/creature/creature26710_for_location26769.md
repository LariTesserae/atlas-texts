The woman leaning against the pillar is not leaning against the pillar. She is *resting* against the pillar, which is a different verb in this city, a verb that implies a temporary arrangement that has not yet been formalized into ownership. Her name is something like Adriana or Beatriz — names that live well in the mouth of this place, that have the right vowels for the wet air. She has been on this platform before. She will be on this platform again. The pillar has a small worn concavity at shoulder height where generations of leaning have polished the concrete into something almost friendly. She knows this concavity. She put her shoulder into it without looking.

She is forty-three, or forty-eight — the wet does something to the apparent age of women here, takes a year off or puts a year on depending on the season and the light. Her bag is plastic, the kind that comes from a particular bakery on the hill, and it has been repurposed for thirty other uses and still smells faintly of bread, which is, in this city, a kind of citizenship. She is going home. She has been going home for twenty-two years by this train, with the same bag and roughly the same bag's contents and a posture of patience that she has refined to an art. She knows the platform. She knows the timetables are a fiction maintained by the transit authority for its own dignity. She knows the train will come, eventually, in the way that rain will come. She does not check her phone more than twice.

A man three meters down the platform is reading a newspaper. The newspaper is from this morning. The newspaper has been the same newspaper for twenty minutes because he is not reading the newspaper. He is holding the newspaper. He is holding the newspaper in front of his face the way a person holds a newspaper in a crowd — not to read but to mark himself as a person who reads, who is occupied, who is not available, who has chosen to wait here rather than somewhere else. He is a foundry worker. You can tell by the dust on his cuffs, a fine orange dust that no wash fully removes and that lives in the weave of his clothes the way the smell of bread lives in the woman's bag. He works the afternoon shift, fourteen hundred to twenty-two hundred, and his hands are wide and his knuckles are scarred and the newspaper trembles slightly in them in a way he has never noticed.

His name is something with a hard consonant at the end, a name his grandfather brought down from a mountain town three days' bus ride east. He has not been to the mountain town in eleven years. He sends money. The respiratory lives in his chest now as a kind of second weather, a tightness that comes on in the late afternoon when the air thickens, and he has learned to breathe around it the way you learn to walk around a permanent obstacle in your house. He has not named it. He does not say the full name. His wife says the full name only to doctors, in clinics that smell of disinfectant and damp.

They live in a fifth-floor walk-up in the colonial center, in a building with a deep eave and a courtyard and a Virgin on the second-floor landing whose paint is peeling. The building is from the 1880s, yellow brick, soot-blackened at the cornices. The walls are a meter thick. This is good for the heat, less good for the damp, which moves through the masonry at its own pace and emerges in the corners as a kind of permanent bloom, dark and soft. They have painted over the bloom four times. It comes back. It is patient. They have come to a kind of arrangement with it. The bloom has its own room. The room is the one without a window.

The foundry worker and the woman on the platform do not know each other. They have shared this platform at the same hour for years. They have nodded. They have, on occasion, in the worst of the foundry noise when the trains do not run, walked together to the bus stop without speaking, because walking together in silence is a thing this city allows and even encourages. They would not call each other friends. They would, if asked, hesitate, and then say *vecino*, which is not exactly *neighbor* and not exactly *stranger* but a category between, the category that this city has invented for the people you stand near often enough to develop a relationship of posture.

The dogs on the platform are owned by no one on the platform. This is not the same as saying they are not owned. The dogs of this city live in a careful, ancient arrangement with the people. They are mostly yellow, mostly medium-sized, mostly calm. They have been here longer than the train system. They know which platforms run at which hours and they appear accordingly, not as pets but as fixtures, the way the pillars are fixtures, the way the yellow lines are fixtures. They sleep on the warm concrete in the late afternoon. They bark at the green parakeets but only when the parakeets are overhead, never when the parakeets are in the cooling towers, which is a thing the dogs understand and the parakeets understand and the foundry workers understand, who share the cooling tower airspace with both and have come to a kind of joint tenancy with them.

The parakeets, right now, are not overhead. They are four kilometers east, in the foundry district, doing what they do in the late afternoon. They are, as a population, some forty thousand birds. They nest in colonies of three or four hundred. Their original ancestors were imported as cage birds for a wealthy woman who lived in the *barrio alto* in the 1970s — this is the story, and it is true enough — and when the aviary failed during a power outage, some escaped, and those that did not die found in the cooling towers a habitat almost perfectly suited to them, warm, drafty, with the particular mineral-rich water that runs off the foundry slag. They adapted. They did not so much thrive as become inevitable. Their calls are high and ragged and at certain hours — five o'clock, mostly, when the day shift lets out and the afternoon shift is settling into its rhythm — they combine with the foundry roar into something that is no longer two sounds but one. The foundry workers hear the parakeets in their sleep. The foundry workers' children hear the parakeets and think of them as a kind of weather. The foundry workers' grandchildren, the ones now eight and nine, imitate the parakeet call without thinking, as a punctuation, as a *you know*.

The rats live under the colonial center in a network of tunnels and cellars that predates the colonial center. They are large. They are not aggressive. They have a stable arrangement with the city, which is that they will stay below the second floor and not chew through the wiring in the older buildings, and the city will not poison them in the foundations. There are cats in some of the old buildings — lean, mottled, kept by the older residents — and the cats and the rats have their own arrangements, which are territorial and which involve the patient mathematics of available cellar space. The cats are named. The rats are not.

In the lowest neighborhoods, near the river, in the streets that flood when it rains heavily, the people have built their ground floors to be sacrificial. They are not poor, exactly, although they are not wealthy. They are *adapted*, which is a different category, a category this city has refined over centuries. Their houses have external stairs to the second floor. The second floor is the real floor. The ground floor is concrete and tile, swept daily, used for storage, for laundry on the rare dry days, for the visiting relatives who come for the saint's day and sleep on mats on the floor and find, in the morning, a fine silt on their shoes from the water that came in overnight and receded before they woke. The people of these streets are mostly old. The young have gone up the hill or down to the foundry district or out to the slope neighborhoods. The old stay because the old know that the river will not hurt them, has not hurt them yet, has come in three times in the memory of the street and each time left more silt than water, and the silt is good for the small gardens they keep in the courtyards at the back, where they grow the heavy greens that will not grow higher up.

There is a small chapel in the lowest neighborhood, accessible only by a footbridge in the wet season. The Virgin inside stands ankle-deep in permanent water. Her blue robe is stained brown at the hem. She has been standing there for ninety years. She is not the only Virgin standing in water in this city — there is a kind of regional devotion to this particular arrangement, which began, an old priest will tell you if you ask, because the first chapel was built by people who did not believe the water would stay and then the water stayed and they did not move the Virgin because moving her would have been an admission that the water had won. So the Virgin stands. So the water stands. The two of them have come to a kind of equilibrium. The brown stain is not decay. It is the water's signature. The priest blesses it once a year, in the dry month, when the water is lowest, and the congregation stands on the footbridge and watches.

The small brown birds come into the city at dusk in undulating clouds. They are starlings, or something like starlings — a species that arrived in the colonial period, maybe in a ship's rigging, maybe in a cargo of grain, and found the eucalyptus plantations a perfect substitute for the trees they had evolved with. There are millions of them. Their flocks register on the weather radar as precipitation, which is a fact that the meteorologists know and do not find funny and that the people of the city find very funny. At dusk the birds pour over the ridges and into the basin and they wheel above the foundry district and the colonial center and the barrios altos, and the sky is briefly more bird than air, and then they settle, in the eucalyptus, in the cracks of the older buildings, in the cooling towers with the parakeets, and the city becomes quieter for a few minutes, the way a forest becomes quieter at dusk, which is a thing that the city used to be and is no longer and is, in this one hour, briefly again.

The cathedral tree is a ceiba, or something like a ceiba — a tree with buttress roots and a trunk that does not quite obey the rules of trunks. It grows out of the bell tower of the cathedral, which is, by every structural logic, impossible. It should not be there. It has been there for forty years. The cathedral has been repaired around it three times. The tree and the cathedral are now structurally dependent on each other in ways the engineers cannot fully model. The roots have entered the masonry and follow the old water channels down into the foundations and out into the plaza, where, in the wet season, a few of them emerge through the cobbles and lift them slightly, and the city walks around the lifted cobbles. The bells cannot be rung the way they were once rung, because the tree is in the bell tower, and the bell ringers have adapted. They ring the bells with a kind of muffled strike that the tree tolerates. The tree, in return, drops a few leaves a week onto the plaza, and the leaves are gathered by an old man each morning before mass, and he has been gathering them for twenty-three years, and he knows each leaf.

The river is alive in the slow sense. It moves. It carries. It is fed by rains that fall on mountains that the city cannot see and that the city knows about the way you know about an absent parent, as a fact, as a weight. The river is the color of a bruise healing, which means it is a particular kind of brown, a brown that has green in it, that has red in it, that has, if you held a glass of it to the light, a faint translucence, like weak tea. The river has silt. The silt is, partly, the city. Buildings have fallen into it over centuries. The custom house, in part. The old bridge, in whole. A foundry, once, in 1947, the night of the flood, when the slag heaps gave way. The river carries this material down to the marshes, where it settles, where it becomes the substrate for the reeds, which become the substrate for the birds, which become the substrate for the small fish, which become the substrate for the larger fish, which become, eventually, the protein on the plates of the foundry workers' children on Fridays. The cycle is not a metaphor. The cycle is dinner.

Above the foundry district, the clouds glow at night. This is a fact. The glow is the combination of the foundry lights, the slag flare, and the sodium vapor of the streetlamps, reflected off the low cloud and returned to the city. The glow is permanent. The glow does not turn off. The people who live below it sleep under a permanent faint amber. They have always slept under it. They do not dream in color. This is not a thing anyone has studied. It is a guess.

You step aboard. The doors close. The train begins to climb.

The woman on the platform remains. She will take the next train. She does not mind. She has read three pages of a novel about a woman in another city who is also waiting for things, and the novel is good, and she will resume it. The foundry worker has gone down the hill, into the noise, into the warm, into the second weather of his chest. The dogs have dispersed, in twos and threes, to their territories. The parakeets have begun their evening scream. The brown birds are wheeling. The river is moving. The tree is in the bell tower. The Virgin is in the water.

The city continues, patiently, in its own time.
