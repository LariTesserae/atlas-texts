# Who Lives in the Hollow Garden

## The People of the Terrace

There are perhaps two hundred of them, though no one has ever counted in a way that produced a number anyone trusted. They call themselves the **Moss-Folk**, though only among themselves; to outsiders — and there are no outsiders — they have no need of a name.

They are not tall. Generations of living in the perpetual half-light, in the cold that rises from below, have made them slight and bent in a particular way: a low, permanent stoop, as if they are always listening to something underfoot. Their skin is pale in the way that things grown in shade are pale — not the pallor of sickness, but the soft, unsalted pallor of mushrooms, of new potatoes pushed up into darkness, of the underside of a leaf. Their eyes are wide and adapted for the green dim, and they see better in the cathedral gloom of the forest floor than they ever could under any honest sky. When they look at you, you feel looked *through*. Their hair, when they are young, is dark and grows in thickets; as they age it lightens, thins, and takes on the color of old thatch.

They are quiet people. Not because they have chosen silence, but because the place has taught it to them. Sound carries strangely here — absorbed by moss, swallowed by the cellular architecture of the living walls — and so they have learned to speak softly, to gesture with the whole hand, to read the small movements of a companion's shoulders the way surface-dwellers read lips. A child raised here will sometimes weep without making a sound, and the parents will know, because the tears come.

They live on the basalt terrace, the old courtyard, where the fitted stones are still mostly level under the moss. They have seventeen houses. (There were once twenty, but two have been absorbed back into the green so completely that the elders will not say which ones.) The houses are round, low, roofed in living thatch. A house, when it is new, smells of cut reed and wet clay; by its third winter it smells of moss and cold stone; by the tenth, it smells only of the forest, and a stranger walking through the village would be hard-pressed to say which hummocks were houses and which were merely the slow tide of growth. The M-Folk think of this as a kind of mercy. They do not want to be a *thing* in the forest; they want to be a *part* of it, the way a gall is part of a tree, the way a knot is part of a board.

They are farmers, but the word is too grand for what they do. They cannot break the soil — the soil is the floor, and the floor is alive, and to break it would be a kind of violence they have collectively agreed not to commit. Instead, they *dig in*. They find places where the moss has piled itself thickest, where a fallen trunk has rotted into a low mound of dark, fibrous humus, and they sink clay-lined pits into it, and into the pits they lower their seedlings — the pale, fist-sized tubers of a root vegetable they call the **stone-turnip**, which grows nowhere else and tastes of nothing in particular but sustains life. They cultivate a low, sour grass that they reap with curved knives and dry on flat stones, and a small bean that climbs the trunks of the fallen columns and is harvested by children sent up on rope. They keep no animals but a kind of house-slug, fat and gray, that eats moss-scrapings and is itself eaten on festival days, though the festivals are not what they once were.

Hunting is a ritual more than a practice. The pale deer are too many and too tame to be hunted in any sustained way, and to kill one is considered an act of poor form, like swatting a fly that has landed on your hand. Instead, the M-Folk set snares in the bamboo runways and wait, sometimes for days, for the small things that pass through — a **velvet-fox**, a **moss-hare**, a flightless bird the size of a chicken that lives in the upper green and sometimes falls, stunned, to the floor. They eat what they catch. They do not waste. They thank the animal, briefly, in a small formal murmur, and they bury the bones in a pit behind the largest house, where, over generations, a small hill of whitened bone has risen, carefully covered with moss, and is tended like a garden.

They have a single shaman, or sometimes two, depending on the year. The shaman is not a healer in the way outsiders would understand. The shaman is the person who can read the **turning** — the slow movement of the vast thing in the canopy that shifts the light-shafts across the floor. The turning is their clock, their calendar, their weather. When the light moves north, the cold deepens and the tubers sweeten. When the light moves south, the rains come and the flowers open. The shaman watches the floor and tells the village what the canopy is doing. To do this requires a particular patience, a kind of seeing that is more like listening. The shaman is usually an old woman, though not always. The current one, **Esh**, is thirty-eight and will not live to see her successor named, but she has already chosen her: a boy of nine who sits at the edge of the village for hours, watching the light move, and does not seem bored.

The M-Folk do not remember who built the basalt terrace, the fitted flagstones, the broken pillar. They have a word for these things — **the old making** — and they treat them with a tenderness that is not quite reverence and not quite fear. They have legends, but the legends have rotted the way the carvings have rotted, leaving only fragments: a king who held up the sky, a fire that burned without fuel, a people who were tall and did not bend. They do not believe these stories in the way that believers believe. They believe them the way one believes in grandparents one never met — as a shape in the family, a presence at the edge of memory.

## The Animals

The **pale deer** are the most seen and the least known. They stand a meter and a half at the shoulder, but their legs are too long for that shoulder, an extra joint somewhere in the canon bone that gives them a stilted, careful walk, as if they are afraid of putting a foot wrong in the moss. Their coats are the off-white of old bone, shading to gray on the flanks, and they have no antlers — only a small, hard boss of cartilage on the brow, smooth and dark. They are browsers, not grazers, and they eat the high moss and the leaves of the lower canopy, standing on their hind legs to reach, swaying gently, like children reaching for a high shelf. They are not afraid of the M-Folk. They have never learned to be. A deer will sometimes walk through the village at dusk, picking its way between the houses, and the people will step aside for it, and the deer will not acknowledge them, and no one will feel slighted.

The **velvet-foxes** are smaller than true foxes and softer in the way they move, more like smoke than like animals. Their fur is the precise gray of wood-ash, and their paws make no sound on the moss. They hunt the insects that hum below hearing, and they hunt, too, the **floor-birds**, the small ground-dwellers that scrabble in the moss. They are solitary except when they mate, and the mating is a strange affair: a pair will sit facing each other in a clearing for hours, not touching, not vocalizing, simply looking, until one of them rises and walks away and the other follows. The M-Folk consider the velvet-fox to be a creature of *good omen*, though they will not say what kind of omen, and they will not harm one, even when it steals a fat slug from a window-box.

The **floor-birds** are the size of a child's fist and have no song, only a dry, clicking rattle when they are alarmed. They live in the upper layers of the moss and feed on the tiny white flowers that ripple across the floor when the air moves. They are extraordinarily numerous — there are tens of thousands of them — and they are the principal food of the foxes and, in lean weeks, of the M-Folk. Their eggs are pale blue and laid in clutches of three in little cups of woven grass at the base of the columns. The M-Folk collect the eggs but never all of them, always leaving one in each nest, a small, persistent superstition that has never been articulated as a law but is observed as carefully as any law.

The **insects that hum below hearing** are not insects at all, or if they are, they are insects in a way that would confuse a naturalist. They are small — the size of a lentil — and they live in the air itself, in the warm, moist layers just above the moss, and their wings beat at a frequency that is felt in the chest before it is heard by the ear. A M-Folk child raised in the village will, by the age of four, have stopped being aware of this hum entirely. A visitor, if one ever came, would feel a low, persistent nausea within the hour, and a pressure behind the eyes, and would have to leave the forest or go mad. The M-Folk do not know this about themselves. They have simply always lived in the vibration, and to them the vibration is silence, the way the motion of the earth is silence to the walker.

The thing that **passes through** — the shape in the deep green, the movement felt through the floor before it is seen — is not spoken of. To call it by any name is to invite it. The M-Folk have a small ritual: when the floor begins to tremble, they all go indoors, they all light a small fire, they all sit in silence and do not look at the door until the trembling has passed. This happens perhaps once in a generation, sometimes twice, sometimes not at all. The oldest living M-Folk, a woman called **Uthe** who is ninety-three and has the bent, lichened look of a piece of driftwood, says she remembers three such passages in her life. She will not say what she saw from the window. She will only say, when pressed, *It was very tall. It did not see us. It was looking for something older.*

## The Plants

The plants are not, strictly speaking, *inhabitants* — they are the place itself. But there is one that the M-Folk treat as something like a person.

It is a **fig tree**, enormous, that has grown into and around a broken basalt column on the edge of the village. The fig is not the largest tree in the forest — those are the great columns themselves, whatever they are — but it is the most central to the life of the M-Folk. Its roots have pried apart the carved acanthus leaves of the column's capital until the stone looks like a fist of fingers forced open. Its branches spread low and wide over five of the houses, and in the warm season it fruits prodigiously, dropping pale figs onto the thatch roofs, where they are gathered by children and fermented into a thin, sour wine that is drunk at the turning of the light.

The M-Folk do not worship the fig tree. They *consult* it. When a child is born, a fig is set on the threshold of the house and left to rot or be taken; if it rots, the child is said to be of the village; if it is taken — by a fox, by a bird, by the turning of some unseen foot — the child is said to be of the forest, and is treated with a slightly more careful tenderness, the way one treats a guest. When someone dies, a fig is split and the seeds are counted, and the number is the number of years the dead person had remaining to them, which is then subtracted from the life of the next child born. The arithmetic does not always work, but no one checks it.

There is a **single orchid** that grows nowhere but on the trunk of a fallen column at the edge of the village. It blooms once in seven years, and only in the week when the light is at its deepest green, and its flower is the color of old bone and smells of nothing. The M-Folk do not pick it. They watch it bloom and they watch it close and they say nothing about it, because it is the one thing in their world that is purely *itself* and has no use. They respect it for that the way one respects an elder who will not be questioned.

## The One Who Is Not of the Village

There is a man who lives apart.

He is called **Kess**, and he is forty, and he was born in the village but he does not live in it. He lives in a small shelter he built himself out of living willow, in a clearing a half-day's walk from the terrace, in a place where the moss is so deep it has buried the broken foundations of something — a wall, a tower, no one knows — and only the tops of the worked stones show, like teeth in an old mouth. Kess is a hunter, but not of the kind the village practices. He hunts *knowledge*.

He is the only M-Folk who has attempted to read the broken pillar in the center of the village — the one with the carved acanthus leaves, the one the fig has pried apart. He has copied the marks that remain onto sheets of bark and has spent years trying to understand them. He believes, or half-believes, or wants to believe, that the marks are language. He has not decoded them. He may never decode them. But he goes to the pillar every morning and sits beneath it and looks up at it the way one looks at the face of someone one loves and does not quite understand.

The village tolerates him. The shaman, Esh, consults him in private, on certain nights, when the turning of the light has been strange. He is not shunned, exactly, but he is set slightly apart, the way a bell is set apart on a wall — present, useful, but resonant with a different music. He has not married. He has no children. He is thin and weathered and his eyes are very wide.

Once, in his twenty-third year, he walked for three days into the deep forest, following a road of fitted flagstone that runs in a long straight arrow away from the village. He came back thinner, quieter, and would not say what he had found. The elders asked him, gently, where the road went. He said, *It went. That was all it did. It went.* They did not ask again.

## What Holds Them Here

The M-Folk do not stay because they are rooted, the way the figs are rooted. They stay because there is nowhere else to go. The cold that rises from below is the same cold that meets the light from above, and the cold is indifferent to the small heat of their fires, and the vast green is indifferent to the small brown of their bodies, and they have understood, over many generations, that they are a small and temporary thing inside a much larger and more permanent thing.

They do not despair of this. They are not a melancholy people. They are a *patient* people, the way the moss is patient, the way the cold is patient. They light their small fires in the hollow of what may once have been a god's footprint and they call it home, and they plant their roofs with sedum and moss and wait for the houses to forget they were ever built. They bury their dead in the bone-pit and they name their children after the light-shaft that falls on the morning of their birth. They eat their sour bread and drink their sour wine and they listen, in their quiet way, for the turning of the great thing in the canopy.

They are not the forest's keepers. They are the forest's *guests*, and they know it, and the knowing has given them a particular kind of grace — the grace of those who do not expect to be remembered.

And they will not be. In a thousand years the village will be a hummock. In ten thousand, a contour. In a hundred thousand, a fossil under the root-mat, and the forest will still be growing, and the cold will still be holding, and the light will still be falling in long, slow, golden shafts through a green so deep and so old that no one will remember it was ever anything else.

But for now — for this one brief, golden shaft of years — the smoke rises and the deer watches and the shaman watches the light and the man apart sits under his broken pillar and tries to read the unreadable, and the fig tree fruits on the carved leaves of a thing it has long since swallowed, and the village, for now, calls it home.
