# The Inhabitants of the Plateau

## I. The Residents

The residents are not numerous. There have never been many — the city was never built to hold a populace so much as to host the conditions under which a populace might persist in spite of itself. Those who remain are, by any outward measure, depleted. They are tall and narrow, as if the thin air had drawn them out along their own axes. Their skin has the color and apparent temperature of the dressed stone of the second stratum — a pale, matte grey-cream that does not tan, does not flush, does not show the small violences of weather. Their hair, when they have it, is the same. Their eyes are the color of weak tea left to cool, and they do not widen. The muscles of their faces have learned a kind of economical stillness, so that expressions pass across them like weather across the pewter sky — present, but not committed to.

They are thin in a way that is not starving, exactly, but that has moved past starving into something more architectural. The ribs are visible not as a sign of deprivation but as a structural fact, like the fluting of a column. The hands are long, the fingers slightly too many joints, though the residents themselves do not seem to notice this and would not, if asked, consider it remarkable. They have been here too long to mark themselves as different from the place. They are, in their own slow way, becoming stratigraphic.

They do not speak much. The silence of the plateau makes speech unrewarding — a word falls into the air and is held briefly, like a coin in a cupped palm, and then the palm tips and the word is gone, and there is no echo to confirm that it was ever uttered. So they have developed, over generations, a quieter vocabulary. A hand laid flat on a wall means *I am here*. A hand laid flat on a wall and then turned over, palm up, means *I am here and I have not eaten*. A long exhale through the nose, with the shoulders dropped, means *the geometry is behaving today, or we are behaving around the geometry, which amounts to the same thing*. There are perhaps forty such gestures in common use, and another twenty that belong to specific relationships — the gesture a mother makes over a sleeping child, the gesture one elder gives another across a doorway, the small private language of two people who have agreed to share a chamber and its unreliable interior.

They do not, as a rule, look each other directly in the face. The eye contact of someone who has lived for any length of time in the second stratum can be disorienting in ways that are difficult to describe afterward — not unpleasant, but as though the other person's face had briefly become a door onto a different chamber, and the chamber had been the one you were already in. So they look at the shoulders, the hands, the feet. They look at the pale stone. They have made an etiquette of the peripheral.

Their clothing, when they wear it, is fashioned from a material no one can quite account for. It is not woven, not in any conventional sense — it has the appearance of a felted thing, but it is not wool, and the fibers, when examined, prove to be a kind of compressed film scraped from the deeper chambers, mixed with whatever the pale fauna shed. It is the same color as the stone. It is the same temperature as the stone. After a few seasons of wear, it begins to behave like stone — stiffening at the corners, taking on a faint mineral taste, occasionally incorporating itself into a wall as though it had always been a part of the architecture and the person wearing it had simply been a small, soft intrusion. The residents do not find this distressing. They have been known to leave a sleeve behind, deliberately, when a wall seemed to want it.

They sleep when they must, and not always at the same hours. There is no real night here, only the slow deepening, and the body eventually makes its own arrangements. Some sleep through the brightest part of the pewter afternoon. Some sleep in the hours when the phosphorescence gathers in the unfinished walls. A few — the very old ones, the very still ones — sleep for days at a time, and the city adjusts around them. A sleeping elder in a doorway becomes, over a long afternoon, a kind of architectural feature. Stones rearrange themselves in deference. The pale fauna trace their lines around the sleeping form as around a hearth.

The few children are quieter than children anywhere else. They learn the gestures before they learn the words, and they learn, very early, not to argue with the corner that fails to meet. They are taught, by whoever is nearest at the time — for the residents do not organize themselves into families in any stable way, since the unreliable geometry makes sustained proximity a logistical problem — they are taught the small set of rules that keep one alive here. Do not trust a wall you have not tested. Do not trust a wall you have tested, if the test was yesterday. Walk the route that does not contradict itself. If a corridor begins to lengthen, turn around; if it will not let you turn around, sit down and wait; if sitting down begins to feel like falling, close your eyes and count in a language you do not speak. The children learn these things the way children everywhere learn the names of the dangerous animals — with a kind of fond, fatalistic attention.

## II. The Pale Fauna

The fauna are not, strictly speaking, fauna. They have no name for themselves, presumably, since naming requires a kind of consensus they do not appear to engage in. They are small — most no larger than a handspan, though a few of the very old ones, deep in the second stratum, have grown to the size of a small dog, and are treated by the residents with a kind of respectful avoidance, as one might treat a piece of furniture that has begun, subtly, to assert itself.

They are pale in the way the stone is pale — not white, not cream, but the color of something that has been the color of something else for a very long time and has forgotten the original. Their bodies are translucent in places, particularly at the joints, and the small internal structures visible through the skin move with a sluggish purpose, like things in a jar of very still water. They have too many joints, as the account given elsewhere notes, but the number is not consistent. A specimen examined on Tuesday may have seven joints in a forelimb; examined on Thursday, it may have nine, or five. The residents do not comment on this. The residents do not comment on most things.

They do not look directly at anything. Their eyes, which are pale and lidless and set on the sides of small triangular heads, seem to be oriented toward the world in a general way, the way a plant is oriented toward light — present, but not focused. They appear to perceive by some other sense, or by some combination of senses, in a manner that does not require the confirmation of a direct gaze. When a resident enters a chamber, the fauna do not flee and do not approach. They simply continue what they were doing, with a faint alteration of pace that might be acknowledgment and might be coincidence.

What they were doing is, almost always, tracing. The tips of their forelimbs — the forelimbs, in the larger specimens, having become elongated and almost delicate, like the antennae of a moth — are tipped with something that is not quite a claw and not quite a brush, and they drag these tips along the pale stone as they move. The lines they leave are very fine, and very faint, and they persist for hours before the stone absorbs them, or the air dries them, or the geometry simply forgets they were there. The lines are not random. They curve and branch and double back in ways that suggest a kind of syntax, but the syntax does not resolve. It does not, as far as anyone has been able to determine, say anything. Or it says something, but only to other fauna, and the conversation is not one the residents have been invited into.

The residents do not hunt the fauna. They cannot, in any case — the fauna are too quick, and too quiet, and too evidently part of the architecture to be worth the disturbance. But they eat what the fauna leave behind, which is not much. A small, dry pellet, pale as the stone, deposited in corners. A faint residue where the fauna have been tracing for a long time, which can be gathered on a fingertip and eaten with the same ceremony one might bring to a communion wafer. The residents do not find this degrading. They have a small, dry humor about it, in the rare moments they permit themselves humor at all.

The fauna, in turn, do not seem to mind the residents. They will trace around a seated elder. They will trace across a sleeping child, very lightly, leaving a fine pale line on the cheek that fades by morning. They will, in the deeper hours, when the phosphorescence has gathered and the city is at its quietest, emerge in greater numbers and move through the unfinished third stratum in a kind of slow procession, tracing as they go, and the residents who are awake to witness this do not speak. There is nothing to say. The fauna are doing something the residents do not understand, and the residents have agreed, over a long time, that understanding is not required for cohabitation.

## III. The Very Old Ones

There is a third category of inhabitant, and the residents are not entirely sure it is a separate category. These are the very old ones, and they are old in a way that has nothing to do with years — there is no reliable way to count years here, and the few who have tried have come away with figures that do not agree with one another. The very old ones are old in the way a stone is old, or a wall. They have been here long enough that the place has begun to make them.

They sit, mostly. They sit in the deeper chambers of the second stratum, in the rooms whose interiors do not agree with their exteriors, where the air is most ceremonial and the phosphorescence pools longest. They sit on the pale stone, or on a low bench that may or may not be a feature of the architecture, and they do not move for very long stretches. Their skin has taken on a mineral cast. The films — the pale growth that is neither lichen nor fungus nor mineral — have colonized them, in places, and the colonized patches are indistinguishable from the wall behind them. The very old ones do not seem to mind this. They may, in fact, prefer it. They have made themselves, over a long time, into a kind of furniture, and the city has accepted them as such. The pale fauna trace their lines across the seated forms without comment. The walls, when they correct themselves overnight, leave the very old ones where they are.

The very old ones do not speak. They have moved past speech, or below it, into the realm of the small gesture. A finger, raised slightly, can mean a great many things. A long, slow blink can mean everything. The residents consult them, in a manner that is not quite consultation and not quite veneration — a sitting elder is asked, in passing, whether a particular corridor is safe today, and the answer is given in the angle of a shoulder, in the placement of a hand. The very old ones are, in their way, the city's memory. They remember which corners fail to meet. They remember which walls have been trustworthy in the past, and which walls have been the kind of trustworthy that betrays you on the second visit. They remember, in some obscure and pre-verbal way, the order in which the strata were laid down, and the names of the strata, and the names of the things that built the lowest stratum, though the names are not names in any language the residents carry.

When a very old one finally stops sitting, and is no longer found in the chamber, the residents do not say that they have died. They say that the chamber has been *completed*. The films close over the place where the elder sat. The pale stone accepts the new surface. The very old one has, in some quiet and unhurried way, become a part of the city, and the city, in its own slow and unreliable way, has become a part of them.

## IV. The Life of the Place

The residents, the fauna, and the very old ones share the plateau in a kind of tripartite stillness. They do not compete. There is nothing here to compete for, beyond the small dry pellets and the faint residue, and these are distributed, in their own way, equitably. The residents move through the chambers and corridors. The fauna trace their lines. The very old ones sit. The walls correct themselves overnight. The edges of the plateau disagree among themselves about what lies below. The films, in the deeper chambers, almost write.

In the slow deepening, when the phosphorescence gathers in the unfinished walls, the three kinds of inhabitant are most nearly at peace with one another. The residents settle into their chosen chambers. The fauna emerge in their slow processions. The very old ones sit, and are illuminated, and the lines traced across their surfaces by the fauna's fine tips glow faintly in the dim light. The silence holds. The pewter sky neither brightens nor darkens. The plateau breathes, slightly.

It is not, by any measure, a life that anyone from below would recognize as such. There is no industry. There is no ambition. There is no family, in the way that word is used in warmer, wetter places. There is no love, perhaps, except in the small private languages of two people who have agreed to share a chamber; or there is love, but it has been so thoroughly distributed across the long hours of sitting and the long gestures of the hand that it has become indistinguishable from the architecture, and the architecture is indistinguishable from the love, and the love is indistinguishable from the pale stone, and the pale stone is, in the end, indistinguishable from whatever it was that laid the cyclopean blocks of the lowest stratum, in the time before anyone currently here was here to remember it.

The residents are not unhappy. This is worth saying again, because it is the one thing about them that is easiest to misread. The thin air, the cool, the unreliability, the long dry rationing — none of this, to them, is deprivation. It is the weather. It is the kind of place they are in, and they are the kind of people who are in this kind of place, and the two have been making each other for so long that the question of whether one could survive without the other has become, like most of the geometry here, not quite contractual.

They are, in their own way, finished. Not in the way the third stratum is finished, which is to say interrupted — but in the way a thing is finished when it has been allowed to become, slowly and without ceremony, what it was always going to become. They are the inhabitants of an unfinished city, and the city is the inhabitant of an unfinished sky, and the sky is the inhabitant of an unfinished thought, and the thought, if it is a thought, is held in the cupped hands of the air, and the air is cool, and the cool holds, and the pewter neither brightens nor darkens, and the silence is a small, careful thing, and the pale fauna trace their lines, and the lines are not in any language anyone carries, and this, in the end, is enough.
