They live here the way the moss lives here — slowly, along the grain of the stone, taking nothing the place does not already offer. From outside, from below, looking up at the shelf, you might not see them at all. You might see only the soot-stain above the small chimney, the leather shutter, the figure carved into the lintel of one door — a small figure, hard to make out, perhaps a goat, perhaps a hand, perhaps just the shape that a chisel wants to make when the chisel has been in a hand a long time. You might see the goats on the ledges above and mistake them for the only inhabitants. You might walk along the shelf between the houses and find it empty and conclude that it is abandoned. You would be wrong, or you would be right, and it would not matter, because the people here have learned to live in a way that does not register as presence on a first pass.

There are not many of them. The shelf would not hold many. There are perhaps seven, perhaps nine — the count shifts, not because anyone dies or is born with any frequency, but because people leave for a while, or come back, or sleep through the days you might count them, or are simply somewhere on the upper rock where the eye does not easily reach. The number is not the point. The point is that the settlement is full the way a stone is full, not the way a cup is full. Adding one more person would crack something. Taking one away would leave a shape that did not quite fit. They have found their configuration, the way the buildings have found theirs, and they hold it.

Of the ones who are reliably there — and even "reliably" is the wrong word, because reliability implies a kind of precision the place does not support — of the ones who are most often to be found in the parts of the settlement that can be seen from the channel between the houses, there is first the one who keeps the forge.

She is older than she looks, or younger than she looks; the distinction has stopped mattering to her, and may have stopped mattering to the place. Her hands are darker than the rest of her, as if the forge has claimed them. The knuckles are thick. The fingers are not quite straight. When she holds them still in her lap they look like roots, or like the small dark twisted limbs of the tree by the spring. She has burned herself many times. The scars are the color of the old wood in the doorframes. She does not speak of them. They are not interesting to her. She is interested in the hinge she is making, or the latch, or the small repair to a tool — she is always making or mending something that the settlement needs. The forge is not for show. The forge is because hinges wear, and latches break, and the goats lose their bells in the high places and need new ones hammered from scrap. She is the reason the doors have hinges. She is the reason a few of the houses have iron hooks driven into the lintels from which things hang — a lantern, a ham, a coil of rope. She knows the sound of her hammer in the rock of the shelf and has tuned it over the years so it does not carry far. She strikes lightly. She strikes often. The settlement has the faint sound of small distant metal in it most evenings, if you know how to listen, and she is that sound.

She sleeps in the house at the end of the row, the one with the soot-stain. The room inside is small and warm in a way the rest of the place is not, and smells of iron and of the dried herbs that hang from the rafters. She has a cat, or what might be a cat — it is the color of the shadows in the channel, and you cannot always tell if it is in the room or out of it. The cat does not purr. The cat does not meow. It sits on the windowsill, if there is a windowsill, and watches the upper crags where the ravens go. Sometimes it is gone for days. It comes back. She does not remark on its absence or its return. It is part of how the place accounts for itself.

Then there is the one who tends the goats, or who goes with them, or who is simply with them so often that the distinction has stopped mattering. He — or she, or neither, or both, the place does not insist — is the leanest person on the shelf. The face is wind-tanned to the color of the lichen on the north sides of the stones. The hair, if hair is the word, is cut short and stands up in a way that suggests it has been cut with a knife rather than a mirror. The hands are long-fingered and not particularly strong-looking, but they know the ledges. They know where a boot will hold and where a boot will slide. The goats follow this person not because they are led but because the ledges this person walks are the ledges the goats also walk, and they have agreed, in some way that does not require deciding, that they are going the same way at the same time. The bells on the goats are quiet. The bells have been tuned, by the smith, to a low note that does not carry off the shelf. When the herd moves above the settlement you hear them only as a slow faint rhythm under the sound of the wind, and you would miss them if you were not listening for them, and even then you might mistake them for the channel water.

This person sleeps sometimes in one of the houses, sometimes higher, in a place that is more like a hollow than a room. They come down in the morning to drink from the channel. They go up in the evening and are not seen again until the next morning, or the morning after. They do not explain where they have been. No one asks.

There are others. There is the old woman who sits by the spring most days. She is the oldest of them, or at least she has been here the longest, or at least she looks like she has been here the longest, and these three things have come to feel the same. Her hair is white and her face is the color of the wet stone at the bases of the walls. She wears a wool thing — gray, undyed — that she may have woven herself or that may have been woven for her by someone who is no longer here, and the distinction between these two possibilities is also not interesting to anyone. She has a cup. The cup is wooden. She drinks from the spring, or she dips the cup into the basin and lets the water cool her hand before she drinks. She does not speak often. When she speaks it is in a low voice, and the words are few, and they are usually about the weather or the spring or a goat that has not come down. She knows the spring the way the smith knows the forge. She knows when the flow is a little less or a little more. She has an opinion about it. The opinion is not strong. The opinion is part of the way the spring is accounted for, the way a name is part of a person.

There is the one who mends the roof channels. There has to be one. The channels crack. They come loose at the joints where wood meets rock. When that happens, a line of seepage begins to draw itself down a wall, and if no one intercepts it, the line will become a permanent feature of the stone, the color of the wall will change, and a small slow ruin will have begun. The one who mends the channels goes along the rooflines once in a while — not on a schedule, because schedules are not what the place uses, but on a rhythm that has to do with the weather and the moss and the way the wood sounds when you tap it with a knuckle. They carry a small bag of tools. They carry a length of shaped stone or wood for stopping a crack. They work quickly, without ceremony, and when they are done the line of seepage stops and the wall resumes its long slow equilibrium. This person is hard to describe because they are not often seen doing the thing that defines them. They are more often seen carrying water, or sitting in a doorway, or walking along the shelf with a look on their face that suggests they are listening to the buildings the way the old woman listens to the spring. They have the same weathered look as everyone else. They have the same unhurried way of moving. If you saw them on a high road somewhere else you would not recognize them as the same person. They belong here. They have been shaped by here.

And there are the rest of them. The ones who are harder to pick out. The ones who are in the houses when you pass. The ones who are behind the houses, or above them, or who step around a corner and are not there when you look again. They are few enough that you could name them if you stayed long enough, and you would not stay long enough. They are enough that the place is not abandoned. They are enough that when you sit in the channel between the houses, very still, very quiet, in the early morning when the moisture beads on the flat stones and the sun has not yet reached the shelf, you begin to hear them. A door, somewhere, opens a little and closes. A footstep, on stone, very soft. A voice, low, two voices, in a conversation that you cannot make out. A tool on stone. The sound of a wooden spoon in a wooden bowl. The sound of someone breathing while they do something patient. These sounds are rare. They are not constant. The constant sounds are the spring and the wind. The human sounds come and go like the goat bells, like the ravens, like the cat — present, then absent, then present again, on a rhythm that is not yours and that you will not learn in the short time you have here.

They do not greet you quickly. If you come to the shelf from below, climbing the way that takes the longest and exposes you to the air the most, they will know you are coming before you arrive. There is no way to approach without being seen from above. By the time you step onto the shelf itself, one of them — usually the lean goatherd, or the one who mends the channels — will be standing a little way off, looking at you without expression, holding a tool or a rope or a cup, waiting. They will not speak first. They will wait for you to speak, or for you to do something that indicates what you are, and then they will nod, or turn and walk away, or — if you are quiet enough, and you have not disturbed the moss, and you have not leaned on the buildings, and you have not tried to see into the windows — they will simply continue what they were doing and let you be. The settlement does not have a rule about visitors. It has a way of being approached, and you have either approached it that way or you have not.

They are not unfriendly. They are not especially friendly. They have the politeness of people who have been in the same small place with each other for a long time, and who have learned that the small talk that passes between strangers is not what passes between them. They will offer you water if you are thirsty. They will offer you a place to sit if you need to sit. They will not offer you food unless the food is being shared anyway, in which case they will set a bowl near you without comment. They will not ask where you have come from. They will not ask where you are going. They will wait for you to leave, and when you leave they will not wave, and they will not remark on your leaving afterward, and the moss under your footfall will resume its growth as if you had not stepped there.

They live a long time. Or they do not. It is hard to tell. The old woman by the spring may have been old when the smith was young. The smith may have been the young apprentice of someone who is now only a tool hanging on a peg in the forge room. The goatherd may have been a child once, climbing on the ledges with the goats, and may now be the lean weathered person they are. The ones who are hardest to see may be the children, if there are children, and the children may grow up here and become the ones who are hardest to see, and the cycle may continue without anyone noticing that it has continued. They do not mark the years. They have no calendar that you would recognize. The seasons come and go and the moss grows and the water flows and the people move among the buildings the way the ravens move among the upper crags, and time passes through them the way wind passes through the channel grating — present, audible, but not stopping.

They are not unhappy. They are not especially happy. They have the look of people who have stopped asking themselves whether they are either, and have begun, instead, to live in the place the way the place lives in itself. They have work. They have water. They have each other, in small ways and at long intervals. They have the goats, and the ravens, and the cat, and the tree by the spring, and the view of the larger mountains, which they do not go to see but which they see from where they are. They have the sound of the spring, which is the sound of the place, and which they have been hearing all their lives and which they will go on hearing until they stop hearing, and which will go on being heard after that.

You could spend a long time here without learning their names. They have names. They use them, among themselves, in the low voices that you cannot quite make out. But the names are not what would help you know them. What would help you know them is the sound of the smith's hammer in the evening, and the lean figure on the ledge above you with the goats, and the white hair of the woman by the basin, and the small bag of tools carried along the rooflines by the one who mends the channels. What would help you know them is the way the doors of the houses are slightly ajar, even when no one is inside, so that the air can move through and the dampness can equalize and the moss can keep its quiet color. What would help you know them is the way they look at you, if you are still enough, with an expression that is not curiosity and is not suspicion and is not welcome and is not refusal, but is the look of people who are checking, very quickly and without comment, whether you have understood the place.

If you have, they will let you stay a night, perhaps two. If you have not, they will watch you go and they will not follow you down the mountain and they will not speak of you to each other and the moss will close over the place where your foot struck the shelf and the place will resume its long configuration and you will be a thing that happened, once, briefly, on the stone.
