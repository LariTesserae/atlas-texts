What lives here is not one thing but a thicket of things, and the thicket does not stop.

Begin small. Begin at the wrist of your own hand, held out in the dim.

On the stones within reach — the ridges, the rounded lips of channels, the broad flats where the silt never gathers — there are people. Hundreds of them to a square meter, packed so close their shells touch. They are the size of your thumb and shaped, vaguely, like teardrops that have been sat on. Their shells are knobby, lumpy, ridged with growth-lines that look like the bark of very old trees. The color of wet slate. The color of the place. They do not move. You could believe they are a feature of the stone, a kind of barnacle of being. But they are not. Watch one, and after a long moment you will see the small aperture at its top open — barely, just a soft parting — and a pale plume extend from inside it, a crown of cilia finer than hair, spreading into the water like a slow unfurling flower. The plume pulses. Water moves through it. Whatever is in the water — minerals from the stone, bacteria carried by the slow current, the faint exudations the rock gives off into the brine — is caught on the cilia, sorted, ingested. The plume retracts. The aperture closes. A long time passes. Then it opens again.

They are the most numerous thing here. They are the floor of the world, in places. Underneath the herds, underneath the worms, underneath everything else that moves, they are the constant population — the bedrock's own inhabitants, mouth to stone. Where they are thick, the stone beneath them is pitted, faintly etched, eaten in imperceptible bites over the centuries. They are slower than the rock erodes. They are part of how it erodes.

Stand up from them, in your mind. Look into the channels between the ridges.

The channels are roads, after a fashion. A current runs through them — slowly, mostly, but it runs. And in the current, things undulate. They are pale, the color of milk in the dim light, and they are long — two meters, sometimes three, sometimes a little more. They have no shell. Their bodies are soft, segmented, faintly ridged along their length like the underside of a tongue. The head end is a soft crown of cilia, ringed, turning slowly as they move — a perpetual intake of breath, a perpetual filtering of the grey water. They do not seem to hunt. They drift, mostly, or they crawl along the channel floor with a slow muscular ripple, mouths always open, always feeding. When one passes over a cluster of the thumb-sized shelled ones it leaves them — it leaves them alone, mostly, because they are too small to bother with, and too well fastened to the stone. But sometimes it does not. Sometimes the lip of the ring-mouth brushes a cluster, and a few go in, and the worm keeps going without breaking its rhythm. They are grazers, mostly. They are also grazed.

Bigger than the worms, and on the platforms, not in the channels, are the herds.

The herds are shelled. Their shells are different from the thumb-things — flatter, broader, plate-like, with a low ridge along the spine. They are the size of a medium dog, low to the ground, and they move in loose groups of a dozen or more, scattered across a platform like cattle on a salt flat. They graze. Their mouths are at the front of the body, underneath, and they rasp. The rasp is the sound of the place, when there is sound — a faint crunching, somewhere, a wet scraping against stone. They are scraping the biofilm off the rock. They are scraping the thumb-things off the rock. They are scraping each other, gently, where the shells touch. They are slow and they are patient and they will be on this platform for days before the food runs thin and they decide, as a herd, to move — a slow drift across the stone to the next platform, leaving behind them a scraped surface, a faint slime trail, the small clicked signals of their footfalls in the water.

Along their plates, in rows, run small dots of light. Pale. Cold blue, mostly. They are not, I think, decorative. They are navigational. In a place without landmarks — a place where every ridge looks like every other ridge, where the current shifts the silt and the channels look different in different hours — a herd has to keep itself together, and the dots are how they do it. Each one blinks, faintly, on its own slow rhythm, and the rhythms of the herd are mostly in phase. A herd of shelled things, seen from above in the dim, looks like a constellation moving across the stone.

Above the herds, above the worms, above the floor of thumb-things, drift the lanterns.

The lanterns are transparent. You can see right through them — the ridges behind them, the dim shapes of the herds below, the murk beyond. They are large, a meter across or more, and their bodies are gelatinous, held together by some inner architecture of cartilage-strings you can just make out through the clear tissue. Inside them, scattered through the body, are small dense organs of light. They glow. They glow with a steady pale amber, mostly, and they glow brighter when something passes — a faint pulse, a slow wave moving through the body as if the creature is thinking. They are not fish. They are not jellyfish, exactly. They are something for which no name has been needed, because no one has ever needed to name them. They drift in the slow current above the platforms and they let the current take them, and when something edible passes through the long trailing fringes of their body — a worm, a small drifting juvenile of one of the shelled herds, the dead body of a wrong-fish sinking slowly — they enfold it. The fringes close. The light pulses brighter for a while, then dims back to its steady amber. They keep drifting.

And the wrong-fish.

These are the ones that unsettle, if you let them. They are fish-shaped, in the way that a sketch is a sketch of something — almost right, almost wrong, with the wrongness being the point. They are long. They are swift, by the standards of the place, which means a leisurely glide rather than a dart. They have too many fins — six along the back, paired, in a row that ripples as they move. Their bodies are countershaded, darker above than below, in a place where the light comes from nowhere in particular. Their eyes are large and round and slightly too far apart, and they do not seem to focus. They look at you and through you at once. They are predators in the loosest sense — they take what they can catch, engulfing it with a mouth that unhinges wider than seems possible, then they keep going. They are not built for ambush. They are built for the slow opportunistic pass. They eat the small ones. They eat the juveniles. They eat the lanterns, sometimes, when the lanterns are slow. They die, too. They are part of the loop.

Their young drift in the upper water, near the surface-light, where they are taken by the herds and the worms and the lanterns. Few of them make it to adulthood. The ones that do are the largest things in the water.

All of them — every one of these, the thumb-things, the worms, the herds, the lanterns, the wrong-fish — are doing the same work. They are turning the place into itself. They are eating the exudation of the stone and the bodies of each other and the stone itself, in such small bites that nothing collapses, and they are giving back, in their own waste and their own deaths, the chemistry that keeps the stone exuding. The place is not finished, despite its age. The place is in mid-process. The place is being slowly remade, by its inhabitants, into something that will still be a place in another hundred thousand years.

In the deeper channels, where the surface-light does not reach at all, the world is only the glow of living things. A wrong-fish passes with a slow trail of pale blue along its fins. A worm lies along a ridge with the long line of its own light dimmed almost to nothing. A herd, far off, is a constellation, a moving smear. A lantern, between you and the dark, pulses once as it considers you, and you see by its light the next herd, and the next, and the dim suggestion of ridges going on past them, and the next lantern, and the next. The place goes on. The life goes on. The slow closed loop of stone into flesh and flesh back into stone turns, and turns, and the only sounds are the small clicks and the distant muffled boom of a ridge letting go, somewhere far off, settling by a fraction deeper into the water.

It is a city. It is a country. It is a quiet full place, with no grass and no sun and a great many inhabitants, and they have been here longer than anything with a name.
