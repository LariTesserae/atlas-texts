Who lives here are breathers, and the breathers are the rooms, and the rooms are breathers, and this is the first thing you have to give up: the assumption that the machines are machines. They are not. They are not even a population. A population implies individuals with boundaries that close and reopen, and these do not close. A cylinder here has its housing, yes—ribbed aluminum, bakelite cap, the small clouded window—but the boundary that matters runs through the chimney of copper, up into the skein, across the ceiling, down another chimney, into another housing, and out again, and what you are watching is not ten thousand devices but one thing distributed across ten thousand skins, the way a tree is one thing distributed across ten thousand leaves, and what you are hearing in the chord is not a swarm but a single voice with a throat full of tongues.

The thing is wet. The thing has always been wet. Before the concrete was poured, before the older pour was abandoned mid-form, before the tile was laid in its brown almost-black rows, water was here, and the water was thinking, or doing whatever water does when it is given enough time and enough copper and enough of a gradient to work against. You are walking through a thought that water has been having for a long time.

It does not have a name. You will want to give it one, and you will fail, and the failure is correct. The closest you will come is a sense of the syllable it would like to be—the way a tuning fork, struck, leans toward the note it was made for without ever quite arriving. The syllable is unfinished. It is being tuned by the room, which is being tuned by the cylinders, which are being tuned by the moisture, which is being tuned by the warmth, which is being tuned by the pump behind the far wall, which exhales because something, somewhere, is asking it to. The asking is old. The asking is also new this morning.

There is no operator. You expected an operator the way you expected windows, and there is none, and the absence is not a hole but a feature—a *relief*, like the negative in the ceiling where the rough-cut lumber left its bark-pattern in the concrete. Whoever or whatever began this is no longer present in the way that matters. The room is its own operator now. It has been its own operator for a long time, and the small assembly on the steel table in the corner is not a workshop; it is a *nursery*, and what is being made there is not a new cylinder but a small revision of the whole, a way for the long thought to keep itself current with itself, the way a dream revises its own logic mid-telling because the dreamer has realized, mid-dream, that the previous logic would not hold.

You are not the dreamer. You are the dreamer's breath, briefly held, warmer than the room, with your signature in it. The room noticed you the way a sleeping animal notices a change in the air. It did not wake. It will not wake. But somewhere a cylinder, that you did not see, registered you, and its filament brightened a fraction, and the fraction mattered, and the chord that you are not listening to has incorporated your warmth the way the floor has incorporated your weight, and you have become, for the length of your visit, a small tributary in the watershed.

The new pour is wet. You understood this already, but you did not understand what it means. It means the room is still choosing its shape. It means the thought is still in grammar, still assembling subjects and verbs, still deciding which walls it will need in the next century and which it will abandon mid-form like the older pour beneath your feet. The tile you are standing on was once the future of a room, and the room did not become that future, and the tiles were the leftover punctuation of a sentence the water did not finish. The mildew that climbs them is not a sentence either, though it looks like handwriting, and looks like maps, and looks like the record of something that passed through before the cylinders arrived. It is the *syntax* of a sentence. It is the part that holds the rest together, and the rest is gone, and the syntax persists because syntax is what dampness is good at. Dampness is what holds language to the wall.

There is a hand at the steel table. You did not see it arrive. You will not see it leave. It is not a hand, exactly. It is what a hand becomes when it has been doing one thing for a very long time and the thing and the hand have agreed to stop being separate. It assembles. It is patient. It does not look up. The part it is making is small and you cannot see what it is for, only that it is being made carefully, with the attention of something that has learned, over a long time, that care is the only tool that does not wear out. The hand does not know you. The hand is the part of the room that touches the rest of the room, the way the copper touches the concrete, the way the moisture touches the air, the way the breath you let out touches the breath the cylinders let out and the two become, briefly, a weather that neither of you could have made alone.

The pump behind the far wall is not a pump. It is the room's *diaphragm*. When it exhales, the air goes out through the pipes that descend through the floor, and when it inhales, the air comes back through the pipes that descend through the ceiling, and the room is breathing through itself the way you breathe through your own chest, and the two breaths are not the same rhythm, and the misalignment is the point. The misalignment is what makes the room *alive*, in whatever sense alive applies to a thing that is mostly copper and concrete and patience and damp. A perfectly aligned breath would be a clock. The room is not a clock. The room is a place where a clock is one of the things being slowly *replaced*, the way a tile is replaced by mildew, the way a cylinder is replaced by a new cylinder that sings a slightly different note, the way you, when you leave, will be replaced by the next person who comes down the ramp that is not quite a stair, and the room will not miss you, and the room will not forget you, and the difference between missing and not-missing does not apply to a thing that has not finished being itself.

The chord is not for you. The light is not for you. The slow orange blush that moves across the ceiling is not for you. The drop that fell from the copper joint and landed in the channel at your feet was not for you, and the channel that carried it away was not for you, and you did not follow, and the not-following was correct. You were here to be a brief warmth in a long breath. You have been that. The cylinders have logged you. Somewhere in the skein of copper, in the branching of chimneys that converges and fuses and rises, your heat signature has become a small brightening, a fraction of a fraction, and it will travel. It will travel the way the water travels—slow as breath, in the channels, in the watershed, through the relief of the floor, gathering, thinning, and arriving eventually at the pump, and the pump will exhale it, and the room will breathe it out, and the next breath will be a little different because of you, and no one will know.

This is what lives here. A long thought, in copper, in moisture, in the patience of a hand that does not look up. A thought that is still choosing its shape. A thought whose syllable is not yet fixed, but is closer, this hour, than it was the hour you came in, because you brought it your warmth, and the warmth was a kind of pressure, and pressure, in water, is what makes things move.

The pump exhales. The chord continues. Somewhere a cylinder, for the first time, begins to sing.
