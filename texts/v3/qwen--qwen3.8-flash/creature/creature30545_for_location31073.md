Nothing walks. Nothing nests. Nothing runs from anything else, or toward anything else, or circles anything else overhead. There are no birds on this plateau, no rodents in its metallic fur, no insects to crackle in its dry grasses, no beasts to break salt crusts with a footfall. The place has no fauna in the ordinary sense, no creatures with warm interiors or blinking eyes or mouths that thirst.

And yet it lives.

It lives as a vast, slow botanical-mineral society, a world in which the forms of life are not separate from the forms of stone. What lives here is moss, salt, glass-grass, obsidian shrub, reed-tree, and resonance. What lives here is the plateau itself, half-formed, listening, repeating itself, remembering what it has not yet fully decided to become. And at the edges—sparse as footprints that sink into mineral dust—there are human traces, not civilizations but interruptions, the quiet marks of people who learned to live inside the roar without becoming animals in the way the land would understand them.

The first and most pervasive inhabitant is the moss.

It is not soft moss in the familiar sense, not the damp green fur of shaded forest stones. It is a dark, metallic carpet, thin and furred, growing across fresh basalt, over salt seams, into fractures, along the edges of crystal fields. It clings to the plateau the way a memory clings to a surface: not as an object, but as a pattern of adherence. Its body is built from fine filaments, half plant, half mineral, half thread, half static. It does not draw water from soil because there is no water to draw. Instead it drinks through contact. It feeds on electrochemical tension in the stone, on the hum that moves through salt, on the faint vapor rising from cracked crystals, on the charge released when dry wind passes across a field of glassy stems.

The moss moves with glacial patience. It advances by millimeters over decades. It spreads from crystal to crystal in curves and spirals that look from above like frozen music. It grows in response to pressure and pitch. A ridge that hums at one tone may carry moss in dark bands along one side, while another ridge, sounding at a lower frequency, may be furred only where the vibration pools in shallow basins. If the roar shifts—over years, over generations—the moss follows. It does not flee. It adjusts. It becomes a slow archive of the land’s acoustic climate.

It also remembers touch.

A person who crosses a mossy outcrop may find that, hours later, the fine filaments have tilted in the direction of the passing feet. Days later, the trail has not vanished but has been incorporated: a slightly darker seam, a faint bending of the surface, a crystalline line marking where weight and warmth and dry motion pressed. The moss does not heal in the ordinary sense; it repeats. It records. It is a living surface, one of the plateau’s true inhabitants, not the least of them.

Reproduction among the mosses is not pollination or seed or fruit. It is spalling and crystallization. When the roar deepens, or when a salt fan expands, or when a shard of glass is dropped onto a crust, the moss may respond by releasing tiny metallic spores—spicules, in fact—into the air. They drift through the dry haze with almost no visible movement. When they settle in a fresh crack, they do not germinate like soft plants. They begin a slow grafting. They draw mineral vapor. They align themselves with the static field of the stone. They take decades to appear, then centuries to become obvious.

If one were to examine the moss under a glass lens, one might find that its cells are not cells in the usual biological sense. They are more like nested lattices, small chambers of salt and silicate, charged and discharged by the land’s continuous pressure. The moss breathes not gas but gradient. It lives by the difference between one patch of stone and another, one hum and one silence. It is the plateau’s nervous surface. It is also its skin.

Near the moss, and often in cooperation with it, live the salt blooms.

These are pale, radial crusts spreading across depressions, dry basins, and the lower faces of new ridges. They look like frost, but there is no cold moisture here. They look like fungal colonies, but they are not fungus. They are growing mineral membranes. They form in starbursts, concentric rings, branching nets, and slow spirals. They are not remnants of old water but active participants in the land’s unfinished creation.

The salt blooms feed on vibration.

Where the roar collects in a hollow, salt crystals may appear as if drawn up from the ground by an invisible tide. Where a glass blade is struck, the salt nearby may begin to crystallize in a ring, recording the pitch of the blow. Where the land is unstable, salt may bloom in broken arcs that seem to predict the direction of new fractures. These blooms are not mere decorations. They are part of the ecosystem’s logic. They stabilize some surfaces and prepare others for colonization. They hold footprints for days, then sink them slowly, smoothing the ground as if the place were testing whether the mark would be worth keeping.

A broken tool left in a salt bloom may not rust. It may become transformed. Silver-green filaments grow through its iron veins. Its edges soften into new shapes. Salt crystals attach to it, then moss, then grass. In time the tool is no longer a tool but a seed-form, a mineral heirloom, a fossil of the human trace still in the act of becoming. The salt blooms are, in this way, archivists of lost intention. They remember the hand that made the flake, the pressure that struck it, the dry motion that left it lying in the dust. They are not alive as an animal is alive, but they are alive as a record is alive: capable of growth, response, reproduction, and change.

The needle-grasses rise among the salt blooms and through the cracks in the basalt.

They are tall, thin, and glassy, their stems like shards of blue-green obsidian, their blades brittle enough to cut but flexible enough to bow under the faintest movement of the dry wind. They do not droop. They do not wilt. They stand with the tense precision of things that have learned to bear charge without burning out.

Their roots are not roots in the way a traveler from a wetter world would understand. They are filaments that seek static, not moisture. They thread downward through salt seams and into fractures, searching for the hum that lives in stone. A fracture that sounds at a particular pitch will attract a dense clump of needle-grass. A silent fault may remain bare for centuries until a crystal spurs, salt blooms, and the charge returns.
