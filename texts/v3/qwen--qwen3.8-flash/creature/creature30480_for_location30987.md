The inhabitants of the Underloom cannot be counted cleanly, because to count them in ordinary terms is already to misunderstand the place. This is not a city with a population inside it. It is a population inside a body, and the body is also the streets, the rooms, the machinery, the water, the mold, the breath. If one asks what lives here, the answer begins with several things at once and refuses to settle into any one.

Something lives here like a beetle. Something lives here like a wall. Something lives here like a factory learning to dream. Something lives here like a swarm of moths that has decided, for now, to be a choir. Something lives here like a drop of water that has been called by name and given a civic duty.

The Underloom is inhabited not merely by animals or citizens, but by processes that have become intimate enough to be considered persons.

## The Contract Folk

The most visible inhabitants are the ones the Underloom seems to have grown into citizens: wet, slow, intricate beings made of multiple life forms in agreement.

They are not human, though from a distance their silhouette can be misleading. Some are tall and jointed, with limbs like braided root and stems that bend against gravity as if they remember wind. Some are low to the ground, broad-backed, moving on chitinous legs with a soft hydraulic hiss. Some are almost plant: a cluster of leaves and pale tendrils that rises when approached and closes when ignored. Others are more animal: a core of breathing flesh wrapped in fungal lace, with beetle-shell segments protecting joints and tiny valves pulsing along the throat.

They are, in a sense, each a household.

A single citizen may be composed of a mycelial knot that acts as memory and coordination, a moss-layer that handles moisture and insulation, several beetle partners that provide limbs, shell, or digestion, one or more small organ-plants that pump condensation, perhaps a leaf-lens for faint bioluminescent signal, and a few bacteria that translate scent into something like grammar. Their body is not one thing born at one moment. It is a negotiation.

This is why they do not age in a single line. A citizen may lose one leg to erosion and grow a new limb from a public bud in a wall. A citizen may shed its beetle partners in old age and become more root than creature. A citizen may graft itself to another citizen for love, labor, survival, or ceremony, forming a composite body that walks with a shifted center of gravity.

Their names are not spoken so much as assembled.

A citizen might be known by a click near one wrist-joint, a scent of iron and crushed mint, a pulse of teal light at the sternum, and a particular way water beads on the left shoulder. To know them is to know the pattern of their dampness, the rhythm of their hum, the route their memory-fungus has taken into the walls.

They speak in pressures.

Their voices are low, not because they are shy, but because the Underloom carries vibration better than clear light. They communicate through hollow stems, through leaf-flaps, through pulses along root-wires, through scent packets released from pores. Some conversations are nearly silent to an outside ear: a tremor in the floor, a shift of spores, a blink from a neck-gland. Others become a slow chorus of dripping, creaking, and wet clicks, like a room full of instruments being tuned by weather.

They are patient. They have to be. In the Underloom, a single afternoon can contain a beetle’s birth, a fungus’s spread, a leaf’s unfurling, and the decision by a root to abandon one street for another.

## Citizens of the House That Is Not a House

A human city would say “dwelling.” The Underloom would call it “resident.”

Many of the buildings are alive in the way an animal is alive: they eat, respond, heal, contract, bloom, sulk, and sometimes refuse to cooperate. A bulbous home in a neighborhood of climbing cords may bud off a new room when moisture increases. A factory wall may close its pores during a dry season and reopen them only when someone coaxes it with mineral spray and a low humming machine. A municipal bridge may itch because it is growing too fast at its eastern end. A doorway may narrow during rain, as if the house has decided it has had enough visitors.

Some citizens live inside structures that are, technically, their elders, partners, or tenants.

A citizen may wake inside a leaf-chamber whose membrane is slowly digesting last night’s condensation into usable water, and the chamber may be considered family. A child may be born in a room, and the room may remember the child’s first breath by thickening near the nursery wall. A citizen may die and be reabsorbed into the architecture, their fungal memory joining the faint blue-green plaques that mark street corners.

This is not metaphor in the Underloom. The building has a body. The body has a metabolism. The metabolism has obligations.

There are houses grown for rest, houses grown for production, houses grown for breeding, and houses grown so thin they function more as thresholds than shelters. Some dwellings are soft and cup-like, lined with moss that hardens beneath the weight of sleeping bodies. Others are tall and ribbed, with interior balconies of curved bark and windows of translucent leaf-glass. Some are hollow trunks split into apartments. Some are vast single rooms that breathe so slowly that an outside observer would mistake them for geology.

A home may be pruned into better manners. It may be fertilized into compliance. It may be punished by having its drainage stopped or its light reduced. It may be praised by being allowed to flower.

The citizens do not own buildings so much as enter contracts with them.

One contract might be for shelter in exchange for mineral feeding. Another might be for privacy in exchange for being grown into a public corridor during a high-water festival. Another might be for memory: a citizen may offer a piece of themselves into the wall after death so that the house becomes an archive.

This is one reason the Underloom feels populated even when nearly empty. A room can be alive without being occupied. A corridor can be waiting. A bridge can be thinking.

## The Beetle Crews

If one looks at the floor, the walls, and the damp undersides of public platforms, one sees the laborers: beetles.

They are broad, polished, and purposeful. Some are domesticated as maintenance organisms, scraped and groomed until their carapaces shine like black leaf-glass. They crawl across public valves with slow authority, removing fungal films that have grown too opinionated. They pack moss into cracks. They carry tiny buckets of mineral dust in their mandibles. They press their bodies into seals and hold them there until a root-wire tightens.

Some are large enough to serve as beasts of burden or mobile work platforms. A citizen might ride on the back of a beetle the size of a child, using its shell as a moving floor. The beetle is not merely a mount; it is a coworker, sometimes a friend, sometimes a rival. Its shell may be engraved with scent-grooves. Its legs may be shod with tiny resin pads to prevent slipping on wet moss. It may wear a living cap of cultivated fungus that lets it signal mood without stopping to speak.

There are wild beetles too: swarms that descend on rotting fruit-machines in the industrial quarters, then vanish again as if they had never existed. Some citizens regard them as pests; others regard them as emergency responders, eating blockages before they become civic crises.

In certain districts, beetle crews are so numerous that the ground seems to ripple when one leans close. The beetles move beneath the surface of moss, opening hidden chambers, clearing condensation channels, and depositing tiny eggs in abandoned walls. Their life cycle is short, their death useful, their numbers constant. They are not slaves, because even the smallest creatures in the Underloom have leverage. A beetledistrict may sulk if underfed. A valve-cleaning swarm may refuse a command that would cause a wall to crack.

The beetles live because the city needs constant repair.

The city needs constant repair because the beetles are learning that there is food in need.

## Moth-Civilizations and the Ceiling of Wings

Above the streets, in the upper vaults and wide leaf-caverns, entire lives exist as surfaces.

At first a ceiling may seem textured with dust, or with pale gray scales pressed into a mosaic. Then it breathes.

Moths hang in vast sheets, folded tightly against the dark until the air shifts. When disturbed, they open in silent waves, revealing wings that reflect the Underloom’s faint blue-green glow like wet stone. Some are as large as a citizen’s forearm. Others are small enough to live inside a single condensation cup. Their wings carry dust that can transmit scent, erase scent, or mark trails. In certain districts, a moth’s shed powder is a kind of language.

Moths live in the Underloom because the Underloom provides air, dampness, light, and silence. They live in the ceiling because the ceiling is not merely an obstruction; it is a commons.

Some moths are archivists. They collect spores on their wing scales and carry them between districts, allowing public memory to migrate. A moth that has passed through three foundries may leave behind a faint chemical record of production, accidents, and repairs. Citizens trained in moth-reading can press their faces to a wing and taste the year.

Some are hunters.

Hunting moths drift through corridors and release dust that strips scent from prey. A creature moving blindly by smell becomes suddenly invisible to its pursuers and to the city itself. The moth does not eat flesh so much as information: it strips away the chemical signature that says *I am here*, and then the prey is left unmoored, unable to be remembered, unable to be recognized by the walls.

The Underloom tolerates this because it, too, values disappearance.

Moths mate in slow spirals, laying eggs in the damp seams of abandoned buildings. The larvae are small, translucent, and almost machine-like, chewing through fungal cords and leaving behind threads that can be spun into leaf-membranes. Some citizens cultivate them. Some fear them. Some are, by composition, partly moth: their backs bear scales, their shoulders unfold when threatened, and their dreams have a papery texture.

When a citizen dies, one common mourning is to cover the body in moth-dust and leave it in a dry district. If the body is accepted by the city, the scales will fade into the walls and begin speaking again as part of the public archive. If not, the dust will fall and the Underloom will remain indifferent.

## The Bats of the Upper Wires

The bats are couriers, gardeners, engineers, and thieves.

They thread through the high levels of the Underloom with impossible precision, navigating by echo, by smell, and by something closer to civic intuition. They carry seeds, spores, tools, messages, and sometimes stolen valves. Their wings are dark membranes stretched over slender fingers, and their faces are too expressive for creatures that have never seen the sun.

A bat may carry a written stem-slip through the transit corridors. A bat may drop a seed into a crack that needs planting, then return later to inspect whether the crack has obeyed. Some bats roost inside factory pipes, where their body heat and movement keep steam valves from freezing. Some live in the highest galleries, where they hang in rows like black fruit. Others are nomadic, moving through the basin according to rumors of moisture and rot.

Their society is noisy, but their noise is not sound so much as pressure.

A bat colony communicates by sharp clicks, wing-folds, and the rhythm of hanging bodies. Some citizens imitate their patterns. Some wear small bat-teeth or claw-bones as badges of office. In certain districts, bats are considered municipal staff with excellent attendance and no loyalty to anything above the job.

A citizen might ask a bat to deliver a request to a factory because the factory is too large, too old, or too sulky to visit. A bat might refuse. A bat might charge.

In the Underloom, even small flying mammals have labor rights written into scent.

## The Blind Deer of the Moss-Halls

The deer are solemn, elegant, and wrong-eyed.

They move through the deep moss districts with a grace that suggests not speed but negotiation with the floor. Their hooves do not sink so much as persuade. Their antlers are not bone but living root, branching and curling, sometimes wrapped in pale fungi, sometimes pruned by citizens into useful shapes. They graze on the soft growth of old rooms, eating fungal mats that have become too thick, too loud, too insistent.

A blind deer can be followed by the way the moss bends before it steps. It can be identified by the taste of the air around it, the mineral residue on its mouth, the particular hum its chest makes when it listens to the ground.

Some are wild. Some are bred. Some are sacred, not in the sense of being worshipped, but in the sense of being difficult to disturb without causing the city to notice.

They live in the moss-halls because those halls are quiet. They live there because moss is not passive here; it records. Deer that graze too deeply may accidentally eat part of an old street’s memory. Deer that walk too often along the same path may teach the moss a new route. In this way, they are both animals and mapmakers.

Their young are born in leaf-caves with soft, folded coats and eyes that never see. They learn to listen through the floor before they learn to stand. If a citizen is gentle, a deer may allow itself to be groomed or pruned. If a citizen is too dry, too hot, too loud, the deer may simply leave a faint smell of iron and green behind and vanish into a corridor that seems too small for it.

The deer eat the Underloom. The Underloom grows around the deer. Both consider this normal.

## Fish Suspended in the Air

One of the strangest inhabitants of the Underloom is the fish that does not swim in water.

It lives in hanging bladders: translucent sacs filled with condensation, suspended from vines, leaf-roofs, or root-hinges. The bladders glow faintly when touched by blue light. Inside them, tiny fish turn slowly, their scales like shards of mineral glass. They drink moisture directly from the air through their fins. They are often ornamental, often edible, often sacred, and sometimes all three.

A fish in a hanging bladder may live through many weather cycles without knowing it has ever touched the ground.

When a rain pocket shakes a district, the bladder may burst. The fish falls. If it is lucky, it is caught by a waiting root-net or absorbed by a drainage channel that carries it to a new water-pocket. If it is unlucky, it lies gasping on moss until a beetle crew or citizen rescues it, or until the city reclaims its body and returns it to the lattice.

Some fish are kept in public reservoirs of air, where they are read by spore-tasters. Their movements are thought to reflect the health of the district. A school turning in the same direction may indicate pressure change, a blocked valve, or civic unrest. A citizen may consult a bladder-fish before undertaking a repair or marriage.

Other fish are not merely animals. They are municipal instruments. Their tiny bodies regulate the pH of water pockets, consume dangerous spores, and signal blockages by flickering scales. In certain foundry districts, fish are bred to live inside pipes, where they keep mineral deposits from hardening into silence.

To the citizens, a fish may be a pet, a colleague, a compass, or a prophecy.

To the fish, it may be simply damp, turning, turning.
