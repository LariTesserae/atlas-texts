What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense. There are animals, yes—great numbers of them, thick as ore in a seam—but the basin’s true inhabitants begin lower, thinner, and stranger than animal. They begin as a wet decision. A surface that could have stayed bare instead decides to be furred. A pool that could have been only water remembers a mouth. A sound that could have vanished instead drops a black shard onto the ground and waits. A shadow that could have returned to its owner stays behind, flattened, breathing the damp.

Most of what lives there are **body-mistakers**: organisms, semi-organisms, or process-creatures that have mistaken the landscape for a body and are slowly growing inside it. They do not merely occupy stone; they insert themselves into its pores, bind its salts, conduct through its veins, feed on its heat, and repair its damage. The basin is not a habitat that contains life. It is a habitat being made into a body by life.

### The Felted Ones

The first and most abundant inhabitants are the **felted ones**.

They are the black mold-like mats, the salt-feathers, the glassy hyphal networks, the mineral films, the white-black threads running under obsidian skins, the pale efflorescences that grow after pressure and fog. They are not fungus, though they thread like mycelium. They are not lichen, though they crust like it. They are a nonbotanical living surface: a vast, slow metabolism of conductive minerals, crystalline filaments, cold-water chemistry, and faint electrical appetite.

The felted ones consume temperature gradients, oxidation, static charge, condensation, dead stone, and the waste fluids of larger creatures. They bind salt and iron and silica into crusts. They grow inside stones until the stone is no longer merely geology but a partially colonized organ. They spread over cracked basalt, climb glass shelves, and line shallow channels with thin living films that glow faintly when charged.

They are the reason there is no clean empty space in the basin. If a stone shifts, exposing a fresh patch of ground, the felted ones do not wait for soil or sunlight. They come within moments as spore-clouds, as glass-limbed colonists, as damp threads that seal and furr the wound. A newly opened surface may first appear bare, but under the dim light it is already beginning to think like stone.

The felted ones have no eyes, no faces, no fixed bodies. Their nearest equivalent to a nervous system is the network of white-black threads that conduct electricity and nutrients together. These threads carry impulses slowly, at the pace of dripping water. A patch of mineral film may “remember” the pressure of a herd for hours after the herd has moved on. A fault line may remain electrically sensitive for days after a storm threads through it. A cracked pool may heal along its old fracture lines because the felted ones have learned the place’s shape.

This is not consciousness, exactly. It is responsiveness at a geological tempo. The felted ones are the basin’s skin and slow first mind: the layer that decides what may become surface, what must be colonized, what will conduct, what will crystallize, and what will be kept in memory.

### Tilebacks

On top of the felted ground crawl the **tilebacks**.

They are low, broad, armored creatures with shells like broken basalt, black enamel, wet flint, and salt-glazed stone. Their bodies are adapted to shallow water and packed mineral flats: flat enough to be mistaken for living tiles, heavy enough to slide rather than walk, edged with gill-slits that open when the fog vibrates and close when the air thickens.

Tilebacks are grazers, but they do not graze on leaves or algae. They scrape fungal-mineral mats, consume salt-fur, eat the thin electrical films that gather on wet stone, and filter heavy brine through internal labyrinthine plates. Their undersides secrete a cold, oily slime that lets them glide over obsidian flats, leaving wet furrows that, in time, become water channels.

They have no obvious eyes. They sense the world through pressure, electrostatic skin, moisture gradients, and the low grinding vibrations that run through the basin floor. A herd of tilebacks can move across a salt flat as though it were one slow machine. When the cold thickens and shallow water contracts into frozen mineral dust, they overlap their shells into insulated plates, huddling together until the land sounds like a field of broken armor when stepped upon.

Their reproduction is as mineral as their bodies. Tilebacks shed seed-plates onto wet flats: thin, round discs of secreted shell and conductive slime. A seed-plate must be carried or pushed into a salt cup, a brine channel, or the shadowed hollow beneath another tileback’s shell. There, in the cool wet dark, a young tileback molts through its first forms: dust-cup, glass-larva, shard-skin, and finally tileback. Its first shell is often grown from fragments of an adult’s molt, fitting onto its new body like inherited armor.

Tilebacks are essential to the basin’s industry. Their grazing keeps fungal mats from choking channels. Their waste feeds the threadmind. Their movements plow and rework the wet ground. Their shed plates become part of the crust. Their deaths do not vanish; they fossilize into ridges, mounds, and hollow clusters that later sing in the wind.

### Wirelimbs and Fretweavers

Among the crystalline growths climb the **wirelimbs**, also called **fretweavers**.

They are insectile in the sense that they have too many limbs and joints that seem made of translucent glass, but they are not insects as familiar ecologies would understand them. Their bodies are too angular, too packed, too partly mineral. Some are no larger than a coin; others are long, spidery, and as big as a hand. They climb salt feathers, glass shelves, and the black lichen-like films that plate every slope.

Wirelimbs feed on mineral dust, spore-clouds, electrical film, and the microscopic salts that gather at the edges of condensation. They spin not silk as we know it, but hypha-like threads loaded with charge and fine particulate matter. Where they settle, crystalline fretwork grows in packed lattices that look like frozen wiring. These lattices are both structure and organism: part nest, part web, part body, part circuit.

They are the basin’s most aggressive colonizers of surfaces. A wirelimb colony may attach itself to a stone so thoroughly that the stone becomes a body to them: a shell to be entered, grown around, cleaned, wired, and eventually hollowed into a lattice. This is one reason the landscape feels packed with material presences. Even where nothing recognizable is alive, the ground may be furred with the remains of wirelimb construction—thin glass growths, salt frets, dusty hyphal veins, tiny tapped crystals left behind like abandoned beads.

Wirelimbs are prey to clickers, lurelights, and many unseen predators. But they are also mutualists. They clean tilebacks’ shells, remove overgrown mold-fur, spread fungal spores, and carry sound-shards. Some wirelimb colonies use small black glass pebbles as ball bearings, eggs, weights, or acoustic tuning pieces. A patch of crystal fretwork may begin as a feeding mat and end as a living circuit, capable of faintly amplifying geyser rhythms across a shallow pool.

### Linebeasts

Across the flats move the **linebeasts**.

At first they seem like long-bodied pack animals: segmented, low, mineral-backed herds migrating in lines that follow invisible gradients of moisture and charge. Up close, the illusion of individuality begins to fail. A linebeast may be one creature, a colony, a temporary consensus, or several organisms moving so closely that their boundaries have been negotiated away. Their bodies are built from smaller units bonded by mineral glue, conductive slime, and pulsing electrical filaments. Each segment may be a tileback, a colony of wirelimbs, a living plate of felted ones, or a specialized body formed only for the migration.

Linebeasts are half herd and half weather. Their bodies plow shallow channels. Their waste feeds piston basins. Their movement creates turbulence in the cold mist, pulling fog down onto wet ground where it condenses and drops in rhythmic intervals. When they pass, obsidian flats may appear suddenly darker, as if their shadow has been dragged along behind them and failed to return.

They follow charge gradients with an almost industrial precision. During periods of electrical storm, they migrate in branching lines, following the blue-white rills that run across the wet ground like circuits dreaming themselves into existence. When the basin’s temperature drops and the shallow waters freeze into skins of broken mineral dust, linebeasts compact into moving walls, their shells and plates overlapping until the herd becomes a single grinding armor. When the cold loosens again, they spread out, leaving behind trails of compressed salt, conductive slime, and scattered spore-clouds.

Some linebeasts carry warm pockets inside their bodies: iron-rich fluid nodules, friction-heated joints, or symbiotic thermal mats that bloom when fog passes over charged glass. These warm places make the air around the herd briefly shimmer with heat, then collapse back into chill. Thus the linebeasts participate in the basin’s loop: warmth lifts fog, cold presses it down, condensation fills basins, and herds follow the wet circuit.

If a linebeast is split, its fragments may survive as smaller independent animals. If fragments meet, they may reassemble. A linebeast is therefore not always a body. It is sometimes a body-plan, a weather-behavior, a long migrating sentence that the basin writes across itself.

### Square-Divers

In the deeper black pools live the **square-divers**.

They are thick-skinned aquatic forms, dense and slow, with bodies so heavy and mineralized that when they surface they displace water in square-wave patterns. The water lifts in abrupt, blocky ridges, then settles as if the animal had pressed a flat weight into its surface. Their movements are not graceful in any familiar way; they are deliberate, industrial, like slabs being lowered through cold fluid.

Their skin is layered: outer oil-ice, middle salt-basalt scutes, inner dark hyphal mats that absorb vibration and perhaps chemical memory. They live in pools that have been ground smooth over an almost inconceivable age, beneath glass shelves, through black marsh water that oozes from the underside of obsidian. Some are no bigger than a table; others are wide as small hills of living stone.

Square-divers feed by pressure. They crush, suction, and filter. They eat bottom crusts, trapped tilebacks, dense spore-lumps, mineral sludge, and sometimes the faint heat produced by oxidation in wet dark places. Their bodies contain pressure chambers that mimic piston geysers. When they exhale, the water around them rattles with liquid percussion. When they move through a heavy brine basin, their bellies produce low, hollow sounds that travel through the water long before they reach the air as metallic clunks.

They are ancient in their habits. A square-diver may spend decades in one black mirror pool, slowly turning it into a home, a nursery, and a waste channel. Young sink through the water column to warm fault lines near the bottom, where iron-rich brine and friction-heated stone allow their shells to grow over years. By the time they return to the surface, they may carry entire communities: felted mats, wirelimb frets, tiny clicker nests, parasitic ashwarmers, and dark patches of detached shadow that cling to their shells like barnacles made of absence.

When a square-diver dies, it does not decay as ordinary flesh does. Its body sinks, fills with mineral flow, and becomes a mound, a shelf, or a fossilized cluster. Later, wind and pressure may make it produce long, mournful notes. A sound that once belonged to a body becomes a sound belonging to the land.

### Clickers and Membrane-Hunters

Predation in the basin uses not only jaws and claws, but the unstable physics of water, sound, and light.

The most common acoustic predators are the **clickers**. They are armored, low-bodied, often tileback-like in shape, but with resonant throat-casps, hollow mineral plates, and sensitive films stretched beneath their shells. They hunt by sound. Their clicks are not just signals; they are actions. Certain sequences cause thin water films to stiffen into temporary membranes. Smaller animals crossing those films become trapped beneath surfaces that have suddenly hardened like skin or glass.

Clickers hunt along shallow brine channels, condensation pools, and the oily ice skins that crack and reform. A group may coordinate its clicks until a small basin’s surface becomes a lattice of stiffened films. Then they move in, tapping and scraping, releasing prey by breaking the membrane they themselves created. In deeper pools, their clicks may travel through dense water before the air hears them, making the sound seem to rise up through a creature’s chest before appearing in the mind.

Their hunting leaves traces. Loud impacts and repeated clicks can drop small black shards, glassy sound-stones that lie on the ground like pebbles. Clickers collect these shards, arrange them around their nests, and use them as resonators. A clicker’s “voice” may therefore include the trapped rhythms of previous hunts. Some clicker colonies near piston geysers have accumulated so much sound that their hunting grounds contain frozen tap-lines, long sequences of clicks lying in rows across the basin floor like abandoned choreography.

### Lurelights

The **lurelights** hunt by thickened light.

They are often translucent, flat, or insectile, with gill-like edges that flash faint colors when charged. Their bodies produce patterns not for beauty but for disorientation. In a landscape where reflective skins, wet obsidian, salt plates, and electrical films all distort appearance, a lurelight can become a predator simply by appearing where prey expects itself to be.

A lurelight may move through shallow water and leave afterglow in crystalline hyphae. The glow continues after the creature has gone, pooling in depressions and clinging to wet stone. Reflective animals mistake this residue for presence. They turn toward it. The lurelight, already elsewhere, strikes from behind a curtain of fog or a black mirror pool.

Some lurelights are aquatic, drifting under oil-ice skins, their bodies pulsing with blue-green or copper light. Others climb the glass cliffs, flickering against salt feathers like small false stars. A few are colonial: a cluster of wirelimb-like units synchronized to create the shimmering outline of an animal that never existed.

They exploit the basin’s strange optical habits. If light has thickness, it can be shaped. If shadows fail to return, they can be used as nets. If a patch of hyphae continues glowing after the source moves away, that glow can become a trap, a lure, a false path, or a scar in the visual memory of the land.

### Ashwarmers

The **ashwarmers** hunt by temperature.

They move toward the faint heat produced when wet mineral oxidizes in the dark, when friction warms fault lines, or when iron-rich brine pools rise through cracks in the basin floor. Their bodies are pale where the air touches them, dark underneath where they press against heat, and riddled with shallow pits that sense thermal difference as we might smell smoke.

Ashwarmers are often small, but some are broad and flattened, like living heat-plates. They attach to warm patches and draw off temperature with their undersides, feeding on thermogenic microbes, conductive salts, and the mineral films that grow where heat meets cold. They are scavengers, parasites, and predators all at once. They may attach to a square-diver’s warm cavity, ride inside the body of a linebeast, or gather around a venting geyser to feed on the oxidation bloom left in the water’s wake.

They are vital to the thermal loop. Warmth makes fog rise; ashwarmers move where fog rises. Cold makes fog descend; ashwarmers move again. Their bodies release faint metallic odors when heated, and their clustering can make a cold patch of ground suddenly seem too warm to touch. Where they die, the ground may remain thermally active longer, encouraging new fungal-mineral films to grow. Their carcasses do not rot; they calcify into white-black thermal scars.

### Echo-Sitters

Because the water remembers, some creatures feed on memory.

**Echo-sitters** are small, flat, wide-mouthed animals that live where the basin’s reality leaks. They gather near drinking pools, geyser edges, brine channels, and black mirrors. When another creature drinks, the water may remember the shape of its mouth for hours afterward. An echo-sitter laps at that hollow, at that indentation, as if memory were food.

They are not drinking the water exactly. They are consuming the residual pattern left in it. An echo-sitter that drinks from the mouth-impression of a tileback may gain a brief instinctive sense of tileback movement. One that laps the footprint of a linebeast may learn a fragment of charge-gradient orientation. One that drinks from a geyser’s remembered rhythm may twitch in synchronized clicks.

Echo-sitters are fragile and uncertain. They often appear as thin films with wide, almost humanlike mouth-slits, or as small dark discs that cluster around depressions. If the impression they feed from fades, they may vanish, shrink, split into spores, or become a dust of borrowed pattern. If enough of them gather, they may form a temporary cloud that hovers over a pool, replaying fragments of previous drinkers: not quite the animals themselves, but their habits, their thirst-shapes, their last movements.

They are among the basin’s most disturbing inhabitants because they blur the boundary between event and substance. A mouth-shape left in water is not a living animal, but it can feed living animals. Therefore, in the basin, the past is not only a memory. It is a nutritional niche.

### Dark-Pools and Detached Shadows

In a place where shadows can detach and remain attached to the ground, shadows become ecological.

Some detached shadows are simply residual effects: darkness pooling in a low place and failing to return to its proper position. Others are organisms, or the byproducts of organisms, living as flattened absence. The basin’s inhabitants call them, if naming were possible, **dark-pools**.

A dark-pool may appear as a stain beneath wet stone, a stretched outline with no source, or a flattened region where light refuses to climb back. It may have a rim of glittering salt or a faint oil-ice skin. Small animals crossing it may sink slightly, as though the shadow had surface tension. Larger animals may avoid it because their own shadows become confused. A lurelight may step through a dark-pool and emerge with an outline slightly delayed, as if it has borrowed a new silhouette.

Some creatures cultivate dark-pools. They arrange obsidian shards, salt plates, and brine channels so that light is trapped beneath them. The detached shadow remains, becomes a shelter, a trap, or a false body. Predators use them to hide movement. Square-divers may carry them on their shells as camouflage. Herds of linebeasts may step around them as though around old wounds in the surface.

Dark-pools feed where they can. They consume light that pools in depressions, afterglow left in hyphae, and the warmth of shadows that have failed to detach cleanly. They do not decay either; they fade when the basin’s thick light shifts, only to reappear where pressure has made darkness stick again. They are not ghosts, exactly, but they are evidence that the landscape has difficulty maintaining the boundary between what is present and what remains.

### Shard-Tappers

Where impacts are loud, sound can leave debris.

Small black shards of glass-like material drop from the air or ground after strong percussions. Some contain trapped rhythms: clatters, ticks, liquid slaps, geyser exhalations. These are **sound-shards**. Not all of them are alive. But they are treated as alive by certain inhabitants, and by the basin’s larger patterns, which is nearly the same thing.

The **shard-tappers** are creatures that collect, arrange, and play sound-shards. They may be wirelimb-like, clicker-like, or colonial assemblages of small armored units that move like scattered coins when disturbed. Their purpose is to organize noise into useful repetition. A line of sound-shards near a venting geyser may not be an accident; it may be a nest, a rhythm, an inherited pattern, or a tool for summoning.

Some shard-tappers build acoustic shells around themselves from black glass pebbles. When wind moves through the basin, or when a piston geyser releases its slow exhalation, the shell speaks. The sound may lure prey, repel predators, synchronize herds, or awaken dormant memories in the water. A shard-tapper’s body may be partly composed of accumulated impacts: each shard a captured event, fitted into the whole like a mineral vertebra.

The strange frozen rhythm of clicks near a geyser vent is perhaps the fossil of one such being, or perhaps its living nervous system exposed in sequence. When the basin’s storms thread through the wet mineral veins, the shard-lines may ring faintly in order, replaying old taps. The land itself becomes an instrument made of former events.

### The Hollow Mounds

Many low mounds are not mounds. They are the fossilized remains of old organisms replaced by silica and salt, leaving hollows where wind produces long, mournful notes. These are not merely dead places. They are inhabited by wind, by spore-clouds, by wirelimbs, by echo-sitters, by detached shadows, and by the felted ones threading through their cracks.

A hollow mound may sing because it was once a body. A sound that comes from it may be the last mechanical echo of an extinct square-diver, a collapsed linebeast consensus, or a colony of tilebacks whose shells fused into a hill. When wind passes, the mound replays not that creature’s voice, but its geometry. The dead remain present as pressure and aperture.

Some mounds are active nests. Others are accidental monuments. Others are tools shaped by generations of fauna. A creature may press against a mound’s hollow to change the pitch of a geyser. A clicker may tune its throat to the mound’s resonant note. A dark-pool may gather under a mound’s shadowed lip and become difficult to distinguish from the ground’s memory.

Death here does not remove presence. It redistributes it. A body becomes crust, channel, shell, circuit, mound, note, or memory water.

### The Wet Parliament

If one asks who lives in the basin, the answer must eventually move beyond creatures. The felted ones, tilebacks, wirelimbs, linebeasts, square-divers, clickers, lurelights, ashwarmers, echo-sitters, dark-pools, shard-tappers, and hollow mounds are not a community in the ordinary sense. They are parts of a single industrial ecology: a wet parliament of body-mistakers.

The wet parliament has no center. It has no walls. It does not build machines because it is built from geological, meteorological, and biological machines already. Its organs are piston geysers, brine channels, electrical storms, condensation pools, salt-fur, glass frets, migration routes, and shadowed basins. Its thoughts are cycles: charge, flow, grinding, cooling, condensing, feeding, crystallizing, forgetting, and remembering again.

It does not think in language. It thinks in repetition with variation. A migration line is a sentence. A piston interval is a pulse. A sound-shard sequence is a memory. A detached shadow is a question the basin asks about the boundary between object and absence. A mouth-impression left in water is an instruction for future feeders. A frozen rhythm line is a fossilized decision.

The wet parliament may be sentient only in the way that a planet can be said to be aware of its own weather. It notices when a stone shifts. It corrects when a channel silts. It exploits leaks in reality when new niches open. It does not panic when shadows detach; it uses them. It does not mourn when an animal dies; it incorporates the shape. It may even use visitors—if one could call them that—by drinking their habits from the water, storing their silhouettes in salt films, and replaying their movements through echo-sitters long after their bodies have been colonized by felted ones.

There is no king, no priest, no maker, no builder. If there is a “who,” it is the basin’s long-running agreement among itself: the agreement that water will carry traces, sound will fall into shards, shadow will linger, stone will be mistaken for body, and life will be understood as maintenance.

### What It Feels Like to Live There

To live in the basin is to be constantly pressed upon by material presence. A creature’s body is not isolated; it is a site of occupation. Tilebacks carry mold-fur on their shells. Wirelimbs grow in their gill edges. Square-divers host entire communities. Linebeasts are sometimes individual herds and sometimes moving colonies. Clickers are tuned by the remnants of their own past noises. Lurelights are surrounded by their own delayed glow. Echo-sitters are shaped by the mouths of other animals. Dark-pools are shaped by light that failed to return.

Every organism is also an industry. A herd moves water. Water carries mineral dust. Mineral dust builds crusts. Crusts feed organisms. Organisms waste into channels. Channels drive pistons. Pistons create sound. Sound drops shards. Shards organize behavior. Behavior shapes the basin. The basin shapes the creatures again.

There is no wilderness in the usual sense because nothing is unoccupied. There is no decay because nothing is ordinary enough to rot in a simple way. There is no clean empty space because emptiness is treated as damage, and damage is immediately colonized.

The inhabitants are therefore not just animals moving through a landscape. They are the landscape’s method of continuing. They scrape, feed, conduct, migrate, crystallize, hunt, remember, and forget. They make the basin run. If they stop, the place would not become silent. It would simply become another kind of thing.

### At the Edge of the Black Brine Basin

Stand at the edge of one of the shallow black pools. The horizon is uncertain. The air tastes of metal, ash, ozone, salt, and faint burnt sugar. A thin skin of oily ice cracks and reforms under dim sideways light. A few inches below the surface, something vast and square shifts, and the pool lifts in a blocky wave. The water remembers nothing yet, but it soon will.

On the glass shelf nearby, a tileback slides with its broad shell edged in salt-fur. Its gill-slits open and close with the vibration of the fog. Beneath its shell, a colony of wirelimbs flickers through translucent joints, eating dust, cleaning plates, and leaving a faint blue circuit behind. Farther out, a line of pack animals moves through the haze, not quite separate from the mist that follows them. They are herds. They are weather. They are the basin’s slow conveyor.

A click sounds from the left. The surface of a smaller pool stiffens. Something small and bright, trapped under the membrane, twitches. A clicker approaches with a throat full of old rhythms. Its shell is furred with black shards. It taps them against the basalt floor, and a faint sequence repeats: tap, tap, clatter, tick, like a sentence being checked for accuracy.

Behind a glass spine, a patch of hyphae glows after no light is near. A shadow lies on the ground where no object stands. The shadow is too still for a shadow, too attached for an absence. A lurelight may have used it. A dark-pool may be feeding. The basin has not decided yet, and its inhabitants will not ask it to decide.

At the basin’s edge, the true inhabitants are everywhere and nowhere. The ground is alive with felted threads. The water is alive with remembered shapes. The air is alive with fog that can be pushed aside by moving bodies. The stones are alive with creatures growing inside them. The dead are alive as mounds and notes. The sounds are alive as shards. The shadows are alive as failures of return.

The basin is inhabited by all of these together. It is inhabited by a crowded, wet, ancient, industrial democracy of bodies and processes, a parliament of pressure and hunger, a life that has mistaken its own land for its body and is now slowly growing inside it.
