What lives there are the Shelf-born.

That is not a name from a textbook, nor a word they use with any pride. They have no single term for themselves, because the idea of a separate “self” is less useful than the idea of a brace, a seam, a heat-line, a load-path. A visitor might call them people, but only if they stretch the word until it is dry and metallic and shaped by survival. They are human-shaped enough to pass as people when seen from behind, in dim light, wrapped in felted mineral cloth, moving quickly through a narrow ladder-way. They are human-shaped enough to have faces, to hold children, to press their palms to a railing and go still for the sake of a memory. But they are not lowland people. Their bodies have been made by thin air, by heat gradients, by dust, by the long discipline of living in a place that does not forgive waste.

If you stand at the mouth of the inner vault and watch them move, the first thing you notice is not their faces but their balance. They are compact, dense, low-centered. Their chests are broad and slightly deep, rib-like structures bulging in ways that suggest a great internal demand for air processing, as if their lungs are not merely breathing organs but reservoirs, exchangers, thermal bellows. Their limbs are not long and graceful but thickened at the joints, hardened near the forearms and shins, as though their bodies learned early that a falling pipe or a slipping brace is a common threat. Their hands are astonishingly capable: long-fingered, dry, with pads of tough resinous tissue that let them grip hot metal and cold metal alike. Their feet are splayed, the toes powerful, the soles roughened to move over grating without sliding. They do not walk with the flat impact of lowland pedestrians. They walk with controlled pressure, as if the ground beneath them might betray them at any hour.

Their skin is dry, oil-slicked, pale to ash-gray to the color of cooled slag. It is not wet skin. It is a surface that has learned to retain nothing it does not need. Salt rime forms in their creases and around their brows, and they do not remove it at once. It becomes part of their appearance, like a faint mineral beard, like white dust in the corners of the eyes, like a crystalline map of their labor. Their skin is sometimes mottled with patches of hardened oil, resin, or chemically bonded dust, especially around the shoulders and knees, where they press against pipes, floors, and engine housings. Their hair, if you call it that, is not the soft human head-hair of humid valleys. It grows in sparse coppery or pale filaments, bristly and stiff, often cut short for work but sometimes allowed to grow into wire-like fringes. During static storms, these filaments lift and arc, turning the head into a small dark constellation of charge. Many Shelf-born wear their hair shaved back or bound in resin caps to prevent accidental discharge near fuel lines. Others deliberately grow it into long, coarse tufts that stand like antennae, because charged hair is useful: it trembles before pressure changes, warns of coming arcs, helps them read the weather inside the mountain.

Their eyes are large for the darkness, but not because they expect light. They are large because they must gather heat, movement, and reflected glow from mantles, resin jars, and red engine seams. The eyes are dark and wide-set, often rimmed with faint salt or soot. A thin translucent membrane covers them against dry dust; when they blink, the movement is slow and deliberate, as if clearing grit from a lens. They do not stare with soft human curiosity. They scan. They read walls, ceilings, floor grates, pressure gauges, and other people’s bodies in one passing glance. They notice the way a glider’s membrane flexes, the way a beetle line shifts from pipe to pipe, the way a metal seam reflects less light than it did before. Their gaze is not polite. It is necessary.

They have no ears that work like lowland ears. Sound is too thin, too swallowed. Their skulls are dense with vibration-sensitive bone, and many have small pits or slits along the jaw, sternum, wrists, and behind the ears. These are heat-and-pressure organs, not quite ears, not quite skin, not quite lungs. They allow a Shelf-born person to stand beside a support strut and feel, through the soles of their feet and through those hidden pits, that the strut has shifted a few millimeters. They can tell the difference between a normal tremor and a collapse-tremor without looking up. They can smell a fault before they hear it, because the scent of newly exposed stone is sharp and strange: hot powder, sulfur, metal, the ghost of ozone. They communicate partly through these capacities. A mother does not call her child with a shout. She presses three rhythmic taps to the nearest rail, or exhales a particular scent-marker from a cloth tied under her nose, or shifts the thermal lamp in their shared niche so its warmth moves across the child’s sleeping mat. The child knows she is being summoned by pressure and heat before the sound ever arrives.

The Shelf-born do not wear garments as decoration, though they can be beautiful in a severe way. They wear layered skins: cured bacterial mat, pressed fungal felt, bronze mesh, resin-hardened cloth, strips of alloy, and plates of mineral paste. Their clothing is a second ecology. It is often alive with small beetles, the half-beetle half-mite cleaners that run along pipes and crevices at night, but which have also taken up residence in the folds of working clothing. A Shelf-born laborer’s coat may be crawling with tiny dark shells, their legs clicking over seams, cleaning salt bloom, mineral dust, and oily atmospheric residue. People do not brush them away as pests. They welcome them. If a person’s clothing is too clean, that is a sign of idleness, exile, or disease. If a worker returns from a hot shift and sits in the dwelling stack, beetles will climb from their coat to the nearby pipes, following the residue home.

Their faces are not hidden behind masks, but they often wear them in dangerous places: hoods of felted dust-mat, respirators of copper mesh and resin, goggles of pane-resin hardened from grub secretion. These face-wraps become their social skin. To remove a hood in a warm dwelling cluster is a sign of intimacy. To stand before another person with your face fully visible is to say, in the most basic language the pocket can speak: I am not afraid of the air you breathe. I will not cover my mouth from you. I allow you to read my breath and scent. Among strangers, they are more guarded. They keep their mouths wrapped, their eyes shadowed, their names unspoken.

Names are not what a lowlander expects. A Shelf-born person is often known by a pressure signature and a material memory. Their name might be something like Two-Click Warm-Left, Salt-Nerve, Red Brace, Sifter-Song, or Fault-Breath. Some of these names are inherited. Some are earned after a fault event. If a worker’s body has a distinctive heat pattern—perhaps one arm runs warmer because of a repaired burn, or one shoulder clicks because of a fused mineral growth—they may be called by that pattern until they receive a formal mark. Formal marks are often scratched into metal plates and hung from the dwelling niche, or pressed into a resin token worn at the throat. A person’s identity is not only their body but their work. To say, “I am Three-Palm-Click,” means less than “I am the one who braced the west shaft and returned.”

They speak in a dry, hushed, click-rich language. It is not a language of long vowels or soaring calls, because those carry poorly through thin air and are swallowed by stone. It is a language of consonants, taps, short exhalations, and hand motions. It has a grammar of pressure. A sentence can be completed not only by sound but by where you stand, how hard you press a railing, which vent you point to, and what scent you wear. Their written language is angular, because it was learned through scratching metal, etching stone, and marking resin plates. It looks like cracks. Their script is often read by fingertips before eyes. In the archive niche, the shelves are lined with thin plates bearing maps of shifting faults, each line a history of movement, each nick a repair, each smudge a failure. Visitors cannot read these with their eyes alone. A Shelf-born archivist runs their hands along the plates and hears the mountain’s biography in the scratches.

The day in the pocket has no sunrise. The world above has light, but here light is local, small, and uncertain. The Shelf-born wake by shifts, by heat, and by rhythm. Some wake because their sleeping niche cools as the engine cycles down. Some wake because a glider above them flutters its membrane in the new pressure current. Some wake because the grubs in the breeding vault begin their slow pulse-song, a deep rhythmic movement that passes through cages and floors. Some wake because a child touches their face and says nothing. There is no alarm bell that clangs, because noise is too precious and too quickly misunderstood. There is no shout because the walls are close enough for breathing to carry. People rise into the warm dark with a practiced economy of motion.

Their dwellings are not homes in the lowland sense. A family might live in an interlocking cell barely wide enough to stand with arms raised, with a sleeping platform, a storage mesh, a heating conduit, and a child-cradle suspended above a vent. There are beds, but many beds are not beds. They are slings, nets, resin hammocks, and heat pads bolted to the wall. Families sleep in shifts because the air becomes too thick and too close if too many bodies rest at once. They learn to sleep through tremor. They learn to sleep with beetles crawling across their shoulders and with grubs pulsing softly in the chamber below. Privacy is not unknown, but it is not a primary value. Intimacy is less about solitude than about shared heat. A Shelf-born person’s closest bonds are often with those who have braced the same seam, shared the same warm niche, or fed the same grub during a cold cycle. Children move freely through the dwelling stacks, passing through curtains of hanging resin thread, climbing over older relatives, being lifted from niche to niche by hands too busy to stop. No one is surprised by the presence of bodies. Space is too valuable for shame.

Their work is continuous, urgent, and quiet. The city is not maintained; it is repaired, stabilized, braced, re-secured, and recalibrated. Every hour something moves. A fault opens with a long sigh of air. A floor flexes. A support joint develops stress lines. A resin seal cracks under heat differential. A conveyor of pale stones jams because the stone is too sharp, too dense, too young. A vent fills with mineral dust from a collapsing upper chamber. The builders do not mourn these events as disasters; they greet them as the daily conversation of the mountain. They answer with tools.

The repair crews are among the most respected people in the pocket. They move in small bands, often no more than four or five, because the passages are too narrow for larger formations. They carry wire, resin, braces, alloy plates, felted wedges, and tapping rods. They climb vertical shafts with hooks and belts, passing over old stairways bolted into abandoned machine shells. They do not speak much while working. Their collaboration is rhythmic. One person places a palm on a crack. Another taps the stone with a light hammer and listens for tone. A third measures heat with a thin filament that changes color with temperature. A fourth prepares the resin mixture from grubs, salt, mineral powder, and solvent. The work is not performed in silence, because there is always vibration, but it is performed in quiet. If you pass near a repair crew, you feel your own chest hum before you hear the tools. You learn that the people are not fighting the mountain, exactly. They are negotiating with it.

Their engines are not separate from life. They are more like livestock, organs, and sacred ancestors all at once. The steamless engines do not hiss. They grind, thump, expand, contract, and breathe through pressure differentials. They are fed mineral pellets, hot stone, resin fuel, and carefully controlled chemical reactions. They are oiled with solvent pastes, not water. They are wrapped in felted mineral mats. Their pipes are padded and bolted with crude geometric discipline. A Shelf-born engineer does not speak of a machine as a dead object. They speak of its temperament. “This engine is cold-tempered,” they might say, or “This one is nervous,” or “This one likes the west vent.” The phrase is not whimsy. The machines behave according to stress and heat, and the people have learned to read them like animals. An engine that runs too hot will make the bacterial mats on its housing swell. An engine that runs unevenly will draw more polishers from the walls. A glider roosting above a valve knows, before a human, that something is wrong.

The animals live with the people so closely that the boundary between domesticated, wild, useful, and beloved becomes almost meaningless. The small many-legged creatures—the polishers, the beetle-mites, the pipe-scrubbers—are the most visible. They are everywhere. They run along rails at night in dense silver-black rivers, their shells clicking faintly, cleaning the oily condensation that is not water but something else, some atmospheric byproduct that accumulates and eats at seals if left untreated. They eat chemical residue and mineral dust. They produce tiny heaps of powder where they gather, which the people harvest or sweep into trays for further use. They are part of the city’s hygiene, part of its material metabolism. A child’s first lesson is often to let a polisher run across the palm and to see how its legs tick along the skin, cleaning salt and oil from the creases. If a Shelf-born person has no polishers on their boots, it is as though they have no breath in their lungs. They are not pets. They are too useful and too integrated for that. But they are respected. People do not crush them without reason. They leave feeding traces of mineral paste in niches where the colonies thrive. When a chamber needs to be sealed, they sometimes encourage the polishers to pass over it first, not because the creatures know the seal, but because their cleaning patterns reveal weak spots.

The wingless gliders are more mysterious. They are broad and flat, like living leaves of ash-colored membrane, but the membranes are thin enough to catch internal pressure currents without any real wind. They launch from balconies, lofts, and engine platforms, riding turbulence through the city’s lungs. Their eyes are wide and shallow, placed low on the body, adapted to darkness and movement. Their limbs are short, their tails long and rudder-like, their heads small with a beakless mouth that tastes vapor. They feed on beetles, fungal spores, and the mineral films that gather on warm stone. They roost in the upper scaffolding, often above dwelling stacks, where their wingbeats create no sound, only faint shifts in air. They are not ridden. They are not beasts of burden. They are too delicate, too tied to the pocket’s pressure, and too easily frightened by clumsy hands. But they are watched. Some are even loved.

In the higher lofts, glider-watchers keep records. Each glider can be identified by the tears in its membrane, by the pale scarring near its shoulders, by the way it drifts in a particular crosscurrent. A watcher may feed it larvae from a resin cup, or scrape warm fungal paste onto a metal ledge. In return, the glider becomes a sentinel. Because the thin air carries tremors upward before the stone fully amplifies them downward, the gliders often know that something is shifting before the humans do. Their membranes stiffen, flatten, ripple, or freeze. If they suddenly crowd into one corner of the vault, the watchers know that a cold pocket is opening. If they go silent and refuse to fly, a static storm is beginning. If they launch all at once toward the lower engines, a crack is forming in the upper wall. The city’s most valuable early-warning systems are not gauges alone. They are creatures.

The burrowing things in the walls are less welcomed. They are many-limbed, eyeless, rubbery, and strange. They tunnel through mineral foam and emerge with dust packed into their joints. Some of them help: they aerate dead chambers, gather loose powder, and mark stable mineral layers. But others will consume supports if the resin mix is too soft, or widen faults if the city leaves a seam unsealed. The people call them sieve-worms, not because they weave silk, but because their tunnels look like filtered stone when cut open. There are trap-keepers who bait tunnels with chemical paste, collect the burrowers, and return them to the breeding shelves. There are seam-readers who inspect their tracks, because a burrower’s path can reveal a chamber not yet collapsed, or a gas pocket not yet tapped. The sieve-worms are feared and harvested, not exterminated. Their bodies are too valuable in paste, their tunnels too revealing.

The grubs are the great soft center of the economy. They hang in cages above the lower engines, pale as blind flesh, segmented and slow, pulsing with heat. They are farmed for resin. They eat chemical film, bacterial mats, oily residue, and mineral paste. They excrete a thick, warm material that hardens into fuel pellets, insulation seals, windowpanes, and joint-filler. Their bodies are warm enough to heat nurseries. Their resin is useful enough to caulk a dying wall. Some people rest their foreheads against them during cold shifts, not from madness but from need: a living wall is easier to trust than a machine. The Shelf-born call them resin-lambs sometimes, though that phrase is tender and not scientific. The animals are gentle, slow, and almost wholly dependent on the engines and the people. They have no eyes, only broad mouths and false feet that grip cage wire. They do not flee. They pulse when fed. They grow heavy with resin. When one is sick, its body cools too fast, and the people can tell before any gauge registers the change. A dead resin-lamb is a tragedy not only because it was food and heat, but because its absence can destabilize a chamber. The city’s survival is stitched through the bodies of its animals.

There is no green in this ecology, no grass, no leaves, no flower. The mineral gardens are alien and beautiful in a severe way. Crystalline polyps cling to walls like frozen coral. Fungal-like mats spread across warm engine housings, pale gray turning yellow with heat. Bacterial films turn rust into something resembling bread. The Shelf-born tend these gardens as farmers, chemists, and priests. They scrape them, roast them, feed them, and watch them grow too fast. When a mat spreads with sudden hunger, it means a solvent leak. When a polyp darkens, it means cold damage. When yellow lightning-veins bloom across a wall, a new mineral seam has opened. People collect those with small chisels and carry them to the refinery shelf, where they are pressed, heated, and folded into resin or fuel. The mineral gardens are not decoration. They are the city’s metabolism. Without them, the beetles starve. Without the beetles, the seals fail. Without the seals, the engines cannot hold pressure. Without the engines, the pocket cools. Without heat, life stops.

Their food is strange but real. It is not cooked in steam, not washed in basins, not served wet. The staple is a kind of mineral bread made from bacterial film and fungal paste, cultured on heated iron plates, leavened with trapped gas, scraped from the walls, roasted, and eaten with beetle oil or grub-fat. It has no sweetness in the lowland sense, but a deep sour-metallic taste, dry and dense, like bread mixed with ash and dried yeast. It keeps for days. It feeds families. It also keeps the microbial systems healthy, because the people harvest the mats before they become too thick and choke the engines. They eat polished beetle larvae, rendered grub resin, pressed salt cakes, and occasional delicacies of flavoring gas, cracked from small pockets in the mineral shelf and inhaled briefly or mixed into paste. They do not drink water. They drink oils, solvents, condensed atmospheric residues, and heated broth made from mineral fats. Moisture is suspicious. A person who tastes dampness in the wrong place becomes afraid in a way no lowlander can fully share. To find water here would not be like finding a spring; it would be like finding a wound in the stone.

Their medicine is similarly dry. They do not wash wounds with water. They clean them with solvent, oil, and abrasive dust. They seal cuts with resin. They cauterize with heated copper. They treat fever by moving the patient to a different thermal gradient: too hot, too cold, too still, too turbulent. A sick Shelf-born body may show it first in the skin’s oil, which turns thin or rancid; in the breathing, which changes rhythm; in the beetles that fail to clean the patient; in the gliders that avoid their niche. Doctors are part mechanic, part priest, part biologist. They are not high-status by vanity, but by trust. To be healed means to be returned to usefulness. To remain sick too long is a social crisis, because a body cannot simply lie idle in a place that has no empty space. Families take turns carrying the patient to warm alcoves. The sick person is given a small resin token or a pressure bracelet so others can read their state without disturbing them. Children learn early not to touch the tokens of strangers.

Childhood is the most visible exception to the city’s seriousness. Children are soft where adults are dry. Their skin is smoother, their heat-signature more unstable, their eyes enormous in the dim glow. They are passed from hand to hand, lifted over pipes, balanced on shoulders, tucked into slings that swing with the tremors of the pocket. A child can stand on a grating and stare at a line of beetles as if they were gods walking. A child can be bribed with a scrap of mineral bread, a resin bead, or permission to touch a warm cage. But they are not idle. They are taught almost immediately. A Shelf-born child learns to read heat before they learn to read plates. They learn where to stand when a fault sighs, where to place their feet on a moving grate, how to taste the vent for sulfur. They learn not to stop in the middle of a corridor, because stopping blocks the flow of people, tools, and animals. They learn to pass in narrow spaces without speaking: shoulder angle first, then hand, then breath. Their games are small, intense, and often dangerous. They play “sifter-race” by releasing a glider from a balcony and predicting where it will land. They play “brace-hold” by standing as still as possible while the floor tremors. They play “new-stone” by sniffing the air with closed eyes and guessing which chamber has opened. Their laughter is not loud. It is felt through the grating: quick taps of feet, sudden breaths, a child’s palm pressed flat against the floor as if trying to understand the mountain’s heartbeat.

Their aging is visible as a slow mineralization. As they grow, they accumulate more than years; they accumulate substance. Their joints develop salt-crusted ridges. Their skin hardens in places. Their hair may grow coarser and turn pale or black with metallic sheen. Their heat-pits become larger or more sensitive, sometimes making them unable to sit too close to an engine without feeling pain. Adults are often marked with scars that are not wounds but repairs: resin seams, alloy pins, metal braces embedded in skin or clothing. The most respected bodies show their history clearly. An elder may be brittle, cool, and difficult to feed, but they are placed in the warmest niches near the deepest engines. Their bodies become living instruments. They can sit in a dark corner and tell by a tremor whether a support is losing tension. They may not be able to lift a tool, but they can remember a crack’s voice from twenty seasons ago. They are not retired. They are reassigned to listening.

Death is not celebrated as peace, because the city has no use for stillness. It is treated as cooling. A Shelf-born body is not buried in earth; earth is too precious, too alive, too necessary for foundations. It is sealed in resin, wrapped in mat, and placed in a niche or brace-cask. Sometimes it becomes part of the structure, after long rites and family consent. The oldest walls may hold ancestors as reinforcement, not as grotesquerie but as continuation. The people believe that the dead still help the pocket hold pressure. A brace-cask may be etched with the person’s name, their workmark, and the line of the fault they braced. A child born into a family that has lost an elder may be given the elder’s tap-pattern, so that the rhythm returns when the rail is struck. Their grief is dry. It has no tears. It is expressed by returning a tool to its hook, by cleaning a niche with beetles, by placing one’s palm on a wall and saying the name in the old pressure-language. The city’s quiet is not an absence of mourning; it is the shape mourning has taken in a place where water cannot be wasted and noise cannot be allowed to echo.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is the discipline of paying attention. They do not worship the sky. The sky is blank, cold, and lethal. They do not worship the sun, because it does not touch the pocket. They do not worship water, because they do not trust it. They worship stability. They worship the held brace, the sealed seam, the working vent, the animal cycle, the warm engine, and the fault that has not failed. Their priests are seam-readers, archive-keepers, and brace-talkers. They carry small hammers and resonance rods. Before a major repair, they tap a wall and ask it to speak. The “answer” is not mystical in the way a visitor might imagine; it is the sound of stress, the pattern of dust, the behavior of beetles, the temperature under their palm. But the gesture is prayerful. They apologize to stone before cutting it. They thank the grubs before harvesting resin. They call the gliders by name when the watch begins. When a new chamber is opened, someone breathes into its mouth from a safe distance and says a formula that is half warning, half welcome: Let this be a chamber that holds. Let this be a chamber that breathes. Let this be a chamber that does not take our walls.

Their art is equally restrained. There are no open theaters, no painted banners, no flowers in vases. But there is beauty, and it is everywhere if you know where to look. The Shelf-born make static murals by allowing lightning arcs to jump across alloy plates, burning patterns into the metal. They carve resin windows until light becomes amber and red. They create wire sculptures of faults, hanging in the archive like three-dimensional songs. They make rhythm instruments from pipes and tension cables, producing music that is heard mostly through the floor. Their greatest performances are not concerts but inspections: a crew moving through a dangerous sector, tapping braces in coordinated sequences, placing resin with precise hands, passing tools without words, their bodies becoming a single mechanism. Visitors would call it labor. The Shelf-born call it the city’s dance.

Their laws are based on consequences, not abstract morality. There is no prison cell because space is too costly. There is no execution without cause because labor is too scarce. The worst crime is not theft of goods; it is theft of safety. To loosen a brace. To hide a crack. To waste resin. To let a glider fail because you wanted a warm perch for yourself. To hoat heat and deny another body its share of warmth. To block a vent with idle storage. Such things are treated as attempted collapse. Punishment is often social: loss of heat rights, assignment to the most unstable seams, exile to the cold side of the pocket near the mouth, where the outer stone can burn the breath and the sky’s pressure change can feel like a blade against the skin. Exile is rare because the settlement cannot afford to lose able bodies, but it is real. The Shelf-born understand cold not as loneliness, but as being outside the flow of the city’s breath.

If an outsider came—if someone ducked through the fissure and climbed the narrow ladder from the plateau—the first reaction would not be wonder. It would be suspicion. The Shelf-born would look at the outsider’s wet skin, their soft clothes, their useless eyes, and their inability to feel the rail’s tremor. They would ask, not your name, but your heat. How much can you hold? How long can you stand without shifting? Can you brace your body when the ground moves? Can you taste the vent? Can you work without breaking? A visitor would be given a place only if they could occupy less space than they contributed. They might be placed in a cold niche, not from cruelty, but because the warm niches are for those who have proven they can return heat. They would be given a felt hood, a resin mask, and a small token with a pressure number. They would learn quickly that no one here shouts. They would learn that a hand on the railing can be a command, a pause, a question, or a farewell.

What lives there is not a civilization that conquered its world. It is a civilization that learned to be inhabited by it. The Shelf-born are held by the pocket as much as they hold it. Their bodies are shaped by heat gradients, their minds by fault patterns, their relationships by shared maintenance. Their children grow up surrounded by animals and machines and chemical gardens until the concept of “living alone” becomes impossible. Their adults understand that every wall is a decision, every pipe a negotiation, every breath a debt. Their elders become part of the structure after death. Their cities are not built in the ordinary sense; they are grown, braced, sealed, repaired, and folded inward until architecture and organism become the same thing.

And yet they are not merely biological machines. They are strange, stubborn, tender, and alert. They sing quietly by tapping. They joke by mimicking a grumpy engine with their bodies. They name their gliders after the way they tilt in a pressure current. They feed beetle-lines from their palms. They argue in hand-language when the engines are too hot to speak aloud. They remember a lost child by the rhythm of their footsteps on a particular grate. They know that the mountain has no mercy, but they also know that the mountain has openings. When a new chamber breathes hot dust from the dark, someone will always be the first to lean toward it, sniff it, tap it, and whisper the prayerful engineering formula: It is possible. Let us brace it before it changes its mind.

If you enter, you will meet them almost immediately. A woman will pass beside you without touching you, her coat crawling with beetles, her face half hidden by a resin hood. She will glance at the seam above your head and tap twice on the railing, not to you, but to someone unseen. A man will climb past on a vertical shaft, his body secured by wire, carrying a bundle of pale stones toward the refinery shelf. A child will sit on a warm ledge with a glider curled in their lap, its membrane twitching as it dreams in pressure currents. In a cage below the engines, a resin-lamb will pulse, slow as a heart. The walls will shimmer with yellow mineral lightning and faint red engine seams. The floor will hum beneath your feet, not as sound but as certainty. The people will not stop for you, because nothing here stops. But if you hold still too long, someone will press their palm to the nearest rail and ask, in the dry pressure-language of the hollow, whether you can carry weight or whether you are only taking warmth.

They live there: the Shelf-born, the polishers, the gliders, the sieve-worms, the resin-lambs, the mineral gardens, the microbial mats, the engines, the ancestors in the braces. They are all of them one city. The city is not mechanical, not biological, not human, not animal. It is the living answer to a world that refuses to give them comfort. They are the ones who learned to read stone with their hands, to speak without echoing, to breathe without drinking, to survive without trusting silence. They are held together by breath, metal, habit, and the terrible patient intelligence of a people who discovered that if the ground is always moving, they must move too—but quietly, carefully, and always bracing for one another.
