It begins as a marsh would in any other world—damp, bright, alive with small movements—but it has no mud, no silt, no dark rot. Instead, life here starts in seams.

There are no ordinary burrows, because there is no earth to dig, but there are gaps. The first inhabitants are the gap-dwellers: white, threadlike chalk-worms that live in the thin channels between tile and slab. They are neither insects nor worms in any familiar sense, but something that learned to take the idea of water and make a home from it. Their bodies are translucent at the ends, milky in the middle, and they leave behind fine lines of calcium as they crawl. If a joint begins to widen, a chalk-worm will find it, spin a lattice of grit and saliva across it, and harden it into a new seam. Menders say the worms are the marsh’s first hands. If you press an ear to a warm tile near the lower fields, you can hear them: a dry, patient clicking, as if someone were polishing the world with a fingernail.

The shell ants live among them. These are no bigger than rice grains, but their bodies shine with a faint nacreous gloss, and their abdomens swell with powdered shell. They march along the grout lines in slow processions, carrying chips as though planting seeds. Where they pass, the tiles seem to knit tighter. They are farmers of chalk-worms. In the damp joints near the edges, they construct tiny pens of cement and tend the worms the way other creatures might tend root lice. If a colony is disturbed, the ants do not scatter. They form little knots of themselves and roll into the cracks, leaving behind trails of pale powder that later harden.

The glaze snails are slower and more solitary. Their shells are perfectly smooth and come in colors that have no business existing in a place without soil: blue as a kiln, green as old bronze, white as a cracked tooth. They graze the surfaces of the tiles, eating the microscopic bloom of calcium that forms in humid hollows. Their trails shimmer faintly, and over time these trails become sealed. The marsh uses them the way a potter uses slip. A glaze snail that dies with its mouth still pressed to a tile will often leave behind a tiny starburst of iridescence. The menders leave these places undisturbed. They call them “first signatures.”

Not far from the snails, in the shallow water that collects in the low channels, live the tile-frogs.

They are flat-backed and broad, with skin like fired ceramic. The color of each one varies: ash-white, pale ochre, gray-blue, sometimes a deep red-brown along the spine. Their throats swell into small round pots when they call, and the sound is not a croak but a bright, hollow tapping, as though a spoon were struck against a jar. At fixed noon, their calls carry for miles. A field of tile-frogs can sound like a kitchen being emptied in a hurry. Their eggs are round and pale and laid in damp hollows where the ground has cupped with age. The tadpoles are rasping-mouthed and dark, and they survive by scraping the moss of lime and the soft mineral films that grow where shade gathers.

The tile-frogs are prey for many things, and so they have learned to sit perfectly still in the shadows beneath slabs, their backs mimicking the surface. When startled, they do not leap first; they flatten. Only when the shadow is gone do they go. Their legs are long and springy, built for hard ground rather than soft, and they can make startling distances, landing on tiles with a crisp clack that makes the whole plain feel like a drum being played by small, nervous hands.

In the deeper waterways there are fish that have never known soil.

The chalk carp are broad and slow, with scales that overlap like small roof tiles. Their bodies are pale at the sides and dark along the back, so that from above they look like moving stone. They have thick lips and broad fins that fan like shells, and they use them to press themselves through channels only a few inches deep. They are clumsy in open water, but on damp tiles they move with surprising purpose, dragging themselves with their fins into pools that would otherwise be unreachable. When the heat builds and a puddle begins to shrink, chalk carp crowd together, their bodies forming a single pale mass. If the water is gone for too long, some of them will breathe through wide, pink mouths, their scales dulling to a matte gray. The menders rescue these stranded fish when they can. The birds do not.

The grout eels are stranger. They live beneath the tiles, in the hidden network of old channels, drainage lines, and porous cement. They are dark and ringed with white, like cords wrapped in tape, with flat heads and tiny pale mouths. They move through the marsh as a slow current beneath a floor. When the heat expands the tiles and the water rises a little, grout eels can push through cracked seams and enter the open lanes. They are feared by children, not because they are violent, but because they seem to be listening. Their bodies are slick with mineral water, and when they crawl onto a dry tile they leave a white trail that dries almost at once. They hunt tile-frogs and small fish from below, rising through a gap like a black thread being pulled from a seam.

There are crabs too, though one might almost mistake them for tools.

The mortar crabs are pale blue and white, with legs like broken shells and claws that carry pellets of cement wherever they go. They build little dams at the ends of rills, trapping water for their young. Their carapaces are uneven, often patched with tile chips, and as they age they grow heavier and rougher until they resemble walking stones. When threatened, they do not flee. They clamp themselves to the nearest tile and wait. Their eggs hatch in the water behind the dams, and the young are tiny, almost invisible, like grains of salt with eyes. The marsh has a way of turning creatures into the materials around them, and the mortar crab is perhaps its plainest proof.

Higher up, where the wind moves across the wide surface of the tiles, the birds of the Bright Handmade Marsh make their lives.

The noon herons are the most conspicuous. They stand in the shadow of slabs so long that they seem to have grown out of the stones. Their plumage is white and pale blue, the color of old glaze, and their long bills are tipped with a dark material that looks like polished metal. They do not wade so much as test the ground. They move by placing one foot at a time on the warm tiles, waiting for vibration beneath. When they tap with a toe or flick a slab with the side of their bill, tile-frogs and eels may rise in panic. The heron eats then, swallowing the small creatures in one slow, deliberate motion.

Their nests are strange. They do not build in trees, because there are none, nor on soft ground. They gather broken tiles and reeds and stack them into shallow bowls. From a distance these nests look like pieces of fallen masonry. The eggs are round and chalky, almost like eggs made of lime. When the chicks hatch, they are covered in down as white as ash, and for the first few days they cannot stand. The parents shade them with broad wings. Even in a marsh of constant light, the newborn need darkness, and so the herons create small tents of themselves.

The glassbill cranes are louder and more social. They walk in pairs or small groups through the open fields, using their curved, polished bills to pry at tiles and probe beneath them. Their heads are bare and pinkish, like clay that has not yet been fired, and their legs are pale yellow. They are not especially shy. When they see you, they stop and tilt their heads so that one eye catches the fixed noon light, and then they begin a rattling display: stepping, bowing, striking their bills against tiles. This is not mere vanity. It is territorial. The sound tells others which shadows are claimed, which channels hold fish, which cracks lead to safe depth.

In the hollows of stacked tile and collapsed masonry, kiln-doves make their homes. They are small and soft-looking, with a blue-gray color and a faint iridescence at the throat. Their coo is like water cooling in a pot: a rounded, damp sound. They feed on the seeds and shoots of marsh reeds, and they spread the plants from one sheltered seam to another. Their wings are quiet. They roost in groups, pressed together under the shadow of a tile overhang, and when they sleep their heads disappear into their shoulders until they seem like little mounds of ash.

The larger animals are slower.

Slab-cattle graze in the cultivated bands where reed, moss, and saltgrass have been coaxed into the cracks. They are broad-backed, short-legged creatures with hides the color of fired clay. Their backs have a rough, calcified texture, as if a thin layer of tile dust had fused to them over generations. The menders ride them only on the longest routes, because they are not fast, but they are steady, and their hooves are made for hard surfaces. Their horns are long and curve inward, like trowels. Their milk is salty and white as lime. They are gentle in the strange way that large things can be when they have no predators left and only heat to answer.

There are shard-martens, too—long-bodied, quick creatures with white-ringed tails and eyes that catch light like wet tiles. They live in old pipes and under slabs, emerging to hunt frogs, small birds, and stranded fish. They crack snail shells by carrying them to high tiles and letting them fall. They are clever enough to learn what a mender will trade. A mender may leave a dead fish near a shard-marten’s burrow and receive, in return, a lost ring, a shard of useful tool, or a message about where the eels have been moving.

And then there are the stilt-menders themselves.

They are people, perhaps, though not in the simple sense. They have learned to live where the ground is made, where the sun does not move, and where the only true shelter is shade. Their bodies are lean and white-browned from calcium dust, their hands cracked with fine seams of lime, their eyes narrowed beneath visors of polished shell. They wear cloaks made of many small layers: dried reed, fired tile, bits of clay, shell flakes, cloth stiffened with mineral paste. From a distance, a mender walking through a field looks like a piece of the marsh moving upright.

They live in houses that are also repairs.

A typical dwelling begins with a stack of tiles, arranged to create a deep shadow. The roof is layered with clay, then reed, then a thin coating of shell paste that hardens under the noon. The walls are porous, so water can be drawn up from below through channels and kept cool. In the darkest corners, small pools are maintained. These pools are not merely for drinking; they are mirrors, stores, thresholds. Life gathers there. Menders farm chalk-worms in them. They keep tile-frog eggs in shallow bowls. They place a shell-cup of water on a high tile to listen to the wind moving across the marsh.

The menders walk on long stilts made of clay pipe or reed bundles, which spread their weight and prevent them from breaking new seams. But this is not only practical. Their steps become speech. A light tap means *clear ground*. A double rap means *water rising*. A long slide along the tile means *repair needed*. A rapid series of strikes means *something has broken beneath*. In the open brightness, where the voice flattens and carries strangely, tapping is more reliable than calling. Children learn to speak by making patterns with a stone on the surface.

Their days, if one may call them days, are measured not by sunset but by pulses. The sun remains at noon, but the heat of the tiles changes. The plain swells and contracts. Water moves in thin films. Dust settles. The menders rest when the stones are hottest, when the light seems to press down like a white hand. Then they rise and begin repair. They scrape lime from one place and spread it in another. They carry water in clay buckets. They press new tiles into gaps using molds made from the shells of dead carp and the backs of broken heron eggs. The ground is not a gift. It is a task.

Their language is full of words for cracks.

There is a word for the first pale line that appears before a tile loosens. Another for the line that runs under water and cannot yet be seen. A word for the crack that sings when struck. A word for the crack that holds a worm. A word for the crack that is already dead—filled, sealed, forgotten. A word for the crack that will become a door if it is not sealed in time.

They do not pray to the sun. They have no gods that come down. The noon is too present to be worshipped; it is simply the fact of the world. What they attend to is what lies beneath it. In their oldest houses, there are pits covered with three loose tiles. These are called ear-holes. A mender will lower their head, close their eyes, and listen to the movement of water, the clicking of worms, the distant shifting of slab-cattle. If the ground is speaking correctly, they mark it down. If it is too quiet, they go to the place where a crack has formed.

They bury their dead in the seams.
