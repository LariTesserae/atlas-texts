Who lives there? Not, first of all, animals. Not even, first of all, plants. The basin begins with a film.

It begins with the wet, mineral skin that stretches across every surface that has not been too recently torn. A thin living layer covers the stone, the mud, the broken edges of shale, the undersides of black leaves, the rims of ephemeral pools, the damp fur of moss, the soft mouths of fungus. It is not one thing. It is a congregation so intimate that one cannot say where one organism ends and another begins. Bacterial threads bind mineral grains into provisional crusts. Fungal hyphae lace through dead matter and living root alike, tasting, dissolving, carrying, responding. Algae and mold-like growths flare into blue-green or sooty violet where they meet warmth and seepage. They are small beyond individual notice, but together they are the basin’s most honest citizen. They make the smell of the place. They make the slickness under the foot. They make the faint glow that does not illuminate but announces: something has passed here. Something has disturbed the skin.

If one were forced to describe the inhabitants from the smallest upward, one might begin with this: the land itself is partly alive, and the lives upon it are only more concentrated versions of that same condition. The stone sweats. The mud breathes. The leaf trembles. The moss sways not only because wind moves through the dark but because countless bodies crawl across its filaments. The basin is not a place where organisms happen to live. It is a place where life has become one of the land’s primary substances, as elemental and unstable as water, heat, and breaking rock.

Then come the crawling ones.

There are beetles with shells the color of old rust or drowned bark, broad and low, built to wedge themselves beneath leaf litter and stone. Their legs ring against one another in the dark, producing a dry, metallic chatter that is never one note but a hundred shifting grains of sound. Some of them have ridged backs, natural combings along the abdomen or femur that scrape when they move. These ridges are not tools, not in the way a maker might intend a tool. They are growths, accidents of lineage, structures selected by chance and pressure. Yet when a beetle draws its leg across a stone shard, or drags a flake of rock across the mouth of a hole that water has already opened, the result is sound, or packing, or blocking. The stone becomes no permanent instrument by being touched. The sound fades as the creature moves on. The action is brief, opportunistic, and already being overwritten by the next vibration.

There are crab-like things with pale, translucent limbs that seem too delicate for the world they occupy. They move through wet leaf-lace and seep-choked soil with the caution of bodies that are mostly nerve. Their shells are thin, almost glassy, and when they squeeze through a narrow space they emit a faint creak, like a wet finger dragged over the rim of a basin. They taste the dampness before they enter it. They do not have eyes in the way an upper world might understand. They have clusters of sense-hairs along their legs and mouthparts, and a chemical palate so acute that the air itself can be read as a text: heat here, mineral there, rot approaching, blood nearby, spores drifting, water turning elsewhere. They live in the spaces between larger bodies, in the gaps made by fallen stems and fungal columns. They are never solitary. Where one crawls, many are already moving. Their passage is a rustle; their death is a meal; their molt is a shell that quickly becomes a scaffold for bacteria and moss.

There are worms too thick for comfort, blind and muscular, feeding on the wet rot that accumulates where black stems meet the ground. They have no faces, or faces so reduced that one cannot tell the end from the beginning except by the movement of the soil. When they pass through a peat-mat, they leave behind a subtle rearrangement of water and air. The surface may ripple. A fungal cap nearby may collapse into a slow sigh of spores. A small beetle following behind may taste the disturbance and turn its body into a different angle. Nothing is wasted. Nothing moves without consequence. In such a place, consequence is not moral or permanent. It is physical: pressure changes, heat changes, moisture redistributes.

There are moths that do not fly toward light because there is almost none. They fly through the dark on wings that look like wet parchment, veined with faint luminescent threads, perhaps remnants of a chemistry that once interacted with sunlight, perhaps new. Their bodies are heavy with scent, with static charge, with spores clinging to their legs and antennae. They navigate by air pressure and temperature gradients, by the taste of damp, by the sound of dripping and crackling stone. When they brush the under-sides of broad leaves, their wings disturb beads of condensation, making tiny sudden rainfalls of water that fall onto fungal caps below. When they die, they fall into the lower layer and are consumed by things that smell of nothing but moisture and decay.

There are things like mammals, but not as any mammal would be known under a clear sun.

They are small to medium in body size, perhaps, or variable, because in such a place a creature might not grow into one stable shape so much as respond to the density of its surroundings. They are low-slung and soft-skinned, with bodies suited to squeezing through damp tangles rather than running over open ground. Their fur is coarse, matted, or absent in patches that allow them to feel the humidity against bare skin. Their hands and feet are broad, almost leaf-shaped, with pads that read the vibration of the ground. Their fingers are long, jointed strangely, sensitive to texture, moisture, temperature, movement. Their faces are flattened, not by choice, but by the physics of touch and smell. There are eyes, but they are narrow, buried, covered by skin or by a veil of bristles. Some are completely blind. Those that retain photoreception do not see shapes so much as gradients: warm here, dark there, faint glow where mold has opened, a pale seam where a pool has just begun to reflect the absence of sky.

They move by hearing and feeling. Their whiskers are arranged in dense planes, like the fronds of a fern, and can be swept forward to read a branch, a stem, the face of a fungal column, or the body of another creature hidden in leaf litter. They have long, flexible necks that allow them to press one side of the skull to a moss-covered stone and listen through it. They can feel the faint percussion of water beneath the soil, the hollow vibration of a rotting trunk, the minute tremor of legs beneath a mat of peat. They do not hunt by sight. They hunt, if they hunt at all, by pressure and rhythm. A creature that is still in the sense we mean by stillness is not hidden here. To be hidden is to be part of the general noise, to match its texture, to vanish into its pattern.

They communicate by touch, by scraping, by breathing, by tapping. They slap their broad tails against wet ground in irregular sequences that may mean presence, urgency, readiness, or simple irritation. They click their tongues against the palate to send short pulses through the air, returning to them altered by obstacles and bodies. They exhale warm vapor from enlarged nasal chambers, carrying chemical messages that can be tasted by another at a distance. They press their ears against the soil and listen through roots. They gather where the sound is loudest and the air is wettest, not because they have built a place to gather, but because the conditions of gathering have been made by the land. Their sociality is not architecture. It is weather.

A small creature with soft skin may carry a seed-pod to the mouth of a thread of water. It does this not to build, but because its body is asking a question: what happens if the water is stopped? what happens if the water is diverted? what happens if I taste another pool? The pod blocks the trickle only briefly. The water finds another way. The pod swells and falls apart within an hour. The creature returns to the general movement of its kind, which is to touch, taste, climb, scrape, feed, mate, die, and be digested by the living floor.

Some of these eyeless mammals are larger, or more robust, with bodies that function as resonating chambers. Their rib cages are wide and loosely jointed, and their throats can inflate with air drawn from warm vapor. When they hum, the moss beneath them sways. When they drum, standing water trembles into concentric patterns. They are not musicians in any cultivated sense. They are simply bodies evolved to make sound in a world where sound is the only stable light. Their voices are not songs so much as maps. A long, wet thrum may tell another creature that a slope is crowded, that a fungal mat is rich, that water is moving through the lower hollows, that a falling boulder has disturbed the air. A short, sharp bark may scatter insects from a leaf-curtain. A low, continuous purr may calm the trembling of a young one against the flank of a parent in a place where the parent cannot be seen and does not need to be.

They are not kind to one another. Kindness would require too much stability, too much deliberate restraint. They crowd, bite, climb over, press, and steal. If two small mammals meet at a water-thread, there is no negotiation in the manner of a built world. There is only the sudden density of bodies and the resulting rearrangement: one pushes, one retreats, one falls into moss, another climbs a blackened stem. The water is used by both, briefly, or by neither because the disturbance has already changed its course. Their conflict is not history. It is turbulence.

Above them are the climbing ones.

Some creatures have become almost indistinguishable from the vegetation they pass through. They are flattened, pale-green or charcoal-dark, with limbs like twigs and bodies covered in fine setae that trap moisture and spores. They cling to the undersides of broad leaves and extend long, hair-fine legs across the space between one leaf and the next, feeling the movement of another body through the leaf’s trembling. They do not build webs, because a web would be an artifact. Instead, they use the natural threads of fungus, of damp fiber, of shed larval skin, of mucus secreted by their own bodies, to make momentary paths. These paths are not made in the sense of craft. They are grown, or left behind, or collapsed by their own weight. If the creature pauses upon one, it is using a byproduct, not a structure.

Among these climbers are creatures with mouthparts like small combs, used to scrape the microbial film from leaves without tearing them. They are essential to the basin, because they regulate the skin. They keep the surface open, moist, exposed to further colonizers. When they feed, the leaf may contract, drawing in a bead of dew that has gathered in its cup. The movement startles a smaller insect below, which drops into a moss mat and is consumed by a pale crawler with pincers too delicate for battle but perfect for pulling soft-bodied prey from dark crevices. Everything is connected, but not as a machine. As a fever.

There are also predators, but their predatoriness is not defined by patience. In a place without stillness, patience is difficult and perhaps unnecessary. A predator may be silent only for a heartbeat. It may lie in wait beneath a mound of crushed leaves, but the mound is not a trap; it is a mound. It may strike when a vibration pattern indicates water, heat, or the shell-ring of an insect. Its ambush is not a long calculation. It is an eruption of appetite from the general churn.

One such predator is a low, wide-bodied thing with skin like damp leather and legs ending in broad pads covered with sticky glands. It presses itself to the underside of a moss-covered shelf and extends a long, pale limb into a nearby thread of condensation. It waits for the tremor of a drinker. If a small mammal touches the moisture with its whisker and the surface shivers, the predator may move instantly, not because it has seen the animal, but because the water has told it where flesh is. Its strike is more of a pull than a bite: it drags prey from one surface to another, into its mouth, into the warm darkness of its own body. It does not store the kill. It does not cache food. To do so would require permanence, and this basin rejects permanence. It feeds, moves, molts, dies.

Another predator-like creature is almost entirely passive, a flattened thing hidden in the leaf-litter whose body is covered in tiny sensory filaments. It looks like a dead leaf with a mouth at its center. When insects crawl across it, it does not attack. It simply closes around them with an action too slow to seem predatory and too quick to seem geological. It swallows them into its own soft body, and within moments it is again indistinguishable from the detritus around it. It is not clever. It is not a maker. It is a strategy of waiting that has become flesh.

The pools have their own lives, or rather, they are born with them.

A narrow rivulet appears over a lip of new stone, and within it there are already things moving, not because they chose to swim there, but because the basin has a habit of filling any brief vessel with life. The water is shallow, murky, warm, tasting of chalk and iron. It reflects nothing, yet beneath its surface there are filaments of motion: larvae with feathery heads that filter mineral sediment and bacteria; small translucent creatures with no shells and too many legs; worms that coil like wet thread; things that look like seeds until one hears the tiny click of jaws. They breed quickly, because the pools do not last. A pool may survive only a few hours, sometimes only minutes, before the land reclaims it through seepage, absorption, or a new crack that draws the water deeper. The creatures that depend on it must therefore be creatures of emergency. They are born in haste, feed in turbulence, mate in pressure, and die before the water can turn to steam.

Some of these aquatic beings are not aquatic because they prefer water, but because water is the only medium in which their bodies can function. They are soft-bodied and blind, with flattened mouths and gill-like structures that resemble frayed leaves. They breathe through every surface and live only as long as moisture remains. If a hand were pressed into the pool and then withdrawn, the disturbance would send them into a frantic shimmering cloud, and in that moment the water would seem to boil with invisible life. They are food for crawlers that dip their limbs into the pool. They are food for moths that hover low enough to taste their spore-veined bodies. They are food for the film on the stones that waits for them to die and then consumes them into the next layer of soil.

The air, too, has its inhabitants. Not birds, not as an open sky might produce them, but things that have wings because wings are useful for moving through a crowded darkness. There are gliders with membranes stretched between elongated limbs and tail-fins, moving from one fungal column to another by reading the heat rising from warm seeps. They are silent when they glide, their wing-margins frayed into softness that absorbs sound, or perhaps not silent at all: their membranes make a faint wet creak when they flex, and this creak may be their language. They land on the undersides of leaves, hang in curtains, and drink condensation. Their young cling to them before they are able to taste the air. When one falls, it is usually consumed by the layer beneath it. There is no rescue in the sense of intention. The fallen one becomes part of the ground’s metabolism.

The most difficult inhabitants to describe are not the small ones, nor the eyeless mammals, nor the fungal towers, but the collective ones.

Sometimes a disturbance produces something that behaves as if it were a single creature but is made of many. A cloud of moths, disturbed from a moss ceiling, rises in a spiral so tight it forms a pale column. At its edges, where wings brush one another, static charge builds and spores ignite in faint blue flashes. For an instant there is a shape in the dark: not an animal, not a plant, not a spirit, but a momentary pattern of bodies and dust. It pulses, contracts, expands. It seems to listen. Then the wind shifts, or a larger creature passes through the middle of it, or the sound changes, and the pattern breaks. The moths scatter. The spores fade. The shape dissolves into the general noise.

Or a swarm of tiny crawlers moves across a fungal mat in a synchronized ripple. No one has ordered it. It begins because one individual tastes a gradient and changes direction, and its legs disturb the surface, and another individual responds to the disturbance, and the disturbance becomes a wave, and the wave becomes a front, and the front becomes something that looks for a few minutes like a body moving with purpose. It slides over leaf litter, around stone shards, into the mouths of brief streams. It may strip a patch of moss and leave it bare, not by choice, but because its density has become a force of erosion. After it has passed, the bare patch quickly fills with new spore-film, and the land resumes its pretense of completeness.

Or a mass of larvae in a warm pool rises into the air as winged forms before the water drains. They emerge so quickly and in such numbers that the pool seems to exhale them. Their wings catch droplets of condensation, and the exhalation becomes a mist of glowing motes. For a moment, the basin is full of living stars that do not illuminate, only indicate. They disperse. Some are eaten. Some fall back. Some die within hours. None of this is dramatic to the land. The drama belongs to the viewer. To the inhabitants, it is simply the next tremor.

There is no hierarchy of being here in the way a human mind would impose one. The small are not lesser, because they are numerous. The large are not rulers, because the ground can open beneath them and the fungus can consume them. The soft are not weak, because softness allows hearing. The hard are not strong, because shells crack and molts decay. The plant, the animal, the fungus, the film, the spore, the pool, the wind, the vibration: these are not separate categories so much as modes of occupancy. The basin has no citizens who are more real than others. A fallen leaf is an event. A beetle is an event. A mammal’s footfall is an event. A seep breaking through mineral skin is an event. Life is not divided neatly into organisms because the medium itself is alive with reaction, movement, and appetite.

They do not have names, but they have sounds. Or perhaps their sounds become their identities, not fixed identities but temporary ones. The beetle that scrapes its shell against a shard is a different presence from the moth whose wings make the tearing of damp paper, which is different from the mammal whose tail strikes the moss in a short triplet. None of these sounds are symbolic. None of them are language in the sense of grammar and intent. They are simply bodies announcing their motion to a world that knows bodies through motion. Yet in their repetition, patterns emerge. A particular rhythm may mean water has opened somewhere else. A particular scrape may mean a creature is large, or desperate, or close. A particular hum may mean that the ground is crowded with listeners. The land does not interpret these sounds as words. It uses them as triggers. Sound changes shape, and shape changes behavior, and behavior changes the land again.

Because the light is almost nothing, the inhabitants are not beautiful in the way a visual animal might be beautiful. They are not meant to be seen. Their forms are meant to be touched, tasted, heard, or smelled. They have the beauty of fit: the shell that rings because it must, the whisker that reads pressure because the dark is too thick for eyes, the wing that carries spores because the air is full of decay, the mouth that opens against wet bark because warmth is more valuable than vision. A predator’s face may be flattened and blind, yet its nostrils flare with such precision that they can taste a frightened beetle three leaf-layers above it. A fungus might seem passive, but when its cap is touched by a crawling limb, it may release a burst of glowing spores, not as a weapon exactly, but as a reflex, a chemical answer to vibration. The boundary between plant and animal behavior is thin here, because the basin does not reward categories. It rewards immediate response.

Their reproduction is no less urgent than their eating. There is no romance of season, no slow courtship under stable weather. Mating may be triggered by a sudden warming of the ground, by the arrival of rain on hot mineral faces, by the smell of a nearby pool opening, by the vibration of a creature passing through a fungal mat. Eggs are laid wherever moisture is already gathering or about to gather: under moss curtains, in the cup of a broad leaf, along the seam of a warm stone, beneath the lip of a newly formed rivulet, in the hollow of a collapsed stem. Some eggs are soft and dissolve quickly if the water fails. Some are armored with minerals drawn from the surrounding film, hardening only long enough to survive until hatching. The larvae emerge into the noise and immediately become prey, food, parasite, passenger, or part of a swarm. They do not have childhoods in the sense of protected growth. They have intervals between consumption and being consumed.

Death, too, is not a spectacle. There are no bones in the usual sense, because the calcium and chitin of bodies are quickly claimed by microbes and mineralizing films. A corpse vanishes into a white bloom of fungus, a black stain of bacteria, a trembling cloud of insects. Within hours, what seemed individual becomes surface, substrate, scent, and food. There are no graves because there is no intention to separate the dead from the living. There are no ruins because what dies is too thoroughly alive to remain. The dead are not buried. They are recycled into the wetness of the next life. The land does not remember individuals. It remembers patterns of decay.

The eyeless mammals carry their young clinging to flanks or backs, not because they build nests, but because there is no fixed place in which to leave them. A mother moves through the leaf-lace, and her young moves with her, tasting her skin, feeling the vibrations of her breath. If the young falls, it does not cry for help in the manner of a child with language. It produces a chemical or a low vibration that draws nearby adults into a response: a sniff, a press of nose, a lick of scent, a shift in the mass of bodies that may protect, consume, or ignore. There is no family unit in the human sense. There is density. In density, care becomes indistinguishable from instinct.

Some of these creatures are more social than others. Some form clusters around warm mineral seeps where the air is thickest and the ground trembles faintly. In these places, bodies press together not for comfort but for orientation: each one becomes a source of sound, heat, moisture, and chemical signal. The cluster moves as a loose, breathing mass, never building a shelter but forming temporary shapes. A wall of bodies may block the wind. A ring of bodies may protect a pool. A line of bodies may bridge a crevice, not because they have chosen to cross it, but because one steps and another follows by vibration. When the pool drains, the cluster dissolves. When the wind changes, the line scatters. There is no ruin left behind. The ground closes around their passage.

The larger mammals, if such they may be called, do not have herds so much as weather systems. They appear in bands when moisture concentrates or when a fungal bloom spreads. They move through the lower layers of black shrubs, pressing aside stiff ferns, crushing moss, disturbing the film. Their footfalls cause water to ripple in basins that are not yet pools. Their voices cause insects to fall from leaves. Their passage opens brief channels in the mud that collapse behind them. They do not make trails. They make scars that are soon covered by new growth. They are not kings of the basin, because there is no throne to claim and no stable ground upon which to stand. They are simply large bodies that move matter by moving through it.

Some creatures seem to exist entirely as instruments of sound. There is a flat-bodied animal with a resonant throat and a shell-like back plate that vibrates when it walks. Each step produces a muted click, and when it moves quickly, the clicks merge into a pattern like a hammer on wet stone. Other creatures respond to this pattern not as meaning but as weather. They shift, climb, freeze, or scatter. The animal does not intend to communicate. It intends to move. But in the dark, movement is communication. Every body speaks because every body displaces air and water. Even the act of being touched becomes a kind of language: the moss knows when a leg crosses it, the leaf knows when a wing brushes it, the film knows when a body dies upon it.

There is no clear boundary between the inhabitants and the inhabited. The beetles feed on the fungal mat, the fungal mat grows over beetle shells, the moss holds the water that feeds the ferns, the ferns shelter the moths, the moths carry the spores, the spores dissolve the leaves, the leaves feed the worms, the worms aerate the soil that feeds the roots. None of this is harmony in the peaceful sense. It is saturation. It is competition without peace. It is occupancy without ownership. Every pore is already being used. Every surface is already a meeting of mouths. To live here is not to have a place in the landscape but to be a temporary rearrangement of the landscape.

If one were to ask what these creatures are like as individuals, the answer would be unsatisfactory, because individuality is too thin a skin to describe. A beetle is a beetle, but also a resonating chamber for the sound of its own shell. A mammal is a mammal, but also a map of humid gradients. A moth is a moth, but also a cloud of potential decay. A creature may be eaten in the time it takes to be seen. It may be born and die without ever being distinguished from the noise of its own kind. Its body may be unique, but its identity is not fixed. The basin does not preserve identity. It dissolves identity into motion.

And yet they are not mindless. That word would be too easy and too false. Mindless suggests absence, and here there is too much presence for absence. They are aware of pressure, moisture, heat, vibration, chemical gradients, movement, crowding, predation, mating opportunity, water availability. Their cognition is distributed through skin, ears, feet, whiskers, antennae, stomachs, and breath. It is not located in eyes. It is not gathered into a single perspective. It is spread across the body’s contact with the world. A mammal may know the slope ahead because its whiskers taste the turbulence of air against broken stone. A beetle may know a water-thread is nearby because its legs feel the minute electrical or chemical shift in the mud. A larva may know it is surrounded by danger because the water it drinks has the taste of crushed shells. This is intelligence without reflection. It is adaptation so immediate that it resembles thought.

There are probably moments of something more than reflex. In the thick sound, in the wet darkness, in the endless contact between bodies, there may be brief recognitions: a mother’s flank matching a child’s tremor, a rival’s shell-sound answered by a pause, a predator’s vibration learned as a pattern that means death. But such recognition would not become story. It would not become ritual. It would not become artifact. It would be too fleeting, too dependent on the conditions that produced it. If the water drains, the lesson changes. If the fungus dies, the food moves. If the ground opens, the path is gone. The creatures learn, but they do not teach through permanence. They teach through presence.

The vegetation is also an inhabitant in a sense that would be too literal for a human forest. The black stems grow so tightly together that they form walls not by intention but by overcrowding. The broad leaves cup warmth and moisture, not because they are gathering for animals, but because their shape is a response to what falls and what rises. The ferns unfurl in tight spirals, sounding like wet leather when touched. The mosses press down into patterns shaped by vibration. The fungal towers push upward, dripping spore-threads, making chambers beneath their caps that are not homes but accidents of mass. A fallen log does not remain a fallen log; it becomes a shelf, a nursery, a mine, a membrane, a bridge. No one clears it. No one removes it. It simply changes state under pressure.

There are no roads, but there are movements. A creature’s repeated path is not a road because it does not persist as a designed thing. It is a pattern of disturbance. Moss may lie lower where bodies pass. Mineral film may thicken where water is redirected. Fungal caps may break where heat rises. These are signs, but signs of occupation, not of architecture. The next rain may wash them away. The next collapse may bury them. The land permits them only briefly.

There are no cities, but there are densities. A place where warmth and water combine may attract so many bodies that it becomes a temporary city of legs and wings and shells. The density is not organized. It is not planned. It is the result of gradients pulling creatures toward what they need. And yet, for an hour, or a day, or until the pool drains, there may be a center. At the center, bodies press, feed, mate, fight, molt, die, and become soil. Then the center moves. The city moves. The city is not a city. It is a condition.

There are no gods, but there are effects that might be mistaken for them if one were a stranger entering the dark. A sudden glow in the spore-cloud might seem like a sign. A tremor through the moss might seem like a voice. A standing wave in a pool might seem like a ring of attention. A swarm that responds as one might seem like a mind. But none of this is made with intention beyond the intention of life to continue. The basin does not worship itself. It simply acts, and in acting, produces the illusion of purpose.

The creatures of the basin are not peaceful. They are not violent in the way that conscious beings are violent. They are simply unable to afford vacancy. To leave space is to lose it. To remain still is to be consumed. To be large is not to be safe, because the ground is unstable. To be small is not to be insignificant, because even small mouths can alter the film that binds stone and water. Every body is both predator and prey, both occupant and occupiable matter. The only stable condition is churn.

If one asks what kind of creature is most characteristic of the place, the answer is not the beetle, not the mammal, not the moth, not the worm. It is the tremor. The characteristic life of the basin is the life that moves by making the dark answer. It is the life whose body is a tuning fork. It is the life that tastes the ground, hears the leaf, smells the seep, and knows the world through pressure. It is the life that dies quickly and becomes part of the next pressure. It is the life that cannot be seen clearly and therefore is always more than it appears.

There are many small creatures, and among them, some are so numerous that they form a kind of weather. They rise when the heat rises from a cracked stone. They fall when a cold wind moves over their fungal ceiling. They glitter in the faintest glow because their bodies are full of mineral grains, or because they carry light-reactive spores, or because their skins have grown so thin that the blue-green bloom of mold shows through them. Their clouds are not migrations. A migration implies a destination. Their movement is more like the breathing of the basin: in, out, in, disturbed, restored, disturbed again.

There are larger things that one cannot see but can feel entering the body of the dark. They have broad feet, or many legs, or a long body that displaces water from leaf-cups as it passes. They make the moss tremble before the sound reaches the ear. They eat fungi, leaves, worms, eggs, shellfish, or the bodies of things too small to name. They are not apex in the sense of rulers. They are apex only because their bodies displace more matter. They too will sink into the ground, be claimed by film, be opened by jaws, be dissolved into heat. Their size does not make them permanent. The young, sharp land does not allow large monuments to stand.

The inhabitants have no concept of horizon because their world has no horizon in the visible sense. They know boundaries as changes in pressure, temperature, moisture, and sound. A ridge may not be seen, but the air grows thinner, the stone sharper, the rasp of wind louder. A basin may not be perceived as a basin, but the ground softens, the water returns in trickles, the fungal smell thickens, the bodies cluster. A cliff face may not be a cliff but a wall of cold air and angular edges. A pool may not be a pool but a sudden change in skin-contact and temperature. Their map is not visual. It is tactile, chemical, and auditory. To know the basin is to move through its gradients without being destroyed by them.

They do not explore the land so much as the land explores them. Each creature is a node where conditions are tested. If a beetle’s legs taste salt, the salt may spread through its body and affect its movement. If a mammal presses its ear to a stone and hears water below, its body may become a record of that sound. If a moth’s wings brush a glowing spore-field, its flight may scatter information into the dark. Knowledge here is not stored as artifact. It is carried as response. It dies with the body that responds. It is renewed in the next pressure, the next vibration, the next heat.

The place may be called a basin, but the creatures do not call it anything, because it is not outside them. Their bodies are built from its moisture, its minerals, its heat, its noise. Their shells are made of its calcium and chitin. Their breath is saturated with its vapor. Their young are hatched in its temporary water. Their dead become its living skin. They do not inhabit the basin. They are the basin’s temporary intensification.

If one were to ask, “Whoever or whatever lives there?” the first honest answer is: the crawling. Then: the humming. Then: the dripping. Then: the glowing that is not illumination. Then: the soft bodies that listen with their faces. Then: the shells that ring because they must. Then: the spores that rise when the ground trembles. Then: the moths that fly without a sky. Then: the pools that die before they can be remembered. Then: the swarm that becomes a shape for a moment. Then: the shape that falls apart before it can become a name.

And if one insisted on describing them as creatures, not processes, then one might speak of the eyeless mammals with whiskers like fern-fronds and tails that drum the dark into rings; of the broad-backed beetles whose shells are combs and whose jaws taste stone; of the translucent crawlers who read the air with their legs; of the moths that carry static and spores; of the larvae that bloom in vanished pools; of the soft predators that strike at ripples; of the climbing things that hide in leaf-veins; of the large resonant bodies that walk through moss and leave the ground shaking with their weight; of the swarms that become one moving body for a few seconds; of the film that is not one creature but a conversation too small to name.

They live there without making it.

They live there without clearing it.

They live there without knowing it as a place.

They live there by being the movement of its water, the pressure of its bodies, the tremor of its sound, the glow of its decay, the hunger of its young, the wet dark that cannot remain still.

And because the basin has no permanence, no ruin, no artifact, no road, no roof, no grave, no monument, the only proof of its inhabitants is that the air is fuller, the ground is warmer, the moss is pressed into patterns, the water shivers, the spores rise, and the dark answers back.
