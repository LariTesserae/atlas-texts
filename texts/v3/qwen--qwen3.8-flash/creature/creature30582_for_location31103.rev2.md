What lives there is not one thing wearing many skins.

It is a crowded, layered, breathing occupation: a parliament of forms that do not merely exist on the plateau but continue it, extend it, repair it, make it more itself. If you came looking for a single inhabitant, the land would defeat the question. The mossbacks are not the place. The clickers are not the place. The rime-swarms are not the place. The stunted conifers leaning sideways along the ledges are not the place. The fungal thread beneath the dust, the white bacterial felt around a steaming seam, the tiny mineral snails licking frost from black rock, the flattened predators with eyes like chips of ice—all of these are residents, yet none is resident enough. The true resident is the system of lives, a being made of process, a civilization without cities, an industry without engineers.

The first names, if you insist on names, belong to the slowest and largest of the surface-dwellers: the mossbacks.

They are broad, wide-bodied animals, low to the ground as if the wind had taught them humility. Their legs are short and folded, thick at the haunch, built more for balance than speed, ending in wide, ridged feet that are half hoof and half stone-polishing pad. Their bodies are dark with dense fur, but not ordinary fur; between the hairs live mosses, lichens, spore-cups, frost crystals, and tiny beetles. In winter, a mossback looks less like an animal than like a rock that has begun to move. Their faces are bearded with bristling plant life, and sometimes a whole cushion of damp moss hangs from a chin, trembling when they chew. They are grazers, but not in any simple sense. They eat with patience. They bite at moss mats, tear at waxed shrub leaves, scrape the mineral skins from wet boulders, and press their broad tongues into porous rock to gather the thin film of water that has not yet evaporated into the high air.

They travel in groups so dense that the herd appears as one rippling body. Older mossbacks walk on the windward side, forming a moving wall; the calves, young, injured, and pregnant females ride the sheltered interior. Their young are born into leeward gullies where the moss has held moisture from melt, where a newborn can lie steaming in dark green velvet and be hidden by the very ground that shelters it. Calves learn the place by taste and touch: the sour rust of a new stone face, the sweetish mineral tang of a hot seam, the salty bloom in a hollow, the cold bite of frost caught on a lichen ring. Before they are strong enough to graze well, they eat mossbacks’ dung, which is thick with microbes and half-digested mineral grit, a living compost that primes their intestines for the difficult chemistry of the plateau.

The mossbacks do not trample the ground so much as edit it. Where their hooves press, the moss is compacted into dense, springy mats that hold water longer. Where their bodies rub against stone, they polish the mineral surfaces and seed them with lichen fragments caught in their fur. Their tracks become small reservoirs in the bright rock, then become gardens within days: frost-lichen, rust-biofilm, thread-fungus, then tiny rooted shoots. Their dung, steaming white with mold, becomes the foundation of mineral gardens, the place where the next generation of moss will find the nitrogen, salts, and living spores it needs to claim bare stone. Even their breath is useful. The warm vapor condenses against north-facing rocks, and in the cold, this breath-water is caught by moss and drunk by small insects before the sun steals it again.

They are not wise, if wisdom means a story an animal tells itself. But they are intelligent in the way a glacier is intelligent: they find the path with the least resistance, they conserve heat by grouping, they remember by movement. A herd may follow a route across the plateau for generations, a route that no one has mapped but that every body knows. The path winds through faults, turns at the mouth of a vent, climbs a terrace where wind is lower, and descends into a gully where iron-ferns unfurl after rain. They do not rush the route. They do not need to. The land changes by millimeters, and the mossbacks move by years.

If the mossbacks are the plateau’s muscles, then the copper-throats are its quicksilver nerves.

They are smaller predators, flattened and restless, with bodies made for slipping through packed moss and between sharp stone teeth. They are covered in pale fur that is not clean-white but speckled with black, copper, and frost-blue, as if each animal has been lightly painted with the ground it walks. Their heads are narrow, their eyes set wide to read movement without lifting the face from cover. Their tails are long and low, used as rudders between rocks. They are not large, but the place does not reward size. The mossbacks are not easy prey, and open pursuit is nearly impossible where every footstep risks sinking into a hidden void or catching on an edge of basalt. The copper-throats therefore hunt by compression, not speed.

They hunt clickers, young mortarworms, and occasionally the unsteady calves of smaller grazers. They learn to follow the clicking tracks under the moss, to pause where the ground sounds hollow beneath their paws, to dig where the air smells of mineral paste. They can imitate the rhythmic vibration of a colony, thumping one paw against the stone until the clickers shift their tunnels toward them, as if answering a mechanical question. In the dry intervals, they lick frost from the underside of shaded boulders. In the wet intervals, they shake their coats with such force that droplets freeze on their fur and fall away as tiny needles of ice.

They den in the carcasses of old mossbacks, but only after the bones have been picked clean and the hide has become a scaffold for moss. A dead mossback’s skull becomes a chamber. Its ribs become a fence. Its teeth become anchors for fungus. A copper-throat will line the eye sockets with glasswing remains, which glitter and repel some of the biting insects, and then with lichen scraps that hold dampness. Their young grow up inside a death that has already been turned into a home. The predator does not hate the carcass; the predator uses its warmth, its architecture, its mineral smell. In this plateau, even violence is a form of construction.

They are often alone, but not always. In hard seasons, when frost seals the moss and the vent heat retreats deeper into the stone, they form loose packs of two or three, sharing dens and hunting along the same fault line. They do not howl. They click their throats, a sharp metallic sound, and the sound travels through packed vegetation in a way the wind cannot drown. Other animals freeze. The glasswings lift higher. The clickers flatten into their tunnels. The plateau registers a small change in the pressure of life, a ripple of attention moving from skin to skin, root to stone.

Beneath all of them, but not quietly, are the clickers.

That is not their name, not truly. A name for a being that has no face is almost a mistake. They are pale, segmented, long-bodied, and many-legged in a way that makes counting them seem foolish, as if one is counting not creatures but movements. Their mouths are not mouths in the human sense; they are grinding assemblies, hardened by mineral dust, fitted with ridged plates that chew stone into powder. They eat the ground. Not the moss, usually, not the roots, not the dung, though they are not above those things. They eat the scree. They eat the fractured basalt. They eat the bright threads of lichen-crusted grit. They swallow what is too fine for wind and too rough for moss, and then they pass it through bodies that act like living mills.

What they excrete is not waste so much as product. Their droppings mix silica, calcium, iron, and biological binders into a pale paste that hardens quickly in the cold, bright air. Over time, those secretions become sheets, seams, plugs, roads, and thin crusts over exposed rock. They do not build castles. They do not raise towers. They do not make anything recognizable to a human mind as architecture. But their work is undeniable. They polish the undersides of stones until the mineral veins look like machined rails. They cement moss cushions to boulder faces in neat overlapping plates. They line tunnels with smooth white material that repels water, then plug those tunnels at intervals with hard lids so that moisture stays longer in the pockets where eggs are laid and fungus grows.

Their paths are visible from a distance when the light is low: faint, branching ribbons of pale grit stitched across the dark plateau. In one section, the tracks run straight as if laid by rule. In another, they curve around vents in concentric arcs, following temperature gradients. In another, they spiral into a rock hollow where a buried spring once moved and is now only a damp memory. They follow one another with such mechanical precision that a visitor might think they are obeying a machine that has no visible source. But the machine is the pattern, and the pattern is their life.

Clickers are colonial in the deepest sense. A single clicker is not a creature, but a fragment of a creature-process. It has no face to name, no personality, no recognizable center of choice. Yet a colony can decide, slowly, by chemistry and vibration. When a seam opens in the rock after a freeze-thaw, the clickers come in waves, not with intention, but with a relentless attraction to the new surface. They test it with antennae hardened into mineral feelers. They taste its salt. They listen to its heat. Then they begin to grind it. The colony is not a brain; the colony is a hand.

Some of them are eaten by copper-throats. Some are scraped from the stones by mossback teeth when the plants are too sparse. Some fall into crevices and are consumed by fungus. Some die in their own tunnels, their bodies clogging the passages until other clickers secrete around them and turn the corpse into a node in the structure. Nothing is wasted. Even a clicker’s death becomes a brick.

Near the clickers, but less visible, are the mortarworms.

They are blind, soft-bodied in youth and armored in adulthood, and they do not grind stone with mouths so much as with their whole bodies. Their skin is ridged and rough, and when they push through scree, they carry grit between their scales and wear the rock down as they go. They are living mortars: where they dig, the ground becomes finer, more workable, more likely to hold water. They burrow not into soil but into the idea of soil, producing the mineral paste that will one day become substrate for moss.

Their tunnels are not hidden. They open like pale wounds in the rock, sealed at intervals with buttons of hardened mucus. The buttons look artificial, almost manufactured, until they crack and release a brief exhale of damp air. Mortarworms are driven by pressure and moisture, not by thought. When a patch of rock dries, they move deeper; when a rain suddenly wets the surface, they rise as if summoned. They are food for almost everything small enough to dig them from their holes: beetle larvae, copper-throats, vent-snails, and even certain moss-feeding birds that do not sing but scrape.

There are no birds, not in any common meaning. But there are rime-fliers, glasswings, the living snow of insects.

They are translucent and angular, with bodies like chips of ice and wings so stiff they appear broken. In calm light, a swarm may pass almost unnoticed, a shimmer above the rock. But when the wind lifts and the mist returns, they become a cloud of sound, a static hiss that can be heard before it is seen. They ride the spray on windward faces, not flapping so much as vibrating, using the electrostatic charge of the air to remain aloft longer than their wings seem able to sustain. They feed on salt bloom, on films of dew, on the microscopic mineral dust that gathers on lichen. They drink water that is not water yet, only the suggestion of wetness held in cold skin.

They are the plateau’s moisture carriers. A glasswing may land on a wet patch in a hollow, absorb a film of melt into its body, fly across a dry ledge, and leave behind a fine residue of moisture as it lays eggs or dies. In this way, dampness is transported against gravity and wind, from the hidden pockets to the exposed faces, from the rock-shadow to the sunlit blade. The swarms also carry spores. They land on moss, on carcasses, on vents, on the waxed leaves of shrubs, and wherever they rest, they leave behind a dusting of life.

They are born in damp crevices, in the wet interiors of porous stone, in the moss-lined chambers of mortarworm tunnels. Their larvae are pale and nearly transparent, feeding on rust-biofilm and decaying fungal threads. They mature quickly in the sunlit intervals and die quickly in the wind. When the storms rise, their bodies are lifted into the air and frozen before they fall, becoming a glittering drift that accumulates in the lee of the boulders. The drift looks like broken glass. In reality, it is a garden in waiting. Mosses grow from the dust of their deaths. The mineral salts in their carapaces feed the lichen rings. The tiny hollows left by their frozen bodies catch condensation.

In some seasons, the rime-swarm becomes so dense that it darkens the light, not with shade but with brightness, as if the air itself has shattered into moving needles. Then the wind changes, and the sound becomes a single metallic tone, high and thin, like a wire stretched across a forge. If lightning comes, the swarm seems to ignite. Not all of them die, but some burst in the charge, scattering their remains over dry stone, seeding it with salts and proteins. These are moments when the plateau works most visibly: the wind, the ice, the electric air, the insects, the moss, the mineral dust—all cooperating without knowing they cooperate.

There are also larger things in the sky, though they are not birds and not mammals. They are the spark-sails.

They live at the highest ridges, where the charged air makes metal taste and the horizon seems too close. Their bodies are flat, leathery, and edged with fine filaments of mineralized tissue, pale with copper and salt. They look like scraps of lichen that have learned to drift, or like sails cut from the skin of a stone that never existed. They do not fly by wing-beat alone. They use the plateau’s electric hum. When the wind rises through the basalt columns, it carries ions from dust and moisture, and the spark-sails tilt their filamented edges to catch the field. They drift above the sharp terraces like living kites, like the loose pages of a book that the wind has refused to finish.

They feed on ozone, on charged droplets, on the fine salt films that collect on the windward edges of columns. They are not predators, though they disturb prey simply by passing overhead, driving glasswings into higher currents and startling copper-throats into dens. They lay their eggs in the hottest vertical faces, where the stone is too warm for frost to stay and where the mineral veins run bright. The eggs are encased in hard, glassy shells, often mistaken for pebbles. Sometimes lightning strikes near them, and the surge of charge cracks the shell without destroying the embryo, because the embryo has been built to withstand the plateau’s moods. Spark-sails are not common. They are too strange to be counted. They are more often seen as absence made visible, a dark shape that is not shadow, a movement that is not wind.

If the spark-sails are the upper inhabitants, the vent-gardens are the lower ones.

The heat seams in the rock do not create ovens, because the cold is too patient, but they create pockets of persistence. Around a vent, the stone is warmer than the air. Steam rises in short, metallic sighs. Frost melts first there. Moss grows thicker. The dust is damp. Mineral salts bloom in white and yellow crusts. This is not a lush place in the ordinary sense; it is lush by necessity, by heat, by chemical pressure. Here live bacterial felts, pale and slick, feeding on dissolved gases. Here grow tube-like worms with chitin armored by mineral deposits, their bodies rooted in the steam-moss, filtering condensation. Here creep shell-lickers, small snail-like animals with translucent, frost-stained shells, grazing the bacterial film that grows wherever warm breath meets cold stone.

The vents are repair shops. When frost splits a seam, the vent-warmth releases a slow pulse of steam. The steam carries mineral dust. The dust settles. The bacterial felt traps it. The shell-lickers scrape the surface into new angles. The moss catches the damp and holds it. In time, the crack becomes a vein, the vein becomes a line of white stone, and the white stone becomes a scar that the plateau wears without shame. Life around the vents does not merely endure the heat; it uses heat as a language. Some species move toward warmth with their entire bodies. Others avoid it, but build their nests near it so that eggs survive the long nights. The vent is both threat and invitation.

There are plants, of course, if the word can be used without implying something too simple. But they are not merely plant-life scattered across the land. They are residents with the same right of occupation as the animals. The mosses are the first claimants. They do not wait for soil, because soil is what they create. They begin on dust, on mineral film, on rust-colored biofilms, on pale lichen lace. They creep from one stone to the next by hair-thin filaments, by spore, by the fragments carried in the fur of the mossbacks and the bodies of the glasswings. Their cushions become black-green where they are wet and pale blue-white where they are frozen. They hold water in their dead cells and release it in their living ones. They breathe not with lungs but with saturation. When a herd passes, the moss underfoot is not flattened like a carpet; it is a living floor that stores the pressure and returns it slowly, releasing dampness like breath.

Lichens form rings around stones, then rings around rings, concentric and patient. Some are acid-miners, dissolving microscopic amounts of glass and iron from fresh basalt. Some are mirrors, scattering the white light across their thalli so that the stone beneath them appears to glow. Some are maps of time: each layer a different claim, a different species, a different success. A patch no wider than a hand may contain a history of centuries compressed into color.

The low shrubs are not gentle. Their leaves are darkened like oil-stained copper, waxed with a substance so hard it reflects light as if painted. When wind passes through them, they click. When frost forms on them, they rattle. Their branches twist and fuse because the place does not allow straight growth for long. They bend around boulders, merge with one another, and form mats so tight that they hold water like living reservoirs. Their roots are shallow but tenacious, gripping the edges of stone rather than sinking deep into soil. They do not stand; they adhere. They do not rise; they occupy.

The stunted conifers are the most tragic-looking of the plants, because they are always leaning, always sideways, always pale with lichen. Their bark is cracked and white, their needles stiff and dark, glossy with frost even in midday. They grow only where shelter exists: a ledge, a crevice, a wind-shadow behind a fault. But even there they do not trust height. The wind and the thin air have taught them to live close to the ground. Their roots grip the rock like fingers. Their branches form cages of wood and moss. Inside those cages, copper-throats sleep. Around those cages, clickers build.

Then there are the iron-ferns, or what pass for them in this place.

They are not truly ferns, not in the way of warm forests, but frond-like colonies of tissue that curl inward during the driest minutes of the day and unfurl when mist returns. They are colored between moss and rust, between plant and mineral, and they smell faintly of wet stone when touched. They grow in sheltered gullies, in black hollows, in the spaces behind boulders where melt water remains long after the air has dried. Their leaves are not soft. They are thin, tough, and slightly metallic to the touch. They drink from the humid air, not from open water. Their spores are carried upward by glasswings and spark-sails, and they land where there is stillness enough, dampness enough, and some small shadow to hide the first thread of growth.

The fungi are the least visible and the most pervasive residents.

They are not merely mushrooms or molds, though both exist here. They are threads, filaments, webs, and membranes that stitch the rock to the soil. They grow in white lace beneath the moss, in black veins through the scree, in golden rime around the mouths of vents. They break down what the cold refuses to rot. They dissolve bone into calcium gardens, turn feathers into nitrogen networks, spread dung into moss. They are the plateau’s memory of death, because nothing here can die twice before being used.

Some fungal mats grow so regularly they resemble woven cloth, but touch reveals them to be abrasive, mineral-rich, almost stone-like. Others form glowing patches in sheltered depressions after dark, a faint blue-green shimmer that does not illuminate enough to read by but is enough to suggest the land is remembering the sun. This glow is not magic. It is chemistry, metabolism, the slow oxidation of compounds made possible by the cold. But it has the effect of thought. If you stand in one of those depressions, surrounded by moss and rock, you may feel watched, not by an eye, but by the ground itself, attentive in its own slow way.

The mycelial network may even respond to pressure. Where mossbacks walk, the threads shift water and nutrients. Where a vent cracks, the fungal film moves to seal it. Where a carcass falls, the network sends out pale fingers to dissolve and redistribute. Where glasswings die in sheets, the threads take them into the mineral cycle. This is not a mind like ours, not a creature with a face, not a being with a name. It is a weave. But the weave is alive, and it is one of the inhabitants, and perhaps the most persistent.

There are also small beings that might be missed entirely: mites that live in the moss cushions, springtails that ride the surface tension of water films, beetles that bury themselves in mineral paste, larvae that consume lichen rings from the inside, worms that live in the plugs of mortarworm tunnels, tiny crustaceans in the dark pockets of condensed rain. They are the citizens of the micro-plateau, each square of moss a city, each droplet a sea, each stone face a continent. Their lives are short, and the land barely notices them, yet the land cannot be the land without them. A moss cushion is not one organism. It is an entire society of moss, fungus, beetle, larva, mite, mineral, and water.

If you put your ear to the ground, you can hear them working. It is not one sound but many. The granular rustle of burrowing. The faint click of mandibles on stone. The tiny pop of ice cracking inside a leaf. The muffled grinding of a colony polishing a rock face. The whisper of spores moving through damp air. The hiss of steam. The clatter of a herd. The static cloud of the glasswings. The wind in the columns. The plateau is never silent. It is a loudness made of small processes, a machine without a source, a factory built by no hands.

There is no predator at the top of this web in the ordinary sense. The copper-throat is dangerous, and the mossback is powerful, and the vent-heat can kill, but nothing sits above the system like a crown. The plateau has no apex. It has density. Every form checks every other form. The mossbacks spread seeds and polish stone. The clickers build roads and seals. The mortarworms grind and aerate. The glasswings carry moisture and spores. The predators keep the smaller populations from filling every crevice. The scavengers and fungi dissolve death into mineral gardens. The moss holds water. The fungi move nutrients. The vents release heat. The wind scours and sorts. The light exposes and accelerates.

Even death is inhabited.

A carcass on this plateau does not remain a corpse for long. First the steam rises from it in the cold air. Then the fungi take the flesh. Then the beetles take the sinew. Then the bones are cleaned by tiny scraping animals. Then the moss grows into the rib cage. Then the lichen circles the skull. Then the mineral salts bloom in the hollows of the eye sockets. Then the bones become anchors for fungal threads, and eventually they become part of the ground, not as ruin, but as structure. A fallen mossback becomes a terrace for plants, a den for predators, a seed for clickers, a gift of calcium to the bright hollows around its ribs. A glasswing fall becomes a prism garden. A shed feather becomes insulation in a crevice. A broken shell becomes a shard of light, then becomes a mineral lens, then becomes a marker for the next organism that can use it.

This is why the land seems built without builders. Its inhabitants are not decorating a world. They are manufacturing it.

If you stand on a high terrace at midday, you can see the plateau as a single body. Not because it has a face, not because it has limbs or a heart, but because every part is working with the same urgency. The dark moss is its skin. The bright mineral veins are its nerves. The herds are its slow, moving organs. The tunnels are its intestines. The vents are its breath. The ice is its shiver. The light is its eye. The clickers are its hands. The glasswings are its spores and signals, rising and falling through the charged air. The fungal threads are its memory. The whole thing is one being made of countless beings, and every individual life is both citizen and tool, both creature and process.

Yet this body is not gentle.

It is too high, too cold, too bright, too packed, too wet in places and too reluctant in others to be gentle. It does not love the mossbacks. It does not hate the copper-throats. It does not reward the clickers for labor. It simply continues. Every creature lives because the place has found a use for its form, and every creature is threatened because the place has no loyalty to any form except occupation. If a stone warms, something climbs it. If a damp patch appears, something feeds there. If a carcass falls, something enters. If a void opens, it is claimed before it can be called empty.

This is why there is no horizon one can trust.

A horizon implies openness, but this land is never fully open. It is too crowded with life, too busy with process, too sharp, too wet, too bright. The eye cannot rest on a far line because the far line is not a limit; it is a continuation of the same relentless occupation. Mountains rise farther off, but they do not look like places one might live. They look like walls. They look like the edges of a workbench. They look like obstacles the plateau has grown against its own spreading body. The air is so clear that distance collapses and expands at once. A ridge seems close enough to touch, but it is not close. A pool seems near enough to drink, but the pool is not there; it is frost, moss, breath, condensation, melt trapped in a hollow, hidden under skin, waiting for the right angle of sun and the right kind of hunger.

A human could pass through, perhaps, if the human had reason enough and suffering enough. But the human would not live there. The plateau would not make the human at home. It would use them the way it uses every new surface: by colonization, by incorporation, by eventual redistribution. Dust would settle in their lungs. Spores would catch in their clothing. Moss fragments would brush from their boots. Their breath would condense on stones and be drunk by insects. Their sweat would feed bacteria. Their urine, if any remained long enough, would salt a patch and become a garden for a day. Their footprints would fill with water and spores, and within weeks the trail would be gone, not erased, but absorbed. They would not become ruins. Ruins require forgetting. The plateau does not forget. It metabolizes.

If they stayed, they would become part of the machinery not by choice but by necessity. They would learn to squeeze water from moss, to read the angle of frost, to follow vent-heat like a pulse, to step where the ground does not shift, to listen for the clicking under the surface, to fear the bright hollows where ice can refreeze without warning. They would not find a city. They would not find a god. They would find work. Everything is work here. Everything is process. Even stillness is a kind of production: a pause in which moisture condenses, minerals settle, spores drift, fungi wait, and the cold renews its bite.

What lives there, then, is not merely animals. Not merely plants. Not merely insects, not merely molds, not merely minerals animated by the pressure of heat and cold. What lives there is a civilization of processes. The mossbacks live by moving across the shelf and making it more hospitable. The clickers live by grinding and sealing. The glasswings live by lifting and carrying. The predators live by shaping, by preventing one life from becoming a monotony. The fungi live by dissolving and remembering. The vents live by breathing. The ice lives by breaking. The light lives by revealing.

If you listen closely enough, the plateau does not sound like nature at all. It sounds like an industry operating in slow motion. There is no whistle because the wind is already whistling through the columns. There is no hammer because the frost is already splitting the stone. There is no loom because the moss is already weaving the dust. There is no furnace because the vents are already breathing. There is no warehouse because the carcasses are already being sorted. There is no road because the clickers are already laying it. There is no machine because the world is already the machine, and it is running without operator, without blueprint, without beginning.

And yet the place is not dead. It is not abandoned. It is not empty. It is not waiting for builders. It is already built. It is built by pressure, by dampness, by occupancy, by repetition, by life that cannot afford to be idle. It is built by moss that holds water and animals that move through it, by insects that carry spores and fungi that carry death, by heat that melts frost and wind that freezes it again. It is built by generations of clickers making plates that will be cracked by ice and healed by more plates. It is built by herds pressing paths into the stone and paths becoming gardens and gardens becoming roads and roads becoming faults and faults becoming new paths. It is built by the slow accumulation of work so complete that human industry beside it seems clumsy, temporary, loud in the wrong way.

At dusk, when the light turns from white to something colder and more golden, the plateau seems to ignite from within. The mineral veins glow. The frost crystals flare. The moss cushions shine with dampness. The glasswings catch the last light and turn into a moving field of tiny stars. The herds stand in dark rows along a terrace, their breath steaming into the clear air, their bodies continuous as one animal stretched across the shelf. The vents hiss. The ice cracks. The wind moves through the columns and produces a note that is not music but something closer to warning.

And below the moss, in the dark, the clickers continue.

They grind.

They paste.

They seal.

They move.

They make the next surface ready for the next life.

This is what lives there: the grinding and the sealing, the grazing and the predation, the spore and the vent-heat, the frost and the melt, the carcass and the garden, the herds and the swarms, the sideways trees and the woven fungal skins, the spark-sails and the shell-lickers, the iron-ferns and the moss-backed animals, the silent, persistent, unrecognizable work of a plateau turning itself into a body. The place is not inhabited by beings in the way a house is inhabited. It is inhabited as a body is inhabited. It is alive because it is occupied, and it is occupied because it makes every form necessary, and it makes every form necessary because nothing can waste itself in a land that works too efficiently to die.

What lives there is every part, every process, every repetition, every creature and every role.

It is the mossbacks, but only as hands.

It is the clickers, but only as teeth.

It is the glasswings, but only as breath.

It is the predators, but only as judgment.

It is the moss, but only as skin.

It is the fungi, but only as memory.

It is the vents, but only as pulse.

It is the light, but only as eye.

It is the plateau itself, alive by density, mechanical by necessity, industrial without artifact, and so busy that it never once has time to be quiet.
