There is no first citizen to name.

If a traveler came into the unfinished city and began asking, *who lives here?* the question would need to be answered in three parts, or perhaps in no fixed order at all: what crawls, what stands, and what condenses. The inhabitants are not arranged into species so much as into processes. They are densities of motion, layers of mineral, slow wet decisions that look like architecture when observed from far away.

The smallest and most abundant are the things that look like moving sand.

At first, on the upper terraces, they are only a granular darkness shifting across black stone. They are too numerous to count and too uniform to seem individual. But when a traveler kneels on a warm ledge and watches the “sand” climb a hairline seam, the illusion collapses into a swarm of jointed bodies no larger than coins. Each is black, hard, angular, made of overlapping plates that have the dull sheen of wet obsidian. They have too many legs, or perhaps legs so fine that the mind invents extra ones. The joints do not merely bend; they strike tiny flints of mineral, and when the swarm turns in unison, the terrace whispers as though it were full of loose glass beads.

These are the siltwalkers, though they would not call themselves that, because they do not call themselves anything. They live in sheets and columns and rivers of bodies across the high terraces, following the heat that rises from the engineered stone. They graze on microscopic films of mineral that are not plants and not quite weather: living precipitates that grow where water moves through rock, eating dissolved salts and exhaling faint acidic traces. Their mouths are not visible. They drink and feed through pores along the undersides of their plates. When one is turned over, it seems almost mechanical: a dark ceramic disk, legs retracted, no eye, no expression, only heat pooling around its core.

Individually, a siltwalker is a scrap of weather. Collectively, it is a maintenance system. They crawl into conduits no larger than a fingernail and clear the fine mineral dust that would otherwise choke the city’s breath. They pack loose grit into cracks with their bodies and excrete a glue that hardens into new stone. When the upper platforms dry too rapidly, the swarm moves down into colder channels; when the ledges become too wet, they climb back toward the thermal veils. They respond to the city’s architecture as though it were part of their own skin, because in many places it is. A balcony that feels warm to a traveler’s hand may be warm because beneath its surface there is a living lattice of siltwalkers pressing through capillary tunnels, keeping the stone at a temperature that no animal can name but all of them can feel.

They reproduce by accretion and by accident. Some females, if the word fits, release tiny beads of glassy calcium into condensation pools, and the beads drift into narrow shelves where the temperature is just right. They hatch not as young animals but as pale soft grubs, almost invisible, tasting the water until it teaches them what to become. Others divide: a heavily laden individual finds a crack, stops, and slowly lets its body split into two or four smaller clusters, each carrying away a share of plates and heat. No birth is noisy. No death is mourned. The dead simply become grit, and the grit becomes swarm.

Beneath the bridges, where the wet dark hangs like a curtain that refuses to fall, live the sifters.

These are less like animals than like living lace. They are pale, segmented forms with translucent bodies, strung together by fine threads they secrete from their own pores. Each sifter is a few centimeters long, soft where the city is hard, blind where the darkness has made sight unnecessary. They dangle from ledges and under causeways in such numbers that they form a second ceiling, trembling faintly when air moves. If a traveler holds a hand close to a colony, the water in the air seems to thicken around the fingers. The sifters taste moisture before it is visible. They pull condensation from the atmosphere through feathery membranes, filter out metal ions and mineral salts, and then grow.

Growth is their form of speech. A sifter colony extends across a gap by building its own thread-mat: a sheet of wet silk and microscopic shards, half tissue, half scaffold. Over weeks it thickens into a membrane. Over seasons it becomes a wall. Some of the city’s suspended platforms are not hung by cables alone but are anchored by these living tissues, grown through engineered holes and hardened by repeated secretion. When a bridge feels as though it breathes, it may be because beneath it there are thousands of pale mouths drinking the air.

Sifters are not gentle. They are patient. A single sifter dies when it dries out, but a colony can outlast a tower. They consume dead insects, shed shells, flakes from larger bodies, and the mineral dust carried by the wind. They excrete a substance that looks like powdered moonlight and can fill gaps in ceramic with the same ease that it seals a wound. When the thermal light shifts, the colony flashes a faint blue-white color, not because it has eyes to see the shift, but because its fluid channels carry a tiny electric current generated by the movement of dissolved ions. This makes them seem, to a cautious traveler, like something more organized than they are.

They may not be intelligent. But they are communal, and in the unfinished city, communal is difficult to distinguish from intelligent.

Across the mineral flats and the broad shelves of basalt move the wave-crabs.

They are named poorly by any human standard, for they are not crabs and not waves, though they are made of both. The first wave-crab one sees will look like a small mechanical ruin: a broad, flattened carapace of fused silica, legs like jointed black iron, antennae that tap the ground in short percussive pulses. Its shell is pitted and layered, sometimes with blue veins running beneath the ceramic plates where a weak electrical current circulates. It moves in quick, deliberate steps, as though it has learned the surface by trial and error a thousand times.

Then the traveler looks up and sees that the flats are covered with them. Thousands. Tens of thousands. A tide of dark, clicking bodies moving in a synchronized wave that can be heard long before it is visible: a dry chittering, like rain on glass, like nails tapped in order by a distant hand. They do not arrive as a group because one of them has decided to go somewhere. They arrive because the architecture has changed. A crack has opened. A basin has filled. A thermal veil has shifted enough to make one side of a ridge cooler than the other. The wave-crabs respond to the city’s stress, as though stress were a scent.

Each carries a small droplet of mineral slurry in a sealed chamber beneath its body. When it reaches a damaged seam, it presses its legs against the edge and releases a thin line of material through its mouth. The slurry hardens. Some wave-crabs die doing this, exhausted or frozen into place by a sudden drop in temperature, and their bodies are then consumed by siltwalkers and sifters, becoming part of the repair again in another way. Others are not repairs but explorers. They click into hollows where the air moves strangely, and if they hear a resonance too deep, they do not retreat. They gather. They form circles around the source and tap in unison, trying to teach the sound a rhythm it can accept.

They have been seen doing what can only be called tuning.

A large wave-crab colony may spend months on a broken plateau, moving from one stress line to another, adding material, withdrawing material, scraping the surface with their legs until the whole stone vibrates in a more stable note. When the plateau finally holds, the colony will shift as if released by a hidden command and move on. Some older wave-crabs do not move at all. They become calcified in the posture of their work, shells fused into the stone, legs permanent. A traveler, touching a railing, may not realize that the railing contains the fossilized bodies of the creatures that helped make it.

Fast and almost impossible to observe are the vein-things.

These are the inhabitants that make the city seem to be full of secret movement even when nothing larger has stirred. They are not animals in any ordinary sense. If one could stop them, they would be seen as coils of dark, flexible mineral, jointed by tiny pale nodes, each node a mouth, a pore, a lens, a heat-sink, a battery. They travel through hairline cracks, through engineered pores, through the spaces between layers of silicate, as if stone were not solid to them. They seem to move too quickly to be tracked, not because they are especially fast, but because they are thin enough to pass behind the traveler’s attention. One hears them: a scratch inside the wall, a soft chitter from below the floor, a sudden pressure change in the air that feels like a held breath released.

When a wall ripples under a traveler’s hand and then becomes cold, that is often a sign of them. They may be feeding on heat trapped in the material. They may be carrying something. They may be repairing. They may be searching for a parasite. They may be fleeing from a larger creature whose slow joint has just shifted. In a city where so much life grows from stone, the line between maintenance and predation is thin.

A vein-thing can enter a siltwalker and replace its nervous system with a lattice of mineral threads, turning it into a carrier. It can lodge in a wave-crab’s joint and alter its tapping pattern, introducing a new rhythm into the swarm. It can crawl into a sifter colony and make the threads sing at a different frequency. Sometimes a traveler hears a wall producing a sound like distant bells and cannot know whether the city is calling or merely digesting.

They are also the city’s sense of touch. In places where the stone feels unexpectedly warm or cool, where moisture gathers where it should not, where dust lifts in tiny spirals, something below has pressed upward and learned the shape of the surface. The city is not finished, and these are the fingers that are still feeling for the next edge.
