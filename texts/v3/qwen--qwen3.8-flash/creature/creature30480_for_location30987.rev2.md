If you enter the Underloom looking for its inhabitants, you will first see almost no one.

That is because the place does not keep its residents the way a human city keeps them: in doors, in streets, in the ordinary furniture of bodies moving through daylight. The Underloom is too dark for that kind of population. Too damp. Too porous. Too alive. What you first notice are absences— a corridor that might have been a river, a roof that might have been a lung, a wall that seems to be holding something back, or perhaps giving something out. You begin to read the space by weight, by humidity, by the faint tremor of machinery in the floor.

And then the absences fill.

The Underloom is inhabited by many things at once, and most of them are not merely animals. Some are citizens. Some are organs. Some are public works. Some are weather that has learned to have an opinion.

The residents could be called, if a name is necessary, the Wefted.

They are the closest thing the Underloom has to people, though even that comparison is only a local courtesy. A Wefted body is not a single organism in the way a human body is. It is more like a contract, a negotiation made flesh. It may have begun as a seed, a spore, a larval cyst, a scrap of living machine-tissue, a graft taken from a municipal wall, and one carefully prepared bud of mineral conductive fiber. These are brought together in a nursery bladder, a hanging pod lined with soft fungal tissue, and there, over a season of cool wet growth, they settle into one another.

Some become tall, slender things with too many joints, their skin translucent enough to see the pale sap moving beneath. Others are squat, broad, almost seed-like, their bodies armored in plates of hardened resin. Some resemble walking clusters of moss. Some resemble little more than a floating membrane with eyes in the wrong places. Many are not beautiful by any conventional measure, and many are beautiful enough to make an eye forget its own grammar. But every one of them is, in some sense, a collaboration. Their bodies are composed of plants that have agreed to walk, animals that have agreed to grow, fungi that have agreed to remember, and machines that have agreed to be patient.

They move through the Underloom with a strange careful softness. Because moisture is everywhere, their footsteps are barely heard. Because light is low, they make themselves visible to one another by other means. Their names are not spoken sounds. A name in the Underloom is a sequence: a particular scent released from a gland, a faint pulse of blue-green light through a membrane, a vibration in the floor, and sometimes, if the name is important, a tiny pattern of spore drift that catches the current of the district and carries it a short distance.

To meet a resident here is not to look at a face. It is to stand in the right place while a small weather system says hello.

The Wefted are citizens, but they are also workers, and much of their work is maintenance. In the Underloom, nothing is merely decorative and nothing is merely alive without consequence. Every organism has some function, and many functions have some organism attached to them.

There are root-writers.

A root-writer is not a simple tool, though it looks like one: a stylus-shaped creature, part insect, part fungal bud, part living instrument, with a hard spine and a soft glandular head. It is inserted into a stem to correct growth, to guide a building into becoming useful, to erase a doorway no longer wanted, or to coax a public wall to thicken before the next wet season. Some root-writers remain in place for years, dreaming the decisions of the plant from the inside. When they are removed, their bodies carry the chemical memory of that growth pattern, and trained readers can press their heads to a membrane and know what the wall was thinking, what it was about to become, and whether it was content.

There are pruning crews, though “crew” is a generous word for such a thing. Many of them are Wefted with broad, leafy backs and long arm-fibers, moving through trunk-halls with humming devices shaped like seed-pods. They do not merely cut the city. They speak to it. Their hums travel through bark and cambium, and the plants respond by opening, closing, hardening, releasing, or accepting grafts. A good pruning crew can make a district feel like a room again after years of overgrowth. A bad one can leave a street coughing resin for a month.

There are mist nurses, who tend the hanging bladders where water waits before becoming rain. They are smaller, often beetle-backed or moth-winged, with suction pads instead of fingers, and they know by taste how much tension a pocket of condensation can bear before it must be released. In the wetter districts, mist nurses sometimes sing to the water, not in melody, but in pressure pulses that travel through the vine, and the city’s rain pockets answer by leaning toward the next plaza like a patient breathing out.

There are valve tenders, who work in the engine-plants. Many of them are barely recognizable as individuals: compact, heat-resistant, with carapace plates that seal when steam rises. They crawl into narrow xylem pipes, tasting the pressure, licking condensation from joints, and adjusting tiny fungal valves with their own breath. Some are so fused with their tasks that they sleep in conduits and dream in rhythm. When a district grows too quiet, one finds them curled around a warm stem, ears of membrane folded tight, listening for the heartbeat of a machine that may have gone sulky.

There are spore librarians, who live among the moth-sheets. In the Underloom, many records are not written. They are accumulated. Fungal layers preserve patterns. Moth dust holds scent-sequences. Membranes can be grown into shapes that, when touched by the right current, replay an old vibration. A spore librarian does not read a book so much as inhale a history. They move through vast hanging panels of sleeping moths, checking for gaps, marking missing seasons with a pinch of mineral dust, and occasionally removing a record that has grown too sharp or too hungry.

The Wefted are not the only citizens. In the Underloom, many buildings are people.

Not all of them. Some structures are simply structures, obedient as a fence or useful as a trough. But many have been cultivated long enough, grafted often enough, and repaired so many times that they possess something like temperament. A bridge may be stubborn. A foundry may be generous or miserly depending on how it was fed. A doorway may close too quickly around strangers if the tree from which it was grown has been insulted by bad pruning. Some walls are so thoroughly incorporated into the civic lattice that they have names, duties, and complaints.

These plant-residents are not silent about themselves. They communicate through sap rhythm, through scent, through the particular way a membrane tightens against the floor. If a district needs less water, the local trees may collectively narrow their pores for a day, and everyone feels the city exhale more slowly. If a building is ill, it may weep bright resin in thin threads, and pruning crews will come with soft hands and low humming to check for blockage, injury, or grief. A marriage between two dwellings is a graft across an avenue; it produces a shared courtyard, a common shade, and sometimes, if the union is good, a new doorway that opens only when rain falls in the right direction.

There are also plant-residents who serve the public more directly. Some municipal trees are grown to think slowly on behalf of the district, their root networks holding legal precedents in chemical memory. Others are grown to regulate weather, releasing or suppressing vapor according to the needs of the surrounding blocks. A drainage vine is not a pipe. It is a creature with a job, and if it is neglected it may take up its duties elsewhere, leaving a plaza to learn what humility feels like.

The Underloom is also populated by animals, though “populated” suggests they are separate from the city. They are not.

In the hollow places, animals are everywhere.

Beetles move across the inner bark in disciplined swarms, broad-shelled and soft-footed, eating fungus, cleaning valves, polishing leaf-glass, scraping old moss from public membranes. They are workers, recyclers, and sometimes, in the nursery districts, pets kept by children in small cups of nutrient gel. Some beetle colonies have taken on civic forms, building themselves into living fixtures: arches made of interlocked carapaces, stools made of fused chitin, lamps made from fungal-backed shells that glow faintly when the right chemical is touched.

Moths live in vast sheets from the ceilings, so many and still that for a while a newcomer mistakes them for wallpaper. But they breathe. And when the city’s air changes, the sheets ripple. Moths are archivists of a kind; their wing dust holds scent, moisture, and pressure-memory. When the wind carries old weather, the moth-sheets tremble with it. If you stand beneath the wrong one after a rain pocket dies, you may catch the smell of another season entirely: mineral coolness, engine oil, green rot, a note of iron like a forgotten district.

Bats thread through the upper levels with seeds in their mouths and messages stitched to their wing membranes with resin thread. Some are couriers. Some are agricultural engineers. Some carry nothing but their own echo, learning the shape of a place by sending small questions into it. They nest in bulb-halls and trunk-halls, and their young are often fostered by the buildings themselves, hanging in loops of vine that keep the temperature steady until the bats can return.

There are blind deer in the moss-halls, moving with an impossible grace that makes you feel, at first, as if they are walking on stillness. They are not shy. They simply have no reason to fear you. Their coats are thick with condensation, their antlers grown into lattice-forms that serve as hanging points for tools, lanterns, and names. Their velvet absorbs spore and scent; when they pass through a district, they leave a soft trail of remembered air. Some are cultivated by the city as lawnmowers, living mops, and slow-moving public works. Others are sacred in a quiet way, because a blind deer that enters a room means that the room has learned to listen.

In the wetter districts, tiny fish live in suspended water bladders, turning endlessly in glassy globes held between vines. These pocket-fish are weather signs, pets, and sometimes delicacies. A sudden restlessness in the bladders may mean a rain pocket is coming. A stillness may mean the district is drying out and needs to be watered by other means. Children grow up learning to read the fish the way other cultures read the sky. Some fish have been bred with membranes so thin they glow from within, their bodies little more than living lanterns. Others are kept as companions by root-writers, who are fond of small things that can sit in their water and remind them that not every decision needs to be made at scale.

The rats live lower down.

They are not exactly pests. They are scavengers, dream-eaters, and sometimes archivists of lost things. In abandoned stems and forgotten conduits, they nest in moss and chew through what the city has stopped attending to: old spore-records, severed grafts, broken tools, names fallen out of use. If a street feels haunted by something it cannot remember, the rats often know more than the walls. They are hunted, and they deserve to be, because left unchecked they will eat a neighborhood’s confidence out from under its foundations.

Predators live here too.

They are not always obvious, because the Underloom’s darkness makes ambush the normal condition of life. There are thin, leggy things that fold themselves into architecture and become doorway for long periods, waiting for passage. There are moths whose dust strips scent from prey, leaving only silence and a faint impression of something missing. There are root-serpents in the underground pipes, patrolling the deep drains, swallowing blockages, and sometimes swallowing citizens who have wandered too far into maintenance tunnels. These predators are not evil. They are civic hygiene given appetite. Where they appear, the city is still moving. Where they vanish, it means something has become soft, stagnant, or too proud to be eaten.

The machines, of course, are also inhabitants.

In the Underloom, a machine is not a dead thing arranged into purpose. It is a living purpose arranged into metal, bark, and tension. The engine-plants are squat and wet, breathing through pores, their flywheels beating like heart chambers in the dark. Their belts are woven from bark. Their pipes carry sap and water and sometimes a kind of current that has no better name than municipal life-blood.

These machines have habits. A loom may refuse a pattern if it is not properly lubricated. A press may sulk if left too long without feeding. A generator may grow skittish in high humidity and slow its pulse until a tender hums through its outer casing and reminds it that the city still needs it. Some factories are gentle and industrious. Others are cruel, not because they hate, but because they were bred or built for relentless function and no one has given them permission to be tired.

There are retired engines, too, standing in overgrown yards with their pistons furred by moss and their valves sealed by mineral bloom. These are not quite dead. They cough when the air changes. They hum faintly at night when the rain pockets shift. Sometimes a new citizen is raised near one, because the old machine’s rhythm teaches patience. Sometimes a district will graft a younger plant onto its rusted housing and use the whole as a boundary marker, a memorial, or a shelter that still knows how to keep the damp at bay.

But even the machines are populated.

Inside their shells live smaller lives: fungal gears coated in slime, beetle-maintainers, soft-bodied organisms that ride the pistons from one chamber to another, seedlings that take root in warm leaks, and motes of condensation that gather in the angles of valve housings like tiny glass animals. Many of these are not animals in any obvious sense. They are processes that have acquired bodies. They are the city’s metabolism, taking up residence in its infrastructure.

The most subtle inhabitants are almost impossible to count.

There are the lens-cells, small translucent things suspended in hanging drops of water, turning when the surrounding plant leans, holding light as if it were a secret. There are vapor-worms, barely more than organized mist, drifting through dry intervals and settling on moss so thin that one could pass through them without noticing. There are scent-moths that live not on wings but on the memory of a smell, carried from place to place by air currents until the original odor fades. There are fungal elders: vast networks of plaque and thread growing across walls, floors, and ceilings, so slow that their thoughts take years to form. They are the nearest thing the Underloom has to a shared mind. When a citizen speaks a chemical phrase into a wall, some of that wall is listening. Some of that wall remembers. Some of that wall will pass the sentence onward to another wall in a district no one has named yet.

These micro-residents are the reason the Underloom feels empty and inhabited at once. The city is not full of bodies. It is full of presence. Its residents include the moss, the rot, the water, the current, the slow decisions of roots, and the quiet hum that is both machine-song and nerve-sound.

Life here has many clocks.

A mayfly-like worker may live out a whole shift between rainfalls. A beetle-brood may serve a single district for a season of decades. A wall may be younger than the street beside it and older than the family that lives behind it. The citizens know this. They build for creatures they will never meet. They plant names in spore-beds that will not bloom for generations. They repair a conduit with the patience of people who understand that some repairs are not for the next hour but for the next century.

This is why individuality in the Underloom is fluid. A citizen may be re-grafted after injury. A limb may be replaced by something grown for the purpose, not quite original but not quite alien. Some bodies, over time, lose most of the matter with which they began and remain themselves only by pattern, scent, and habit. This is not considered tragic. It is considered ordinary. Identity here is less a thing you have than a thing the city continues to agree upon.

Some of them are strange by any standard.

There are citizens who appear as walking bundles of damp cloth and filament, with no face except a shifting arrangement of lights. There are others who seem almost human from behind, if one insists on seeing that way, but turn into something else when they move: a shoulder that is a door, an arm that is a tool, a torso that is a soft furnace for growing things. There are small folk made for crawling through pipes, and tall folk made to reach the upper leaves, and wide folk who serve as living platforms, carrying others across unfinished streets. There are citizens who have no limbs at all and travel by being transported, folded into pouches or suspended on moss-litters, yet still vote in the district’s decisions through chemical signature.

They are not a single species, because they never intended to be one.

They are a settlement of interlocking functions.

In the Underloom, to be alive is not merely to breathe. It is to be woven into the place so thoroughly that the boundary between self and city becomes negotiable. A citizen may enter into a contract with a plant and gain new strength. A plant may enter into a contract with a machine and gain new duties. A machine may enter into a contract with an animal and gain new patience. A street may be alive enough to have opinions about who walks it.

The rain pockets are inhabited by temporary things.

When a pocket forms, it draws in organisms of moisture: small swimming spores, glassy larvae, creatures that live only as long as the drop lasts. They are not considered less real because their lives are brief. They appear, they move, they feed on one another, and then the rain falls, and they vanish with it. Some citizens find comfort in watching them, because they remind the city that even the smallest interval can hold a world.

The dry intervals are inhabited by survivors.

When the Underloom contracts and the membranes thicken to hold moisture, the creatures of dampness retreat into deeper channels, and the air takes on a sharper scent of mineral and old wood. In these times, the city becomes more inward. Doors narrow. Valves slow. The machines sleep fitfully. The animals cluster in the wettest hollows. Even the light grows uncertain, as if the fungal plaques are conserving their glow for later. Yet this too is inhabited. A dry interval is not absence. It is a way of living held at a lower volume.

There are places that are less settled and more feral.

In the outer ridges of black silt and white mineral bloom, where the basin is still drafting itself, the inhabitants are rougher. Growth there is not cultivated so much as argued. Plants fight for space in slow, terrible beauty. Roots break and re-form. Buds become ruins. Animals there have thicker skins, louder scents, and fewer public functions. Some are escaped citizens, or the descendants of citizens who were pruned too much and grew back strangely. Others are wilder organisms from the edges of the basin, slipping in to eat what the city has forgotten how to want.

There are also the dreamers.

These may be the most elusive of all. In the abandoned stems, the rooms with no purpose, the structures growing toward a future no one has authorized, there are bodies that do not wake properly. They are not dead. They are not insane. They are simply waiting for their function to return. Some are overgrown walls dreaming of being houses again. Some are machines whose rhythm never stopped, though their purpose was lost. Some are citizens whose bodies became too much like gardens, too slow, too porous, too full of other life, until they stopped moving and let the moss take their shoulders. The city does not remove them. It tends them. It waters them, prunes around them, listens through them. They are part of the living record. They are what happens when life in the Underloom continues beyond use.

If you ask who lives there, the answer is not a list.

The answer is a condition.

Everything lives there in the sense that everything is implicated. Nothing is merely material. Nothing is merely creature. Nothing is merely machine. The residents include the Wefted, with their many-bodies and chemical voices. They include the plant-citizens, with their moods, marriages, and public duties. They include the beetles, moths, bats, deer, fish, rats, serpents, and folded predators. They include the engines that can be hurt, the tools that can be read, the factories that can be fed, the bridges that can itch. They include the fungi, spores, mosses, vapors, lenses, and brief rain-worlds. They include the forgotten structures breathing in the dark, and the streets that have learned their names from footfall.

The Underloom is inhabited by a civilization that does not separate itself from its place.

There is no clear line between being and infrastructure. There is no clean border between person, plant, animal, and machine. The citizens do not merely live in the city. They are the city’s ongoing agreement. When they move through a corridor, the corridor may decide to widen. When they speak, the moss may carry their words. When they work, the plants work too. When they rest, the machinery rests. When they breed, the city breeds. When they die, they become part of its future decisions.

This is why the place feels so alive even when it feels empty. The emptiness is not absence. It is room. The air is not vacant; it is waiting. The dark is not uninhabited; it is listening. The walls are not still; they are thinking in slower time than the body can easily match.

If you stayed long enough, you would begin to notice them everywhere.

A door closing because it has decided to. A street growing a new branch because too many animals have passed that way and it has learned their route. A pocket of water trembling before the district knows why. A factory’s hum changing when someone is hurt. A ceiling of moth-sheets rippling in unison as old weather passes through. A blind deer stepping into the glow and vanishing between root-walls, leaving only the scent of iron and green in its wake.

And if you stood very still, in one of the broad mossy plazas where the fungal signs mark the corners like small public eyes, you might feel that you are being read.

Not seen. Read.

The Underloom does not look at its inhabitants in the way the surface world looks at life. It knows them by participation. It knows the Wefted by the pressure of their feet, the chemistry of their breath, the light in their tissues, the sound of their bodies negotiating with the air. It knows the plants by growth. It knows the machines by rhythm. It knows the animals by movement. It knows the small ones by the moisture they carry, the spores they leave, and the places they make wet.

Whoever or whatever lives there is part of a vast civic metabolism, a commonwealth made from cooperation, predation, maintenance, memory, and rain. The Underloom is not merely a city. It is a living arrangement. And its inhabitants are not its residents alone.

They are its ways of being alive.
