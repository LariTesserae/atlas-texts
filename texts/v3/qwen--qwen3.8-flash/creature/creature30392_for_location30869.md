There it forms a fine mist, clings to the absorber flutes, gathers into curtains, and falls again in a slow, internal rain. What lives here is built out of this circulation. The cistern is not empty because it lacks life; it is empty because its life is flat.

The first inhabitants are not animals but films. If one looks closely at the black floor plates, at the bronze-tinged tiles, at the pale ceramic bands and the porcelain masonry, one sees that none of them is truly bare. They are varnished by growth. Cyanobacteria spread in translucent skins where moisture sits longest. Mineral-eating microbes consume iron salts from the ironite and exhale a faint glassy bloom. Tiny lichens, pale as ash, colonize the joints between tiles. In the recessed sumps, where dust collects with condensation, there are blooms of orange and violet biofilm that pulse with heat. These are the cistern’s first skin, and they have done such patient work that much of the floor now appears to be patterned not by manufacture alone but by living stain. A stranger might not notice them at all. They are too thin, too slow, too quiet for the roar. They have learned to become part of the structure.

Above them, in the moss curtains, life thickens. The moss is not an accident of construction. It grows only where light and moisture meet with the same rhythm: on the shaded upper faces of the terraces, in the hollows where condensation pools, along channels where mineral water drips at a pace that never quite dries. It feeds on airborne salts, on dust, on the exhalations of the machinery below, and on the faint warmth rising from the floor. It forms drapes and shelves of felted green, some hanging from the walls, some lying across recesses like cloth left behind by a careless weaver. Within those drapes live slower animals. Pale slugs crawl over ceramic surfaces without shells, their mantles dusted with tiny white grains harvested from tile edges. They leave behind trails that seal microscopic cracks and make water bead in new patterns. In the shallow films that rest in the lowest channels, there are slender translucent worms with no eyes and no color, moving in the water as if the current were a memory. They are fed by the microbial varnish. They, in turn, feed larger things.

And then there are the midges.

When a single insect is seen crossing the central air, it is not rare because the chamber is empty. It is rare only because the chamber is so wide that its own swarms become invisible. They live near the upper aperture, where the air is thinnest but the light is steady. They are small, pale things, no larger than a thumbnail, with broad wings that look like mica. Their bodies are almost empty except for a faint pulse of water beneath the carapace. They do not fight for dominance of the air. They ride the slow thermal currents rising from the warm floor, drift in long spirals around the perforated ring, and drink from droplets suspended on the absorber flutes. Their eggs are laid in the upper channels, where condensation is freshest. Their larvae crawl through biofilm. Their adults die by falling onto the tiles and dissolving into the next generation of microbial life. They are not heard. Their wings beat too slowly to make anything the cistern has not already made.

In the deeper channels there are small animals with names like rain-efts, though no outsider ever asked them what they were called. They are perhaps fifteen centimeters long, smooth, almost amphibian, with ribs faintly visible under skin so thin it resembles wet porcelain. They do not swim in pools; they travel in films of water, clinging to tile seams and moving along mineral streaks that glow faintly with algae. Their feet have soft pads that grip ceramic. Their tongues are broad and sticky, used for scraping microbial films. They lay eggs under loose plates, in gaps made intentionally by the cistern-born so that the young may hatch into darkness and crawl toward moisture. They are pale because light reaches them only indirectly, scattered by mist. They are slow because the cistern’s own motion is slow. They live in a place without storms, without seasons as people elsewhere understand them, only cycles of condensation and pressure. They are part of the machinery’s economy as surely as the counterweights and valves, because wherever they crawl they keep the channels from clogging.
