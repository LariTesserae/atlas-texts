No creature with a spine or a wing or a burrow’s narrow ambition lives there. That much is true. If “whoever” means a bounded self with a face, a stomach, a territory, a voice distinct from the voice of its environment, then nothing lives in the high wet plain. But the plain is not empty. It is full of living patterns that refuse to separate into bodies because every body is also a wall, a channel, a membrane, a pressure, a future stain. If the definition of life is widened until it includes anything that persists, absorbs, resists, negotiates, remembers, and changes its surroundings enough to leave evidence, then the place is a teeming country of beings without edges.

The first inhabitant is the Mat.

It is the most obvious and the least understandable. To say it is moss is to say an ocean is “just wetness.” It is not a layer of small plants resting upon soil; it is the soil’s becoming biological, the rock’s becoming patient with growth, the water’s becoming architecture. The Mat has no head because it cannot choose where to notice. Its senses are pressures: a droplet’s weight, a fiber’s pull, a chemical threshold, the slow complaint of stone under the insistence of wetness.
