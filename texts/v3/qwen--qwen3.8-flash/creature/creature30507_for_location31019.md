No one lives there, if one means a single resident with a face, a burrow, a nest, a mark on the ground that says *here is the center of me*. But the phrase *no one* is already too human. The hollow is not unpeopled. It is uncentrally peopled. It is inhabited by a thousand small lives so thoroughly arranged that they begin to seem like one life when viewed from the edge, and by one vast distributed life so ordinary and so complete that none of its parts can be called a ruler, a servant, a maker, or a stranger.

If one comes looking for an animal, one must begin very small.

In the moss along the floor and the lower walls, each tuft is a country. A single cushion of moss, no bigger than a fist, contains more lives than a human hand can hold without disturbing them. The upper leaves of moss shoots are tiny green hillsides to creatures that cannot see them as green. Springtails, pale and quick, jump from one leaf to another when the film of water breaks, as though the whole world has shifted. Mites the color of dust or rusted needles crawl through the felted underside, grazing fungal threads, carrying spores on their backs, dying and disappearing into the moss in a way so gradual that their deaths resemble the softening of color. In the droplets caught at the tips of grass, rotifers open whorls of cilia and feed. Tardigrades, if one were to look with instruments small enough, move through the wet margins like slow weather. Nematodes writhe in the films of water between grains of soil. Protozoa live and divide in places where a human foot would erase a civilization.

For these creatures, a drop of condensation is a lake. The channel of a moss leaf is a river. The edge of a reed is a horizon. A falling droplet is rain, flood, and catastrophe at once. The moss does not merely shelter them. It is their whole world: their forest, their seabed, their architecture, their climate, and the medium through which every life begins and ends. They do not live in the moss as one lives in a house. They live in it as breath lives in a lung, as salt in water, as warmth in a body.

Along the shallow worn channels move beetles and weevils. Some are dull, some have faint armor, some are small as a pinhead but strong enough to carry pieces of seed husk. They run along the damp paths where the moss has been pressed smooth by repeated passage. Their legs make no sound large enough for a human ear, yet in the hollow’s own scale they are heavy animals crossing a stone floor. They eat fallen parts. They carry broken seeds. They enter and leave tiny openings in the moss. They are not random. They are granivores, scavengers, grazers, carriers, decomposers. The seed husks that clatter in the channels have passed through their jaws or their passage. The worn tracks that look as if they were made by a mill have been polished partly by water and partly by the slow traffic of bodies.

The ants are more visible. They march along the root ridges in steady lines, not as if constructing anything, but as if maintaining what already exists. They carry grains of sand, fragments of grass, midge carcasses, tiny pellets of fungal matter. Their bodies are dark and quick. They do not build a colony into the hollow’s walls; the hollow’s structure is already built, and they move through it as though it has always been theirs. They are less architects than accountants. They tally and distribute. A dead leaf is too large for them, but a dead midge is a harvest. A seed casing is a cargo. A patch of sugar film is a sudden wealth. They leave no artifacts, only lines that become easier to walk with time.

Between the reeds, spiders set their threads like slack cables. They are not the large dramatic spiders of gardens or caves, but small ones, often nearly lost against the green. They spin between reed-cables, between stem and leaf, between root and roof. Their silk catches midges, pollen, spores, fragments of dew. Their bodies are tuned to the vibrations of the hollow. When a droplet strikes a leaf nearby, the thread trembles. When a seed husk rolls through a channel, the reeds resonate. A passing shrew sends a pulse through the lower stems. The spider does not hear music, but it knows when the structure of the hollow has altered. It sits at the center of its web as though listening to the weather of the entire room.

The midges hang in drifting columns, and these columns are one of the hollow’s most characteristic presences. They do not seem to be swarming toward anything. They move with the slow draft, rising and falling, visible because ordinary light enters from above and catches their wings. If one watches for a while, the columns seem less like insects and more like breath made visible. They pollinate some of the small flowers hidden in the grass and sedges. They fall into damp pockets, where they are taken in by moss, consumed by spiders, swallowed by frogs, or dissolved into the thin chemical rain that feeds fungal threads. They are not waste. They are circulation.

Moths and butterflies enter through the upper openings when the air outside is warm enough to carry them. They do not belong to the hollow the way the moss belongs to it, but they pass through it as though through a cool chamber where their wings can rest. Some lay eggs on grasses or reeds. Some are drawn to the faint sweetness of moss and fungal mats. Some die inside and are taken into the system so cleanly that they never appear as corpses, only as a slight brightening of the moss, a faint increase in a thread, a small adjustment in the color of nearby growth. Their scales drift through the air and settle on damp leaf surfaces, where they become part of the hollow’s quiet inventory.

In the temporary basins of water, the frogs are among the loudest residents. They are not large frogs. They gather in the brief pools that appear and vanish with the hollow’s internal dampness, not with the ordinary seasons. A frog sits on a moss bank, throat swelling. Its call is blunt and wet, a note that seems to come from the earth’s own breathing. Tadpoles move in the trembling skin of water, dark flickers at the bottom.
