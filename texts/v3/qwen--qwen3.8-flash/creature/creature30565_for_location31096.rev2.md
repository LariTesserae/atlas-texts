What lives there first is people, but people in the basin’s special condition: unfinished people, still learning their shape, as much resident to the valley’s metabolism as to each other’s. They are compact and layered, dressed in wool beneath oilskin, in boots with thick soles that have been tarred against the wet and wrapped in strips of felt to keep out the chill that rises from the ground. Their faces are often pale, not from lack of weather, but because pale light is the weather. Their hands, though, are not pale. They are reddened by cold, darkened by brine, softened by the green undergrowth they spend their days tending, scraping, gathering, and pressing into place. They smell faintly of smoke, salt, metal, and moss. They move quickly when they must, but their quickness is not frantic; it is the quickness of those who know that the ground beneath them may decide, at any moment, to become a little more like a riverbed or a little less like a floor.

They are not loud. Not because they are solemn, but because the basin has taught them what volume cannot survive. Voices here are rounded down. Arguments do not crack; they muffle. Children learn early to run with a kind of sideways carefulness, because the wet clay can be treacherous beneath a step and a house that has just begun to lean does not care to be startled. They speak in soft consonants, in breathy sentences that seem almost to dissolve into steam. A mother calling a child back from a canal bank does so by name and not by command, because the name, once it has found its way through fog and rust and hanging pipes, is easier to locate than a shout.

There are canal people, and they are many. These are the ones who know that a street may still be a street this afternoon, but by the following week may only be a memory painted faintly along the stone. They walk the young channels at dawn and dusk, measuring with marked poles, reading the fresh cuts in silt, noting where water has taken a new interest in a corner of the settlement. They are not judges of the water. They are more like midwives to it. When a lane suddenly becomes a stream, they do not curse; they draw it, name it if it seems likely to last, and then begin the slow work of deciding what must be raised, moved, or accepted. Their ledgers are kept in oilskin covers, and in them one finds not only distances and widths, but omens: *blackbank channel widened at north bend; foundry lane flooded to second riser; house on Clinker Hill now facing a new line.* To an outsider, this would look like accounting. To them, it is poetry written in the present tense, always ready to be amended.

There are moss-farmers, too, and perhaps they are the most central of all the basin’s residents. A child’s first work is often to press a thin mat of moss onto a cooling seam in the outer wall, to pat it smooth and hold it in place until it begins to take. A moss-farmer’s life’s work is to know which greens will quiet a pipe, which will insulate a boiler-house, which will grow too fast into a drain and which will not grow fast enough to stop a crack from widening. They carry small leather pouches of spore powder and damp charcoal. They have spongy hands and forearms scarred with pale lichen burns, and they know the seasons not by festivals but by the texture of the felt they harvest: thin in the first warm damp of spring, thick by the grey-green of late summer, brittle and black-edged when the frost finally returns. They say a city is not soundproofed when the walls are built, but when the walls have learned to breathe without making a noise.

There are wetite surgeons, though they would not use such a grand word for it. They are those who know how to spread the gray paste over cracked foundations, how to mix it with the brine of certain canals, how long to leave it unsealed so that it may drink the damp and become stone again. They do not repair as one repairs a dead thing. They coax. A wall with a fracture is like an animal with a limp: you feed it the right paste, you keep it shaded, you keep the temperature of the moisture steady, and if you are lucky and attentive, it closes slowly, forming a seam stronger in that one place than the whole original surface. Children grow up knowing the difference between a bad crack and a good seam. They learn to press a finger to a wall and say, *this one is still thinking* or *this one has settled into its opinion.*

There are condensation mechanics, the ones who tend the tall brick-and-iron houses where the basin pulls water from the air and sends it through thermal loops. These people are often copper-stained at the knuckles and smell of hot stone and damp mineral. They walk the platforms with small brass taps and paper chips, listening not for the usual hum of machinery but for the absence of it: the wrong stillness, the wrong breath. When an engine cools in the evening and gives off a series of soft ticks, they can tell from the rhythm whether the seals are tightening, loosening, or merely remembering the day. They do not fear these machines. They respect them the way one respects a large animal with a complicated temper. They know that if a condenser vents too suddenly, the fog that comes out will not be dramatic so much as inconvenient; it will chill the hands, dull the iron, and make the walkways dangerously quiet for an hour or two.

There are the eel-keepers, who do not really keep the eels so much as cooperate with them. The silver-throated creatures are everywhere in the young canals, and the people know their ways: where they rest under the warm discharge of a pipe, where they follow the beetles into deeper water, which stretches of silt they prefer after a slump. In the market sheds, the eels are sold salted and smoked, their skins still dark and oily, but in the canal quarter they are understood as citizens of a sort. If an eel-run blocks a grate, it is not removed with fury. It is coaxed by a paddle and a handful of crushed shell, and if it will not go, the people know the channel is changing around it anyway.

There are the otter folk, a smaller and stranger group, because their business is one of trust rather than labor. The river otters that fish in the engineered channels are not fully wild and not fully tame. They come and go, carrying stolen pieces of soap, a button, a fish-head, a bit of wire. A few people have learned which of them can be followed to newly formed pools where the water has run clean into the mud. These followers move with a particular care, stepping as lightly as possible over tar-felt and moss mats, breathing shallow when they approach the storage sheds. The otters tolerate them if they are quiet, if they bring nothing too bright, if they know when to stop. In return, the followers learn where the water has turned, where the city is leaking, and where a new crevice may open in the coming season.

There are also the heron-watchers, mostly older children and retired scribes, who stand at the edges of the reed gardens and count birds not for beauty but because herons are weather-glass in feather. If a heron stands too long near a collapsed culvert, the channel below is likely to fail. If the birds move suddenly from one pond to another, something warm has shifted beneath the water. They are not omens to the people here. They are signals, and in the basin, signal is more reliable than prophecy.

The deer-like grazers of the outer marshes are not pets, but they are known. A few of the terrace farmers know their moods, their favorite routes through the blackfern, and the way their burr-matted coats catch on the drying racks. In bad seasons, when the fog does not lift and the reed roots rot faster than they grow, a family may leave a salted cake of bran near the edge of the garden wall. This is not worship. It is a very old kind of neighborliness. The grazers come because the basin leaves gaps for them, and in return their browsing keeps certain paths open and prevents the young saplings from strangling the lower platforms.

Life here, then, is not simply human. It is crowded, and crowded in a way that feels less like invasion than conversation. Birds nest in the lattice of pipe supports because the structures protect them from the wind and the cats. Beetles work the mineral runoff under the platforms because the runoff is not waste to them but food and route and nursery. Frogs crowd the warm mouths of steam vents because the vents are among the few places the night is not too sharp with cold. Even the rats, hated in other cities, are here regarded with a grudging civic tolerance. They make the foundations settle more evenly. They eat the waste into something the moss can use. A good wall in the basin is not a wall without rats. It is a wall with rats and moss and mineral veins and damp all working out, together, how to remain in place.

And the moss does work. The lichen does work. The pale green felts that cover the boiler cladding are not decoration or neglect. They are infrastructure. They are quiet and insulation and memory and slow repair. A wall that has been allowed to green properly becomes softer to the ear, less likely to vibrate, less likely to crack in sudden temperature changes. A roof covered in the right moss may stop leaking long before a human hand ever touches it, because the moss holds water in the exact way the seam requires. People know this. They tend moss the way other people might tend children: with patience, with pruning, with the acceptance that some parts will grow strangely and still be useful.

So the city’s true inhabitants include things that most people would call merely growing, merely dripping, merely settling. The water itself is not passive here. The people treat it as though it has habits. They speak of the house finding its line, not as a metaphor but as an observation. If a building begins to sweat in a new pattern after a flood, it is not malfunctioning. It is reorganizing. If a channel remains faintly marked in the stone after the stone has moved above it, that is not strange; it is the old slope still being followed, a kind of memory in the ground. The most respected elders are often those who can look at a patch of wall and say, in a matter-of-fact voice, that the house is thirsty on that side.

Their homes, then, are not separate from them. The people live inside a condition that is always slightly becoming. A family may spend the spring reinforcing a terrace with stakes and wetite and the autumn wondering why the kitchen has tilted two inches toward the canal and what to do with the new angle of their doorframe. There is a common saying among the lower basin people: *do not curse the wall that has begun to lean; ask whether it has found a better way to speak to the water.* They do not say this lightly. In a place that shifts by inches, a tilted wall may mean the next slump will go one way instead of another. A house that has always been stubbornly upright may be the first to fall in a wet winter, because it has not learned how to yield.

Their interiors are dense in a way that outsiders find unsettling until they get used to it. Rooms are small and rounded by moisture. Shelves are lined with felt so the plates will not ring too sharply. Windows are thick and wavy, and they turn the view into a soft impressionist smear: a black canal bank becomes a brushstroke; a stack of drying fish becomes a pale blur; a man crossing a bridge looks as though he is already halfway into a dream. Beds are set against the walls with the thickest insulation because the coldest floors are near the center. Food is kept in shallow water basins so it will not freeze too suddenly. Dishes are washed in hot brine that leaves a faint mineral taste on the rim. Nothing is quite clean in the way a city elsewhere would be clean, but nothing is quite unmade either.

The kitchens of the basin smell of eel fat, salted tubers, boiled fern shoots, and the sharpness of struck slate. The steam-fed gardens supply pale roots, oily greens, damp herbs, and fibrous grasses that can be chewed raw when one must travel far and cannot light a fire. In the poorer quarters, people eat reed cakes and drink broth made from boiled bones and mineral-filtered water. In the better quarters, which are not luxurious so much as merely drier, one might find glass-roofed troughs in the interior of a house where pale edible shoots grow in year-round steam. Even in those houses, though, the food has a wet, low taste. Nothing burns. Nothing crackles too loudly. Everything is softened by the same damp that fills the streets.

Their workdays begin in gray light and end in gray-green dusk. There are no dramatic sunrises. There is no theatrical shift from day to night. The basin gives a pale ordinary noon and a slow evening that feels less like an event than a thinning. Workers move with the efficiency of people who do not wish to stand still too long. They know that cold is patient and persistent. They know that wet metal bites, and wet rope chafes, and wet stone can make a boot slip with almost affectionate malice. The forge-workers move slowly and precisely, because the presses are not quick and not silent; they are large, cold, and wet, shaped by pressure rather than flame, and the best forgers are those who can feel the grain of metal through damp gloves and know whether the vibration is beginning to go true.

There are the glass-makers of the blue-clay pits, and their faces are often marked with faint white dust. They know that the veins revealed by a crack in a street corner are a gift and a hazard, because mineral glass made from wet basin clay must be cooled slowly in moss-lined kilns or it will shatter in a dozen small pieces instead of one great one. They speak of their glass the way other people speak of temper. Good glass is not silent, but it does not scream either. It rings soft and short. If it rings long, something in the body of it is still angry.

There are the map-makers, but their maps are almost never fixed. A good map of the basin will show a settlement as it is, and then beside it the waterline of what it was last week, and beneath that a pale trace of where it may be next month. The maps are drawn in mineral ink on treated felt, and they are allowed to be wrong if the world changes. The best map-makers are the ones who listen more than they draw. They are sometimes called line-readers by outsiders, but they dislike the term. They are readers, yes, but not readers of fixed signs. They read movement.

Children grow up knowing this kind of reading. Their games are often quiet games of finding edges: where the moss stops being useful and starts being too thick, where the canal is too shallow to cross safely, where the terrace has shifted since yesterday. They are good at noticing small changes in the tilt of a stair, the angle of a beam, the new dampness in a corner. They can look at a wall and tell whether it is sweating from a pipe, from the ground, or from itself. They know what it means when a puddle has a name on a stone. Some of them grow into canal scribes. Some become wetite surgeons. A few become otter-folk or eel-runners or heron-watchers, and those are respected not because they are exotic but because they know how to live with patience and attention.

The people here do not have the same relationship to ownership that a drier city would have. A house may be yours until it slants too far into the neighbor’s roof. A terrace may be yours until it cracks into three useful pieces and becomes a shared clay quarry. A street may be yours only in name; after a flood, the river will decide otherwise. This does not make them lawless. It makes them unusually attentive to the law of place. When the ground shifts, people do not simply argue; they come together to ask what the ground has begun to mean for them. There are meetings on balconies, in the low hush of rain on thick glass, in the steam gardens, in the reed-market, where people speak in calm, practical voices about which foundations need feeding, which channels need redirecting, which terraces must be braced, which ones must be let go.

They are not sentimental, but they are deeply attached to process. A woman will say of a wall that it is *good now*, not because it has finished, but because it has stopped arguing. A man will say of a bridge that it is *settling into its mind*, not because it is perfect, but because it has found a way to bear its weight in the wet. This is why the place feels so strange to a visitor: because it has almost no final form. It is not unfinished in the sense of neglect. It is unfinished in the sense of life.

The people’s art is of a kind too. There is no loud music here. The nearest thing to music is the soft percussion of tools in felt-lined market sheds, the muffled rhythm of water through moss-packed channels, the slow creak of a crane lifting stone through mist, and the thin high whistles of heated metal as it cools. If they make songs, they sing them quietly, in breathy chords, in the engine-houses where the air is warm enough to let the voice travel without fogging. The songs are not grand anthems. They are often maps in melody: a line to follow, a warning about a spring, a lament for a house that leaned too far. When an otter-follower returns to the sheds after a long walk, he may say only that the water has moved, and then sing two notes that mean something different depending on the season.

Their religion, if one can call it that, is closer to attention than worship. There are no shrines to the river because everyone knows the river is already present and will not be pleased by a small stone image. There are no altars to moss because moss is everywhere and will grow whether praised or ignored. But there are rituals of recognition. When a new spring appears in a wall, people touch the wet plaster and say *the house finds its line*, and then they step back to watch, not to interfere immediately, because they have learned that the first path is sometimes the truest. When a bridge shifts and holds, a worker may press his palm to the cable housing and listen, and if he nods, everyone knows it is done for now. Grief too is muffled. When a house collapses into a reed bed, there is no dramatic keening. There is a pause, a counting of names, and then a slow conversation about whether the family should live on another terrace for the winter and whether the river should be allowed its new claim.

This is not because they do not care. It is because in a place so wet and so dense, sharp feelings can still be sharp, but they need somewhere to go. The basin absorbs sound, but it does not absorb meaning. People simply know how to let meaning be quiet until the next drip comes.

What lives here, then, is not a population so much as a network of residents. Some of them have legs. Some of them have roots. Some of them have only the faint memory of a groove in stone, and yet that groove is still followed by water as though it were alive. The people are part of that life. They are not masters of the basin. They are its most articulate residents, but not the only ones. The birds are articulate in a different way, the eels in another, the moss in a stillness so complete it is almost a kind of speech.

And the basin itself lives there. That is the deepest truth of it. Not as a monster, not as a deity, but as a place whose ordinary processes have taken on the habit of conversation. Water talks to metal. Metal talks to stone. Stone talks to wetite. Wetite talks to the ground. The ground talks to the houses. The houses talk to the people. The people talk back. Everyone is always slightly answering someone else, even when they think they are just walking to market.

So if you asked what lives in that low, cold, industrial, green, damp, quiet, turbulent place, the answer would not be simple. It would not be “humans” or “animals” or “machines” or “moss.” It would be: all of them, at once, in a wet and patient negotiation. It would be: the canal-scribe and the eel, the moss-farmer and the wall, the child and the leak, the worker and the breathing engine, the bridge and the new line the river has found beneath it. It would be: the place itself, still assembling, still becoming, still learning how to keep its voice down.

That is who lives there. Not conquerors. Not caretakers. Not owners. Residents.

And because the place is still unfinished, so are they.
