What lives there cannot be shown as a village, a species, or even a single image. It must be shown as a series of presences, one after another, emerging from the stone: first the living film on the basalt, then the green pockets, then the small restless creatures, then the herds, then the people. If a visitor entered the shelf and looked for inhabitants in the ordinary way—searching for houses, roads, fields, towns—they would conclude that the place was empty. But the emptiness is not absence. It is the shape the life has learned to take in order to survive here.

The first life is not obvious. It is nearly invisible.

Over the black-gray stone, especially where moisture lingers, there grows a thin biological crust: a living film of cyanobacteria, fungi, mineral-depositing microbes, algal remnants, and dust-bound cells that has become, in effect, a skin for the plateau. In dry light, it looks like soot, ash, or weathered iron. After a rain, or when condensation beads along a basalt edge, the crust darkens and gives off a faint sharp smell—warm stone, diluted salt, and something greener. This crust holds water in its pores, feeds on mineral dust, and binds the surface just enough to keep the flour-fine ground from blowing away in the first strong gust. It is where many small lives begin.

Beneath it and through it spread pale threads and tiny bodies: springtails no larger than grains of dust, mites that move in microscopic rivers through moss cushions, bacteria that thrive in warm mineral water, dormant spores that can wait out long dry stretches in the cracks. Some of the salt-ringed basins support crystalline microbial mats that bloom after rain and shrink again as the water vanishes. These mats are not flowers or forests; they are more like living geometry, expanding across wet stone in films so thin they might pass for reflections. When they dry, they become part of the crust again, feeding the next generation with trapped water and minerals.

This is life tuned to intermittency.

Nothing here depends on continuity. A stream would die. A river would be strange. A pool that lasts too long may become dangerous, because something else will move into it. Instead, the plateau supports creatures and plants that live by bursts: condensation at dawn, steam after pressure shifts, rain that comes only in one place and not in another, pools that appear and vanish like breath.

In the shaded fissures, the first more visible life begins. Mosses grow in cushions so dense they look like dark green wool pressed into the stone. They are not gentle mosses; their leaves are thick, waxy, and closely packed. When dry, the cushion shrinks and turns a dull gray-green, seeming almost dead. When steam passes over it, or rain beads on its surface, it swells with water and darkens. A handful of this moss can hold water like a sponge. If squeezed carefully, it releases a cool trickle. The people who live here have learned that not all water must be gathered from pools. Sometimes it can be wrung from the stone itself.

Around the moss, lichen makes broad, embroidered maps. Some lichens are pale as bone, others iron-brown or sulfurous green. They creep across tilted basalt plates and old boulders, filling in the rounded scars left by ancient water. Where they are thick, they form a surface almost like leather, trapping moisture and dust. In some places, a patch of lichen has been polished by wind and footfall until it feels smooth as cloth. In others, it is broken by tiny pores and grains of stone, alive but so slowly that it seems to belong to the geology rather than to biology.

There are ferns, too, but not tall or decorative. They grow in cracks where steam softens the air, their fronds curled tight when dry, unfurling only when the vent above them releases its breath. These ferns are low, thick-stemmed, and wary. Their young leaves are covered in fine hairs that trap mineral dust. If the steam stops for too long, they do not die at once; they shrink, fold, and darken, becoming part of the shadowed crack again. When moisture returns, they unroll within hours, as if they had never left.

The flowering plants are stranger. Small, tough, low-growing shrubs and herbs appear only in protected pockets. Some have silver hairs over their stems and leaves, reflecting the strong high sun. Others fold their leaves at the joints, turning reddish or blackened at the edges, as if burned. Their flowers open briefly when the light and humidity are right, then shut before the wind can tear them. One kind of basin plant bears tiny white blossoms that last only a few hours in the morning. When the air is still and the ground warm, its scent is faintly sweet. If the plateau begins to roar, the petals collapse inward so quickly that the plant seems to have withdrawn into stone.

These green pockets are not separate from the land’s behavior. They depend on its turbulence. Steam supplies warmth and moisture. Wind supplies disturbance, killing weak growth but carrying spores. Fissures give shelter and drainage. Condensation supplies drink to creatures that could never reach open water. The plants live in the intervals between the rock’s moods.

There is even a kind of moss whose spore capsules burst only after a specific vibration passes through the stone. The wind over the shelf produces many sounds, but certain gusts, when they cross a narrow crack, create a clear, trembling note. The capsules respond not to wind itself, but to the note the stone is making. At the right sound, the moss releases dust-thin spores into the air. This is not intelligence, exactly. It is something older: a rhythm. The moss has learned the plateau’s voice as an animal learns the rhythm of its own heartbeat.

The fungi follow the same logic. In the shallow basins, after rain and before evaporation, pale mushrooms may rise from the damp crust: thin stems, cracked caps, gills that darken with age. Some appear singly, like small stones; others grow in rings or arcs, following mineral deposits. They are called, by those who know them, drumcaps, because their spores release most readily when the ground is vibrating. A nearby vent hissing, a pebble striking another in the wind, a distant crack shifting under pressure—these sounds disturb the delicate caps. The fungus fruits when the land speaks loudly enough.

Micro-ecosystems form in unlikely places. In basins where droppings have accumulated from rare animals, moss and fungi grow thick. On high ledges where wind shelters a flat surface, pale lichens hold moisture enough for tiny invertebrates. In the mouth of a crack, a dark smear of algae may appear after rain, then vanish. Where a fallen boulder blocks the wind for only a few hours each day, a patch of life can survive behind it, rooted in the small shadow it casts. These are not landscapes of plenty. They are landscapes of exceptions.

Animal life is thin but specific.

The insects are more numerous than the vertebrates, though not numerous in any crowded sense. Pale flies hover near condensation. Beetles with bright shells move through mineral films, their armor catching light in a way that seems almost metallic. These bright shells may be warnings, or may simply be minerals taken in by the beetles; in a place with so little vegetation, a beetle’s body may become part of the geology it touches.

There are tiny gnats that swarm briefly over newly formed pools. Their eggs lie dormant in the dry dust of vanished hollows, waiting for the next breath of water. When rain falls or steam condenses into a shallow basin, the crust softens and the eggs hatch. The larvae feed on microbial films and mineral slime. The water evaporates within days. The larvae pupate or die. The next generation begins again when the plateau offers another brief drink. Some of these insects have no relationship to continuous water at all. Their entire lives depend on the land’s irregular moistening.

There are also creatures that seem almost to serve the plateau’s music. Small beetles and weevils live under tilted basalt plates, tapping their bodies against stone in short rhythmic bursts. This is not song for human ears, but it is a kind of communication: mating calls, alarm signals, territorial markers.
