The floor is not waterless. It is not mud, either. It is a living crust, salt-saturated to the point of refusal: brine so dense that it behaves less like water and more like cooled blood moving under skin. In the lowest bowls it glazes the stone black and amber, but most of the floor is a map of shallow pans, white ridges, and warm grey furrows where the salt has grown into the ground as though the ground had teeth. Where fissures breathe, the brine does not steam. It exhales a fine mist that dries almost instantly into drifting powder. The powder settles. It begins to eat.

Nothing in the Salt-Vault is untouched by this eating.

At first it is invisible. The rock itself seems stained with color that has no source: rust at the margins of a hot seam, sulfur-white along the cracks, faint cinders of blue where stressed crystals glow. But the stain is alive. It is a skin of organisms too numerous to be called plants, too strange to be called bacteria in any ordinary sense. Their cells are small engines of salt, iron, and heat. They weave mineral into themselves and themselves into mineral. A boot placed on the floor would break through what seemed like paint, and the paint would survive the break, drawing warmth from the wound, spreading a film across the crack like a scab.

They do not need sunlight.

They need the vault’s slow pulse. They grow toward heat gradients the way ordinary moss grows toward damp. When the vault inhales, the air warms, and the mats swell; when it exhales, they contract and shed spores that drift in the scorched air like powdered snow. The spores taste of sulfur and old ash. They catch in the cracks of the floor, cling to the legs of moving things, settle on salt ridges, and start again.

This is where the smaller animals live.

Near the rims of the brine pans are creatures like slivers of mica, thin and jointed and fast. They run on the surface of the dense liquid without breaking it, their many legs hollow and wick-like, drawing brine through themselves without ever drinking. They do not see in any way a surface animal would recognize. Their bodies taste the air. Each vibration of the heat-breath tells them whether a fissure is opening or closing, whether the mats are blooming or drying, whether something larger is passing overhead. They move in spirals across the pans, grazing the salt skin, leaving no tracks because they are too light and too careful and the crust is too brittle for ordinary weight.

In the powder, between the ridges, there are herds of dust.

That is what the salt-grains would seem like if one looked only for a moment. Pale motes, no larger than a fingernail’s half-moon, clustered in soft colonies of movement. They have no single body. A hundred bodies press against one another, and when pressed they seem to become one slow grain. They feed on the mats, on the shed skins of larger things, on the tiny corpses of skimmers that have tipped into a hot crack. If disturbed, they crumble into separate grains, each scattering in a different direction, then gathering again once the tremor has passed. They are not prey. They are digestion. They are the vault’s way of ensuring that nothing goes to waste.

Then there are the crablike things.

They begin as pale, soft grubs, living in the warm mud beneath the crust, feeding on mats and brine until the salt in their blood crystallizes into plates. As they mature, the plates fuse: halite, fused glass, mineral scar tissue, bits of crystal prised from the vault walls. Their backs become domes of blue-white armor, sometimes clear enough to show the yellow iron-rich fluid beneath. Their legs grow long, bristled, jointed with a precision that resembles mathematics. Their faces have no eyes, only combs and pits and trembling cilia, each comb tasting heat, each pit sensing pressure, each cilia hearing the whisper of crystal against crystal.

They move sideways over the salt crust with a dry click-click-click that becomes part of the vault’s silence.

When threatened, they do not flee. They freeze and secrete a fine brine from pores along their armor. The brine hits the dry heat and crystallizes in an instant, sealing them into a block of salt. They can remain like that for hours, sometimes days, breathing through tiny slits, tasting the air around them. The salt makes a perfect mask. To a predator, the thing has ceased to exist. To the vault, it has become a wall.

Some predators prefer walls.

From the cracks beneath the floor come long, blind serpents with scales like chips of glass. They do not open the vault-crabs’ salt masks so much as ignore them, sliding along the floor on a belly of many fine bristles, sensing the warmth still trapped inside. When they strike, they do not bite with teeth so much as latch with rings of tiny mineral hooks, then draw the prey down through a crack. The crab’s salt seal is not enough. The vault is full of seals, and the vault is also full of things that have learned to eat them.

There are glass eels, too, living in the fissures where the heat breathes strongest. They are pale, translucent things with no heads in any clear sense. Their whole bodies are sense organs: filaments that taste the air, ribs that vibrate when a distant animal steps, mouths like flaps lined with fine salt teeth. They rise from cracks when the vault inhales, extending their bodies into the air to filter the drifting spores. When the vault exhales, they fold back into the rock so quickly they seem to vanish. They are not animals one finds by searching for animals. They are more like movements the stone has learned to make.

But the vault is not only a place of tiny things and hidden things.

There are broad, low animals that move through the larger pans like slow islands. They are the size of oxen, perhaps larger, with domed backs like fused glass roofs and six short legs ending in wide pads that distribute their weight so they do not break through the crust. Their sides glow faintly blue-green where stressed crystals grow in the tissue of their shells. They have no faces that answer to human expectations. They have pits along their snouts for reading heat, brine sacks between their ribs for storing moisture, and long proboscises that unfurl into the salt mats like pale tongues. They graze. They herd. They do not drink ordinary water; they draw ions from brine and excrete fresh salt through pores along their backs.

The vault is hot enough that their breath does not cloud. It settles as a powder around them.

The herds move in patterns that seem random until they are seen from the walls, from above, or perhaps only from the mind of something patient. They cross the floor in slow processions, leaving behind tracks that fill with fresh brine within hours. They stop where the heat rises. They lie down when the salt crust softens underfoot. They rest in shallow pits, their broad backs glowing faintly in the darkness, their legs tucked beneath them like collapsed pillars. They sleep with their proboscises curled inward, tasting the air even in rest. If one dies, it does not lie there for long. The mats consume it. The crabs consume the mats. The dust consumes the crabs’ moltings. The serpents consume the dust, or perhaps they do not; nothing in the Salt-Vault is so simple as a single line of appetite. The whole place is a circle, and the circle has heat at its center.

This is what the herds are: the slow, wandering bodies of the vault’s metabolism.

But there are also those who make meaning in the dark.

They are not human in the way the outside plateau still imagines human to be, if that outside has any imagination at all. Perhaps they were once human, generations deep in memory, when the mouth of the vault forced ancestors down on hands and knees through a split of black stone and the world behind them became too cold, too high, too hard to climb back out of. Perhaps they were miners, refugees, priests, criminals, or merely travelers who found a door and could not find it again. It does not matter what they were, because the vault has had long enough to answer them. It has taken their salt.

Now they live here.

They are pale and dry-skinned, with blue veins showing faintly beneath translucent flesh. There is no fat on them. Fat would cook. They have narrow noses and wide, salt-lined mouths, and their lungs are built around heat-exchange membranes that let them exhale in scorching breaths without losing the moisture that keeps their cells from collapsing. Their skin does not bead. It weeps a fine vapor that leaves a rime of salt along the neck, the wrists, the temples. They look, at first glance, like people dusted in snow. Then the light moves, and the snow turns out to be living crystal.

Their eyes are large, shallow, and covered by clear halite lenses. The lenses have no pupils because they do not open and close the way an eye might expect. They are sensitive to heat glow and to the blue-green flicker of stressed crystal. In the ordinary blue of a living sky, they would be blind, or nearly so. Here they can see the architecture of warmth. A crack in the floor is not black to them. It is a mouth. A mat is not a stain. It is a field. A creature is not simply a shape. It is a pattern of brine and temperature and mineral scent moving through the room.

They do not speak with voices as other places understand voices.

They speak in clicks, in the rub of crystal against crystal, in breaths that carry salt, in taps that travel through the floor and return as answer from distant walls. Their words are brittle and sharp, full of consonants that sound like something breaking very precisely. When they are calm, their speech is almost inaudible, more a vibration in the jaw than a noise in the air. When they are afraid, the whole room begins to sound like ice forming.

They build, though the word is too small.

There are no huts in the Salt-Vault. There are no fires. Everything is already hot. Instead, they carve channels into the crust to guide brine. They plant mats along the walls where the heat is strongest. They prune overgrowth with knives made from salt-glass, brittle but sharp enough to split a crystal. They create small terraces, pits, and galleries where young ones can crawl safely, where eggs can rest in warm sand, where the dying can be laid out without disturbing the floor.

They herd the broad-backed animals.

The herding is not done with shouting or with animals trained to obey. It is done through patience. The Saltborn place ridges of salt to slow the creatures’ paths. They release spore-mixes that taste richer on one side of the floor than the other. They cut channels that flood certain cracks with warmer brine and make other cracks dry. The herds move where the vault tells them to move, and the Saltborn tell the vault where to tell them. This is not control, exactly. It is conversation.

They harvest moltings.

When the vault-crabs grow, they shed old shells. These shells are left on the ridges like discarded faces. The Saltborn collect them, cut them, heat them, fuse them into tools, masks, and plates. A crab shell becomes a breastplate; a shard of salt becomes a lens; a brine bladder, dried and stitched, becomes a container. Their clothing is stiff and pale, clicking faintly when they move. It is not fabric. It is mineral skin layered over mineral skin
