What lives in the Basin That Does Not Drain cannot be inventoried as if the basin were a room and its creatures furniture. There are no owners, no households, no dens, no territories, no marks of habitation. If one must answer the question plainly, the answer is this: the basin itself is the inhabitant, and everything that moves within it is a node of its wet, breathing, unremembering body. The creatures there are not beings who happen to live in the basin; they are processes of the basin taking shape as skin, wing, call, film, throat, leg, leaf-water, and trembling sound.

Even so, shapes can be named.

The first and most pervasive inhabitants are not animals at all, or not animals in any ordinary sense. The pale stone is coated in living films so thin they look painted: black-green algae, blue-green bacterial veils, rust-colored mineral stains, microscopic threads of fungal growth. These are the basin’s true surface. They grow across tufa rims like delicate breathing skins. They collect in the pores of nascent stone. They line the hollows of pebbles filled with warm water. They spread over damp flats in fractal mats. They are never bare for long, and they are never thick enough to become soil, never crusty enough to become permanent rock. They exist as a living gloss.

Within these films swim and crawl countless minute things: transparent grains that drift in capillary water, ciliate-like bodies no larger than pinheads, tiny worms with no obvious head, slow blinking organisms that may be animal, plant, or merely the basin’s mineral life momentarily confused by observation. These micro-inhabitants are eaten, dissolved, reborn, and reassembled without decay. They leave no skeleton, no shell fragment, no dark stain. The stones draw them in and exhale them again as though moisture itself were carrying them through the basin’s long, wet breath.

The pools are also inhabited, not only at the bottom or in the water column but at the surface itself. Each shallow pool has a living skin: a membrane of algae, protists, and microscopic larvae so dense in places that the water seems to hold itself together. This skin is not a film of pollution; it is a community. It breathes through trembling. It gathers dissolved minerals. It catches spores, wing-hairs, and drifting droplets. Small transparent organisms ride it on legs too fine to break the tension. Others move beneath it like shadows under glass. The surface is not truly still; it is held still by the mutual pressure of countless lives.

The leaf-bowls are worlds. Where broad low plants cup their leaves into small bowls, the basins within them contain pools no deeper than a fingertip, yet they hold translucent eggs, tiny swimming bodies, and minute larvae. These are the basin’s most complete inhabitants: complete ecosystems contained in a single leaf. Some of the eggs are too clear to be seen except where light bends around them. The swimming forms inside are not quite tadpoles, not quite nematodes, not quite ciliated spores; they are living water that has learned to move. They mature, disperse, disappear, and return without ever making the leaf heavy. The leaf does not sag under them. It supports them as though they were droplets and droplets as though they were leaves.

Moss cushions are not merely ground cover. They are dense, sponge-like habitats with warm internal air pockets and channels of condensation. Inside a single moss mound one may find glassy snails, springtail-like grains, slow threadlike larvae, and small soft-bodied forms that move through the cushion without disturbing its surface. The moss breathes. It holds water so completely that it appears to be a solid made of wetness. Its green mass is porous enough to shelter eggs, damp enough to support fungal threads, warm enough to encourage tiny organisms that could not live in open air. If a snail crosses it, the moss closes after it like a curtain of water. If an animal rests on it, the moss accepts the weight and returns to its own shape the moment the weight is gone.

Among these moss-dwellers are the veil-snails. Their shells are almost transparent, thin mineral films through which one can see a dark threadlike body moving. They are slow processions rather than a species in the ordinary sense: they travel in lines along condensation tracks, across dripping leaf edges, along the rims of small pools. Their slime does not remain. It is reabsorbed almost immediately by the same moisture they ride upon. They feed on the living films of stone, not by scraping them away but by drinking through them, as though their mouths were made of water and their hunger a gentle osmotic pull. They are not eaten because they leave no remains, but they are present in exact numbers, and their shells sometimes host small colonies of algae that look like green hair growing from clear glass.

There are also thread-walkers: soft, many-legged things that hang beneath curled leaves. They are not insects in a clean sense; their bodies are segmented but damp, almost like folded leaf-tissue given legs. They hang under the fronds, collecting condensation, dripping warm water back into the pools below. Their legs move without hurry, and where they rest, the leaf does not mark. They seem to participate in the basin’s water cycle more than in its food web. Their bodies may be half plant, half animal, or merely so saturated with moisture that the distinction has lost its meaning. They drip. The leaf holds them. The pool accepts their drops. Nothing accumulates.

The air is inhabited too. The basin is full of tiny flying things that look like dust when stationary and become sound when moving. One might call them glosswings, though that would make them seem more definite than they are. They are winged bodies no larger than eyelashes, with wings so thin and wet they never seem dry enough to flutter. They rest in clusters on leaves, wings folded tight and slick. When they rise, they do not scatter; they form a suspension. They hover in clouds over the pools, over the moss ridges, over the mouths of the calling animals. Their trilling is not the sound of wind, because there is little wind; it is the sound of countless small wings beating through humid air that offers resistance like warm water.

These flying inhabitants feed on films, spores, dissolved sugar-scented minerals, and the microscopic life suspended in leaf-bowls. They carry moisture from one surface to another. Their deaths are not deaths as one normally imagines them: there are no fallen bodies, no piles of chitin, no dust on the stones. They dissolve into the same damp air, or are consumed so completely that the act leaves no trace. Their numbers remain constant. The cloud is not expanding. The swarm is not starving. It is simply there, as a condition of the warm breath rising from below.

The most obvious inhabitants are the pale, broad-mouthed creatures that sit on wet stones in dense rows. These are the chorus of the basin, though calling them frogs would be too simple. They are throat-animals, or pulse-sitters, or basin-throats. Their bodies are low, wide, and damp. Their skin looks soft and porous, like water that has learned to hold a shape. Their mouths are broad and slightly upturned, as though made not for biting but for swallowing sound. Their throats pulse in visible rhythms, expanding like wet balloons filled with warm water and air. Their eyes are shallow and glassy, set low in flat heads, seeing little by ordinary light and reading the basin instead by vibration, moisture, and thermal gradient.

They do not sit in rows because of family, territory, or hierarchy. They sit because the basin’s acoustic field is arranged like an instrument. Each stone rim, each moss cushion, each pool edge has a tone. When the animals occupy those positions, their calls lock into one another. The result is not song so much as atmosphere made audible. Some throat-animals have hollow, bone-like vocal chambers and produce calls like air forced through soft caves. Others have rasping skin-ridges that rub together underwater or against mineral surfaces, producing stone-rasp sounds. Some have thin, iridescent plates around their mouths that vibrate at a metallic pitch, a trill that one feels in the teeth before hearing with the ears. Together they make a sound without beginning or end.

These animals reproduce without nesting. Leaf-bowls always contain their translucent eggs. Moss cushions always contain their larvae. Shallow pools always hold their swimming young. There is no season of birth because the basin has no season. Adults and juveniles occupy the same warm present. They do not age into death in a way that leaves remains. If one disappears, another appears in its place with the same weight, the same wetness, the same pulse. This is not resurrection and not replacement; it is persistence. The population is both enormous and exact.

They feed on the glosswings, on tiny larvae, on the living films of stone, on eggs in leaf-bowls, and perhaps on one another, but feeding there does not look like violence. They open their broad mouths and draw in a small wet sound. The prey vanishes without blood, without struggle, without waste. The basin takes the transfer back into its equilibrium. A throat-animal swallows a larva; the pool trembles a fraction differently; the light bends through another film. The act is completed so softly that it is barely an act.

Above the throat-animals and below the moss are the lichen-backed waiters. These are small reptilian forms, but the word reptile is almost too dry for them. Their bodies are flattened and broad, with legs tucked wide like the feet of a thing grown from stone. Their skin is patterned as if by lichen: pale green, chalk-white, warm gray, brownish rust. They sit among the mineral crusts and look like part of the tufa. They do not blink often. They do not move with urgency. When they shift from one rock to another, it happens between one glance and the next, without scraping the stone, without disturbing the moss, without leaving a mark where they were.

The lichen-backed waiters are predators of the basin-throats. They take them with a sudden, almost ceremonial slowness: mouth opening, pale body engulfing another pale body, then returning to stillness. There is no chase. The throat-animals call near the waiters for hours, perhaps because the calls themselves are necessary to the basin and cannot be suppressed by fear. There is no fear as we would understand it, or perhaps no consequence that fear could prevent. The waiters eat. The waiters digest. Their skin becomes more greenish, more chalky, more like the stone beneath them. They do not build nests. They do not hoard prey. They do not mark stones. Their presence is a pause in the mineral landscape.

Among the waiters’ kin, or perhaps their opposite, is the tension-strider. This is a small, lizard-like form with flattened, translucent toes and a body so light that it seems to run on water without touching it. Its legs are long and jointed in a way that distributes pressure like the spread of leaf-hairs. Its tail is thin and leaflike, balancing it above the pool surface. When it runs across a pool, the surface does not wake into ripples. A momentary dimpling appears, and then the water forgets it. It feeds on glosswings at the surface and on tiny organisms trapped in the pool-skin. It can rest on water as a droplet rests on waxed leaf. It is one of the basin’s proof that nothing there need sink merely to exist.

The largest inhabitants are the moss-ridge waders. They stand in the open shallows with bodies long and low, legs wide-set, backs covered by a ridge of green growth that looks at first like another creature living upon them. In a way, it is. Their ridges are living gardens: mosses, liverworts, algae, small fern-like filaments, and creeping mineral films grow along their backs, anchored to their damp skin. The ridge is not a burden; it is an extension of their bodies, a photosynthetic crest that breathes with them and releases a faint scent of warm chalk and wet leaf into the vapor above the pool.

These waders are not herds. They are not families. They are more like moving low islands, spaced apart by distances that seem chosen by the moisture itself. Their feet are broad, soft, and webbed in the sense that water clings between their digits; they do not trample. Their legs are not dry or hoofed or feathered; they are thick, rounded, and always glistening. They stand so still that the water at their legs becomes a lens, reflecting the pale sky and the green mounds around them. If one moves, it does not step as though crossing land; it shifts as though the land itself is rearranging a single fold.

Their backs host small ecosystems. Veil-snails travel along their moss ridges. Glosswings rest in clusters between their spines. Tiny eggs may settle in the damp hollows behind their shoulders. Small larvae swim in the water clinging to their skin after they have stood in a pool for a long time. A single wader is a portable basin within the basin. Its body does not merely live in the ecosystem; it carries it.

The waders feed without violence. They dip broad, flat heads into the shallows and draw in films, larvae, and drifting organisms, their throats expanding like the throat-animals but slower, with a deeper sound. They also seem to feed through the green ridges on their backs, absorbing warmth and light and perhaps the minerals dissolved in the films that grow there. Their digestion is invisible. Their waste does not foul the water; it becomes part of the mineral balance of the pool, a thin brightening, a slight change in the way light passes through. When they stand, the water seems to give up more light. This is not metaphor. The pool appears altered by their breathing, as though they are slow instruments played by the basin’s heat.

Their eyes are not the eyes of predators, or at least not of hunters. They do not scan for movement. Their vision is diffuse, suited to a world without shadows. They perceive heat, pressure, the taste of humidity, the vibration of the chorus, the subtle shifts of light through suspended mist. If a throat-animal falls silent, a wader turns its head a fraction. If a glosswing cloud thins above a leaf-bowl, its moss-ridge trembles. If the stone exhales a little more vapor, it lowers its broad body into the pool with the patience of something already home.

These waders do not build platforms. They do not trample trails. They do not scrape the ground. They do not carry nests, stones, mud, feathers, or sticks. Their bodies are low enough to avoid disturbing the vegetation; their feet wide enough to avoid sinking; their skin wet enough to prevent drying; their rhythms slow enough to leave no permanent mark. If one stands motionless beside a pool for long enough, moss may begin to grow along the edges of its skin, not as an injury but as an expression of the same damp equilibrium.

What is most disturbing, and most beautiful, is that none of these creatures seems to know it is there. They do not look as though they have selves. They do not appear anxious, curious, aggressive, territorial, lonely, bonded, mourning, or anticipating. They do not seem to remember. They do not seem to forget, either, because forgetting requires memory to have left. Their lives are actions without accumulation. The throat-animal calls, and the call does not become a language. The wader steps, and the step does not become a trail. The glosswing rests, and rest does not become sleep in the way we know it. The veil-snail crosses moss, and moss does not wear a groove. The lichen-backed waiter takes prey, and the prey does not become a scar in the web.

The basin’s inhabitants are, therefore, beings without histories. They have biology without biography. They have bodies without ruins. They have reproduction without inheritance. If one followed a throat-animal’s call, one would not find a nest. If one followed a wader’s track, one would not find a destination. If one followed a thread-walker’s drip, one would not find a source. Every movement returns to the same abundance. Every presence is exact. Every disappearance is already replaced.

Their ecology is layered in the way a dense membrane is layered. The stones are not inert; they are substrates, skin, lungs, and nurseries. The mosses are not plants alone; they are habitats, sponges, air pockets, and breeding waters. The leaves are not leaves alone; they are basins within basins, holding eggs and larvae and tiny films of mineral life. The pools are not pools; they are living surfaces with membranes of organisms that tremble in response to sound and heat. The air is not air; it is a suspension of wings, spores, droplets, breath, and warmth that behaves almost like a slow liquid.

If you try to divide the inhabitants into plant and animal, stone and flesh, water and skin, the categories begin to fail. That failure is not mystical. It is physical. Everything is so saturated that boundaries blur. A pale mineral dome may look like an egg. A curled fern tip may look like an insect’s folded wing. A moss cushion may look like fur. A small pale throat-animal may look like a piece of wet paper. A lichen-backed waiter may look like a growth of stone until it breathes. A wader’s moss ridge may look like a hillside until it blinks. The basin’s inhabitants do not merely resemble their surroundings; they are made of the same continuous wet substance as their surroundings.

There is no single species that dominates. There is no keystone animal that builds, digs, clears, or fertilizes in the ordinary way. The closest thing to a keystone is the chorus itself, the sound made by all the throat-animals and insects and slow-breathing waders together. The sound does not communicate in the sense of conveying individual intentions. It regulates. It moves the warm air. It trembles the leaf-bowls. It agitates the surface films. It carries chemical scent through the humid atmosphere. It may influence the behavior of the animals as breath influences flame. The throat-animal that stops calling is not silenced; another throat begins the same note elsewhere. The chord continues because the basin requires its fullness.

The animals have no shelters. They have resting places, but no structures. They have microclimates, but no homes. They have positions, but no territories. A throat-animal may sit on a stone for what appears to be hours, but when it moves, another throat-animal occupies the exact thermal and acoustic conditions the first requires, even if it is not the same body. A wader may stand in one patch of shallow water so long that it seems rooted to the place, but it does not claim the place. When it moves, the place does not become empty in any meaningful way. It becomes a different arrangement of the same living fullness.

Reproduction, too, is without ceremony. There are no mating dances, no courtship territories, no egg-guarding, no nursery sites, no juvenile stages that change the adult world. The larvae are already present in the pool. The eggs are already present in the leaf. The young look like small versions of the old, except that their bodies are softer and their sounds higher and thinner. But they do not grow older so much as continue. The basin’s time is not developmental. It is persistent. A larva becomes an adult not by aging but by the same fact that a droplet becomes mist when the heat calls it upward.

Death is not absent, but it is not visible as absence. Perhaps it is more accurate to say that death there is not an event that produces waste, memory, or scar. Something consumed becomes absorbed. Something ended becomes redistributed. A throat-animal eaten by a lichen-backed waiter does not leave bones, scales, skin, or smell. Its substance joins the wet mineral cycle of the basin. The pool accepts the transfer. The stone exhales it. The moss receives it as water. No mark remains, because no mark can remain in a place whose equilibrium prevents accumulation.

This does not mean the inhabitants are mindless. That would be too simple. They may have something like attention, because their bodies respond to light, heat, sound, and moisture with uncanny precision. They adjust to one another without collision. They sit without pressing each other into the water. They rest without breaking leaves. They call without drowning one another. They move without disturbing the basin’s surface. This implies a sensitivity, a spatial awareness, a shared field. But it is not awareness in the way humans imagine it, nor is it a collective soul. It is an ecological choreography so complete that no single participant appears to be choreographing.

The most alien quality of these inhabitants is their lack of permanence. They are permanent in the basin, but not in themselves. They are individual in form, but not in consequence. If a creature lived for centuries and built nothing, if it called continuously and taught nothing, if it mated endlessly and left no line, if it ate without waste and moved without trail, it would become difficult to regard it as an individual at all. It would be more like a function. It would be more like a wet organ of the landscape.

If one asks who lives there, then, the answer might be: no one. If one asks what lives there, the answer might be: everything, but only in the form of continuous, unaccumulating presence. The basin is full of beings without names, without nests, without memory, without ruins. It is full of life that does not use up the world. It is full of sound that does not become silence. It is full of motion that does not become history. It is full of birth that does not produce a future. It is full of death that does not leave remains.

The veil-film lives there. The pool-skin lives there. The glosswings live there. The veil-snails live there. The thread-walkers live there. The basin-throats live there. The lichen-backed waiters live there. The tension-striders live there. The moss-ridge waders live there. The microscopic swimmers in leaf-bowls live there. The algae in the pores of nascent stone live there. The drifting filaments in the warm air live there. The slow breath of the stones lives there. The tremble of the pools lives there. The unchanging chorus lives there.

But none of them lives alone.

If you stand at the edge of one of the warm, dark pools and let your eyes adjust to the dim, wet light, you begin to understand that the basin’s inhabitants are not separate bodies scattered across terrain. They are local intensifications of one living system. The moss ridge you think is ground may be a cushion of thousands of organisms. The throat-animal you think is calling may be pumping water through the basin’s acoustic field. The wader you think is standing may be a moving garden. The pool you think is still may be a membrane of microscopic life trembling at the presence of sound. The air you think is hot may be packed with bodies too small to see.

They do not look at you because they have no need to. You are an intrusion of consequence into a place without consequence. Your footprints would matter. Your breathing would leave something behind. You would build a memory by looking too long. That is why the basin feels both alive and unreal: it has no room for an observer who takes anything from it. It has room only for presence that returns every pressure to the whole.

The throat-animal continues calling. The glosswing rests. The thread-walker drips. The veil-snail crosses. The lichen-backed waiter shifts. The wader stands. The pool trembles. The moss holds. The stone breathes. None of these acts leads to another in the ordinary way. None of them builds toward a future. They are not signs of a life to come; they are life itself, complete without becoming.

Thus the basin does not have inhabitants so much as manifestations. Every form is a way the warm, wet, loud, green fullness has folded itself into a temporary shape. The shapes are countless, overlapping, nested, and intimate. They touch one another through films of water, vibrations of air, chemical breaths, and the slow pulse of heat from below. They are so dense that space itself seems alive, and so perfectly balanced that the whole system resembles a single body whose organs are not organs at all but creatures.

If one insists on naming the who, the who is the chorus.

Not a person. Not a god. Not an animal. Not a forest. Not a marsh. Not a river mouth before it becomes a river. Not a shore before it becomes a shore. Not a rock before it becomes sand.

The who is the full wet sound of the basin: throat-animals on stone, insects in air, mosses breathing, pools trembling, waders standing with gardens on their backs, leaves holding small living seas, stones forming and cooling, heat rising and returning, life so complete that it leaves no trail behind it. The who is not a being with a home. The who is the home without a being. The who is the living without history. The who is the first wet instant that never ages, never dries, never becomes, and never ends.
