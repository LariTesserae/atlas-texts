The world has rules.

First: nothing leaves without returning in another shape. Second: heat is more precious than light, salt more precious than heat, and patience more precious than both. Third: every mouth in this place—stone, human, beetle, moth, vent, lantern, roof, root—must be careful. Waste is not merely foolish here; it is a form of forgetting.

So the inhabitants are not one thing at first sight. They are not simply people in a town, nor plants in a hollow, nor animals among stone. They are a working body made of many smaller bodies, all folded into the same dry grammar. If you tried to name what lives here, you would begin with the people, because the people build, sing, cook, argue, breed, carve, remember, and dream. But even then you would be naming only one organ of the bowl.

The people are human enough to be human. They are short, dense, broad-chested people, built more like climbers than like striders. Their bodies have obeyed the dryness. They are not pale cave-folk, because light does find them, though not freely. Their skin has the deep burnished tone of oil-darkened clay, often faintly white at the shoulders, knees, and forearms where salt dust settles and is rubbed in. Their sweat is sparse and strange: when they do sweat, it leaves a crystalline film that stiffens in the hide of their clothing. They do not need to be washed, because washing is impossible. They are cleaned by scraping, by wind, by fine salt, by rubbing with resin, by being licked, as it were, by the town’s dryness.

Their hands are broad and short-fingered, with thick pads and hard nails. They learn as children to pinch stalks, scrape salt, feel the grain of stone, and tell a warm seam by the breath against the palm. Their feet are also broad, flat, and tough, soles shaped to grip smooth terraces, salt blocks, and curved walls. They rarely wear boots, and when they do, the boots are cured hide and braided cord, soft enough to fold, stiff enough to protect from salt crystals. Many people prefer bare feet, because feet, in this town, are part of the work.

Their faces are wide, cheek-boned, with small deep-set eyes protected by heavy lids. They can look at salt glare without wincing for long. At night, their pupils dilate until their eyes seem black as beetle shells. Their noses are broad and dry, trained to smell sulfur, pitch, animal fat, heated stone, resin, seed-smoke, and the faint sweetness of a living person in a crowded house. A visitor might be overwhelmed by smell before seeing anything. The people move through it, recognizing one another by the particular blend of oil, smoke, salt, and skin.

Their hair is often cut very short, bound close, or rubbed with fat to keep salt dust from matting it. Children’s hair is sometimes worn longer, because long hair means a child still needs carrying. Adults braid short cords with sinew, stalk-fiber, or thin strips of hide, marking terrace-line, work-task, or memory of the dead. Some shave their heads smooth and rub the scalp with resin to prevent dryness. Others wear their hair twisted into little coils at the temples, so wind cannot catch it.

They do not drink water. At least, they do not drink water as a valley people would drink it: as something found, gathered, poured, cooled, and wasted. The little wetness a human body needs is made inside them, a secret debt paid by every seed, beetle, fat, bulb, and roasted salt-cake they consume. Their mouths taste thick when hungry, not dry alone but coated, like the inside of an oil flask. When they are thirsty, they know it not by a craving for a pool, but by the way their gums tighten and their skin draws closer to the bone. A skilled person can make thirst into a slow discipline, like fasting or prayer.

Their first meals as infants are not liquid in the ordinary sense, but warm emulsions of seed paste, rendered plant oil, chewed fat, and salt. A mother may press her nipple to an infant’s mouth, but the milk is not blue-white water; it is thick, rich, almost creamy, faintly bitter with minerals, and golden at the edges. The people call it something like “sun-fat,” though the word is soft and worn, not a formal name. Babies are wrapped against the body in hide slings and carried through the bowl’s terraces, hearing the town’s noise from infancy: the scrape of salt blocks, the hum of vents, the rustle of moths, the clack of grinding stones, the songs that keep labor even.

Children grow up quickly in certain ways and slowly in others. By the time they can walk, they know not to stand in the path of falling dust. By five or six, they can carry small loads, sweep salt from thresholds, and fetch oil in clay bowls. They learn to climb before they learn to run. They learn to squeeze through roof gaps, to read the direction of warm air, to put a finger near a vent and know whether it breathes hot or merely warm. Their first toys are often polished beetle shells, salt beads, carved bone animals, moth wings, and little braided cords that can be tied into knots and undone.
