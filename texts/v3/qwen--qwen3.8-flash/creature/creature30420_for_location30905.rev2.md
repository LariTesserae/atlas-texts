What lives there is not a landscape of organisms you would recognize by name from first glance. It is a living system built from stains, films, pulses, and thresholds. Life begins before bodies become visible: as a sheen on mud, as a breath of sulfur, as a line of salt at the water’s edge, as the faint pink tint of brine turning in a shallow pan. If one looks without expecting leaves, one finds abundance anyway, but abundance of another kind: bacterial, mineral, insectile, avian, industrial. The place is bare only to the eye trained to equate life with greenery.

The first inhabitants are the mats.

They cover wet ground in tight, slippery skins, some black as oil, some olive-brown, some cyan-blue, some sulfur-yellow around seeps where warm water rises. They are not grass, nor moss, nor algae in the loose sense, but layered communities of microbes that have made a living on surfaces too harsh for plants. A mat is a world seen from above and from inside. Its upper layer drinks light and gives off oxygen in faint bubbles, like a thin green gasp. Beneath that, oxygen dies away and different forms of life take over: organisms that eat sulfur compounds, that turn iron from one state to another, that live on heat and chemistry rather than sunlight alone. The mats grow slowly. They crack when dry, then heal when water returns. Their surfaces are sticky, then gritty, then papery. A boot prints into them and leaves a dark stain that will remain for hours. If a canal is diverted, a mat can bleach, shrink, and re-form along the new margin within weeks.

These mats are the ground of the basin’s economy. Everything larger depends on the boundary between salt and fresh, heat and cooling, dry and wet. They collect the first light, break down dead things, build organic films, and feed the smallest animals. They are the meadow of the basin. Without them, the place would be only a chemical waste. With them, it becomes an ecology.

The water itself is full of unseen populations.

In the broad pans where brine has concentrated into something heavy and pale, there are crustaceans so numerous that the water appears to be moving on its own. Brine shrimp hang in the shallows or flick through the surface with a faint, constant trembling. Copepods, tiny as grains of salt, pulse in the columns of water, turning the air into a mist of life. Ostracods drift through the margins, and sometimes whole layers of the pond seem to shimmer with their movement as a bird passes overhead and the surface reacts. They are invisible one by one, but together they become a visible substance: a rose-orange cloud near the edge of a pan, a grey ripple where the wind touches the brine. In some basins where the salt is strongest, there is no fish, no tadpole, no dragonfly nymph—only these animals, living in a water that would kill most life outside.

Where fresh water is diverted into canals from wells or from condensed, purified pools, the ecology changes at once. Small silver fish dart into the shadows of pipes, then out again, then vanish when a bird’s foot touches the water. They feed on larvae that live inside the microbial film. Their scales look dull in the hazy light, not bright like polished metal. They have learned to live in narrow, cool lines cut through the pale heat of the basin. Their presence is local and conditional. A channel is alive only as long as the pumps keep its water from turning briny or warm. If a pump fails, the fish go silent under the surface, and insects take over.

In the damp margins of the industrial ponds, there are amphibians.

They live near fresh or half-fresh pools where rainwater collects long enough for tadpoles to finish their work. They are not numerous by the standards of forest swamps, but they are present as punctual sounds: a short wet call from beneath a pipe, a trill from a crack in concrete, a small dark shape leaping into shallow water and vanishing into silt. Their skin holds moisture when the air does not. They shelter in drainage culverts, beneath pump-house floors, in the damp shade of stone foundations, in the hollows of disused basins where the water is cooler and the salt has not yet crystallized into a hard crust. If the heat intensifies, they go still. If a light rains on the basin after days of vapor-thick air, they emerge to feed.

The insects are the most immediate life, and they are enormous in number.

At dawn and evening, when the heat loosens its grip, swarms rise from the water surfaces and fill the air with soft movement. Midges stand in clouds over the edges of pans, not buzzing so much as vibrating. Brine flies cluster in grey masses on stones and rails, lifting and settling as though the wind itself has a pulse. Chironomid larvae live in the mud and mats, feeding on microscopic matter, then emerge and climb the air in slow curtains. Small beetles move across the films of shallow water, their legs leaving tiny lines that vanish instantly. Where there is fresh water, there may also be dragonflies or damselflies, but they are less visible than the brine flies because their bodies are darker and their numbers smaller.

These insects are not only life in themselves; they are weather made alive. A swarm over a tank can darken a metal roof. A cloud of midges can blur a walkway. At night, the lamps of the plant and the lights of worker housing draw them in like moths, and they settle on glass in trembling layers, then fall into drains. They are food for everything that follows. Their abundance turns the emptiness of the basin into a feeding ground.

Reptiles live low to the ground.

Lizards occupy the same narrow world that the industrial structures make possible: the warm edge of concrete, the underside of a valve cover, the shadow between two salt-stained walls, the lip of a pump chamber where heat radiates upward. They are small, quick, and colored to match the place: grey-brown with pale stripes, or dark-backed for warmth. They run in short bursts, stop, press their bodies against stone that has been heated since morning, and flick their tongues at the air. Some hunt flies; some avoid being eaten by birds. They do not climb trees, because there are no trees, but they climb metal rails, pipe supports, and the vertical edges of concrete slabs where moisture gathers and insects settle.

There may also be snakes, though not as obvious as the lizards. They move through the gaps under foundations and along the edges of canals where the ground is too warm and too damp. Their presence is announced only indirectly: by a startled lizard freezing, by a slight ripple in the grey silt, by the way a bird hesitates near the water. Their bodies are dull, not shining, because this is not a place of clean surfaces. They are as mineral-toned as the rest of the basin.

Birds are the loudest visible answer to the emptiness.

They come for the shrimp, the fish, the larvae, the beetles, and the eggs of insects. At certain seasons, the canals and evaporation ponds become lined with waders: long-legged, patient, with bills built for probing and picking. Some stand in water shallow enough to show their toes in the brine, their heads moving in short, deliberate dips. Some walk along the mineral crusts, leaving small holes and footprints that quickly fill with water. Others hover above the shallows, wings trembling against the heat. Gulls and terns circle over storage basins. Ducks feed in freshened channels, their bills filtering the water. Long-necked fish-eaters gather near discharge points where warm water cools and small life concentrates. And in the deeper pink brine pans, if the chemistry allows it, flocks of bright, long-legged birds can turn the pale salt into a field of moving orange and white.

Their lives are synchronized with the plant and the sky.

In the cool hours, birds arrive in noisy, restless ribbons. They feed along canal edges, roost on tank roofs, nest under steel lintels, and shelter from the midday glare in the shadow beneath pump houses. Their droppings whiten metal surfaces and enrich the ground where insects and microbes live. At night, the roofs become crowded, but the sound is not continuous: it is scattered, with sudden cries from one tank to another, wingbeats starting and stopping, the low shuffle of feet on warm metal. In the morning, the birds lift in flocks, and the roofs are briefly empty, dotted with feathers, eggs, and pale smears of droppings.

The small mammals are fewer and more circumspect.

They do not form herds. They live near water, near shade, near human structures where food and protection can be found. Rodents may occupy the low ground beneath rail spurs and maintenance sheds, feeding on grain dust, insects, and whatever the settlement discards. They are pale or grey, not because the place colors them so, but because this is a landscape where pale ground makes dark bodies too visible. Bats live in gaps between girders, in the upper spaces of pump houses, and in the cracks of concrete foundations. They emerge at dusk to feed on the midges and brine flies, and their movement is almost invisible in the haze. Small carnivores may come at the edges of the basin, drawn by water and insects, but they are rare visitors, not residents, unless there is enough shelter. In such a place, a mammal must be local, patient, and quiet.

The humans are there, but they are not the population one notices first.

The settlement has the scale of a town, yet it is made of workers rather than families, maintenance crews rather than crowds. They come to live by the water, not in the water. They live in low housing near the rail spur, in rooms shaded from the hottest afternoon, with walls painted pale, windows fitted with mesh, floors cold because they are built over foundations that sink slightly into brine. Their days are arranged around the basin’s rhythms: before heat, after heat, night shift, emergency repair, harvest, sampling, pump test. They move between buildings in a way that is less like walking through a town than walking through a machine.

Their faces are squinted from glare, their hands dry and cracked despite the wetness of the place. They learn which surfaces will be hot at four in the afternoon, which will be cold at dawn, which drains return salt, which pipes carry clean water, which canals smell of iron, which ponds smell of sulfur. They know that if a metal railing is left uncoated, the salt will eat into it faster than the sun will. They know that the light in this basin does not behave like light elsewhere: it comes from all directions, from the ground and the water and the haze, and it hides color. They do not wear bright clothes because the place is dull, but because bright colors look too new, too temporary, too unearned.

They are careful with water.

Fresh water is kept close, measured, stored in cisterns, protected from brine. Some of it is drawn from wells. Some is separated in condensation traps. Some is collected from rain channels in wide metal roofs. Workers wash from the salt off their boots before entering buildings. They drink often, but not much at a time. They are aware, without always thinking it, that the basin can take life from the air: that a hand left on a wet stone will feel moisture, but a mouth left open too long in the heat may taste iron and dust.

The people live among insects and birds without much surprise. The lights of the housing block are swarmed every evening. Fish gather in the shade of the pump station. Lizards run along concrete ledges. Birds nest under roof beams. The human settlement is not separate from the ecology; it is another set of edges for the ecology to occupy.

But the most peculiar inhabitants are the machines.

Pumps, turbines, condensers, tanks, valves, rail lines, pipes, and electrical cabinets belong so completely to the place that they seem to have acquired habits. They breathe brine. They perspire steam. They cough condensation at night. Their joints swell in the heat and contract in the cool. Salt crusts over their lower fittings as though they were stones in a river. Rust grows along welds and drain outlets in patterns that change with the seasons. Birds nest in the gaps beneath cooling fans. Microbes form slime inside wet chambers. Insects accumulate at light panels. The plant is not alive in the ordinary sense, but it is part of the life of the basin: a secondary organism made of metal, water, and maintenance.

The machines live because the people keep them living.

Maintenance crews are always at work, not because the industrial structures are falling apart in a dramatic way, but because the environment is a slow solvent. A valve must be turned. A coating must be reapplied. A crack must be filled. A drain must be cleared of salt crust. A pump must be tested because if it stops, water backs up, heat rises, and the ecology shifts from one state to another. The work is repetitive, quiet, and never complete. It is as though the plant and the basin are engaged in a long negotiation: one trying to preserve form, the other trying to dissolve it.

The industrial activity itself creates new habitats.

Warm water discharged into a cooling basin can support fish where they could not otherwise live. Shaded pipe trenches can shelter lizards and amphibians. Condensation channels can concentrate fresh water in the middle of salt flats. Evaporation ponds can become brine-shrimp marshes without vegetation. Roofs of pump houses become bird roosts. Rusty steel edges become insect ledges. Old foundations sink enough to make depressions where water gathers after rain, and within days a microbial film begins to grow there. The human works do not erase the ecology; they add surfaces to it.

The basin, viewed closely, is a place of gradients.

There is no single living world here. There are worlds separated by a few centimeters. A canal edge may have freshwater film, and the adjacent salt crust may have brine, and the next meter may be hard salt with only dormant microbes. A pan may have pink shrimp in its middle, while its border is a zone of white crystallization and black mats. A pump-house wall may be cool and wet on one side and baked and dry on the other. A tank roof may be hot enough to burn skin, while the water beneath its base is shaded and full of life.

This is why the place feels both empty and overfull.

The eye sees emptiness because there are no trees, no grass, no fields, no familiar vertical shapes. But the basin is crowded with small bodies, films, eggs, larvae, feathers, droppings, rust flakes, salt grains, vapor, and sound. It is a living place in which the largest creatures are birds and fish, but the true inhabitants are the microbial mats and the insects that make the water tremble. The visible life is an outer skin. Under it, the chemical life is doing most of the work.

The mats photosynthesize in the hazy daylight. They convert weak light and mineral-rich water into organic matter. They build films that stabilize mud. They release gases that smell faintly of sulfur and warm stone. They feed larvae and shrimp. Those feed fish and insects. Those feed birds and bats and lizards. The cycle does not pass through leaves and stems. It stays close to the ground, in water, in film, in crust, in the thin zones between fresh and brine, between hot and cool, between dry and wet.

It is a short food web, but it is dense.

Because there are no plants, there is no layer of stems to hide in, no canopy to shade, no roots to anchor a larger mammal community. Life is therefore concentrated at margins: the edge of a canal, the lip of a pan, the underside of a roof, the warm shadow beneath a pump station. These edges are not decorative. They are the real habitat.

The humans know the edges.

They walk them. They sample them. They clean them. They place handrails along them, paint lines over them, and mark them with signs about depth, salinity, heat, and danger. The workers’ boots print in the same crusts as the birds’ feet. Their lamps collect insects the same way the water collects salt. Their cisterns and drains are shaped by the same chemistry as the microbial films. They are not outside the place. They are living inside its rules.

At night, the basin seems to belong entirely to the machines and the animals.

Pumps continue. Condensation drips from pipes. Salt crystals tick faintly as the ground cools. Bats pass through the dark in short, silent arcs. Insects settle on lamp glass in shifting clusters. Birds shift in their roosts on roofs. Lizards rest in warm hollows near foundations. Fish hang motionless in canals, waiting for the water to change. The human housing may be quiet, windows dark, curtains drawn to keep out glare and heat. The workers are inside, drinking water, cleaning salt from clothing, sleeping after shifts. Outside, the plant breathes.

There is a particular moment, just before dawn, when the basin is most itself.

The air is cool enough to hold moisture, but not cool enough to be dry. Mist lies along the canals. Salt flats glow pale under the first light. Microbial mats become glossy, revealing the colors that had been hidden by dryness. Brine shrimp stir at the edges of ponds. Insects rise in soft columns. Fish begin moving near the shade of pipes. Birds wake on the roofs of pump houses, and their first calls carry strangely across the flatness: not bright and musical, but clear, thin, and scattered. The industrial hum is already present, but low, as though the plant had never stopped.

Then the heat comes.

It does not arrive as a sudden blaze, because the sky is often hazy and the air is thick. It arrives as a weight. The ground warms from below and from above. Metal surfaces become too hot to touch. The microbial mats dry and crack at the edges. The shallow water begins to evaporate visibly, leaving rims of salt. Insects settle near shaded surfaces. Lizards move from stone to stone in short, urgent paths. Birds scatter to the shadows beneath structures. Fish retreat to deeper, cooler channels. The mats darken with moisture but then lighten as the water film thins. The entire basin shifts from the abundance of night into a kind of waiting.

Even in waiting, the place is active.

Water seeps upward in some places. Salt grows along cracks. Rust advances. Condensation forms in shaded hollows. Microbes metabolize. Insect larvae feed in the mud. Birds conserve their energy. Pumps adjust pressure. Workers drink, rest, inspect, return. The landscape seems suspended, but nothing is still.

What lives there, in the largest sense, is the cycle itself.

It is a living arrangement of heat, water, salt, mineral, machine, and organism. The microbes are not decorations on the ground; they are the engine. The crustaceans are not curiosities of brine; they are the middle body of the basin’s food web. The insects are not pests; they are the abundance that makes the birds possible. The birds are not the only life; they are its visible crown. The humans are not the owners of the place; they are inhabitants of it, maintaining a machine that maintains water that maintains life. The machines are not dead infrastructure; they are another kind of living edge.

If one insists on a single image early, one will miss the truth: there is no single species, no single population, no single dominant being. The place is a colony of processes that have become biological.

Some of the inhabitants are so small that they exist only as effect: a green-black coating on mud, a pink cast in water, a sulfur smell near a crack in stone, a slight movement in a pan that is not quite current. Others are larger but still marginal: a lizard on a warm pipe, a beetle crossing a film of brine, a fish hiding in a canal shadow. Others are more public: a flock of waders moving along the edge of a salt pond, a midday roost of birds on a tank roof, a swarm of flies rising from a drain at dusk. Still others are barely noticed: bats, rodents, amphibians, snakes, bacteria, archaea, fungi, diatoms, dormant eggs, microbes living in the wet pores of stone.

The industrial town supplies surfaces. The basin supplies conditions. Together they produce a strange, settled vitality.

The roofs are covered in salt dust and bird waste. The concrete is stained with mineral water. The pipes are warm to the touch and smell of metal. The canals have hard edges and soft bottoms. The ponds are shallow but chemically deep. The air is wet but not clean. The light is bright but diffuse. The sounds are low but continuous. The place operates with the quiet, patient confidence of something that has learned how to live under constant pressure from heat and salt.

If a visitor looks away quickly, they will see emptiness.

If they stay, they will see that emptiness is a surface. Beneath it, the basin is thick with life, but not the life of forests or grasslands or gardens. It is the life of crusts, films, swarms, shallow water, mineral edges, and shaded metal. It is the life of organisms that thrive in the spaces between. It is a hard ecology, but not a cruel one. It is an industrial ecology, but not an empty one. It is a biological system that uses the machines as part of its ground and the machines as part of its metabolism.

The people who live there know it, even if they do not say it. They know the smell of brine at night, the way insects gather on glass, the way lizards appear on warm stone, the way birds come in from the pans at sunset, the way the pumps change tone when the water level drops, the way salt blooms on a handrail after one dry hour. They know the basin as a living instrument. They are not its masters. They are its technicians, its passengers, its temporary inhabitants.

And if one asks what lives there, the answer must keep widening.

Life there is microbial mats and sulfur films.
It is brine shrimp and copepods, moving in clouds through salt water.
It is midges and brine flies, rising and settling in curtains.
It is larvae in mud, beetles on film, wasps on warm concrete.
It is small fish in freshened channels, frogs in rain pools, tadpoles turning in shallow water.
It is lizards running along pipe supports, snakes in foundations, bats above the pans.
It is waders in rows, gulls circling, ducks filtering, long-legged birds standing in black water.
It is rodents under sheds and insects against lamps.
It is workers walking between pump houses, collecting water, cleaning surfaces, checking pressure.
It is machines breathing steam, sweating condensation, and growing rust.
It is the basin itself: hot, wet, pale, mineral, industrial, alive at the margins, abundant in invisible bodies, settled in its rhythms, and full of a life that does not require leaves to be visible.
