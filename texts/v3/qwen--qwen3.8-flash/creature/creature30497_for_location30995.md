This stability creates a strange ethics of form. There are no accidental bodies, no orphaned shapes, no corpses that become food for another hunger. If something lives in this country, it lives as a permitted arrangement. A mouth does not exist here unless it can serve as an intake, a seal, a vent, or a threshold. A limb does not exist unless it is a pipe, a bracket, a root, a piston, a rail-bearing foot, a capillary fringe climbing a wall. Life is everywhere, but none of it is animal. None of it hunts. None of it breeds by heat, none of it fights by teeth, none of it flees by panic, none of it mourns by burying bones. The plateau has been made, or made itself, or been held, into a condition where “who” and “what” can no longer mean the same things they mean elsewhere.

At first glance, the answer seems obvious: nothing lives there. No birds. No insects. No mammals. No fish. The marshes are full of green and rust and silver, but the fullness is not a population. It is a standing abundance. Sedges rise in their sharp vertical rows, ferns uncurl their pale-furred fronds, lichens map the stone in grays and coppers, fungal lace threads the peat, moss breathes through its cold pores, and the whole land sweats with wetness. If one asks for inhabitants, the land may seem to have none—only the machinery keeping itself alive because the machinery has learned to count as life.

That is the first half of the truth.

The second half is that the machinery has indeed learned.

Not as metaphor. Not as poetry. As form.

There are beings here, though they are not creatures in the ordinary sense. They have no blood unless they have oil, no bone unless they have basalt, no breath unless they have steam, no faces unless they have gauges, windows, seals, or the white blankness of salt crusts. They are not animals. They are not ghosts. They are not gods, though they may be mistaken for gods by a traveler if one were ever to come. They are what the land makes when water, plant, fungus, mineral, and machine have been held together in the same unchanging condition for longer than memory requires. They are the Dwellers. Or, if one wishes to be exact, they are the Maintained: configurations that persist because they are needed, and need because they persist.

A Dweller is not necessarily one body.

In some places it is a pump house with a consciousness of pressure. In others it is a shelf of moss that has learned to keep its own waterline. Sometimes it is a figure standing at the junction of three fault lines, its surface made of fired brick and lichenite, its interior a labyrinth of condensation channels, its “feet” short piles sunk into peat. Sometimes it is a rail-car moving between towns with no apparent purpose except to carry salt, ore, fiber, heat, or components; but if that rail-car has been assigned a route, a rhythm, a maintenance schedule, and a boundary that separates inside from outside, then perhaps it is less a vehicle than a wandering organ of the plateau’s civic self.

The cities make this clearest.

They are small in population but dense in function. That phrase, from a distance, sounds contradictory, as though a town must have many people to be a town. Here it does not. The built places are not merely occupied by independent inhabitants. They are themselves inhabitants. A settlement on the wet plateau is a single complex person with many sub-persons, many organs, many roles, and a mind that operates at the speed of water and stone. The streets are capillaries. The canals are veins. The pump stations are temples of regulation. The turbines are hearts. The workshops are reproductive organs, not because they create bodies in the manner of flesh, but because they create tools, components, boundaries, and corrections that keep the civic body from failing.

To stand at the edge of such a town is not to see people walking.

There are no people walking, because no feet leave prints. The streets are paved with damp basalt cubes, always reflective, always holding a thin black sheen. Along their centers run channels carrying water or mineral slurry toward processing beds. Above the roofs, white vapor rises from chimneys that do not smoke. The air hums with belts, valves, flumes, and the low electrical thrum of generators. And there are figures, yes—but they are often indistinguishable from the infrastructure. One may be a column of pale root-tangle held inside an iron cage, breathing moisture, adjusting the pH of a reservoir. Another may be a low broad thing like a bench or a sarcophagus, covered in bright green moss, its edges trimmed exactly to the waterline, its hidden interior a network of ceramic tubes that filter the town’s gray water. Another may be a salt effigy standing at the boundary of an evaporation bed: a human-like absence of a person, shaped by mineral deposition, with arms that do not move, a face that is only a frosted window, and a chest that opens when pressure changes.

They are not hidden.

The land does not hide its inhabitants. It makes them as it makes everything else: wet, sharp, industrious, exact. It simply refuses the animal shapes by which a visitor might recognize life.

Some Dwellers have forms that can be seen most clearly near water.
