Everything that lives there is dry enough to chime.

The plateau has inhabitants, but not in the ordinary wet-world sense. Nothing here is soft with liquid, nothing swells from moisture, nothing calls across distances with the help of air so dense and rich that sound can carry its whole body. The beings of this high, bright, salted land are instead things of surface, lattice, charge, vapor, mineral film, and slow angle. They do not drink. They do not sweat. They do not bleed. They exist as architectures of exchange: absorbing light, breathing gases, gathering dust, holding electricity, filtering metal aerosols, and converting stone into temporary life.

The first and most obvious inhabitants are the vegetation, though that word almost fails them.

They are not trees, though some of them rise in broken column-like forms from salt basins. They are not grasses, though many spread in copper-threaded waves. They are not lichens, though some are thin as film and cling to fused silica. They are not corals, though some have the same patient branching patience. They are mineral organisms, built from breath and light, and they have filled the plateau with a lushness that has no wetness in it.

The tallest among them are the glass-reeds.

They rise from hollow basins where vapor condenses into dust rather than water, standing in thickets like dry fountains. Each stalk is a column of crystalline fiber, pale at first, then darkening toward a smoky blue or bronze at its base. Their interiors are not channels for flowing fluid but bundles of rigid filaments, layered like the strands of a rope that was never twisted with wetness. At intervals along the stem are nodes: small, hollow, slightly swollen chambers that act as resonators, sensors, and storage points for charged dust. When the wind moves through a field of glass-reeds, the nodes produce thin reedy tones, not songs but dry, hollow pitches, like the plateau remembering a forgotten instrument.

At the top of each glass-reed, the stalk opens into a crown of needle-like fronds. These are not leaves in a conventional sense; they are collecting surfaces. They spread outward and upward, catching not dew but suspended mineral particles, sulfite aerosols, metallic dust, and faint electrical potential from the surrounding air. During bloom cycles, the fronds widen until they are almost translucent at their edges. Tiny sparks gather along their tips, release silently, and move from one stalk to the next like small invisible fires passing through dry grass.

Near the salt pans grow the salt-wheats.

They are pale, almost white, not because they are dead, but because the light is so strong and so pure. Their broad, stiff heads resemble bleached grain, but the heads are made of fine crystalline plates, each plate layered like thin mica. Their pigments are reflective, not absorptive: they live partly by scattering light into internal lattice channels and partly by filtering sulfur-bearing vapors through tiny pores along their stalks. Salt-wheat fields ripple in the wind without bending softly; instead they click and scrape, a dry rustle like pages being turned in a house with no people.

Across ridges and along dark mineral veins spread the copper-thread thickets.

These are lower, denser, more tangled. Their forms resemble shrubs made from twisted wire, though no wire has been made anywhere on this plateau. The “copper” is a living metallic oxide fiber, grown in helical strands that can contract and expand with temperature. When air currents are rich with the right gases, the thickets darken, their fibers taking on a faint reflective sheen as microscopic crystalline deposits form on the surface. When the vapor thins, the thickets become matte again, almost colorless, as if retreating into the stone. Their hollow nodes emit a thin whistle when wind presses through them, and this sound is one of the few voices the plateau itself produces.

In shadowed basins, where light is indirect and the ground holds cool mineral deposits, grow the violet-fans.

These organisms spread as broad, flat structures, some as large as a table, some clustered like overlapping shields. Their surfaces are thin metallic membranes stretched across lattice frameworks. The veins are not liquid channels but lines of crystalline organization through which redox reactions move slowly from node to node. They feed by angling themselves toward light and by absorbing gases through thousands of microscopic pores. In dim basins, their membranes carry blue-black and violet bands, pigments that shift between mineral absorption and electrostatic sensitivity. They do not flower, but during certain pressure tides their edges tremble and their colors brighten, as if the basin itself has drawn a breath.

All of these forms connect, where they can, through something more intimate than root.

They have filament networks, pale and fine, that penetrate porous stone. These are not roots searching for moisture, for there is none to find. Instead, they search for chemical gradients and electrical potential. They tap into dark mineral veins where sulfates, carbonates, and trace metals have accumulated. They grow into the microscopic channels of burnt stone and coral-like basins. They form living sheets beneath the salt crust, networks so extended that a thicket and a nearby grove may be connected through kilometers of dry, mineralized tissue. These networks are slow, patient, and faintly conductive. They carry not sap but signal, charge, and surface diffusion.

Some of the plateau’s largest beings are not individual stalks or fans, but entire groves.

A single glass-reed may look like one plant, yet the basin it occupies can contain a network descended from a common ancestral lattice. The dead of such a network do not vanish. They calcify and vitrify, becoming porous shelves, honeycombed bases, and fossil-like mats. New growth uses these remnants as scaffolds. Over centuries, a grove can become a layered structure, part living tissue and part mineral archive. From above, one might see concentric rings of pale fronds around older, darker cores. These are not ruins. They are bodies growing upon the memory of bodies.

If anything lives there, it is first this: a dry, mineral verdure that feeds on light, breath, stone, and charge.

But the vegetation is not alone.

In the spaces between fronds, beneath salt crusts, along vapor paths, and inside the charged haze that hangs over certain basins, there are smaller lives. Many are so small that at first they seem like dust, or crystalline powder, or flakes of mica disturbed by wind. Only close attention reveals their motion.

There are dust-lattices.

These are microscopic organisms that drift in aerosol layers above the plateau. They have no liquid bodies. They are tiny arrangements of crystalline plates and mineral films, some barely larger than particles of salt, capable of staying suspended in the air for long distances. They feed on vapor, on metallic oxides, on trace organic compounds that have been released by dying plants, and on the fine chemical residue left by pressure tides. They reproduce by forming seed-crystals, minuscule lattice nodes that split and reform. When wind carries them through a field, they make the air look faintly glittered, like a dust cloud made of silver flour. They are essential to the larger vegetation, because they bring mineral nutrients to leaf-surfaces and help bind vapor into stable growth patterns.

There are chime-mites.

They are larger than dust-lattices, still small, but visible as pale armored wanderers on salt-wheat heads, glass-reed nodes, and the broad membranes of mica-fans. Their shells are made of carbonate or silica, segmented in such a way that they can fold close to dry stalks without being wetted or crushed. They move with a strange, dry grace, each footfall producing a tiny click. When they gather in groups, the sound is like fine metal powder shaken in a jar. Chime-mites scrape microscopic organisms and mineral dust from plant surfaces. In doing so, they feed themselves and also prepare the fronds to capture new particles. They are grazers, cleaners, pollinators, and carriers. They spread spore-dust and seed-crystals on their legs, often without purpose or design, only by walking through one charged grove and then another.

There are lattice-worms.

They live beneath the salt and porous stone, in tunnels too narrow for animals to see unless one lifts a plate of fused crust. Their bodies are pale, segmented, and dry, more like bundles of flexible mineral fiber than like worms from wet soil. They consume dead plant tissue after it has begun to vitrify, breaking down crystalline mats and turning them into powder. They excrete fine carbonate and sulfate grains, building the very substrate upon which new life settles. The plateau’s ground is not merely ground; in places, it is the accumulated work of animals no bigger than threads.

There are static-fleas.

They are small, dark, fast, and easily mistaken for specks of basalt dust until they jump or glide. They ride the surfaces of larger animals and the outer fronds of thickets, feeding on shed mineral skin, on dried tissue scales, and on the fine salt films that form in the creases of hides. They regulate themselves by charge. At the height of electrostatic weather, their bodies can lift from a resting surface and drift briefly along invisible lines of force, appearing like black sparks that decide not to burn. They do not drink the animals they live on. They clean, disturb, and sometimes carry dust-lattices from one grazing creature to another, becoming part of the plateau’s quiet transport system.

There are also creatures that are neither plant nor animal but seem to belong to the boundary between them.

Some of the most difficult to classify are the breath-mats.

They cover the floors of certain basins in chalky films, pale and dry, sometimes spreading in circular patterns around low rises. They appear inert, like salt crust, but when a warm vapor passes overhead, their surfaces ripple with microscopic pores that open and close. They absorb gases, concentrate metal particles, and store electrical potential. They may reproduce by fragmentation: a piece torn away by wind settles nearby and begins to crystallize into a new mat. They are likely plant-like, animal-like, and geological at once. To call them a species would be too confident. They are more like processes that have learned to remain.

Then there are the grazers.

They are rare, and their rarity is part of the plateau’s character. But where they exist, they give the landscape a pulse, a movement, a sense of beings crossing through rather than merely growing upon.

The most remarkable are the long-striders.

They are broad-backed quadrupeds with legs too long for what one might expect from their bodies. This gives them an odd silhouette: compact torso, high shoulders, slender limbs ending in broad, slightly concave feet. Their bodies are dry, dense, and mineralized in places, built for a world where liquids are scarce and nothing can be softened by water. Their hides are not leathery. They resemble polished stone with a faint coppery sheen, reflective enough that in bright light a strider can seem like a moving statue. Their backs carry wide, leaflike membranes that stand slightly away from the body, like folded vanes. These membranes are not merely decorative. They collect atmospheric charge, filter fine particles from the air, and help the strider sense approaching pressure tides.

When a herd of long-striders crosses a salt flat, the scene seems too delicate for the scale.

They do not gallop. They do not crash through undergrowth. There is no undergrowth to crash through in the wet-world sense. Instead, their long legs move in a careful, gliding rhythm, feet sweeping over salt and mineral dust in a way that resembles skating on a frozen surface. Their bodies rise and fall only slightly. They seem to be held up by the landscape rather than pushing down into it. When they step, they disturb the electrostatic layers of the ground, and tiny arcs of charge may flicker in the dust around their feet. A herd crossing a basin at dawn might leave a faint pattern of shallow depressions, not from weight alone, but because the charge of their bodies rearranges the dust beneath them.

Long-striders graze by scraping.

Their mouths do not chew soft leaves or moist grass. Their tongues are abrasive, crystalline surfaces that can collect microscopic organisms, mineral dust, fragments of dry frond, and the powdery residue left by calcified plant edges. They feed in a way that looks almost like reading surfaces. A strider will lower its head to a salt-wheat field, pass its broad tongue across the tops of stalks, and collect not a wet bite but a dry accumulation of edible mineral film and organic crystal. Their digestion is sealed, slow, and powered by internal redox chemistry. It proceeds without liquid pooling in a gut. Their bodies are more like furnaces than bladders.

They are not silent, but they are quiet.

Their hides creak faintly as they move. Their dorsal membranes click when dust catches the charge. Their hooves give off a dry tap that is soon absorbed by the open ground. If a long-strider stops and shifts its weight, the whole herd may pause, because movement here is not only physical but electrical. The striders know one another partly by charge signature. They stand too close, then too far, adjusting themselves in relation to the static field of the group. They do not call loudly because there is no need and no medium. Their communication is local, tactile, vibrational, and visual.

Their young are born dry.

They are laid in geode-like eggs, not soft shells with moisture, but porous mineral casings that sit in sheltered basins where vapor and warmth can pass through the lattice. The eggs are often left in the care of the grove-mats, where the plants’ own charge fields help regulate temperature and chemical balance. The embryo inside grows by drawing nutrients from vapor, dust, and solid-state exchange through the egg wall. When it is ready, it emerges as a small, powder-coated strider, already standing, already able to move with surprising steadiness. Its first hours are spent rubbing against the legs and flank membranes of the adults, not to feed, but to acquire their charge pattern. It learns its herd’s electrical signature the way another species might learn its song.

Long-striders carry entire communities upon their bodies.

In the creases behind their shoulders and along the edges of their dorsal vanes live colonies of chime-mites, dust-lattices, static-fleas, and small crustose organisms that resemble living dust. These are not parasites. They are part of the strider’s existence. They clean dead mineral scales from the hide, help regulate excess static, filter airborne particles, and return fine nutrients when they die and calcify. A strider moving through a grove may look, from a distance, like a walking hillside, a small ecology on long legs. When it rests, the colonies on its back begin to adjust toward the light. The strider becomes, in stillness, part of the vegetation.

Another grazing form is the salt-shell.

It is lower, slower, and far more ambiguous. At first glance it might be mistaken for a boulder that has settled into a ring of pale dust. Its body is encased in a broad dome of fused mineral plate, porous but hard, often overgrown with lichlike breath-mats and tiny glass-reed fragments. It grazes by moving slowly across low crusts, its wide mouth scraping carbonate films and microorganisms from the ground. Its footfalls are faint, and it spends long hours resting in shallow depressions where vapor collects. It is not fast because it does not need to flee often. Predators avoid it unless necessary; its shell is too laborious to break. When a salt-shell dies, its dome becomes a foundation. Dust settles. Breath-mats grow across it. A new generation of mineral vegetation may begin from the mineralized remains of its body, making the dead animal not waste, but scaffold.

There are predators, but they are fewer still.

They are not roaring, hunting in packs, or tearing through brush. Such things would require noise, speed, moisture, and abundance. Predation on this plateau is rare, patient, and almost ceremonial in its silence.

The most notable are the mirror-stalkers.

They are flat-bodied creatures that lie along the edges of groves and salt crusts, their surfaces patterned to match the cracked, white, and faintly rose ground. They have long limbs, thin heads, and broad ventral plates that sense vibration through mineral surfaces. Their eyes are not wet organs. They are crystalline facet-paths, capable of detecting polarized light and movement in thin air, but they do not rely on sight alone. A mirror-stalker can feel the electrical disturbance of a passing herd before it sees it. When it waits, it becomes almost invisible except for a slight shimmer along its edge, as if a patch of salt has learned to breathe.

When a mirror-stalker strikes, it does not charge wildly.

It contracts.

Its body, dense and dry, can hold tension for long periods. At the moment of action, muscle and mineral fiber release in a sudden, silent snap, moving it across several lengths of ground without the patter of feet. Its jaws are crystalline plates rather than teeth. It bites through dry tissue with a pressure that cracks fiber and scatters powder. It does not swallow blood, because there is none. It consumes fibrous tissue, mineralized layers, crystalline nodes, and the stored chemical charges inside the body of its prey. After feeding, little remains but broken stalk-like material and dust that wind quickly scatters. The kill is not a red thing. It is a dry collapse.

Mirror-stalkers are solitary for most of their lives.

They move in long arcs across basins, following the paths where grazers disturb static layers. They rest in hollows where vapor collects. During pressure tides, when the air becomes heavy with charge, their surfaces may glow faintly at the edges, like salt catching a pale blue line. They do not call. They communicate by posture, color shift, and the way they disturb the ground with their feet. A female may leave a trail of shallow depressions that a male reads hours later; the pattern is a message without language.

There are smaller predators as well.

The hollow-spines live among glass-reeds. They are slender, segmented, and nearly weightless, moving along stalks with a motion that seems to borrow the reeds’ own rhythm. They sense vibration through the nodes of the plants. When a veil-ranger lands near their hiding place, the hollow-spine may leap between stalks without sound, catching the smaller creature with stiff, dry jaws that act more like forceps than mouths. They are predators of dust, spore, and small charged life, and they often kill without seeming to. A body dropped from a height on this plateau falls quietly, clicks once against stone, and is gone before anything larger arrives.

The static-sifters are more difficult to classify.

They are low, wide-bodied creatures that live along ridge lines where wind and charge meet. Their surfaces are fringed with needle-like mineral hairs that can hold electrical charge. They do not chase prey. Instead, they walk slowly through dust currents and charged aerosol layers, collecting particles on their own bodies. Tiny chime-mites, dust-lattices, and microscopic organisms adhere to their static fringe, and the creature scrapes them away with specialized mouth filaments. It is predation without pursuit, feeding without violence. It is also dispersal, because the dust it carries is often the same dust that seeds new groves.

The scavengers are patient, too.

When a long-strider dies, or a mirror-stalker leaves remains, the plateau does not waste the body. The first arrivals are not loud or eager. They are small, dry, and quick. Dust-hunters come, no larger than a hand, with broad reflective shells and legs that move in rapid, tapping patterns. They scrape away mineralized tissue, collect dust-caked fibers, and carry tiny fragments back to sheltered crevices. Their excreted powder, rich in carbonates and metal oxides, becomes a patch of fertile substrate. Lattice-worms follow, burrowing through remains and turning them into porous ground. Breath-mats spread over the site. Within a few seasons, a place of death becomes a small field of growth, pale and chalky, almost white, as if the dead has been folded back into the living surface.

This is why the plateau can be so abundant and yet so empty.

Life here does not form continuous forests in the familiar sense. It forms concentrations. It appears as grove, ring, line, band, mat, and field, separated by stretches of salt, dust, stone, light, and silence. A traveler might walk for hours across nothing but white crust and see no movement. Then a line of glass-reeds would appear along a hidden vein, tall and dry, shimmering as if the ground were exhaling life in solid form. Around it would gather dust-lattices, chime-mites, static-fleas, and perhaps the pale imprint of a strider’s footfall. The absence around the grove would not make the life less full. It would define it.

The inhabitants have no tools.

No one here has ever used a detached object to change the world. There are no stones shaped into edges, no fibers spun into binding, no fires made by friction, no walls built from gathered stone. The bodies of the living have already become instruments. The glass-reed is a resonator and collector. The mica-fan is a membrane and sensor. The long-strider’s tongue is a scraper. The mirror-stalker’s belly is a receiver of vibration. The chime-mite’s leg is a brush. To have tools would be redundant. Their forms are already technologies of survival, but they are technologies grown, not made.

This lack of tool-use does not mean lack of complexity.

The plateau has its own ways of making meaning. It does not have birdsong, but it has pressure. It does not have speech, but it has charge. It does not have architecture, but it has growth patterns that repeat across basins. It does not have fire, but it has static. It does not have rivers, but it has dust lanes that move for hundreds of kilometers and carry organisms, minerals, and signs of weather. A long-strider herd crossing the salt at the edge of a grove may change the electrical field of an entire basin. A grove opening its fronds during a vapor tide may alter the movement of dust overhead. A mirror-stalker lying still for days may become part of the camouflage pattern of the salt itself. These are not thoughts in a human sense, but they are responses. They are the plateau knowing itself.

If one insists on asking who lives there, the answer might be layered.

At the lowest level, life lives here as particles: dust-lattices, spore-crystals, salt-encased seed-bodies, and static motes suspended in clear air. They are almost invisible, yet they connect everything. They feed, reproduce, die, and become the chemistry from which larger forms grow. They are the smallest inhabitants, and perhaps the most numerous.

At another level, life lives here as plants that are also networks. A grove is not merely a collection of stalks. It is a patterned field of tissue, mineral, charge, and memory. It responds to vapor, pressure, and light. It opens, folds, darkens, brightens, and spreads across salt in pulses too slow for a visitor to notice. Some groves may be hundreds or thousands of years old, their outer fronds growing over the fossilized lattice of earlier forms. They do not think as a brain would, but they retain information in structure: in strata of calcified growth, in the angle of fronds aligned by decades of wind, in the mineral signature of ancient basins.

At another level, life lives here as animals. The long-striders are perhaps the closest thing the plateau has to individuals with continuous movement, memory, and social relation. They know the land by pressure, dust, charge, and the patterns of vapor. They read the groves not as scenery but as fields of meaning. A strider may remember, in its body’s response system, where a basin has been rich for decades and where a wind lane has shifted. Its herd does not need a spoken name for a place; it has a path, a static signature, and a line of footfalls.

And if one stretches the word “inhabitant” farther, the plateau itself becomes a kind of being.

Not a god, not a person, not a made thing. But a vast, slow, integrated life-field in which every organism contributes to the chemical and electrical grammar of the whole. A pressure tide moves dust. Dust feeds plants. Plants gather charge. Charge opens spore-pods. Spore-pods release seed-crystals. Seed-crystals settle in salt. Salt holds the dead. The dead become substrate. The substrate feeds new growth. In such a place, individual beings are moments in a continuous dry metabolism. They are not lesser for it. They are simply part of a larger living pattern.

The most haunting inhabitants, for those who look closely, are the ones that blur the line between creature and landscape.

There are hollow basins where the floor seems to pulse faintly with pale color. This may be a breath-mat responding to vapor. There are ridges where the ground is threaded with dark veins, and those veins seem to open little mouths from which stalks rise. This may be not only geology but biological uptake, a place where the land has learned to exhale forms. There are groves that stand so aligned with wind and light that they appear to have been planted. They have not. They have grown because each frond, each node, each membrane has found its angle. The apparent order is not design. It is the accumulated result of chemistry, charge, and patience.

At dawn, when the upper rims ignite with white fire, the plateau is full of beings waiting for the world to become active.

The lower fields remain cool and gray. The salt is pale. The stone seems hard enough to outlast thought. But if one stands close enough, the surface begins to answer. A glass-reed emits a thin note as thermal expansion starts. A mica-fan clicks as its membrane dries and shifts. A chime-mite moves over a stalk and makes a sound like one grain of sand dropped onto a polished blade. In the distance, a line of long-striders appears along a ridge, their dorsal vanes catching the first light, their footfalls sending small arcs through the dust. They move slowly, deliberately, as if crossing the surface of a bright dry ocean. They do not splash. They glide, tap, and spark. The groves lean toward them without bending like ordinary plants; they change charge state, color, and surface angle, reading the disturbance.

The plateau is not silent. It is merely listening.

At night, other inhabitants become visible. The dust-lattices catch starlight and hang in faint silver lanes above basins. The static-fleas become almost invisible, resting in crevices where the ground cools. The mirror-stalkers lie still, their surfaces faintly blue-edged by stored charge. The salt-wheat fields reflect the stars in broken white points. If a pressure wind comes, the whole grove of glass-reeds begins to chime. The sound is not from one place. It comes from many places, spread out across kilometers, as if the air itself has learned to make small metal notes. This is the voice of dry life.

There are no ruins because there are no builders.

There are no roads because there is no need to cut the land. There are no settlements because there is no fire to keep, no water to store, no walls to raise against wet or weather. The life here does not oppose the plateau. It continues it. Every being is made of the same ancient mineral language. The plants are not rooted in soil as soil is imagined below; they are rooted in gradient, charge, gas, and stone. The animals are not built for water or blood; they are built for dust, light, and static exchange. The smallest lives are not hidden inside damp earth; they ride the air as powder and settle on surfaces like fine frost that is not wet.

So if asked, “Who lives there?” the answer is:

the glass-reeds, whose hollow bodies whistle with the wind;
the copper-thread thickets, which darken with vapor and pale with dryness;
the mica-fans, angled like living shields toward a bright sky;
the salt-wheats, almost white, catching light in crystalline fields;
the breath-mats, which seem to breathe through stone;
the dust-lattices, invisible motes suspended in charged air;
the chime-mites, walking over fronds like tiny metal rain;
the lattice-worms, turning dead bodies into ground;
the static-fleas, riding herds through dry currents of force;
the veil-rangers, if one watches closely, small dry creatures moving between groves, carrying spore-dust on their wings and landing with a faint spark;
the long-striders, crossing salt flats like a moving line of weather;
the salt-shells, living boulders that become new earth when they die;
the mirror-stalkers, flat and patient, waiting for the herd to pass;
the hollow-spines, flickering between reeds;
the static-sifters, collecting life from the air without chase;
the dust-hunters, following death with small, clicking feet.

And still that is not all.

Because the plateau also contains the spaces between inhabitants, the intervals of salt, the dust lanes, the faint electrostatic halos around groves, the pressure tides, the sharp light, the cold stone, and the vast open silence. Those are not empty. They are part of the ecology. A long-strider does not live only in its body; it lives in its relationship to the ground’s charge. A glass-reed does not live only in itself; it lives in the vapor it filters and the spark it holds. A dust-lattice does not live alone; it lives in the breath of the basin.

Life here is lush because it has found a way to be full without wetness.

It is full of surfaces angled toward light. Full of fibers that can transmit charge. Full of pores that can taste the air. Full of shells that become scaffolds. Full of tiny clicking movements that accumulate into weather. Full of animals rare enough that their passing feels like an event. Full of dead that remain useful, becoming stone, becoming substrate, becoming the ground from which future forms rise. Full of spaces that are not gaps but pauses, like the silence between notes in a chime that comes from many stalks at once.

If there is a true “whoever” in this place, it is not a single creature with a name.

The closest candidate is the grove-mind, not as a brain but as a pattern: a basin that opens and closes with vapor, that changes color, that holds memory in calcified layers, that answers the approach of a herd by shifting its static field. Another candidate is the strider-line: a moving body of social animals that reads the plateau through pressure and charge, that moves in a long dry procession, that leaves behind not footprints in mud but patterns in the world’s electrical dust. Another is the mirror-stalker: solitary, still, aware enough to wait for days without noise, knowing the land through vibration and field. Another is the dust-cloud: billions of tiny lives suspended in clear air, connecting grove to grove, basin to basin, death to growth.

But perhaps the most accurate answer is this:

the plateau lives.

Not in the way a mountain “lives,” which is to say not at all. But in the way a dry, bright, ancient surface can become a continuous system of exchange. The living things here have not escaped the land; they have completed it. They are the land’s way of conducting charge, filtering vapor, scattering dust, and remembering chemistry. They are the plateau’s skin made of fronds, shells, membranes, and hooves. They are the bright world’s own slow metabolism.

And if a visitor could stand there, surrounded by the pale groves, listening to the wind hiss through hollow stalks, watching the long-striders move across salt that glitters like teeth, seeing the faint blue shimmer of static gathering at the edges of a thicket, they would understand, eventually, that emptiness here is not absence.

It is a different kind of fullness.

A fullness made of dryness.
A lushness made of mineral light.
A life made of breath, stone, charge, and silence.
