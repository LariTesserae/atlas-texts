No one lives there alone.

That is the first thing to understand about the inhabitants: the place does not contain a species the way a forest contains a stag, or a house contains a family, or a coastline contains a gull. There are too many layers, too many contacts, too many surfaces touching other surfaces, for anything so simple to appear. If a stranger entered, expecting to find “the creatures,” the creatures would not present themselves as a clean object. They would be distributed. They would be in the dust, on the salt, beneath the crystal, inside the warmth, and between one another. They would be less like animals than like a condition the stone permits itself.

The smallest of them are not wholly separate from the air.

Those drifting motes that move as if carried by a current that does not exist are not dust in any ordinary sense. They are tiny static-born forms, transparent and angular, with no wings, no legs, no obvious body. They are too small to be called creatures by surface habits, yet they do more than fall. They hover. They turn in slow, accidental geometries. They approach a glowing crystal facet as though recognizing a temperature they need. They settle, briefly, into a cluster of salt filaments, and for a moment they seem to become part of the cluster’s faint pulse. Then they lift again, not because they have rested, but because their presence in that spot has completed something too small to observe.

They are the most tentative life here. They do not eat in any way a stomach would recognize. They drink the charge held between crystals. When two mineral bodies touch and a thin tone rises, some of them are drawn toward the seam, not to hunt the tone, not to follow the sound, but to stand within the electrical afterimage left by the contact. They pass through these afterimages and emerge faintly warmed, as though they had touched a lamp without flame.

Around them grow the non-plant things.

There are thin filaments like brittle glass, but they bend. They are transparent except where faint heat moves through them, a yellowish or violet glow passing along the thread like a slow thought. They rise from the crust in tufts, and when touched they do not wilt; they answer. A being with a dry shell presses one limb against a tuft, and the tuft changes its angle. Not dramatically. Not with surprise. Simply by rearranging itself, as if the touch had been expected for a long time, or as if expectation were the only form of time it knows.

There are crusts like burned lace, dark and porous, pulsing when warmth changes. These crusts are not moss, not fungus in any familiar sense, not mineral in any inert sense. They are a border state: a surface that conducts, a skin that remembers, a tissue that can be stepped on, rested upon, scraped by a tool, and yet remains neither wounded nor healed. A creature lying against one of these crusts does not merely take heat from it. The creature and the crust enter a brief calibration. The crust’s faint red flicker slows or quickens. The creature’s plates settle into a new alignment. Neither changes kind. Neither becomes something new. But for a moment, the relationship is exact.

There are crystalline rosettes as well, pale blooms grown in dry air. They are salt-like without water, glass-like without fire. They open from shelves and cones in geometric flowers too regular to be accidental and too dry to be botanical. They can be stepped on, touched, held to another crystal, used as a tool. Some of them are inhabited by smaller things that nest inside their facets. Some of them are the remains of things that were once almost separate. Some are neither. To classify them would require a category the world does not provide.

That is what life is here: a dense continuum from “stone” to “being.”

The glass-shelled crawlers are among the most obvious animals, if obvious is the right word for something so small and so numerous. They have six legs or eight or some number that seems less fixed than convenient. Their shells are transparent, but not like glass on a window; they are the kind of transparency that lets light pass while hiding the interior from the surface observer. Inside them there are no organs that would be recognizable, only facets, dark dust, and faint moving warmth. Their legs tap with dry precision, each footfall a measurement. They read the land by rhythm.

They crawl along salt ridges in lines that seem meaningful. They are not migrating. They are not foraging. They are reading surfaces, checking the warmth of crystal seams, testing whether a shelf has become resonant enough for the next creature to lie upon. When they meet, they do not stop. They touch one another’s shells with brief clicks, then separate, continuing their slow routes. If they are communicating, the content is minimal: a temperature, a pressure, the presence of a suitable surface, the existence of an adjacent pattern. They do not need to speak of danger, because there is nothing lurking. They do not need to speak of scarcity, because the place is already full.

There are also dust-readers, flatter and wider, whose bodies look like thin shields left on stone. They glide over crusts with no visible mouth or eye. Their surfaces are covered in tiny pits that open and close, not breathing, not filtering water, but sampling static. They feed by moving over mineral fields the way a hand moves over velvet. The motion is not pleasure. It is collection. They gather charges from places where crystal and salt meet in tight, awkward junctions. When they are full, they lie still, and their plates emit a faint copper gleam. They do not sleep. They settle. The distinction would matter only to creatures that needed to dream.

Then there are the herd animals.

They are larger, though not always larger in a way that gives the observer a comfortable scale. Some stand as tall as a person’s chest; others are long and low, their bodies spread like living shelves. Their hides are thin as parchment, almost translucent where the inner warmth presses outward. Their faces are folded back against the rock, not in pain, but in the ordinary posture of feeding. They press their faces to warm mineral surfaces and absorb heat through pores that are not pores. They drink dryness the way a lake might drink light, not as substance, but as a condition.

They do not graze. There is no grass, no leaf, no root to bite. They stand in rows or in overlapping groups where the crust glows faintly red beneath their bellies. Their movement is slow and economical, never hurried. They are not hungry with lack. They are full with a fullness that requires constant re-establishment. A herd moves only because the density demands it: one animal settles, another shifts, a third turns a plate to allow a smaller being access to the warmth beneath. There is no feeding chain, no collapse, no season. There is a continuous arrangement of bodies pressing close.

Some herd animals carry life on their backs.

Their broad salt plates become shelves. Glass-shelled crawlers nest along the seams of their hides. Dust-motes gather in the warm depressions between their ridges. Mineral-fungal crusts grow in lines along their flanks, fed by body heat and the faint static produced by their movements. When two herd animals stand close together, their plates sometimes align and produce a chime, a low metallic tone that passes through the ground and is answered by other plates farther away. That sound is not music. It is adjustment. The herd is tuning itself.

There are creatures that seem to be neither animals nor places.

Some beings are so densely layered with other beings that the line between individual and colony fails. A flat salt-shelled thing may lie motionless for ages, and upon it grow filaments, crusts, and smaller crawlers. The crust pulses through its shell. The filaments bend when the shell shifts. The smaller creatures ride and rest and feed upon it. At some point it becomes impossible to say whether the large animal is the host, the colony, or merely the shape taken by many lives pressed together. The place does not object. It has no need for a clean taxonomy.

There are also things that unfold from stone.

They begin as apparent formations on the ground: a ridge, a fold, a dark seam, a cluster of salt cones. When approached, they separate. Not violently, not with the suddenness of prey being startled. They unfold the way a book opens when held in the hand for a moment. Edges lift. Thin plates become limbs. A shape that seemed fixed becomes mobile only because the observer changed the local pressure, the warmth, the resonance. They then crawl, or lie, or touch nearby surfaces, and after a time fold back into what they were.

They are not born in the ordinary way. They are not dead when they close. They are arrangements the land can activate temporarily.
