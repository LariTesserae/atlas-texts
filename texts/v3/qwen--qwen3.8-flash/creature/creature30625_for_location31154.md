Around the broad black mirror of obsidian at the basin’s heart, the settlement does not gather so much as breathe.

The oldest structures rise there not as walls but as continuations of the stone: fitted platforms curving over older foundations, low cisterns worn into perfect circles, shallow channels where water beads along the lip and remains a moment longer than expected. The obsidian flat itself is used less as floor than as a kind of common eye, its surface reflecting the luminous ceiling until the whole place appears to have a second light beneath it. In this brightness, where the air smells of wet chalk, mineral dust, and something faintly yeast-sweet, the inhabitants move in careful, separated paths.

At a distance, one might think they were people.

Up close, the resemblance becomes stranger and more complete. They are human-sized, yes, with two arms, two legs, faces, hands, postures that recall human posture. But they are worked over by the basin as if the basin had taken some old idea of “person” and polished it, added layers, removed unnecessary edges. They have broad backs and dense limbs, torsos compacted into a low center of gravity, arms long and strong from reaching into cisterns, climbing mica-slick ledges, pressing fungal skins into place. Their shoulders are not merely rounded; they are mineralized. The outer layer of skin across the upper body has hardened into fine crystalline flakes, pale as frost or old bone, shedding in small sheets when they stand too long in dry places. Beneath that is softer flesh, cool to the touch, threaded with faint blue-gray veins that pulse slowly with a fluid only a little thicker than water.

They do not have hair, or not as surface creatures would understand it. Across the skull, shoulders, forearms, and backs grow fine white filaments, not exactly fur and not exactly fungus, but a symbiotic skin of pale fungal down that absorbs moisture from the air and darkens or brightens depending on the dampness around them. When the basin is preparing to shed salt, those filaments stand out in a subtle shiver of crystalline dust. When a new bloom of minerals begins, they darken faintly, as if tasting the change before it reaches the ground.

Their faces are the least ambiguous. They have faces, yes, with brows, cheeks, mouths, eyes—features that would be recognizable if not so softened by use. Their brows are flat ridges, useful for guiding condensation away from the eyes. The skin around their cheekbones has small damp pores that release a clear brine when touched, a fluid that tastes of iron and stone. Their lips are thin, slightly chapped at the corners, often streaked with salt from wiping away the slow sweat of their own faces. Their teeth are not sharp but blunt and ridged, shaped to crush fungal mats, shell-meat, and the chalky crusts of mineral tubers. Their tongues are dark and furrowed along the center, and when they taste the air they sometimes press them against the roof of the mouth as if listening for water.

Eyes are their most unsettling feature. They are wide, wet, and set somewhat low on the face, adapted to the diffuse, heatless light of the basin. The irises are silvery or pale amber, flecked with tiny black points, and the pupils do not dilate dramatically in the way surface eyes do. They are lids that seem always slightly moist, lined with a crystalline film that clears salt from the surface. In the brightest hollows, where the light seems to thicken like liquid glass, their eyes catch the glow and hold it, so that when they look at you, you feel as if you are being seen through water.

They do not hear as the surface world hears. Sound in the basin is swallowed, muted, softened. The inhabitants have learned to rely on pressure, rhythm, and vibration. Their ears are wide, low-set, and partly enclosed in thin shells of chitin—pieces of shed animal plates they cultivate on their own bodies by embedding small fragments near the temples and along the sides of the neck. These fragments do not serve only as ornament. They become tuning points. When a wall sheds salt, when a fissure opens far beneath a platform, when a pool drains through a crack below the floor, a trained basin-born person can feel the change first as a faint pressure against the cheekbone or a ripple through the chitin ear-shields.

Their hands are broad, with palms ridged by fine mineral calluses that grip wet stone without noise. Fingertips are damp and slightly concave, able to hold a bead of water long after it has slid away from a drier hand. Their nails are not keratin alone; they are often reinforced with thin slivers of obsidian or shed shell, set beneath the skin like tiny plates. This gives them a strange, double-edged look. When they work, the nails make almost no sound. They press, pry, scrape, and peel with a silence that makes them seem to be part of the landscape rather than beings moving through it.

The feet are similar: wide, flat, with pads like old leather and toes able to separate and grip. They wear no shoes unless traveling across a fresh crystal bloom. Instead, they bind their feet in strips of fungal skin, which dry stiff and then soften when moistened, forming a second sole that absorbs pressure and muffles the footfall. Even when bare, they step as if afraid of waking the stone.

Their clothing is minimal, not because they are cold, but because their bodies have already become a kind of architecture. They wear plates of shed chitin over chest, shins, and shoulders, laced with cordage spun from fungal fibers. Some wear translucent sheets of mica over the torso, cut into rectangles and rounded corners, which catch the light and give them the appearance of moving glass houses. Others decorate themselves with copper beads, glass rings, and flakes of iron so thin they seem almost like black leaves. A finished garment may take years to assemble: this plate from a grazer, this fastener from old cordage, this salt-crusted membrane dried from a communal cistern, this strip of obsidian polish rubbed smooth by generations of thumbs.

Names among them are not spoken as surface names are. Because the basin swallows sound, and because shouting would be almost useless, names are formed through pattern. A child receives a name by touch: a sequence of taps, strokes, and pressure-points along the palm and wrist, learned by the hands of the family. Later, when called from across a platform, that name may be left in a small arrangement of salt grains, a flick of a finger, or a particular rhythm tapped on stone. Some names are so long they require a hand to trace on a flat surface before they are recognized. Others are almost invisible, a single breath, a tap of knuckle to shoulder. They are not “silent names,” exactly. They are names suited to a world where noise is scarce, attention is slow, and space is generous.

The settlement itself is a reflection of this attention.

It is not built from the center outward or from the street inward. It is built from the land outward, a scattering of low houses, platforms, pens, drying frames, cisterns, and communal halls placed where the stone has already learned to bear weight. Nothing rises into a tower. The inhabitants prefer to stay close to the ground, because the ground is the first sign of change. A dwelling may begin as a simple raised floor supported on stone pegs. Later, when a new mineral shelf forms nearby, a side wall is added. When an old spring becomes a kitchen channel, the dwelling shifts toward it. When a wall stops shedding salt, rooms are built along it. Houses are repaired not by replacement but by negotiation.

A typical home is low, broad, and slightly curved, built of dry-stacked stone, obsidian slivers, and stretched fungal membranes roofed with mica sheets. Inside, there is little division. Platforms serve as bed, worktable, drying space, and storage. Niches are cut into walls to hold salt cups, shells, obsidian knives, bone wedges, copper needles, and small bundles of white filaments harvested for cordage. Every floor has at least one drain—a shallow channel leading to a catch basin or a crack through which excess moisture can escape. Every roof has a collection lip, because condensation is not waste; it is harvest.

The people wake when the basin’s light changes, which is not often and never dramatically. There is no sun. There is no dawn. The luminescence may dim slightly when the mineral veins shift beneath the ground, or brighten when moisture moves through crystalline salt. There is no true night, though some periods feel more like “low hours,” when the air cools further, when the black threads drink more from the pools, when the fungal mats exhale their faint, breadlike smell. The inhabitants sleep in shallow arcs of warmth, lying in the hollows where their bodies have pressed the stone smooth. Their sleep is not long. The basin does not punish sleeplessness, but it rewards attention. A person who sleeps too deeply may miss a rising floor, a draining pool, or the soft click of new crystal forming above an entryway.

Morning, if one calls it that, begins with checking the water.

A family may leave its sleeping platform and go first to the nearest cistern, not to fetch a cup, but to listen. They tap the stone with a bone wedge, press a palm to the damp face of the basin, watch the way droplets gather along the lip. If the beading is too sparse, they adjust a channel. If the water trembles, they may pull back and wait. If a crack has opened nearby, they mark it with a small pile of white salt, a kind of quiet warning that only other people who understand will read.

Then they begin to work.

Some harvest fungal mats. This is done with thin obsidian blades and bone wedges, scraping away pale ripples only after checking the underside for mineral hardness. A mat too wet becomes useless in storage; too dry becomes brittle and loses flavor. The best mats are those whose color shifts under the hand, moving from pale white to faint ochre, then darkening slightly as they dry. They are laid on drying frames to become cakes or skins, sometimes stacked, sometimes hung like sheets of parchment. These cakes are eaten fresh, soaked in brine, or ground into pastes. They have a strange taste—neither meat nor vegetable nor bread, but something closer to mineral sweetness, with a dry finish that lingers at the back of the throat.

Others tend the grazing animals.

The grazers of the basin are broad-backed, pale, slow-moving creatures with translucent shells and damp, eyeless faces. The inhabitants do not drive them. Driving would be pointless and, worse, disruptive. Instead, the herd-keepers lay chitin plates along paths, smear salt water in channels, and open or close small drainage gaps. The grazers follow the moisture and the mineral bloom. Their herders move alongside them at a distance, silent, carrying obsidian blades for peeling mats and copper needles for stitching torn membranes. When a grazer sheds, the keepers collect the discarded shell with great care. Shells are used for plates, weights, pins, blades, roofing scales, and the curved ear-shields worn by people and children.

Children learn the animals before they learn many other things. They are taught to watch the way the grazers’ wide mouths press the ground, because the animals will always find moisture first. They learn not to stand too close when a grazer drinks, because the water trembles before it moves and a child who understands tremble will step back before the liquid spills. They learn that the shells can be warm where the body beneath them is cool, and that the underside of a shell may hold fine threads of living fungal down, soft as ash, which children are allowed to pluck and braid into tiny cords.

The floating creatures that rise and settle in the condensation are watched more carefully. The basin-born treat them as signs. When those small shell-like beings gather in a hollow, the water there is usually changing. When they scatter without cause, the ground may be opening a new fissure.
