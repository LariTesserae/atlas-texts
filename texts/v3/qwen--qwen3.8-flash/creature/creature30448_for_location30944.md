What lives there does not live in houses. It lives in thresholds: between heat and stone, between steam and cold metal, between mineral crust and water film. There are no crowds, no children’s footprints, no market cries, no names carved over doors. The inhabitants are sparse, strange, and mostly surface-dwellers: colonies, grazing insects, blind reservoir things, waiting moths, patient predators, and—perhaps—the city itself, if one is willing to call a working process a being.

The true residents begin at the microscopic level. Over basalt, over cut stone, over ceramic and corroded iron, a living skin grows: ochre bacterial mats, black thermal films, white crystalline molds, silvery lichens, and branching filaments like glass thread. They are not plants. They do not need light as plants do, because the pale gray sun here is never enough to govern a world. They feed on heat differentials, on dissolved minerals, on the chemical tension between warm stone and condensed vapor. Where a vent breathes, they crystallize into brittle armor. Where steam pools, they soften and spread. Where a surface is built to catch moisture, they form first, making the stone itself into a kind of food. They are not a garden. They are a metabolism of the terrain, a living record of temperature.

The visible creatures are few, but each one seems fitted to the place with an almost engineering-like precision.

The pale six-legged beetles are the city’s cleaners. They cross condenser basins and shallow reservoirs, their shells slick with warm film, their slow legs finding purchase on rounded stone. They do not search for leaves or soil; they search for the bacterial skins and mineral deposits that accumulate after each breathing cycle of steam. A beetle moving across a basin is not a visitor, not an intruder. It is part of the maintenance: scraping, grazing, disturbing just enough to expose fresh stone, then carrying back organic dust into cracks where its larvae hatch. The larvae are white and soft, living in pores, feeding on microbial film, blind in the dark interiors of the rock. They are the beetles’ waiting phase, the next generation hidden inside the city’s warm crevices.

The eel-like things are creatures of still water. They lie in the shallow reservoirs where condensation gathers, motionless for long periods, their translucent skins showing the blue pulse beneath. They do not hunt with speed. Their world is too heavy with warm moisture for frantic movement. Instead, they are ambushers of slow cycles. They sense pressure, heat, chemical change, the faint disturbance of a larva slipping from a crack or the drift of salt-dust settling on the surface. They feed on the small dead things, the microscopic debris, the mineral-rich organisms that fall from the towers and mats. Their bodies are filtered vessels of patience. They belong to the reservoir as much as the water itself, and the reservoir is the city’s lung and stomach.

The ash-moths are creatures of the taller structures. They cling to the cooling ribs of the towers, their dull gray wings pressed against ceramic and metal, waiting for the moisture cycle. They are not colorful, because color has no purpose in a place where light is thin and steam is gray. They wait for the dust of crystallized salts, for dead microbial film, for the powdery residue that condenser masts shed when vapor turns to water and leaves mineral behind. Their life is one of timing: emergence at the right breath of fog, feeding in the brief moment when the tower’s surface is damp enough to release food. Their larvae spin through salt crust and bacterial film. Their bodies, when they die, become part of the surface again, another layer added to the city’s skin. They are not pollinators; there is nothing ordinary to pollinate. They are distributors of mineral life, carrying microscopic food to crevices, seeding the stone.

The predators are the thin-necked lizards with glassy scales. They move near the lower channels, in the shadows between warm and cooler surfaces. They are not large, not abundant, not noisy. They hunt beetle larvae, perhaps weak or fallen eels, perhaps moth larvae loosened from a crevice. Their bodies are built for thermal economy, not for endurance. They slide from one shadowed stone to another because the environment itself tells them where life may appear. A patch of condensation opens a feeding ground. A receding film reveals a crust of salt. A valve click means a pipe warms, which means something else moves. The lizards are the city’s visible nervous system: still, watchful, reacting to the smallest change in heat and water.

But there is also a stranger inhabitance.

The city itself appears to be occupied, though no bodies are seen. Channels open before steam reaches a basin. Pumps begin at the exact moment condensation gathers. Gates lift and spill water onto mineral beds. Lights adjust without hands. Engines hum as though dreaming. If there are citizens, they have vanished so completely into their works that only the works remain. If there are not, then the city behaves as if attended by a ghostly intention, a maintenance ritual left running long after the last performer. The absence is not empty. It is arranged. The distances, the terraces, the masts, the reservoirs, the slow machines—these are all part of a pattern that functions like a body without a brain.

In that sense, the place is alive as a system. The flywheels turn like organs. The pipes carry water and heat like blood. The condenser masts draw in vapor and release it like lungs. The reservoirs breathe, fill, and fall. The streets are channels more than roads, and even their slopes are a kind of pulse. The beetles, eels, moths, lizards, and microbial skins are the city’s biology, its small warm inhabitants. The engines are its skeleton and heartbeat. The steam is its breath. The emptiness is not vacancy. It is spacing, like the distance between ribs, or between neurons, or between the notes of a very slow song.

If a person walked there, they would not meet a population. They would meet processes. They would feel their feet grow warm on the basalt, see the fog gather and break, hear the distant click of metal cooling, watch the salt skins expand by a hairline. They would see a pale beetle appear from a crack, or notice the blue pulse of an eel in a reservoir no larger than a room but still too vast to fill with life. They would not find homes, because no one lives here in the human way. They would find a settlement scaled to absence, a city whose inhabitants are not primarily bodies but cycles.

What lives there, then, is the hot wet stone that grows skins of chemistry. It is the beetle that grazes the condenser basin. It is the blind eel that waits in the reservoir. It is the ash-moth that feeds on salt dust in the towers. It is the lizard that moves through shadow to shadow. It is the bacterial mat, the glassy filament, the white mold that seems to eat steam. It is the pump that begins without a hand, the valve that clicks without a voice, the city that breathes without lungs.

And perhaps, underneath all of it, there is a kind of consciousness too slow to notice, too distributed to name: the awareness of a place that has become a machine, the patience of a machine that has become landscape, and the quiet strangeness of a landscape that still lives.
