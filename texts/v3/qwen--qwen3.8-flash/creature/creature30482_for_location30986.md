## What lives there

What lives there is difficult to name because the place refuses the ordinary distinction between creature and house.

The residents of the hollow are not a species. They are a collective of very slow bodies that have learned to touch without stopping. If you call one of them an individual, it is likely already lying down. If you call one a building, it is likely alive. If you call one an animal, it may only be passing through.

The basin is too small for wilderness, too wet and dense for emptiness, too young for true antiquity, and too crowded to be anything as simple as a thicket of plants. Life there has arrived from every direction—seed blown from a higher slope, spore carried on frost, rhizome dragged out of a crevice, fragment dropped from a dying mat—and once inside, it has not been able to leave. Water has drawn it inward. Cold has held it still. The steep walls have given it shelter and confinement. Over seasons short enough to be counted by a human, long enough to be felt as centuries by slower creatures, the hollow has filled itself into an almost solid body.

So the first inhabitants are not “things with eyes and appetites,” as we tend to imagine life. The first inhabitants are forms that live by becoming surfaces.

They begin as mosses.

The mosses of the bowl are not gentle velvet. They are civic tissue. They make cushions so tightly packed that they can be cut with difficulty, their layers compacted like frozen bread, like felt pressed under the weight of centuries, even though the centuries are only a handful. From the rim inward, the moss rises in terraced mounds, each mound a separate weather system. The top is dry by the standards of the basin: silver-green, pale, bristling with tiny capsules. The underside is black-green, sodden, threaded with rootlets and fungal filaments. When the air warms even slightly, the moss expands. When it freezes, it contracts. It breathes with the day.

These moss-mounds are not homes in which something else lives. They are themselves the home. A mound shelters a pool above it, and that pool shelters a mat below it, and the mat shelters a cavity in which a pale lichen-crab rests. Remove the mound, and the water collapses into chaos. Remove the water, and the mound dies. There is no hierarchy of owner and dwelling here, only mutual dependence.

Near the moss grow the glass-rosettes.
