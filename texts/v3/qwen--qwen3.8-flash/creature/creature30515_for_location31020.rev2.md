No person lives there in the ordinary sense.

There are no hearths, no roads laid by hands, no houses with rooms assigned to families, no doors that were once made by tools. If a human were to come, the city would offer openings where there were no doors, warm hollows where there were no beds, and passages that seemed to be waiting for feet, but this would not mean the place belonged to the visitor. The basin is too occupied to be claimed. It is already full of inhabitants; it is simply full of inhabitants who cannot be pointed at the way one points to a man standing in a doorway.

What lives there is, first of all, the city itself.

Not a city as a metaphor, and not a ruin, and not a forest pretending to be a town. A city that is also an animal-shaped ecology, a basin that has learned to organize living bodies into streets, walls, courts, thresholds, bridges, rooms, and roofs. The whole settlement behaves as if it is one enormous, slow, distributed creature, or as if many creatures have made an agreement so old that they have forgotten the difference between architecture and anatomy. Its trunks are towers. Its streets are circulation. Its membranes are windows. Its mosses are roofs and cushions and, sometimes, benches. Its root-floors are the pale bones of the town. Its apertures are not doors so much as reflexes: a wall opens because something approaching disturbs the air, or perhaps because the wall wishes to know what is there.

The walls are residents.

They are not silent barriers. They are thickets that have learned to stand still in geometries, that have arranged themselves into corridors and courtyards without knowing they are doing anything as formal as building. A wall in the basin is a body that breathes on a scale too slow for ordinary patience. Its surface is furred with moss and pale threads that are not quite fungus and not quite plant. Behind its bark is a network of roots, vines, living wood, and mineralized nodules warm to the touch. When pressed, a wall gives slightly, like flesh. When listened to, it hums. When spoken near, it may open a hollow it had no reason to open until that moment.

The streets are residents too.

The pale root-roads are not dead matter. They grow in patterns, tighten around footfalls, sink where water passes, and heal after being walked upon. They are alive in the way a tongue is alive, or a palm, or a spine: not moving dramatically, but changing with pressure, moisture, heat, and time. If one stands on a street for long enough, one can feel the faintest pulse beneath the surface, a slow hydraulic shiver moving through roots and fibers. The streets are not for traveling. The streets are travel itself made into geography. The city does not contain roads. The city is roads that have learned to be seen.

The ground is populated as densely as the air, but most of that population is too still or too small to be recognized at once. The moss is not simply moss. It is a citizen, a roof, a carpet, a listener, a sponge, a nursery, a record of water, and sometimes a kind of soft furniture that adjusts itself to weight. Tiny green organisms form patterns that look like streets and courtyards from above, but at close range they resemble a woven crowd. Between the roots, pale six-legged mammals rise from holes no larger than fists when the ground is disturbed, scattering with a sound like dry rice poured from a jar. They are among the city’s most literal residents: small, quick, blind-eyed in the way of animals that have learned the ground better than the sky. They dig and are dug over. They carry seed and are eaten by seed. They are street-dwellers in every sense.

The air lives.

Hanging roots, blossom nets, seed chains, curtains of pale thread, and translucent membranes of leaf all drift above the streets as though the town is suspended in a second city of filaments. These are not decorations. They are filters, roofs, ladders, resonators, breeding chambers, and nets for collecting moisture. Some of the thread-organisms hang between branches like the remains of a soft green sea. Others cling to walls and tremble when particular tones pass through. The city breathes through them, partly. The air is full of spores and pollen, but also of movement: insects do not merely fly here. They flow. They form ribbons of small living matter that follow warmth, dampness, and pressure rather than landmarks. They move like currents in a transparent river, and when a current turns sharply, it is because the city has made a sound that the insects understand.

The birds are perhaps the most obviously strange residents.

They are everywhere and nowhere in the way one expects. A branch can appear empty for a long minute, and then the same branch becomes dense with winged things so numerous that the shadow beneath them shifts like a dark leaf. Some birds have wings broad enough to seem made of feathered leaves, catching the ordinary sun in a green-gold way that makes the canopy look as if it is thinking in light. Some have voices that carry weight. When one of these birds calls, the air thickens for an instant, and nearby leaves turn toward it not from wind, but from attention. In the Avenue of Hanging Bells, the floral tubes sway and release tones too low for human ears to hear, yet the bellsingers hear them, and the mosses near the ground respond by leaning a few degrees toward the sound.

Other birds nest not in trees but in folds of the air itself, as though the local atmosphere had become a cloth capable of holding eggs. Other birds glide without moving their wings, passing above rooftops with the patience of stones falling upward. Some do not fly so much as tune themselves into the city’s standing waves, circling in long spirals, using sound and warmth as though the invisible were a ladder. These are not merely birds that happen to live in the town. They are the town’s bells, its messengers, its weather-readers, its living punctuation. A bird call here can change the behavior of a wall, summon a pool of water, cause a seed-mat to click in sequence, or reveal a corridor that was only a rumor a moment before.

The mammals are equally distributed and equally impossible to crowd.

There are long-necked root-ferrets that stand on hind legs to peer over hollows, their bodies pale as damp bark, their claws clicking against root-stones. They live in the spaces where walls thin into passages, and they seem to know where the city is about to open. There are broad-backed grazers with mouths like bellows, filtering spore-clouds from the air. Their backs carry moss, lichen, small epiphytes, and sometimes colonies of insects that use them as temporary gardens. They move slowly through courtyards and under leaf-vaults, and their passage is quiet except for the wet sound of their feeding, like cloth being pulled apart by patient hands. They do not eat grass so much as they eat atmosphere. Their bodies are part of the city’s air-treatment.

There are tiny creatures that resemble beetles and cats at once, moving in perfect silence despite the noise of the place. They leap from membrane to membrane, catch flying things in damp folds, and vanish before they can be examined closely. There are larger animals with soft, dark eyes and too many joints in their legs, standing so still among the tree-walls that they are mistaken for part of the architecture. When one of them moves, it does so in pieces, first the shoulder, then the long dark knee, then the whole body, as if it has decided only after consideration that moving would not disturb the room too much. These jointed watchers are not hidden. They are integrated. They are citizens of the town in the same way a buttress is a citizen of a cathedral: they stand where the city expects them to stand.

There are pale deer-like beings that graze along the edges of hollow squares, their ears rotating independently to sample the layers of noise. They do not eat by sight. They eat by resonance. When a sound-shape forms in the air, they turn their heads and move toward it, or away from it, according to whether it is safe, whether it is young, whether it has enough moisture to make their grazing worthwhile. A flock of birds may ride a standing wave of wind and tone nearby, circling endlessly without flapping, as if the city has given them a current to rest inside. Near the damp hollows, creatures like frogs with wide, leaf-like backs sit perfectly still for hours, then vanish in a flash when a particular pitch sounds. The pitch is not a warning in the ordinary sense. It is more like a key. It tells them when the air has opened and when it has closed.

The ecology does not function simply through hunger. It functions through tuning.

Every resident must learn what the place is making sound by. Flowers open only when a particular tone passes. Mosses grow faster where echoes linger. Fruits ripen in response to vibration rather than sunlight. Trees at the edge of the basin lean inward as if drawn by the city’s noise, while trees at the center grow in spirals around standing waves of sound. This means that the animals and plants are not merely living beside one another. They are living in response to one another. The birds are not only birds; they are architectural events. The walls are not only walls; they are listeners. The streets are not only streets; they are instruments. The water is not only water; it is a brief teacher, appearing where needed, draining away when excess would spoil a passage, and leaving behind pockets that reflect the sky with impossible accuracy.

Water, too, is a resident, though it is not permanent.

It comes from condensation that beads on every vertical surface at dawn and falls in short rainfalls of its own making. It fills channels carved into root-stones for hours, then empties. It appears in perfectly round pools set down overnight as if by a careful hand. It collects in pockets no larger than cups that persist for days, reflecting not only the sky above but sometimes parts of the city that are not directly visible: roofs nearby, doors elsewhere, or no reflection at all. Water in the basin behaves like a creature with a sense of timing. It appears where something needs moistening, drains where a root would rot, and gathers in hollows with the patience of a being that has learned to ration itself. It is not a lake. It is not a river. It is a series of small arrivals and departures, and the town has been shaped around this rhythm as surely as a human town around roads.

The city’s residents do not have fixed homes, because the homes are alive.

A building is a trunk of braided wood with interiors hollowed by time and habit. It is not inhabited by a separate creature so much as inhabited by the habits of many creatures at once. Birds nest in its upper chambers. Insects make currents through its membranes. Mosses soften its floors. Root-ferrets burrow beneath it. The larger jointed watchers stand in its shade as if they were its columns. To ask who lives in a building is like asking who lives in a lung. The answer is not one thing. It is an arrangement of lives, all of them performing the building’s function.

The city has districts, and each district has its own kinds of residents.

In the District of Pale Streets, where the root-roads glow faintly from within, the inhabitants are small, quick, and ground-minded: the six-legged mammals, the silent beetle-cats, the root-ferrets, and the mosses that grow in patterns resembling tiny footprints. The streets are clean and bright, not because anyone sweeps them, but because the root-fibers shed old growth when walked upon and the falling material is eaten by organisms beneath the surface. Here the city breathes most evenly. The walls lean away from one another as if giving space to light.

In the Hollow Square, where one enormous canopy rises above empty stone and drifting motes, the residents are mostly absent to the eye. The square is not empty. It is occupied by wind, sound, and flocks that ride invisible currents. The motes are alive: spores, pollen, insects, fragments of membrane, all suspended in a slow procession. If one stands at the center, the canopy overhead sounds like a held breath, and the birds circling above seem to be tracing a thought that has not yet decided where to land. The square is inhabited less by creatures than by the conditions that allow creatures to gather without needing to touch one another.

In the Court of Seeds, the ground is studded with objects like stones that open and shut in the wind. These seeds are not passive. They are houses, traps, instruments, and weather-readers. Small creatures live in them, eat them, guard them, or wait for them to open. Beetles crawl from seam to seam. Pale mammals nest in their shells. Some of the seeds click in sequences too regular to be accidental, and this clicking becomes part of the city’s sound. The Court of Seeds is a marketplace of potential lives: each closed object is a room not yet furnished, a door not yet answered.

In the Avenue of Hanging Bells, the residents include the bells themselves. The long floral tubes sway and emit tones too low to hear with the ears but not too low for the walls. They are not ornaments. They are sensory organs extended into the air. They make the avenue alive with pressure. Birds roost along them. Insect ribbons pass through their mouths. The vines that support them tighten or loosen depending on how much sound the avenue needs. Here the city is less a town than an instrument being tuned by wind.

Some residents are not visible bodies at all.

There is a courtyard where the air feels thick enough to touch. Its walls are pale root-columns, evenly spaced, each faintly vibrating. At its center is a shallow depression always slightly damp, no matter how dry the surrounding ground becomes. Moss has grown along the perimeter into the shapes of benches. Birds overhead move in a slow coordinated way, not because they are being directed, but because the tone of the place invites them into pattern. This courtyard feels prepared for an audience, but the audience is not human. It may be the city listening to itself. The resident of that place is the sound so dense it becomes pressure, the humidity that has learned to remain, the moss that has learned to offer sitting, the birds that have learned to answer the columns. Together they make a room that no one built.

There are rooms with no visible entrances, found only by listening. To enter such a room, one must move toward a tone, or perhaps away from it, depending on how the city is arranging its organs at that hour. These rooms are occupied by echoes that have collected long ago and never fully departed. An echo, here, is not merely a repetition. It is a residue with texture. It clings to leaves, falls on the ground, nests in corners, and can make a place loud for days after a shout. Some echoes are benign; they settle into moss and become a low hum. Others are sharp; they make membranes tremble and keep birds circling without landing. Some are false, arriving before their cause: a footfall hears itself first, a voice comes out of the future of the room, a call returns from a side of the building that the caller has not yet reached.

There is, in some places, an absence that behaves like a creature.

A long straight street may be bordered by tall leaf-walls with no visible doors or windows. The ground is smooth. The air is cool. The light falls evenly, though the canopy above is plainly there if one looks up. On this street there are no animals. No insects. Not even moss. Yet the sound is still deafening, because the street itself emits a continuous low tone, not from anywhere, not from nowhere, but from all directions at once. This street is not empty in the ordinary way. It is inhabited by its own voice. Its resident is the tone, and perhaps the tone is a boundary condition of the city, a place where the city has made a room so pure it repulses bodies. Walking such a street is not safe. It is not dangerous either. It is merely a place where the city has removed the usual citizens and left only its own sustained attention.

The light is ordinary, which makes its habits feel like the habits of another kind of resident.

Sun falls through the canopy in normal beams, but inside the city it behaves as though it has been taught the wrong lessons. It bends around corners. It pools with no visible cause. In certain hollows, shafts of light stand almost solid, like pillars that can be touched without being seen. In others, light collects along edges as if heavy. This is not hallucination. The light is not glowing with magical fire; it is simply obeying the city’s local logic. The city has so many membranes, wet surfaces, curved walls, and standing air that ordinary light finds itself domesticated. It lives in the angles. It sleeps in the pools. It wakes the mosses. It reveals rooms that were there all along but only become visible when the sun has been admitted into the right hollow.

There may be a central inhabitant that no one has seen.

Near the city’s lowest fold, where the ground slopes toward a hollow whose bottom is not visible, warm air rises with a sound like slow breathing. The breath is not human. It is more like the exhalation of a large forest coming through one opening. In wet seasons, there may be a pond there. In dry seasons, there may be nothing. Or there may never have been a hollow in the simple sense, only a place where the earth folded itself inward and left a breathing aperture. The animals avoid it, or else stand too still around it. The leaf-backed frogs gather near its rim. The bellows-backs drink the spore-thick air rising from it. The jointed watchers sometimes stand at its edge and do not blink for long minutes. Whether a specific creature lives there is uncertain. What is certain is that the hollow behaves like a mouth. The city inhales through it. The city exhales through it. It may be the basin’s heart, not a heart of flesh, but a heart of pressure and moisture and low tone.

No one lives there alone.

The phrase “all life is part of the construction” is not poetic decoration here. It is literal civic law. The moss is wall. The vine is bridge. The bird is bell. The beetle is street-sweeper. The root-ferret is alarm system. The six-legged mammals are soil-turners and seed-carriers. The bellsingers are courtyards’ voices. The leaf-tubes are wind-pipes. The water is plumbing. The echo is memory. The city does not have a population that uses infrastructure. The city’s population is infrastructure, and its infrastructure is population. Every resident is simultaneously a dwelling, a tool, a signal, a passage, or a shelter.

If one insists on naming the principal resident, the name must be plural.

The Town of Living Roots. The Breathing Basin. The City That Listens. The Cupped Hollow. The Choir of Vegetative Architecture. None is sufficient. The place is not a single animal, because it does not behave with a single center. It is not a colony, because its parts are not identical. It is not a machine, because no device was ever made. It is not a natural forest, because it has streets. It is not a ruin, because it is too established, too self-possessed, too alive. It is a settlement-scale organism whose organization is urban without being human. Its citizens are root, leaf, animal, fungus-thread, water, light, sound, and growth.

It is warm, and everything lives too well.

The softness is not comfort. The warmth is not a hearth. The dampness is not a bath. The city’s climate is an environment that encourages overgrowth, and the inhabitants have learned to live inside this pressure. They grow toward the loudest places. They turn toward the driest or wettest air depending on their function. They adjust, adjust, adjust. That is the main activity of life here: not conquest, not escape, but adjustment. A creature that cannot tune itself becomes moss. A wall that cannot listen becomes timber. A room that cannot breathe becomes a hollow where something else grows into it until the mistake is corrected.

The city’s loneliness is real, but it is not the loneliness of absence.

One may stand in a great leafy hall and see only a single beam of ordinary sunlight touching a curved wall. There may be no dust in that beam because dust has learned not to move there. There may be no shadow shifting, no figure approaching. The city is full, yet it can feel abandoned by its own scale. This happens because the spacing is so deliberate, so acoustic, so strange. The emptiness is not empty. It is waiting. It is resonance room. It is the pause between notes that makes the chord possible. The inhabitants are distributed according to hidden rules, not because they are hiding from each other, but because the city thinks in distances.

A person entering would be noticed, but not necessarily in a way that includes greeting.

The city has no eyes for a human as a person. It has membranes that open when approached. It has streets that yield slightly to weight. It has hollows that offer sitting, moss-benches that expand by degrees, rooms that warm when a body enters, walls that breathe more audibly when someone stands too long in the dark. This is not hospitality in the human sense. It is responsiveness. The city does not welcome. It reacts. It incorporates movement, sound, scent, heat, and perhaps intention into its ongoing pattern. If a human stays too long, the human may begin to hear the echoes as words, may begin to step to the clicking of seed-mats, may begin to feel the wall behind them as a second back. If the human leaves, the city will not grieve. It will simply resume its arrangement of birds, water, root, tone, and moss.

The truth is that whatever lives here is not “someone” and not merely “something.”

It is a collective body made of many bodies. It is a town that breathes. It is a forest that has learned geometry and forgotten why. It is a low basin whose warmth holds like a word after speech. It is a small place containing great rooms of nowhere. It is a city of apertures, membranes, hanging roots, bell-birds, root-ferrets, spore-grazers, jointed watchers, pale mammals, water pockets, echo-residents, and moss that has grown into benches. It is a place where architecture is biology and biology has become settlement.

To ask who lives there is to stand in the Court of Seeds and listen as the ground opens and shuts in the wind, as small things scatter like dry rice, as a bird calls once and the courtyard fills with resonance so heavy it presses against the ribs, as a wall opens where there was no door, as a pool fills somewhere without reason, as a mossy bench unfolds slightly wider, as if offering room.

The city answers that it is inhabited.

Not by one creature. Not by many creatures alone. By the whole arrangement: the living floors, the breathing walls, the brief water, the ordinary light behaving strangely, the sound that does not fade, the spacing that feels empty but is full of interval, the animals that are bells and bridges and gardens, the fungi-threads that are ceilings, the root-streets that are roads because something in the organism wanted movement, the hollows that are rooms because something in the basin wanted meeting, the whole small cupped town of bark and pollen and wet limestone, alive in the way a body can be alive, alive in the way a chorus can be alive, alive in the way no one built and no tool made.

The city breathes.

The city listens.

The city lives.
