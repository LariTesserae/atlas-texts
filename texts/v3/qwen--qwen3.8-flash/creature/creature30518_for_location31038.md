Nothing that lives in the hollow was born there.

That is the first fact, if fact can mean anything so quiet. There are eggs, perhaps, but no hatching. There are young forms, perhaps, but no growing. There are old forms, perhaps, but no aging. The animals of the hollow exist in the way a held breath exists: fully shaped, fully present, and suspended before the next movement. They are not a population in the sense of generations, deaths, replacements, and hunger. They are an arrangement that has learned to keep itself alive by doing nothing else but being.

If one insists on names, the hollow’s residents could be sorted into the green ones, the wet ones, the clicking ones, the standing ones, and the ones that have too many wings. But the names do not hold. The living things bleed into the stone, the stone bleeds into the water, the water bleeds into the sound, and the sound itself seems, at times, to have a body.

---

The mosses are the first inhabitants, though they are not mosses as lowlands understand them. They do not creep with patient, indifferent appetite. They build. They maintain. A cushion of moss in the hollow is not a soft patch on rock but a bounded room, with edges almost too regular, as if the moss had consulted an invisible floor plan. It grows thick and green in places where the light is weakest and where the stone is most recently broken. It holds its own shape with unnatural discipline. Where one cushion ends, another begins at a precise distance, separated by a thin gutter of black silt, a crack, a line of mineral salt, or a tiny river that may not be water at all but liquid light.

Inside the moss, if one lifts it carefully—and lifting it seems almost an act of violence, though the moss cannot be killed by it—there are threads. Pale, almost translucent fibers run through the black silt. Some resemble fungal hyphae. Some resemble nerve filaments. Some resemble cable insulation stripped from a wire. They pulse, but not with fluid. They pulse with pressure. When a droplet strikes basalt somewhere in the hollow, the moss does not shake. It receives the information. A half-second later, a faint tremor passes through its green surface, as if it has remembered something before the event has finished occurring.

The moss also hosts smaller things. Lichen dots the margins like frost that never melts. Tiny crusts of mineral growth form rings around the shallow pools, and within those rings, almost invisible particles move in spirals. They may be spores. They may be mites. They may be fragments of some larger life that has chosen to live as dust. They drift through the moss without disturbing it, and the moss does not consume them. Between them, neither eats nor is eaten. They are merely parts of a pattern too slow for any visible mind to finish.

The pale mineral threads beneath the moss connect to the roots of the sedges, and the roots of the sedges do not root. They reach down into iron seams, into quartz fissures, into dark capillary cracks where moisture is not quite liquid and not quite vapor. They are not anchored as trees are anchored. They are wired as machines are wired. The sedges drink the vibration of the hollow. They drink the pressure of dripping stalactites, the hum of crystalline antlers, the cold shock of water striking metal-like rock. They flourish because the machine works, not because the soil is rich.

The shrubs are the most stubborn residents. There are few of them, but each one behaves as if the whole ecology has been arranged around its solitary dignity. A single shrub may stand in the middle of open basalt, its gray-green leaves waxy and tight, its branches holding shapes too careful to be accidental. It does not spread. It does not seed. It does not lean toward any sun, because no sun is truly there. Yet it lives with fullness. Its leaves remain green without fading. Its branches do not break. Its shadow falls sometimes where no shadow should fall, and if one watches long enough, the shadow may appear to breathe before the shrub does.

The flowering plants are even stranger. They rise from shallow pools or wet margins, their stalks impossibly delicate for such a cold, mineral place. Their petals look like translucent paper held up to a weak lamp. They are white, pale blue, silver-green, and a color close to rust but too clean to be rust. Each flower stands alone, or appears to stand alone. In the water beneath them, their reflections are wrong. Some upside down, some sideways, some duplicated in directions that do not match the pool’s surface. The flowers themselves do not turn to correct the error. They continue blooming in a place where blooming and reflection are not the same event.

Their petals fall without rotting. Petals lie on wet stone, fresh and intact, though no one can say how long they have been there. If a petal is lifted, it may feel warm, but the air is cold. If it is replaced, it may not be the petal one set down, though one could not prove that by sight, smell, or memory. The flowers do not fruit. They do not drop seeds. They bloom as a verb that has forgotten how to conclude.

In the black pools live the pale eel-thin creatures.

They are not eels, except in the way a current is a river, or a vein is a wire. Their bodies are narrow and translucent, long enough to move in one smooth gesture but slow enough to seem still. Through them run veins of copper-colored light. The light does not flow the way blood flows. It brightens in response to pressure changes: a hiss from a crevice, a click in the basalt, a droplet breaking on a ledge far below. When the hollow’s engine-like sounds deepen, dozens of copper veins flare beneath the surface of the pools. When silence seems to enter—though silence here is only a lower register—the creatures glow dimly, as if the machine has relaxed without stopping.

They do not swim. There is no water for them to push through. The pools are too shallow for depth, and the eel-like bodies lie suspended in a medium that behaves like water and yet refuses to obey water completely. They drift without current. They bend without muscle visible. They turn without beginning. Sometimes one faces the center of the pool; sometimes one faces the edge; sometimes one faces a direction that seems irrelevant to the pool’s shape. If approached too closely, the creature does not flee. Its reflection flees instead, scattering into smaller reflections in droplets that have not yet formed.

They have eyes, perhaps, but not eyes as animals have eyes. They have dark spots, pale spots, mineral nodules, or slight thickenings near the head, none of which can be confirmed as organs. They do not hunt. They do not feed visibly. Their mouths, if they have mouths, are not for taking. They are more likely for tasting the surface of the water’s tension, for receiving the chemical language of the basalt, for reading the pressure of air moving over a wet stone. They may consume vibration itself. They may live by being slightly more resonant than the pools that contain them.

At the edges of the black water, cilia-like structures tremble in perfect arrays. They may belong to the eels. They may belong to the moss. They may belong to no individual organism at all but be the hollow’s way of keeping the pools alive without flow. Each cilia moves, and the movement creates a current that goes nowhere. The current circles once, or many times, and then becomes the stillness it was made to disturb. Bubbles rise from the black silt, reach the surface, burst, and appear again in the same instant. If one watches a bubble, one may catch it before it is born, and afterward one may see it burst before it has formed. The eels pass through these events without noticing. They live inside the mistake of motion, or perhaps they are the mistake that has become flesh.

On the dry-looking stone—dry but never truly dry, for moisture creeps upward everywhere—live the larger insects.

Some are the size of a thumbnail, which in a high, cold, windless hollow feels absurdly large. Their carapaces are faceted as though cut by industrial machinery. They shine dull brass, copper, pewter, and black. Their legs are jointed like wire. Their antennae are thin and straight, and when they touch the basalt, they produce taps so small that the ear almost dismisses them, while the chest feels them. They crawl in geometric patterns across the glassy rock. Not foraging. Not fleeing. Not searching. They trace. A line of them may form a hexagon near a pool, then break into angles, then reform along a mineral seam. If one steps too near, the pattern does not scatter. It completes itself in a different sequence, as if the insects had been waiting for the disturbance to correct the design.

Some insects are too angular to be wholly biological. Their bodies have edges like cut metal, and their wing covers reflect the dim light in rectangles rather than curves. They crawl over basalt columns as though the rock were a working surface. They do not eat the moss. They do not drink from the pools. They walk through water and remain dry, or seem to. Their feet touch the trembling surface without breaking it. Sometimes they pause beside a floating flower petal and touch it with their antennae, and the petal moves slightly against the wrong direction of the current, and the insect continues on.

Others are tiny and numerous, moving in clouds that appear and disappear without dispersing. A cloud like this may hang above a marshy hollow for what seems minutes, then vanish, then reappear in a place one was not watching. It does not behave as a swarm. It behaves as a single event. One can count individual specks inside it, yet the total slips away each time. If a hand passes through the cloud, no insects scatter onto the skin. If a breath disturbs the air, the cloud does not thin. It remains, a perfect smudge of life that cannot be divided. These small ones are not born of the hollow’s air so much as the hollow’s air is born of them. They seem to belong to the upper darkness, where winged things move, but they also settle briefly on the moss, and where they settle, the moss glows faintly for a moment afterward.

There are also things on the silt margins that may be larvae, or beetles, or fragments of crustaceans that never completed themselves. They lie still in shallow water, their backs covered in pale mineral dust. They do not grow. They do not change. When a droplet falls on one, it tilts, and the water rings, and the droplet appears to have fallen again from the ledge above. Whether this is cause and effect or merely the place rehearsing itself cannot be said.

The clicking rodents live in the wet hollows beneath the sedges and moss.

They are small, quick, and too warm for a place that feels only cool. Their fur is the gray-gold of old iron seen through cloud, sometimes spotted with lichen-like pale rings. Their eyes are black and polished, reflecting everything but revealing nothing. They dart between roots, fungal threads, and mineral seams with movements too precise to be nervous. Their feet do not sink into the silt, though the ground should be soft. Each footfall produces a small metallic click, not from the rodent’s foot but from something beneath the surface, as if each strike depresses a hidden spring.

They do not dig. There are no burrows. There are no nests visible in the moss. They live in the open, between cushions of green, along the edges of pools, under pale flowering stalks, as if shelter were not required because the hollow has forgotten danger. If one stands still, a click-rodent may pass close enough that its shadow moves, though no light source changes. It may pause with one front foot raised, holding the posture of a leap. That posture may continue for a long time, or it may simply appear to have been there all along. If one watches it, it does not resume movement because of being watched. It resumes because movement is its native state.
