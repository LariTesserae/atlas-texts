There is no native that walks, crawls, feeds, hides, or mates. No burrowed thing shifts through the dust; no eye reflects the low glow; no mouth opens on the dry air to make sound. If the place has inhabitants, they do not come into the world and then stand in it. They are made of the world’s own making.

They are the Unmade Ones.

Not a people, not a species, not a race of spirits. The phrase only serves as a way of naming a presence that is almost impossible to separate from the land itself. They are not hidden. They are not absent because they fled. They are simply not there in the way ordinary beings are there. They are the warm emptiness inside a dome; the shape of a body-shaped hollow that contains no body; the pressure that raises one column of salt beside another until the columns seem to be a procession; the tendency of broken plates to fuse into something that almost looks like a wall.

A face is unnecessary to them because they have no eyes. They do not need to see where they are. They are the location.

They do not have hands either, so they do not build. They have conditions. When the heat rises through a fissure and the mineral fog thickens and settles, the ground changes. A shard lifts. A crack seals. A ring of stones stands in a place where no stones stood before. One might call this construction, but it is closer to a body growing a new limb, or a fever leaving a shape on the skin. They do not assemble tools because tools imply distance between maker and material. For them, the material is already flesh.

Their bodies are therefore not fixed.

A low dome of fused glass, split by a circular hole through its crown, may be the skull of something that cannot be seen, or it may be a place where the land is learning to breathe. A shallow rectangle of black glass with one side missing may be a torso, or a threshold, or a wound that wants to heal in the shape of a person. A body-shaped space pressed into warm ground may not be a memorial to some missing traveler; it may be a current body. If it is, then the inhabitant inside it is too diffuse to notice. It might be a heat gradient. It might be the faint sweet smell of hot clay. It might be the tiny pressure change that makes one’s ears pop while the air remains strangely warm.

They are most easily suspected at the edges of things.

A stone ring may be a footprint if footprints were circular and stood upright in the ground. A stack of slate-like fragments, balanced without mortar, may be a hand if hands were made of patience and fracture. A cairn of warm obsidian chunks that clinks when disturbed may be a tooth, a bone, a knot in a joint, a memory left behind by something that does not remember in the way living things remember.

There are no corpses there, because they do not die in that manner. They cease to be coherent. A colonnade of salt columns becomes an arcade becomes a street becomes a flat place with no trace except a subtle warmth in the air. This is not burial. It is revision. The land tries a shape, then tries again elsewhere. A new ridge may appear in the place where something has ended. A bowl of raw stone may appear in the place where something has been withdrawn. The place is full of endings that look like beginnings, which is why it feels unfinished even when it looks complete.

The Unmade Ones do not roam.

To say one “moved from here to there” would be a misunderstanding. They move the way heat moves: by transformation. One moment a section of ground is flat and silent. The next, two glass hills have risen beside it, bulging, cooling, curling at the edges. Did a being stand there? Did it lie down? Did it grow from the seam? No one can say. A line of black smoke rises from a crack, but instead of dispersing, it solidifies into a vertical filament, shatters, reforms farther along the seam. This is not a body walking. It is a body attempting itself in fragments.

Their territory is not marked by fences or nests. It is marked by repetition.

When a doorway faces where a chasm once was, it is not an error. It is a habit. When a low wall ends against an empty slope, it is not ruined. It is still remembering the slope’s previous shape. When markers are set in perfect circles around a hollow that has already filled with new stone, the land is not decorating a grave. It is saying, “Something stood here,” or “Something will stand here,” or “I know what it means to make a circle around a place.”

They may not know anything at all.

That is the terror and the beauty of them: they have all the signs of intention without a recognizable will. They do not scheme. They do not hunger. They do not love, protect, worship, or fear. And yet the ground keeps arranging itself into forms that suggest a resident. The forms are always faceless. That consistency is too precise to be meaningless.

If one insists on a “who,” then the answer is uncomfortable.

The who is not someone. The who is the fact that the place will not stop almost becoming a world.

There are lesser forms of them, if lesser forms can be called such.

The salt blooms are not plants, but they behave like colonies. Pale crusts spread in geometric rings, crackle when the thermal tide rises, then contract when the warmth retreats. They expand with something like appetite, though there is nothing to eat. Their edges glitter where a crack below leaks hidden glow. One could almost believe they are grazing on heat.

The crystal veins are not nerves, but they feel stress.

Branching veins of warm crystal spread across the floor of a hollow like dried capillaries. They are thin enough to be mistaken for roots, but they do not draw water from the ground because there is no water. They draw tension. A nearby fissure closes and one of the veins brightens faintly, its red-gold glow spreading inward from a hot seam, then fading. A slab shifts and a whole network of veins shivers with a sound like glass under wool. They are not alive, but they have the behavior of something that has learned how to be alive and then discarded cells for mineral economy.
