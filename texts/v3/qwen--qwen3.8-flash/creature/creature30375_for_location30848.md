In such moments the wind’s note is felt first in the rails, then in the teeth, then in the cistern lids, which answer with a faint metallic tap as though the city itself is being asked a question. The people of Anvane do not run from the question. They pause, raise chins, tilt ears, and wait to see what must be tightened, shifted, blessed, or moved.

The inhabitants of the Half-Made Terrace are not a single thing, not a tribe, not a species, not even a fully urban people. They are the product of a place that has not yet decided what it will be. If one speaks to them in formal terms, they are Anvaners, citizens of a high municipal district registered under odd legal headings and load-rating clauses. If one speaks to them in market voices, they are Terrace folk, High Works hands, Damp Side residents, fog-tenders, soft-decision people, basalt-born, bridge-walkers, cistern-rats, greenhouse-families, pump-hound kids, salt-herders, tram-sleepers, shadow-watchers. Most names are affectionate insults, most insults are accurate.

They live in a town that is not really a town so much as a series of inhabited islands in a sea of unfinished stone. There are districts that could be cities elsewhere, but here they appear as clusters of lamps, warehouses, gardens, schools, and narrow stone streets floating apart from one another. Between them lie open flats, dry basins, black-iron gaps, raw slopes, and wind. People travel from one district to another by tram, cable ferry, or long uphill walks. They do not speak of the distance between neighborhoods as “far.” They speak of it as “empty,” which is more honest.

The people who live on the Terrace are adapted to its strangeness, though not in a dramatic way. They are not strange because they are strange. They are strange because they have lived for generations in thin air, damp stone, and unsteady ground. Children grow up with wide rib cages and quiet voices. Not quiet because they are polite, but because shouting on a high plateau makes one dizzy too quickly and because most communication is meant to carry through wind. The adults are careful walkers. They know how to place their feet so they do not disturb fresh scree. They know when a sound beneath a floorboard is ordinary settling and when it is a warning. They can often tell by the rhythm of a pump whether a pipe is healthy, whether a bearing is tired, whether someone in the condensing station is asleep on duty, or whether a fault has opened somewhere below the visible world.

They value warmth above wealth. Gold, silver, copper, and iron matter, of course; Anvane exports machinery, condensed mineral water, salt-white hides, greenhouse herbs, and small precision instruments. But the first question when visiting a house is not about rooms or gardens. It is about the stove. It is about whether the walls hold heat, whether the greenhouse attached to the dwelling has cracked glass, whether the cistern is covered, whether the foundations have been recently surveyed. A family’s dignity is measured less by finery than by the stability of its ground and the reliability of its drip.

Most houses are built to endure without becoming arrogant. They are low or squat, their roofs long enough to throw water away from entrances, their eaves lined with iron channels that catch condensation and send it to storage barrels. Stone floors are common, but not always solid stone. In poorer districts, floors are made of adjustable slabs over felt-lined frames so that when the ground shifts a half-inch, the house does not shatter. Doors hang on iron rails rather than ordinary hinges. Windows are small in winter, wide in summer, and always shaded by condensation-collecting cloth that drips at night into collection troughs. Greenhouses are attached to many dwellings, not out of vanity but survival. They are warm boxes of glass, stone, and steam-pipe metal, holding turnips, beans, barley starts, medicinal herbs, moss tea, and sometimes a stubborn lemon tree that has lived forty years without producing fruit.

The people of Anvane do not build their whole lives indoors, but they do live with a practical reverence for shelter. Their public buildings are surprisingly grand in the way that engineering structures are grand: market halls with vast truss roofs, tramways with open sides, foundries with high windows, hospitals with thick insulated walls, schools with listening corridors built so that children can hear the difference between a stable basalt block and a “soft decision” substrate. A soft decision is not a place people avoid entirely; it is a place people respect. It is ground that the city has allowed to carry weight but does not yet trust. On a soft decision, houses may be moved by jack teams after an inspection. Streets may be repainted with warning rings. A tram may slow to a crawl when passing through a singing zone. Families who live on soft decisions often pride themselves on their adaptability. “We are not unstable,” they say, “we are merely available to change.”

The city’s inspectors are among the most recognizable inhabitants. They walk with measuring rods, listening tubes, chalk, small hammers, and notebooks. They tap stone. They watch shadows. They lean on railings and press their ears to pipes as though the infrastructure has secrets it is willing to tell if spoken to with patience. They are neither admired nor disliked, because they do something essential: they translate the land’s indecision into municipal action. If a building’s shadow falls slightly before the building itself, the inspector marks it and a crew arrives to adjust the frame, shift the roof, or move a load-bearing wall to the other side of the building. Some people call this the “shadow half-second,” a phrase used with equal fear and pride. “We had a shadow half-second on the east side of my grandmother’s kitchen,” someone might say, and the listener understands: the house was almost wrong.

The industrial workers are another visible class, though their neighborhoods are not separate so much as woven around furnaces, pumps, and rails. Condenser-tenders climb the lattices to clear coils and report mist density. Pump-houses throb with patient mechanical breaths. Tramwrights oil wheels and straighten tracks after nights when frost has made iron brittle. Foundry hands move in orange light, their faces shiny, their bodies thick from heat in a city that otherwise keeps everyone slightly chilled. Cable-ferry operators understand the tension of ropes and the silence of deep gaps. They know which cables hum when a wind passes, which bridges need to be walked slowly, which platforms are stable enough to hold freight but not crowds.

The water workers are perhaps the most respected and the most anxious. They are not always called water workers; in Anvane, water is too uncertain for such a simple title. Some call them condenser-readers, cistern-walkers, seam-followers, or “the people who know when to wait.” The Terrace’s rivers are tentative things. They appear as wet lines one season and shallow ponds the next. An irrigation channel may be official in one district and forgotten in another. The water workers follow them, carve new channels, repair old ones, monitor moss basins, and decide whether a wet seam should be made public, turned into a supply route, or left alone because it has not yet committed to becoming anything useful.

In the gardens, life is dense, green, and quietly extravagant. The terraced fields are maintained by families whose hands are never fully dry. They know that barren stone and sudden fertility can exist three feet apart. They plant hardy barley where wind is too sharp for trees, turnips where soil is new, beans where nitrogen must be coaxed into the ground, and ferns where water refuses to stop making itself into a secret. Imported lower-country soil is treated almost like livestock: fed, turned, covered, warmed. A garden with imported soil is not a luxury. It is a declaration that a family intends to stay. In districts where soil is made from composted moss, industrial ash, and human care, the ground feels like cloth underfoot, thick and living, and after condensation it steams like a bakery.

The animals of Anvane are as numerous as the people, perhaps more so.

On the high flats beyond the outermost lamps move the salt-white herds. These are the broad-chested grazers, the creatures sometimes called weather cattle, sometimes simply the Herd. They are not domestic in any familiar sense. Some are managed, some are avoided, some are hunted with care, some are counted by schoolchildren from rooftops. They have thick hooves for sharp stone, wide eyes for the glareless light, and coats the color of salt or dry cloud. Their breath rises in white plumes in the cool sun. They move slowly, in long patient lines, not because they are lazy but because they are reading the ground. A herd turning east may mean a moisture pocket is moving west. A herd clustered near a pale depression may mean frost is forming in a wet basin no human has yet noticed. A herd walking straight through a district at night may mean something older than the city has shifted below.

Herders are a small, independent people. They live at the edges, in shelters built around cable routes and condenser towers, where they can watch both human machinery and the open flats. They speak less than other Anvaners and are more willing to tolerate weather. They collect shed hair from the salt-white herds, not necessarily to harm them, because the hair is valuable when woven into rope, felt, or winter cloaks. They know which herds may be approached, which are too wary, which calves belong to mothers dangerous in the damp. They also know where bodies lie. On the high flats, the dead of both animals and people are sometimes left in marked stone circles, where weather and salt work them slowly into the land. This is not a romantic custom. It is practical, local, and accepted. The city does not encourage it, but it does not forbid it, because there is too much open ground and too few laws able to reach into it.

Birds are the Terrace’s messengers, whether anyone wants them to be or not. Long-winged birds circle above the rim, their calls flung across the basin like stones. They perch on viaducts and lamp towers, their wings dark against pale stone. Some are hawks, some are crows, some are things too large and too patient to be named with ordinary bird words. People watch them because the birds know distance. At this elevation, distance does not behave normally. A bird that looks tiny on the eastern rim may arrive over the market as a great shadow, silent until it folds its wings into the iron roof. Children learn to identify birds by the sound of their wings against wind, not just by shape. A flock leaving a ridge in one dark smear can become a thousand individuals before noon, and the change is so abrupt that visitors sometimes say the birds have multiplied. Locals only shrug.
