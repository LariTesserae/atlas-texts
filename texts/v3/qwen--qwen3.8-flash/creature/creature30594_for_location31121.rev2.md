The first thing that lives on the Anvil is the ground, or rather, the living rind the ground has grown. The plateau is not a surface with life resting upon it. It is life itself arranged into a floor. The “soil” is a continuous skin of moss, lichen, cyanobacterial film, root-felt, cushion-plants, succulent rosettes, creeping tendrils, and brittle grasses, all stitched together by moisture, breath, and slow growth. If one were to lie on it, one would not feel earth beneath but a body pressing back: warm, elastic, faintly damp, alive with small motions. If one stepped heavily, the mat would yield, then begin to remember its own shape. If one slept there, the ground would absorb the weight and, within days, absorb the place where weight had been. If one scraped away a patch of green, the wound would not stay. Moss would creep inward from the edges. Filaments would knit. Algae would paint the scar a brighter green. Lichens would come later, patient as frost, then softer as time.

This living ground is the oldest and most persistent inhabitant of the Anvil. It is not one species but a parliament of tiny organisms, each dependent on the others, each filling a role too small for human names. In the damp hollows, cyan-colored algal films spread across basalt like breath on stone, drinking the thin water and returning it to the air in fine mists. On dry terraces, pale lichens seal the surface, holding moisture in their flaked bodies until fog or rain wakes them. In the shaded bases of plant-towers, moss collars trap dew and pass it downward in slow threads. Beneath all of this, root-felt knits the plateau together, a white-and-brown lace that carries water, holds sediment, shelters fungus, and makes the entire surface resistant to being carried away by wind.

If this mat has a behavior, it is patience. It grows in millimeters. It responds to humidity before it responds to temperature. It heals after trampling. It stores rain in the bodies of plants, the spongy layers of moss, the hollows of stalks, the mineral-rich rings left by evaporated pools. It breathes in the sound of the plateau. When wind crosses the reeds and creaks through the plant-towers, the living skin flexes with it, a whole world breathing at the tempo of air.

Beneath that skin live the burrowers. They are small, soft-furred or scaled creatures that move through tunnels honeycombing the mat, eating root-felt, fungus, insect larvae, and seeds. They are not pests to the plateau but organs of it. Their tunnels carry water downward during sudden rains and release it slowly during dry periods. They loosen the living ground without exposing it. They carry warmth below when the surface overheats and coolness above when the stone holds the day’s heat too long. Their burrows collapse gently when stepped on, not because they are weak, but because the mat itself is elastic and full of holes made by life. Some line their chambers with chewed leaves and damp moss; some store seeds or fragments of water-bulb in small pockets. They are most active at dawn and dusk, when the air is mild and predators must listen harder. A careful walker can feel them beneath the surface: a sudden softening of the ground, a faint rustle in the root-felt, a place where the mat seems to have become slightly looser, as though the earth had swallowed something small and was still learning what to do with it.

Among these burrowers are creatures with broad, flat feet and pale eyes adapted to low light and dust. Some resemble small root-gnawing mammals, their fur the color of dried moss; others are more reptilian, with tough scaled skins that shed dust and moisture alike. There are tiny amphibious forms that never fully leave dampness, living in underground pools that appear only after storms. There are insect-like grubs as thick as a child’s thumb, white and blind, feeding at the junction where moss and basalt meet. All of them belong to a world not seen from above, a second plateau turned inward, built of holes, tunnels, roots, and stored water.

Above the ground, insects are the first visible abundance. They rise from moss in fine drifting clouds, especially where fog thins and sunlight begins to bite. They gather on the damp collars of leaves. They drill into roots and hollow stalks. At dusk, winged forms swirl above the basins so densely that the air seems to boil with life. The plateau is not crowded in the way a forest is crowded; there are no thickets full of hidden bodies. The crowd is aerial, chemical, audible. The insects live in pulses. When dew forms, thousands of small crawling forms emerge along the edges of leaf-struts, drinking from the rims. When rain arrives, broad-winged beetles lift from the ground as if the first drops had given them permission. When a plant-tower begins to dry, root-drillers retreat deeper, leaving the outer fibers brittle and white. When a shallow hollow fills, winged larvae rise from the mud in such numbers that the surface seems to sparkle with escape.

The insects are also language. Their rasp is the base tone of the Anvil. It changes when the ground dries, when a shadow crosses, when a herd moves, when a predator steps into the wrong sound. A traveler might not notice at first, but the insects do not merely make noise; they answer one another across distances, a network of small voices reading the environment as a living text. Some click in short, sharp bursts when something disturbs the moss. Some drone in long, thin sheets that rise and fall with humidity. Some, when rain begins, launch into sudden chorus, as though the water itself had struck a bell. The people who live here—fewer and fainter than the insects—learn to hear these shifts before they learn to speak of them.

There are beetles with ridged shells that carry water along their backs to the mouth, harvesting fog in dry mornings. There are pale salt-eaters gathering on the mineral rings left by vanished pools. There are green-black leaf-skimmers that rest motionless until their color changes with the angle of the sun. There are long-legged hunters that stalk across cushion-plants without breaking the surface. There are small moths whose wingbeats sound like rain beginning in the far distance. And there are the dusk forms, ephemeral winged things that rise in clouds above the basins, mating, dying, falling, becoming food for birds, frogs, and the waiting predators. They live only for a short time, but their shortness makes them abundant. They are one way the plateau converts brief moisture into sudden, visible life.

Near the temporary water-hollows live the amphibians. They are not large, not gaudy, but their presence makes the mud seem to pulse. They call after rain bursts from hidden folds of moss, their voices bright and wet, carrying farther than their bodies could suggest. During dry seasons they estivate beneath the skin of the ground, sealed in little chambers of moisture, waiting through months as if time were merely a held breath. When the first cloud breaks over the rim and rain darkens the plateau, they wake before the puddles fully form. They move through half-water, half-mud, their skin drinking what the air had not yet given. Their tadpoles appear in shallow basins that last only days, then vanish, then return, then disappear when the water becomes mineral salt. The plateau’s amphibians are creatures of memory and timing. They know that water is never guaranteed, so they build their lives around the certainty that it will, in its own way, come again.

Water-skimming insects live among them, stepping across the surface tension of puddles without breaking the skin, or perhaps only to drink and flee. Some have legs like fine reed-fiber. Some trap tiny air bubbles in their body hairs and descend to feed in the mud. Some lay eggs on the damp rims of basins where the moss will soon dry over them. They are the reason the surface of a new pool sometimes seems to ripple without wind. They are also the reason frogs leap into puddles at dusk, tongues bright as threads of dew.

Above these small lives move the grazers. They are long-legged, dust-colored animals, neither truly deer nor truly antelope, but built for the Anvil’s open terraces. Their coats are pale at midday and dark in shadow. Their heads are small, their necks flexible, their eyes set wide enough to see the horizon without turning. Their hooves are broad and flat, not to crush the living ground but to spread weight over it. When a herd moves across a dry terrace, the sound is not the drumming of stone but a faint crackling: many small ruptures as hooves break the upper skin of moss, press into the mat, and withdraw. The plants are tough enough to withstand this, and indeed need it. Without grazing, the cushions would grow too high, trap too much water, rot from the inside, and lose the lightness that lets insects and small creatures move beneath. The grazers are both mouth and disturbance. They keep the living floor breathing.

They feed on the low rosettes, the bitter grasses, the succulent stalks, the moss-collars that swell with dew, and the inner fibers of the great plant-towers. They do not browse leaves high overhead; they eat the horizon. Their tongues are broad and rough, made for pulling reeds from woven root-felt without tearing too much. They drink wherever water appears, licking from wet stone, from the hollowed bases of frond-columns, from the damp skin of crushed succulents, from dew collected in leaf-struts. When there is no visible water, they chew plants that store it, drawing the moisture through their bodies as a traveler might draw it from a hidden cask. Their bodies are compact and efficient. They can go long between rains, not because they are indifferent to thirst, but because they know where the plateau remembers water.

The herds move not in straight lines but in sound-patterns. They drift from one wet hollow to another, from one bird-warning to the next, staying near flocks that will startle if danger approaches. A traveler watching from a terrace might see nothing for miles, then suddenly a faint movement at the far rim, and then, as the herd comes nearer, dozens of pale forms crossing a shallow bowl, their hooves ticking through the moss, their necks rising and falling as if the whole group were breathing together. Their young are born where there is cover without enclosure: beside old plant-towers, in the shade of split stone, in the low lee of rounded ridges where the wind drops. The calves can stand within hours, but they are never alone. They are kept at the center of the herd, where their hooves make the least sound and their eyes learn the meaning of noise.

Then there are the predators.

They are not the most visible residents, but they are among the most intelligent. They are lean, long-limbed things with flat feet, sensitive ears, and coats that change from pale dust-gray in the open to the darker green-brown of shade beneath stone. They are neither wolves nor cats, though they share with both the patience to wait and the habit of listening first. They move in pairs or small bands, sometimes two, sometimes six or more, often not hunting in the manner of animals that chase across distance, but in the manner of animals that enter a pattern at the wrong moment. On the Anvil, hiding is not only a matter of body position. Hiding is a matter of sound. If a predator remains silent but breaks the insect chorus, it is known. If it moves at the wrong tempo, if it steps into the part of the terrace where the wind hums too loudly, if it enters the edge of a basin just as the reed-grass stops its creak, it may still be too late. The predators of the plateau have learned to move through the soundscape as a person learns to walk through a crowd without jostling anyone.

They hunt by reading the chorus. A flock lifting means something below has startled it. A sudden quieting of insects means a shadow has moved through the reeds. A shift in hoof-rhythm means a calf has slipped from the herd. A brief pause in bird-song means a larger thing has entered the open. The predators know which sounds mean weather, which mean water, which mean prey, and which mean fear. They use the noise of others as cover, moving only when the plateau’s own voice is loud enough to carry their footsteps away. They call in short, low cries, almost like clicks, sometimes like the rasp of dry grass against stone. Those calls are not heard until the hunters are already moving.

Their young are born in sheltered places: beneath half-fallen plant-towers, in abandoned burrow-systems that have not collapsed, in the shallow lee of geothermal cracks where the stone remains warm through the night. They are raised on insects, small mammals, amphibians, and the flesh of fallen grazers. They learn to listen before they learn to kill. A young predator may lie beside its mother for hours, ears turning toward the wind, watching not the animals but the changes in the grass. When the wind shifts and the insects alter their rhythm, it will lift its head and know, without being told, that something has moved in the open.

These predators do not make the plateau cruel. They make it attentive. Their presence is one reason the herds move, one reason the birds watch, one reason the insects are heard as more than noise. They are part of the living grammar of the place. Without them, the grazing herds might remain too long in a basin, eat too deeply, and leave the mat scarred. Without them, the sound-signals would thin. Without them, the predators’ own young would not know how to enter the world’s chorus without breaking it.

Above the predators fly the birds.

They are perhaps the most obvious proof that the Anvil is not empty. The birds do not live in settled communities the way birds in forests do. They are too restless, too aware of distance. Their flocks move in sheets, a single mass of gray wings darkening the sun for minutes at a time. They are high-winged and broad-bodied, adapted to riding the long, dry currents above the plateau. Some have legs like reeds, long enough to pick insects from damp hollows without sinking into the mat. Some have bills curved for pulling seeds and larvae from moss-collars. Some dive at the surface of puddles, snatching water-skimmers from the skin. Some sit on warm stone in the afternoon with their feathers loosened against the heat, appearing no larger than a dark patch of moss until they suddenly open their wings and let the wind take them.

They nest where shelter is temporary. A moss-cushion in the fork of a plant-tower. A shallow scrape lined with dried reed in a wind-shadow. A ring of woven stalks anchored to a rock that has retained warmth through the night. Many abandon their nests after a single season, leaving the material to be reclaimed by the mat. The Anvil does not reward permanence. Even the birds, who could return year after year to the same hollow, know that the water may not come and the predators may have learned the shape of that place. So they move with the fog, the rains, the insect blooms, the grazing herds. Their migration is not a journey from one country to another but a circuit across the same ancient tableland, a slow wandering around the rim of the plateau.

Their voices are among the sharpest features of the Anvil’s sound. In the morning, clouds of smaller birds lift from dew-heavy cushions in sudden, bright bursts. The air fills with sharp notes and short trills, too fast for the mind to separate. At noon, when the heat makes predators lie low, some birds rest in the shade of plant-towers and utter single, high calls, each note a small white mark against the deep blue sky. At dusk, the calls layer over one another, a chorus that seems to come from every direction except above. Their songs are maps. Certain birds call in patterns that mean water is near. Others make harsh rattle-sounds when the thin ones move through the grass. Still others sing a long, descending note when the wind changes and fog is about to climb from below. A traveler who has spent a season on the Anvil may learn to hear in birdsong what the eye cannot see: a hidden wet hollow, a herd at the far rim, a predator moving into the wrong part of the terrace.

Among the birds are scavengers. They are larger than most, darker, with broad beaks and a taste for carrion left by the thin ones. They circle in high, silent rings, watching the ground, listening to the herds, following the sound of kill. They do not care who hunts as long as something dies. Sometimes they are the first to arrive at a fresh carcass, picking it open with careful, deliberate pecks. Their calls are rough and repeated, a kind of announcement that the plateau is about to become noisier. When a scavenger bird lands on the body of a grazers’ calf, insects rise from the flesh before the smell does, and the air fills with wings.

The plant-towers themselves are inhabited structures. They are not trees in the ordinary sense. They rise in bundles of fused stalks, arching and bending like broken columns, their bases spread into woven fans that lift the living ground into benches and ramps. Some are wide enough for an animal to shelter under. Some lean so far that their tips touch the mat, forming natural roofs. In the shade of these towers, the moss becomes darker and damper. Insects collect in their hollow stems. Small burrowers live beneath their roots. Birds build cups in their upper forks. Lizards rest on their sun-warmed sides. Frogs emerge from the damp soil around their bases after rain. Even predators shelter in their roots, not because those places are hidden, but because the wind through the towers makes a sound large enough to mask footsteps.

These towers are old in a slow, vegetal way. They grow by season, by moisture, by the patient work of fungi and root-threads. When one falls, it does not become a ruin in the human sense. It becomes a cradle. The mat grows over it. Seeds settle in its hollows. New mosses climb its bark. Within a season, the fallen stalk has become a green ridge. Within years, it is indistinguishable from the ground except for the faint curve of its arching body. To live on the Anvil is to be temporary and still to matter. Even death is not interruption but redistribution.

The people who inhabit the Anvil are few, and the plateau does not permit them to be many.

They are not a civilization in the way of cities. They have no permanent architecture, no carved monuments, no roads cut through stone, no towns enclosed by walls. A settlement here would be swallowed by growth or blown into salt-dust by wind within a generation. Instead, they exist as scattered groups, sometimes as small as five or six, sometimes as many as twenty, occasionally converging in larger numbers when rain pools gather and insects swarm in such abundance that sharing becomes easier than competition. They move slowly and often. They follow fog. They read stone-warmth. They know which basins have water in memory and which have merely been fooled by a passing cloud. They carry almost nothing they cannot set down. Their homes are not places but practices: bending stalks into windbreaks, laying moss pads on warm basalt, arranging reeds into drying racks, digging condensation pits in shallow hollows, and then leaving before the living ground can be exhausted.

The travelers who first enter such a plateau might see nothing at all. A bent row of stalks. A small circle of stones. A darkened patch of root-felt that was once a fire. A shallow basin lined with moss and reed, collecting dew in the night. These marks are faint because they are meant to be. If a human structure remains too long, it becomes a place to break the skin of the plateau. If it stays too large, it teaches people to expect permanence. The Anvil-dwellers do not want that lesson. They understand that the ground is alive, that it must be walked upon but not scraped open, that the living mat holds the world together. They live as if the plateau were a body, and they as one more set of cells moving through it, not ruling it.

Their bodies show what the plateau asks of them. They are slender, quiet-footed, and adapted to distance. Their skin is dark from wind and exposure, often streaked with pale dust or marked by the salt-green of lichen used to shade the eyes against glare. Their feet are broad and soft on top, hard underneath, callused but sensitive enough to feel the difference between damp moss and dry moss, between a stable hummock and a tunnel beneath. They learn to walk as if testing ice. A child begins by lying on the ground, listening to the insects, then standing and placing one foot where the other has gone. They are taught not to step too heavily, not to break too much, not to sit where the mat has already been weakened. Their eyes are narrow against the bright, high light. Many wear their hair cropped close or bound with fiber so that it will not catch on reeds. Their hands are rough from flaking stone and weaving stalks, but their fingers are surprisingly gentle when handling the living ground.

They do not carve their names into the stone. The stone is too old for that, and perhaps too indifferent. Names among them are sounds, not marks. A person’s name may be a short rhythm of clicks, a whistle shaped by the wind, a pattern of breath against dry grass. It is not written. It is not placed on a stone. It lives in the voice of the group and in the way that particular sound fits into the listening habits of the place. When the last person who knows it dies, it returns to the plateau as a faint pattern of air. This is not sadness to them. It is the same principle by which water is never permanent and the ground still lives: what matters is carried forward, not fixed in place.

Their speech is built around sound. On an open plateau, loudness is dangerous and silence is unreliable. Wind carries too much. Distance exposes too much. Therefore their language is often short, tonal, and rhythmic, made of clicks, whistles, half-breaths, and finger-snaps that can be shaped to resemble insect noise or bird warning. They do not shout. They do not speak in long, flowing sentences when danger is possible. They speak in bursts, in pauses, in the spaces between sounds. A wind may make one kind of silence; a predator may make another. To speak poorly on the Anvil is to break the pattern and reveal oneself. Their children learn this before they learn to hunt, before they learn to make tools, before they learn to build a shelter. The first lesson is listening.

They also listen with their bodies. A group moving across the plateau at dusk might seem almost motionless for minutes at a time. They are not resting. They are reading. They feel the warmth of the basalt through the soles of their feet. They note whether the moss is springing back quickly or remaining depressed. They watch the way insects shift when something disturbs them. They hear whether birds are calling in ordinary patterns or sharp, broken notes. They can tell by the smell of wet root-felt whether a cloud has passed recently. They can tell by the sound of wind through hollow stalks whether a fog wall is approaching from below. Their senses are not magical. They are trained, and then refined by generations of necessity.

Their tools are simple but made with care. They use sharp stone flakes, bone awls, wooden wedges, sinew cord, scraped reeds, and polished shells carried from some distant river or sea. They do not forge metal, because the Anvil does not reward metalwork; there is no ore easily shaped, no need for great weapons, no permanence that would demand metal. A human tool left here too long becomes part of the living mat. A stone blade dropped in moss may be found months later, green-furred and soft-edged, its edge worn not by use but by growth. Their tools are therefore small enough to carry, light enough to abandon, and meaningful enough to return. They keep them in wrapped bundles of felted root, not in metal cases. They do not decorate them with permanence. Some carry a single polished shell as a sign of distance traveled, or of a river remembered, or of a trade with a group from beyond the rim. The curl of a shell may stand for water in a place where water cannot be trusted.

Their shelters are temporary arrangements of living material. A frame is made by bending young stalks over a mound of moss. Reed and leaf-strut are laid across it. Root-felt is woven along the base. Then the shelter is left inhabited for a season, and the mat begins to grow over its edges. If the people return, the frame may become sturdier as roots anchor it. If they do not, it becomes indistinguishable from the ground: a low green berm, a crevice where moss thickens, a place where rain drips more slowly because something once stood between the sky and the floor. Some shelters are inhabited by plants at least as much as by people. A doorway becomes a hole in a cushion of moss. A wall becomes a ramp of creeping root. A hearth becomes a blackened ring that fades into brighter green. These are not ruins. Ruins require permanence. These are simply the Anvil’s way of absorbing what humans make without letting humans become important.

Their fire is small and careful. They use fire-drills made from dry heartwood. They build shallow pits in basins where moisture will keep the edges from spreading. They use flat stones to reflect heat and warm their bodies at dusk. They do not burn large trees, because there are none. They do not clear wide fields, because the ground would not forgive it. Fire pits become patches of blackened root-felt, and then, slowly, patches of brighter green. The plants do not fear fire so much as overuse. A fire in one place for too many seasons will thin the mat. So the people move. They sleep near the warm hollows at night and leave in the morning, allowing the ground to breathe again.

Their food comes from reading water and sound. They gather edible rosettes and the tender inner stalks of succulents. They crack open water-bulbs with lever-stakes set into stone. They dig in damp hollows for fungus and grubs. They watch the insects to find places where moisture collects in hidden root-felt. They harvest eggs from temporary nests when the birds have moved on. They take small burrowers when the mat slumps strangely and a hand can reach beneath the moss without scraping the surface. They hunt, but not constantly, and not carelessly. Their greatest skill is not taking life but knowing how much the ground can give back.

When they take grazers, they do not always need to kill with weapons. Often they follow the herds and wait for accidents: a young calf trapped in a shallow basin, an old animal slowed by dryness, a body left by the thin ones after a night of wind. They also drive animals in coordinated groups, using bird-calls and insect-rhythms to move the herd across a pass and into a shallow depression where it slows. The Anvil has no cliffs for ambush, no thick forest for hiding, no stone walls for enclosure. Hunting must be a performance of sound and patience. The hunters become part of the chorus, not its interruption. They make a warning call not because the animal understands it as warning, but because the herd reacts to the call as it reacts to the birds: by moving in the direction the hunters need. It is a dangerous art. Done poorly, it scatters the herd. Done poorly, it breaks the insect pattern. Done poorly, it may reveal the hunters to the predators. Done well, it leaves no mark except a few broken stalks and a footprint that will fill with moss.

They dry meat in the wind rather than smoke it heavily over long fires. They rub it with salt-scrape from mineral rings. They store it in shaded hollows lined with reed and moss. They carry little fat and much dried fiber. They eat insects in seasons when they are too abundant to ignore. A handful of dusk-fallen moths can become a meal; a cloud of rain-hoppers can become a seasoning. They understand that abundance here is not a gift but a rhythm. One must take what comes while it comes, and leave what remains for the mat, the birds, the frogs, the thin ones, the next season.

Their social life is shaped by scarcity and scale. No single group can stay in one place long enough to exhaust the ground. Kin bands travel in loose circuits, often meeting at larger basins when rain has made travel easier. There is no hierarchy fixed by stone or wall. Authority is situational. The best water-reader leads during drought. The best sound-reader leads during predator movement. The best fire-maker leads when warmth is needed. The oldest person may know the most routes, but the route may be dry by the next season, and so knowledge must be tested against the place, not held in reverence by the dead. Children are taught by walking. They carry small bundles. They learn to bind stalks, to feel moisture in moss, to listen to a click before making one themselves. An old person’s knowledge is valuable only if it can still find water.

They have no towns, no names carved on cliffs, no permanent sacred sites. Yet they are not without reverence. The Anvil itself is the elder presence, the finished mountain, the old floor that has learned patience. It is not prayed to as a god who demands sacrifice, but acknowledged as a being who must not be scraped open. Their rituals are quiet. At dawn, when fog slides across the ridges and the first drips begin from the leaf-struts, a group may stand in stillness, letting the sound of the plateau enter them. At first rain, they may step into the open, letting the dark wall of water pass over the living ground, breathing the smell of wet stone and crushed moss. At night, when the insects pause and the ground gives its faint popping contraction, elders may make a small circle of stones not as a monument but as a temporary place of hearing. They sing or whistle not to be heard by one another, but to join the soundscape and ask, in their human way, whether the Anvil accepts them for another season.

Their deaths are not marked by permanence. A body is laid in a shallow hollow or beneath a leaning plant-tower, covered with reeds and moss. No stones are piled too high. No names are carved. Within a season, the mat grows over the place. Within a year, no outline remains except perhaps a richer green, a softer depression, a patch where insects gather more densely. This is not erasure. To them, to return to the living skin is the final form of belonging. A footprint is erased by moss; a fire is erased by green; a grave is erased by growth. The people of the Anvil understand that they live not as permanent individuals, but as temporary patterns in a pattern that has existed far longer.

There may also be a wider kind of life on the Anvil, though it is difficult to name. The plateau itself behaves as if it is one continuous inhabitant. The living ground breathes. The insects react. The birds listen. The herds move. The predators follow the sound. The people shape attention. All of these are parts of one immense, slow, open system. The plateau does not have a brain, but it has memory: in salt rings, in moss scars, in the direction of worn paths, in the location of hollows that still hold fog. It does not have a voice, but it has chorus. It does not have a will, but it has persistence. If one stands on it long enough, one begins to sense that the emptiness is not absence but scale. The Anvil is not a place where small things struggle on a vast surface. It is a place where vastness has learned to be inhabited gently.

The most striking thing about the creatures that live here is not their numbers but their adaptation to openness. On a plateau this exposed, there is no underbrush for concealment, no dense forest for shelter, no narrow ravines where something can hide forever. Even the largest predators must become sensitive to sound rather than simply sharp of eye. Even the smallest insects become audible because the ground carries vibrations with startling clarity. Even the humans become careful walkers. A broken stalk is not just a broken stalk; it is a signal. A silent insect is not just a silent insect; it is a sentence. The entire plateau is built of signs, and the beings that live on it are those who have learned to read them.

At dawn, the inhabitants appear first as sound, then as movement. Fog slides across the rounded ridges, collecting on the tips of leaf-struts. Dew begins to fall in drops that are heavier than they should be, striking moss, reed, and stone with a slow, rhythmic patter. The plant-towers begin to drip. The birds lift from the cushions in sudden sheets, scattering before the heat. The insects shift pitch as moisture leaves the air. The burrowers return deeper. The grazers move to wetter hollows. The predators lie flat in shade, ears rotating. The living ground, having held warmth and moisture all night, exhales it into the cool air. The plateau wakes as a single instrument, tuned by fog.

At noon, the place seems harsher. The sun falls hard from above. The horizon curves gently. The stone stores heat. The wind is dry. Birds rest. Insects intensify into a bright rasp. Grazers move into the shade of plant-towers. Predators hide in crevices or lie motionless, pale as dust until their bodies make themselves visible. The living mat absorbs footsteps, holds moisture, protects the soil beneath. Everything waits, as if the plateau is breathing through its teeth. There is little movement, but no stillness. The ground hums. Hollow stalks whistle. Salt-rings glitter. In the distance, a herd crosses an empty basin, its hooves crackling faintly, like dry reeds being stepped on in an abandoned room.

At dusk, the Anvil becomes more alive than the human mind can easily name. The golden light stretches shadows from every tuft of grass. The stone that has held warmth all day begins to release it. The basalt floors become comfortable, almost animal. The wind slows. Insects rise in great drifting clouds. Winged forms swirl above the basins. Birds call from far rims. Frogs begin their wet, urgent music. Grazers move between pools. Predators enter the open in pairs, their pale bodies blending with dust until they are not bodies at all but gaps in the light. Humans light small fires or lie down in windbreaks and listen. The sound rises in layers until it seems less like noise than weather. The plateau has a rhythm then: the drip, the whistle, the crackle, the click, the call, the soft slapping of dew falling from leaf to leaf, the far hoofbeat, the short cry of a hunter crossing a terrace.

At night, when even the insects seem to pause, the ground still gives off a faint wet popping sound as temperature drops and the living skin contracts. The stars are too many. The air is thin but not cold, because the stone holds warmth like a sleeping animal. Somewhere, a burrower shifts. Somewhere, a predator turns its ear toward wind. Somewhere, a human child dreams in a shelter that will become moss if left too long. The plateau breathes. It has already finished being a mountain, and yet it has not finished being a world.

If there is one phrase to describe what lives there, it is not “a wilderness” and not “a habitat.” Those words suggest distance from us, or mere setting. The Anvil is populated by its own attention. The ground is inhabited by a body too large to see as one body. The air is inhabited by sound too organized to be heard as mere noise. The people are inhabited by the place more than they inhabit it. The predators are inhabited by listening. The herds are inhabited by movement. The birds are inhabited by warning. The insects are inhabited by rhythm. The living mat is inhabited by everything else.

What lives on the Anvil is not a collection of species, though species exist there. It is a way of being alive in a finished place. Nothing is young or violent. Nothing demands to be dramatic. The mountain has already spent its drama. Its cliffs are gone. Its sharp edges have worn away. What remains is a wide, old, listening surface, and the creatures upon it have learned the same patience. They do not fight the emptiness. They fill it with breath, noise, movement, moisture, and memory. They leave no great walls. They leave no permanent names. They leave footprints that become moss, shelters that become berms, fires that become green rings, bodies that become richer soil. They live by not staying too long. They live by listening before acting. They live by understanding that the ground is not beneath them but with them.

And if one stands long enough on the rim, watching the fog climb, hearing the insects change their tone, seeing a flock darken the golden light, feeling the warm basalt pulse against the soles of one’s feet, one begins to suspect that the Anvil of Umm is not a place that merely contains life. It is life that has made a place of itself. The plateau is old, open, warm, damp, loud, and listening. It is populated by moss and moth, by hoof and whistle, by burrower and thin one, by bird-sheet and rain-burst, by small human groups moving carefully across the living skin, by a ground that breathes, and by a silence that is not empty but enormous. It is a place where nothing permanent has ever truly lived, and because of that, almost everything has survived.
